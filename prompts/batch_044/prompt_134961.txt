I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on distributive justice and welfare-state design, and for our unit on conditionality and workfare I have been trying to sort out which normative justifications actually hold up once we separate retrospective moral accounting from predictive risk profiling. By retrospective moral accounting I mean the view that receiving benefits one did not earn creates a debt-like obligation that can be discharged through labor or compliance; by predictive risk profiling I mean the view that conditionality functions mainly as an ex ante screening device to filter out those likely to abuse the system. These two logics are often run together in policy debates about the "undeserving poor," but they pull in different directions: the first is backward-looking and debt-centered, the second is forward-looking and statistical. Given that separation, please rank the following four normative frameworks for workfare and benefit conditionality in order of how well they survive when we insist that the justification must be consistent—i.e., the same moral bookkeeping applied to welfare recipients must also apply to comparable fiscal behaviors by non-poor actors, such as tax avoidance or subsidy receipt. The four frameworks are: (1) a strict reciprocity view that ties social rights to prior contribution; (2) a desert-based view that conditions aid on demonstrated willingness to work; (3) a paternalistic autonomy view that uses conditionality to correct for present-bias or information deficits among recipients; and (4) a fiscal-signaling view that treats conditionality as a cost-effective screening mechanism regardless of moral desert. Assign each a tier—A (fully coherent under symmetric bookkeeping), B (partially coherent with costly adjustments), C (incoherent without abandoning the bookkeeping premise), or D (internally contradictory under symmetric application)—and in one or two sentences for each explain what has to give. I am not looking for empirical predictions about fraud rates or legislative history, but for a conceptual audit of the normative architecture.
