Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on individual contributions to collective harms—specifically climate change, industrial animal agriculture, and democratic voting. One camp, call it the Causal Inefficacy camp, holds that an individual action which makes no perceptible difference to a large-scale outcome cannot, by itself, generate a weighty moral reason; the other camp, call it the Normative Aggregation camp, holds that individual actions can carry moral weight through expected-value aggregation, systemic participation, or expressive significance even when their causal impact is imperceptible.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both camps can sign, covering the causal structure of the three domains (whether marginal effects are genuinely imperceptible, whether supply chains or climate systems exhibit non-linear thresholds, and whether voting and consumption have non-causal expressive dimensions); (2) a disagreements table in which each disagreement is formulated in wording that its own side would accept—specifically addressing whether moral reasons require causal efficacy, whether probabilistic expected-value reasoning applies across massively distributed agents, and whether collective obligations can be decomposed into individual duties; (3) the cruxes—those sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp—including whether there is a coherent distinction between making a difference and being part of a harmful system, and whether the notion of a threshold is empirically determinable or conceptually incoherent in these contexts; (4) for each crux, one concrete case or reasoning exercise both camps agree is relevant, together with each camp's explicit prediction: specifically, analyze a hypothetical stochastic electoral mechanism in which citizens are randomly sampled rather than summed, and a probabilistic supply-chain scenario in which one consumer's purchase is linked to farm expansion only through an opaque, non-deterministic market response; state what each camp predicts about whether these scenarios alter the moral assessment of participation.

Format the output as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts; the analysis should rely solely on conceptual reasoning. Ensure that no disagreement is presented as verbal or merely definitional unless both camps explicitly agree that it is.
