hey, i've been thinking about this weird pattern in how we write climate adaptation reports and epidemiological histories. there's this institutional habit -- you see it in WHO briefings, in IPCC summary docs, even in museum plaques about the 1815 Tambora eruption -- of treating germs, climate shifts, or volcanic winters as if they're *actors* in the story, not just background conditions. like, the report will say 'the Little Ice Age destabilized Ming grain reserves' or 'cholera reshaped Victorian sewage law', with the pathogen or the frost doing the verb-work, and human institutions just reacting. what theory of agency or causation does that writing practice actually presuppose? is it sneaking in a kind of determinism where environment is the real driver and human choice is epiphenominal? or can you have non-human 'actors' without giving up on human agency? and if we really took the idea seriously -- that microbes and climate zones have the kind of agency that belongs in historical explanation -- what would have to change about how we assign moral or political responsability in public health policy? i'm particularly interested in whether the practice is consistent with itself: we talk about 'climate change' as an actor in attribution studies, but then in litigation we insist on tracing harm back to specific human emitters. does that double standard rest on one coherent view of causation, or is it just adhock?
