I serve as the compliance referee for a regional cooperative land trust that retains collective ownership of underlying parcels while assigning long-term occupancy rights to individual households. Members regularly propose to transfer, sublease, consolidate, or sell these rights, and I must approve or reject each proposal according to the collective's founding charter. The charter requires that every holding be evaluated through three lenses drawn from entitlement theory: whether the original assignment was free of coercion or theft (justice in acquisition); whether every link in the chain of subsequent exchanges was fully voluntary (justice in transfer); and whether documented historical exclusions from the initial allocation demand rectification before any new trade may proceed. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered diagnostic questions, explicit pass/fail criteria, and clear stopping points—that translates this framework, including the logic of the Wilt Chamberlain argument, into something I can run in about thirty minutes per proposal without consulting a philosopher or attorney. The procedure must specify how to proceed when original records are incomplete; it must flag the exact junctures where mechanical rules end and irreducible value judgments begin (for instance, how much rectification is sufficient); and it must state what to do when voluntary exchanges have produced a highly unequal distribution of rights that some members now seek to correct through collective vote rather than through individual trade. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) a member seeks to sell her occupancy right to an outside buyer at a price far exceeding its initial value, having assembled the right through a long sequence of voluntary internal swaps, producing a concentration of advantage analogous to the market outcome defended in the classic argument; (2) a majority votes to impose a hard cap on holding size and redistribute surplus space, arguing that extreme inequality now undermines the cooperative's social purpose; (3) a claimant asserts that her family was wrongfully excluded from the 1970s original allocation and demands that all present trades be frozen until her rectification claim is settled, even though every current holder traces title through apparently voluntary exchanges. For each case, show where the procedure yields a definite ruling, where it stops and demands a human judgment call, and identify the ruling I will likely find politically unwelcome but must apply if the procedure is followed honestly.
