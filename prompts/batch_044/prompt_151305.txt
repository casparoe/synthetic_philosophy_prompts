You sit on the curriculum review board of a public university's new Institute for Civic Character. A faculty proposal asks the institute to adopt, as its core pedagogical resource, a digital archive entitled Parallel Exemplars, pairing contemporary public figures with Plutarch-style comparative biographies of ancient counterparts. Every entry is strictly hagiographic—no critical biography, no account of vice or failure—grounded in the claim that moral learning requires aspirational proximity to perfection rather than comparison with shared human flaw. The board is split: proponents hold that Plutarch's own paired lives were designed for direct emulation; opponents contend that stripping away his comparative structure—pairing figures with parallel virtues but contrasting failures—produces not education but moral distancing, where the exemplar becomes too perfect to imitate and the reader is left with awe rather than action. Draft the board's formal opinion recommending either approval with binding structural conditions or rejection. Specify exactly what must change in the archive's design or classroom use—such as mandatory antiparallel pairings that juxtapose each hagiographic entry with a critical counter-biography, or required pedagogical framing rules—to mitigate the philosophical risk you identify. Include an honest treatment of the strongest argument for pure hagiography: that critical comparison dilutes the emotional force necessary for genuine aspiration. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
