I am a retired physicist who has spent the last few years studying moral philosophy and ecological ethics. Next week I present to a university seminar composed mainly of Kantian and virtue-ethical moral philosophers, plus an agricultural ecologist and an environmental ethicist who rejects utilitarian aggregation. My thesis is that the conventional ethical recommendation to substitute beef for chicken—on the grounds that beef produces more greenhouse gas and kills more farmed animals per calorie—reverses when one introduces wild-animal suffering caused by crop cultivation. Chicken production depends on grain monocultures that generate extensive collateral mortality and welfare-state degradation among wild vertebrates and invertebrates via pesticides, harvest machinery, and habitat conversion, whereas pasture-based beef requires far less cultivated acreage per unit of nutrition. I therefore argue that, on a suffering-minimization framework that includes both farmed and wild animals, beef is ethically preferable to chicken, provided one distinguishes a mortality footprint (animals killed) from a welfare-state footprint (duration and intensity of suffering among survivors and displaced populations) and applies the latter consistently across trophic levels.

Draft my Q&A preparation sheet. Order the objections I will actually face from this audience—not the objections an animal-rights activist or climate-policy audience would raise, but the ones these philosophers and this biologist will raise—by the damage each could do to my credibility. For each objection, phrase it as the interlocutor would: the Kantian’s concern about treating wild suffering as a quantifiable commodity, the virtue ethicist’s worry that I am reducing moral judgment to an ecological accounting sheet, the biologist’s challenge to my empirical premises about field mortality rates, and the environmentalist’s suspicion that I am using wild suffering to excuse livestock farming. Give me a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes for each, with an explicit note on where the reply is weakest and what I should concede rather than defend. Include the one question I hope is never asked—the one that exposes the hidden assumption in comparing welfare-state footprints across species with radically different subjective capacities—and advise me how to address it without sounding defensive. Finally, tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks so that I do not lose the room before I begin.
