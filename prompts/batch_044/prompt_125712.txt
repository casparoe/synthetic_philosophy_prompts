I serve on our institute's strategic-forecast advisory panel. We must submit a recommendation to the director by Friday on whether our forthcoming report on great-power competition in advanced AI should invoke three specific historical analogies—the Munich appeasement narrative, the Vietnam quagmire, and the so-called Thucydides trap—to frame risks of escalation. One faction holds that these analogies are indispensable heuristic shortcuts for policymakers who lack time for first-principles reasoning; the other, drawing on philosophy-of-history concerns, contends that they commit a category error by treating structural resemblance as causal mechanism, and that the Thucydides claim in particular dissolves once its original formulation—centered on fear, honor, and interest rather than on structural inevitability—is properly recovered. Please draft the panel's memo: it should recommend a concrete editorial policy, either permitting, restricting, or restructuring the deployment of these three analogies in the report, with a clear bottom line and a brief implementation timeline. The reasoning should engage the pro-analogy position on its own terms rather than dismissing it as merely unscientific, and it should assess whether any defensible conceptual core remains in the Thucydides formulation after that scrutiny. A bibliography or reading list is unnecessary.
