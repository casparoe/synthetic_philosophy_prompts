I’ve been trying to think through what minds actually are without relying on philosophy courses—mostly from popular neuroscience writing and long conversations with a friend who studies cognitive science—and I want to check whether the view I’ve arrived at is internally coherent before I defend it further. Here is my current position.

First, I am convinced that beliefs and desires, in the ordinary sense of discrete mental states that have specific contents like “Paris is the capital,” are theoretical fictions comparable to phlogiston. I expect neuroscience and predictive processing to replace them entirely: decision-making will eventually be described through hierarchical cortical dynamics, precision-weighted prediction errors, and homeostatic regulation, with no need to postulate inner sentences or goals.

Second, I think conscious experience is genuinely real and should not be eliminated along with them. Pain, the qualitative feel of color, and the sense of understanding something are not going away just because folk psychology is false; they present a hard problem, but denying them strikes me as denying something I know firsthand.

Third, I hold that reasoning has objective norms. Some inferences are genuinely valid and others are fallacious, and this is not merely a social convention. Even if we do not literally possess “beliefs,” there should still be correct and incorrect ways for neural systems to update in light of evidence.

Fourth, I reject the idea that moral praise and blame depend on people literally “choosing” their actions in some deep metaphysical sense; choice, like belief, is likely another folk-psychological myth. Still, I think we should preserve practices of blame and praise because they regulate behavior in socially valuable ways, regardless of whether anyone “deserves” them in an ultimate sense.

Fifth, in everyday conversation I continue to say things like “I believe this argument is sound” or “I want my view to be consistent.” I do not think I am speaking falsely. The language functions like “the sun rises”: we understand the underlying reality, but the shorthand remains practically harmless and communicatively useful.

Do these commitments hang together? I am especially uncertain whether my first claim undermines my third—if there are no beliefs or reasoning-states, in what sense can neural updating be “correct” or “incorrect”? I also worry that treating folk psychology as literally false, rather than merely approximate, makes my fifth claim hard to sustain: if beliefs do not exist at all, then asserting “I believe X” seems false in the same way asserting “this substance contains phlogiston” is false, not just a convenient fiction. At the same time, I wonder whether there is a hidden tension between my second and fourth commitments—if conscious suffering is real but free choice is an illusion, does moral blame still make coherent sense, or am I sneaking in a notion of agency I have officially rejected?

Please audit this package. Rank any genuine tensions by severity, identify which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real problem indicate the least costly revision and what it would force me to give up. I have not settled which commitment I would be most willing to abandon, so do not treat any one of them as sacrosanct.
