Consider the interpretive practice—widespread in serious Nietzsche scholarship and in intellectual biography—of treating his meticulous dietary prescriptions, digestive complaints, and preferences for specific climates, documented across his late correspondence and autobiographical writings, not as private medical background but as continuous with his philosophical arguments about culture, physiology, and the conditions of thought. Interpreters who adopt this practice read his rejection of heavy national cuisines, his linking of indigestion to intellectual decadence, and his advocacy for precise meal rhythms as parts of a theory about the bodily preconditions of value-creation, rather than as eccentric biographical detail. What philosophical commitments about the relation between bodily regimen and conceptual work does that practice presuppose? Must it assume a reductive materialism in which stomach conditions determine ideas, or can it be reconciled with a more metaphorical reading in which digestion is only an image for intellectual assimilation? Reconstruct the implicit theory of philosophy's embodiment, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether the same interpretive practice could survive if we adopted a view—say, a strict dualism or a purely symbolic hermeneutics—that severs bodily health entirely from philosophical authority.
