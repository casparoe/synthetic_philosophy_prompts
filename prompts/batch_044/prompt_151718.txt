I've been trying to work out a puzzle about two arguments that both turn common sense upside down. Darwin showed that you can get exquisitely designed competence—eyes, instincts—without any designer who comprehends the design. Turing showed, in a different way, that you can get correct, intelligent-seeming behavior from a mechanism that comprehends nothing at all. Most people treat these as parallel cases of competence without comprehension, but I'm wondering whether they actually pull in opposite directions. Darwin's inversion removes comprehension from the origin of competence; Turing's removes it from the manifestation. So here's my question: if we accept Darwin, does that make Turing's case stronger—because we've already accepted that nature builds competent things mindlessly—or does it actually weaken the analogy, since evolutionary competence is distributed across populations and time in a way that a single Turing machine is not? And does this distinction matter for whether we should treat artificial systems as genuinely understanding anything, or only as displaying the same kind of empty competence that evolution produces?
