I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to work out whether the symbols in my code actually cause changes in the hardware or whether it's only ever electrons and the "program" is just in my head. To think it through concretely, I invented a hypothetical substrate I call a careenium—built from elementary units I call simmballs that interact solely through local repulsion and binding energies, with no built-in global controller. By arranging simmballs into a specific lattice, I can implement what looks like a symbolic computation: one cluster-state represents symbol A, another cluster-state represents B, and the lattice geometry enforces a transition from the A-configuration to the B-configuration. Here is the argument I built, which now strikes me as proving far too much:

(1) The local physical rules of the simmballs underdetermine the global transition: given only the local forces, the substrate could in principle settle into many other cluster configurations besides B.

(2) What actually selects the path to B is the global lattice constraint, which we interpret as the symbolic rule A → B.

(3) Therefore the symbolic rule performs causal work that the local substrate physics does not perform on its own; in other words, the symbols push the substrate around.

(4) If this holds for an artificial careenium, it holds for any substrate that implements symbols, including brains composed of neurons with only local synaptic dynamics.

(5) So mental representations—beliefs, intentions—have direct downward causal efficacy: they push neural substrate into specific configurations just as the rule A → B pushes simmballs.

(6) By the same reasoning, any system whose dynamics can be symbolically described—weather patterns captured by Navier-Stokes equations, DNA replication captured by genetic code, planetary motion captured by gravitational formulas—is causally governed by its description, because local physics always underdetermines the global pattern.

(7) This eliminates purely physical causation everywhere and turns every scientific law into an active causal agent, which is absurd.

(8) Hence one of the steps must be wrong.

I'd prefer not to abandon (5): it feels right that running software on silicon isn't a pure illusion, and that my intentions genuinely shape my brain's activity. But if keeping (5) commits me to (6) and (7), I need to know where to cut. Tell me exactly which premise or inference fails, explain why it seemed plausible from an engineering perspective, and price the alternatives—what I pay if I reject (3) versus (4) versus (6), and whether there is a stable position that preserves downward causation in computers and brains without letting equations push planets around.
