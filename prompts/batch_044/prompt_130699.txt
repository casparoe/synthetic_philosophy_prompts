I'd like a consistency audit of my current position on archival power and historical method before I present it in a seminar. Here is the set of commitments I hold:

(1) Archives are not passive repositories but active technologies of governance that produce the subjects they record.
(2) The absence of certain populations from official records is not a neutral gap but a structured omission—what I think of as a negative document—generated by the same categorical logic that produces presence.
(3) Where direct traces are missing, historians have an obligation to reconstruct the probable experiences of excluded subjects through comparative inference and material context; leaving them unspoken reproduces their erasure.
(4) Any reconstruction of absent voices necessarily imposes the historian's present conceptual framework onto subjects who never authorized those categories, risking a second-order epistemic violence.
(5) Ethical practice therefore demands strict evidentiary minimalism: assert only what remaining documents can directly corroborate, and treat silence as absence rather than as a coded message.
(6) There is no view from nowhere in historiography; all interpretation, including minimalism, is situated and partial.

Please go through these commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which conflicts are only apparent, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine conflict, indicate the least costly revision and what it costs. Finally, tell me which single commitment I could revise or drop to resolve the largest number of conflicts with the least damage to the overall view, and whether doing so would leave the position coherent or merely consistent.
