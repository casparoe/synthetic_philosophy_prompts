Having spent three decades studying consistency conditions in non-linear dynamical systems, I have recently begun attending philosophy seminars and have formed what I take to be a coherent package of views concerning Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the related problem of one thought too many in contexts of partial attachment. Before presenting these to a reading group composed largely of professional ethicists, I wish to know whether my commitments actually hang together or whether I am importing assumptions from my scientific training that generate hidden contradictions.

My positions are the following.

(1) I accept Williams's integrity objection without reservation: a moral theory that systematically permits, or requires, the abandonment of an agent's ground projects—those long-term commitments that constitute practical identity—treats that agent as a replaceable vehicle for value rather than as an irreducible source of reasons.

(2) I take the problem of one thought too many to be structural rather than merely psychological: when a husband rescues his spouse from danger, the presence of an explicit impartial justification—because I ought to save whoever I can—does not supplement the reason because she is my wife; it displaces the relational logic that makes the action intelligible as an expression of that particular attachment.

(3) Translating my old scientific habits into philosophical terms, I understand integrity as a gauge-invariance condition on practical identity: just as a physical law must retain its form under permissible coordinate transformations, an agent's core commitments must remain stable across revisions of belief and plan, not because they maximize welfare, but because they preserve the coherence of the system that is the agent.

(4) Despite (3), I maintain that in genuinely catastrophic scenarios—where preserving a ground project would entail allowing mass harm—the moral demand to intervene is absolute and must override integrity, even if doing so fractures the agent's practical identity irreparably.

(5) I hold that Williams's objection applies with full force only to direct act-utilitarianism; sophisticated indirect forms, including rule-utilitarianism and two-level consequentialism, escape the objection because they do not require agents to perform explicit case-by-case calculations within intimate or project-defining contexts.

(6) I deny that considerations of the form being the kind of person for whom X is indispensable carry any independent normative weight; they are descriptive features of psychological architecture, not premises that can justify resisting a moral demand.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only seem so, and what is the least costly revision for each conflict? I am particularly concerned that framing integrity as gauge-invariance in (3) strips it of the normative force required to support the moral criticism in (1), and that my absolute override clause in (4) contradicts the preservation condition in (3). At the same time, I wonder whether (6) is consistent with treating violations of integrity in (1) as moral failures rather than as mere practical breakdowns analogous to mechanical failure in an engine. Rank the tensions by severity, specify for each whether it requires abandoning a commitment or only refining its scope, and indicate whether a single targeted adjustment—perhaps to (3) or (6)—can save the package, or whether I must surrender more than one pillar.
