I'm preparing a closing exercise for a small philosophy seminar that has spent the term on rational choice and practical reason, and we've just reached the session on Buridan's ass — not as a historical curiosity, but as a live problem about whether rationality can ever be self-starting. I would like a poem to read aloud, something formal enough to hold the room but strange enough to leave people unsettled. Write a sestina — six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi in which all six end-words must appear — taking Buridan's ass not as foolish but as the extreme case of perfect rational consistency. Choose six end-words from the semantic field of equivalence and decision — for example "equal", "hunger", "bale", "stand", "break", "will" — and rotate them through the stanzas in the traditional order so that the poem's structure itself enacts the paralysis: each stanza should present the same situation from a slightly altered angle, with the end-words recurring like the two identical bales of hay, never allowing linear progression. In the envoi, where the form conventionally gathers everything into resolution, do not let the ass choose. Instead, let the convergence of the six words expose the deeper difficulty: any tie-breaking mechanism — a random impulse, a minute asymmetry in posture, an arbitrary preference for left over right — merely relocates the problem, since the criterion for selecting that mechanism is itself ungrounded in the face of perfect symmetry. The poem should therefore refuse closure, leaving open whether pure practical reason contains any internal resource for action at all. Write in a contemplative, non-didactic voice; the philosophical point should emerge from the form's refusal to resolve rather than from explicit argument.
