Notes for an essay extending Hofstadter's account of the self as a strange loop. The core claim is that the first-person symbol I functions as a self-referential pattern sustained by recursive representation, not by any localized neural substrate. I want to argue that this loop has a specific vulnerability: not physical interruption, but symbolic decoupling, where a system develops a fully explicit self-model that is no longer automatically reintegrated into the first-person symbol. In this state, the system can report on itself in the third person without the narrative gravity that binds representations into a single I. This suggests split-brain phenomena are less about two loops and more about one loop losing its integrative grip, and that artificial architectures risk producing decoupled shadow-models rather than genuine strange loops unless their self-representation is structurally recursive rather than merely descriptive. I am particularly unsure whether this distinction between decoupling and simple self-modeling is coherent, and whether it unfairly privileges Hofstadter's notion of narrative gravity over standard functionalist accounts.

Please provide comments on three things: first, whether symbolic decoupling is genuinely distinct from existing accounts of depersonalization or higher-order thought failure; second, whether my reading of split-brain cases as integrative failure rather than loop duplication is defensible against the standard literature on corpus callosum division; and third, whether the condition for artificial strange loops—structurally recursive self-representation rather than descriptive self-modeling—actually adds a meaningful constraint, or whether it collapses back into standard functionalist requirements for consciousness.
