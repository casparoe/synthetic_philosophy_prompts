I recently encountered a summary of Gould and Lewontin’s 1979 critique of the adaptationist program—apparently in a popular science essay—that frames their argument in a very specific way, and I want to determine whether it actually does them justice. According to the summary, they introduced “spandrels” (the triangular spaces between arches in San Marco) to argue that many biological traits are not direct products of natural selection but inevitable structural byproducts of other adaptive architectures; because these spandrels arise from geometric or developmental constraints, they are never subsequently modified by selection, and any usefulness they later acquire should be classified as “exaptation” rather than as true adaptation. The summary further claims that Gould and Lewontin intended this distinction to show that natural selection is a relatively minor evolutionary force compared to internal structural and developmental constraints, and that their essay therefore effectively dismantles the idea that organisms are optimally engineered. I am inclined to think this captures the general spirit of their critique, but I worry it may conflate spandrels with exaptations, overstate their dismissal of selection’s ongoing role, and present a methodological warning about storytelling as a quantitative claim about evolutionary mechanisms. Could you assess whether this summary is fair? In particular, please separate what is accurate about their use of the spandrel analogy, what amounts to a common simplification, and whether they actually denied that spandrels can be secondarily shaped by selection or that exaptations count as adaptations in any meaningful sense.
