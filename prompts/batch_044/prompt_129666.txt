Here is my position:

(1) In conditions of genuinely ambiguous evidence, rational belief should be modeled by imprecise probabilities—a range or convex family of precise probability functions—rather than by a single Bayesian prior. I take this primarily to dissolve the arbitrariness problem that afflicts precise prior assignment.

(2) The principle of indifference is legitimate when applied within a given credal representation, but it is not legitimate when used to select between different representations of the same evidential situation; applied across representations, it generates contradictions.

(3) In my own fiction, the "probability" that a given plot branch will occur is not merely unknown to me; it is genuinely indeterminate because I have not yet fixed a unique narrative weight for that branch. This indeterminacy is ontological, belonging to the story-world's modal structure, not merely epistemic ignorance on my part.

(4) Nevertheless, I maintain that for any completed or planned narrative there is always a precise fact about what I, as author, intend the plot trajectory to be, and this authorial intention fixes the true probabilities of narrative events.

(5) When I model a fictional character facing a decision under the kind of imprecision described in (3), the normatively correct decision rule is maximin: the character should evaluate each option by its worst-case outcome across the credal range and choose the option with the best worst case.

(6) I also hold that if the imprecision in (3) were merely epistemic—pure authorial ignorance—then applying maximin to the character's choice would be an unjustified conservative bias, because the true probabilities are in fact precise and should be weighted by expected utility.
