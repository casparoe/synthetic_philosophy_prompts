Read Denis Villeneuve’s film Arrival (2016) through W.V.O. Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—specifically the “gavagai” scenario and the underdetermination of analytical hypotheses—rather than through the more common framing of linguistic relativism. The heptapods communicate via circular logograms that appear to lack linear syntax, and Louise Banks eventually decodes them as encoding temporal experience non-sequentially. I want a reading that asks whether the film treats translation as Quine understands it: that is, whether any consistent body of heptapod linguistic behavior is compatible with multiple, mutually incompatible translation manuals, with no further fact of the matter about which manual is correct. Focus on compound expressions. Quine’s critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction implies that there may be no atomic “word” or isolable concept inside a logogram corresponding to time, weapon, or offer; there may only be holistic patterns whose internal boundaries depend on the translator’s background ontology. Does Louise’s breakthrough—recognizing that the language reshapes cognition—represent the discovery of a uniquely correct manual, or is it better understood as adopting one adequate manual among infinitely many, selected for its predictive and practical utility? Then connect this to the film’s determinism: Louise comes to accept a predetermined future because the language makes it visible. From a Quinean perspective, does that “visibility” depend on resolving an ontological indeterminacy that Quine claims is permanently open—whether the heptapods refer to time, causality, or event-structures not present in our ontology? Identify scenes—such as the dispute over whether a particular logogram means tool or weapon—where the film assumes that accumulating evidence collapses the ambiguity, and say whether Quine’s thesis of ontological relativity undermines that assumption and, with it, the moral urgency of the heptapods’ message. Finally, say what the film contributes back to the philosophical debate: does it merely dramatize Quine’s abstract point about radical translation, or does it show that in high-stakes contexts the practical need for a single working manual creates an illusion of uniqueness that philosophical analysis should resist? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of Quine, and do not distort Quine’s skepticism about fixed reference to make the plot cohere.
