I've come to think that Goodman's grue paradox is dissolved rather than solved: the asymmetry between 'green' and 'grue' isn't grounded in metaphysical naturalness but in the inductive bias of any efficient learner. Specifically, if we model confirmation as approximate Bayesian updating over computable hypotheses weighted by a Solomonoff-style prior, then 'grue' carries a hidden discontinuity—an exact temporal boundary condition that must be hard-coded into the predicate—whereas 'green' doesn't. That discontinuity raises the description-length penalty discontinuously at the boundary, so rational learners will down-weight grue-hypotheses without appealing to natural kinds. What would count against this dissolution? I want the defeaters sorted by decisiveness: formal results showing that Kolmogorov-complexity priors don't actually penalize grue-like predicates in the relevant way; empirical cases in human categorization or ML generalization where subjects treat grue-type predicates as equally projectable; arguments that this just relocates the problem into the choice of prior; and whether the view has been qualified so that any failure of the prior-based explanation can be absorbed by tweaking the approximation scheme. Which of these would a committed defender concede, and which would they dismiss? Also: is there a fatal objection I'm missing, or has the position been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a vice for a dissolution of this kind?
