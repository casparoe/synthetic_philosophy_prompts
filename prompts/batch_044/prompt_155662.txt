I sit on the methodology review panel for an interdisciplinary research institute that funds cognitive-science and artificial-intelligence projects, and recently we have seen a wave of proposals that include what applicants call phenomenal consciousness assays — structured protocols asking human subjects or artificial systems to report the contents of awareness, then treating those reports as direct measurements of a unified experiential stream. Many implicitly presuppose what Dennett criticized as the Cartesian theater: a central, unitary locus where experience comes together for a single observer. Others claim metaphysical neutrality yet still embed theater-style assumptions, such as a canonical temporal ordering of conscious events or the existence of an internal original experience that verbal reports may approximate or distort. I need a reusable evaluation procedure my panel can apply to determine whether a given assay protocol is conceptually compatible with Dennett's multiple-drafts model — distributed, parallel processing with no central audience and no fact of the matter about which draft is canonical until later mechanisms fix a narrative — without requiring every reviewer to be a philosopher of mind.

Please structure this as an ordered decision procedure: the specific questions a reviewer asks about the proposal in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stop points where the protocol either clears the bar, fails for conceptual incoherence, or must be handed to human judgment because the issue involves values rather than consistency, and clear flagging of where the procedure will misfire. Address in particular: how the protocol handles temporal sequencing of reported contents, since the multiple-drafts model denies a single timeline of consciousness; whether the protocol permits competing, unreconciled drafts of the same stimulus rather than forcing unified narrative reconstruction; how it treats post-hoc confabulation versus direct access without assuming a hidden theater; and how to evaluate protocols that claim to be purely behavioral and make no metaphysical commitments.

Ground the distinctions in Dennett's actual framework — no central observer, no unique real draft prior to narrative fixation, and the idea that asking what was really experienced is often asking an ill-formed question — but translate them into operational criteria accessible to reviewers with only basic philosophy-of-mind background. Include a small set of test protocol descriptions the reviewer can apply, with worked examples showing both a passing case and a failing case. Explicitly mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. Finally, include one worked case where the procedure returns a negative verdict on a protocol that is otherwise methodologically rigorous and practically appealing — explain precisely why the conceptual flag is warranted despite the protocol's strengths — and write the response in polished American English.
