I'm grading papers for my philosophy of language course and this one's right on the line. The exam question was:

Davidson's radical interpretation requires that we assign beliefs and meanings to a speaker simultaneously, guided by the principle of charity. Suppose we're interpreting a newly discovered community, the Kelp-speakers, who consistently utter sentences that literally translate as "all kelp is green" and "not all kelp is green" in identical contexts, with no visible confusion or correction. How should Davidson's framework handle this? Should charity lead us to revise our translation of their logical vocabulary, attribute contradictory beliefs, or take some other route? And how does this differ from Quine's claim that translation is underdetermined by all behavioral evidence?

Here is the student's answer, copied from teh exam:

Davidson thinks interpretation is holistic: you can't separate what someone means from what they believe. The principle of charity says we should maximize agreement, so we should avoid attributing contradictions. In the Kelp-speaker case, since they show no distress, charity tells us they don't really hold both beliefs. Instead, we should reinterpret their logical constants—maybe "all" doesn't function like universal quantification for them. This is basically the same point Quine makes about indeterminacy: since behavior underdetermines translation, there are multiple correct manuals that preserve rationality. So Davidson's view actually implies there's no fact of the matter about what the Kelp-speakers mean; any interpretation that makes them rational is equally good. Also, charity is a constitutive constraint, so Davidson would never allow attributing false beliefs, only mistranslation.
