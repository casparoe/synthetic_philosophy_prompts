I am preparing a discussion paper for a philosophy of science seminar, and I have found the conventional treatment of the Rietdijk-Putnam argument frustratingly one-dimensional. In most courses, the argument is presented as a swift deduction from the frame-dependence of simultaneity in special relativity to the unreality of temporal becoming: if every event stands in a relation of simultaneity to some other event in some inertial frame, then there is no metaphysically privileged present, and all events are equally real within a four-dimensional block. I want an essay that takes this reasoning seriously but does not treat it as self-evidently conclusive.

Your essay should weigh whether the Rietdijk-Putnam line genuinely undermines an A-theoretic ontology of time, or whether it depends on an unexamined slide from physical geometry to metaphysical presence. You must defend a specific verdict—either that the block universe follows with rigour, or that the argument fails at a definite stage—but the analysis should also accommodate a proposal I have been developing: that indexical temporal expressions ('now', 'then', 'present') do not describe an ontological slice at all, but serve a performative function in structuring practical reasoning, emotional orientation, and agential commitment. Is it coherent to hold that the block universe is metaphysically correct, yet that indexical presentness retains an irreducible, non-descriptive role in human agency? Does this 'practical A-theory' avoid contradiction, or does it collapse back into either a full-blooded B-theory or a disguised ontology of passing? Reach a clear conclusion about what survives of the phenomenology of becoming once the Rietdijk-Putnam argument is accepted, and about whether that survival is philosophically significant or merely a psychological residue.
