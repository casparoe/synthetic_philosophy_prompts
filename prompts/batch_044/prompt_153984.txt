I'm preparing a seminar module on epistemic risk in the human sciences, and I've become fixated on a problem I haven't seen addressed clearly in the standard literature: when archaeologists reconstruct institutional beliefs—norms, prohibitions, shared cosmologies—from material remains alone, different inferential strategies carry radically different false-positive risks, yet textbooks rarely compare them on a single scale. To help me structure the discussion, I'd like you to rank the following five approaches according to their vulnerability to spurious institutional attribution—that is, the risk of reading a collective belief into objects that actually reflect practical constraints, preservation bias, or individual idiosyncrasy. Please use a 1-to-10 scale, where 10 means maximally vulnerable, and keep your reasoning concise.

The five are: (1) direct ethnographic analogy, projecting documented practices onto materially similar but culturally distant assemblages; (2) functionalist spatial analysis, inferring social norms from the layout of buildings and resource flows; (3) iconographic decoding, treating representational motifs as transparent statements of collective belief; (4) stratigraphic correlation, linking abrupt material discontinuities to independently documented historical events; and (5) what I call "absential reasoning"—treating systematic patterns of omission, non-use, or deliberate destruction not as preservation accidents but as negative evidence of active institutional prohibitions.

I'm especially interested in the comparative judgment between (1) and (5). Many critics treat analogical reasoning as the baseline method and absential reasoning as the most speculative; but in contexts where ethnographic parallels are culturally remote and textual attestation is absent, might absential reasoning actually be more defensible, precisely because it relies on internal pattern consistency rather than cross-cultural similarity? Please address that tension directly in your rankings and justifications, and don't hesitate to split scores closely if you think the comparison is genuinely ambiguous.
