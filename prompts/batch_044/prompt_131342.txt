As a doctoral student working at the intersection of moral philosophy and the sociology of norms, I have been tracking what strikes me as a curiously immobile debate about how practices like chattel slavery and aristocratic dueling actually disappeared. On one side is a rationalist or argument-driven view, often associated with Appiah’s account of moral revolutions through honor codes, according to which these norms collapsed when people came to see them as incompatible with values—equality, human dignity, civilized conduct—they already implicitly held; the revolution is essentially an internal critique that gains social traction. On another side is the Williams thesis, which holds that dueling in particular did not end because anyone was rationally persuaded it was wrong, but because it became dishonorable, unfashionable, or socially costly: honor itself was reconfigured rather than overcome by reason. A third camp emphasizes material and institutional shifts—plantation economics, state monopolization of violence, legal prohibition—treating moral rhetoric as post-hoc justification. The positions are well rehearsed, the historical examples are canonical, and yet in the literature and in seminar discussions the disagreement has barely moved in decades; each camp can redescribe any archival anecdote in its own vocabulary without concession.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive causal dispute waiting for better data, but a disagreement about what kind of explanation counts as moral, or perhaps that the camps are answering different questions—normative justification versus historical etiology—under the single heading “why did the norm disappear?” But I am unsure, and I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Specifically, I want you to examine what each side actually says about slavery and dueling and determine whether the impasse is verbal (centered on “explanation,” “morality,” or “honor”), substantive but empirically intractable, or a case of hidden conceptual divergence—perhaps about whether “honor” functions as a primitive motivation, an epiphenomenon of interest, or something else entirely. I also want you to consider a framing I have been developing but do not want to impose: the possibility that both camps miss that honor operates not as an individual drive but as a coordination mechanism on second-order beliefs—common knowledge about what others will regard as respectable—so that a moral revolution is neither persuasion nor interest-shift but a cascade across a threshold in shared expectations. If that is right, the debate may be ill-posed because it seeks a single causal mechanism where there is actually a multi-level coordination problem.

Using the actual claims made by the argument camp, the Williams-style honor camp, and the material-interest camp, diagnose the structure of the disagreement: is it about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, about bedrock intuitions regarding “naturalness” or agency, or about starting questions that neither side can argue for? Are they answering different questions under one name? Is there a hidden formal or conceptual distinction—say, between first-order preferences and second-order norms, or between individual belief revision and collective expectation updating—that could resolve or dissolve the conflict? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a new kind of archival study, a formal model of norm cascades, a clarification of “honor” that breaks the false dichotomy I sketched, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why? I am not asking for a survey of interpretations or a verdict on which camp is right, but for an audit of the disagreement itself and an honest assessment of whether anything—empirical, formal, or conceptual—could move it.
