I've come around to this view I'm tentatively calling the blackout thesis — that ontology should only commit to entities and distinctions that lie within our causal-epistimic reach. If a region or system is in permanent informational blackout (no signal, no indirect inference bridge, ever), then any ontological differences that depend solely on what's inside that blackout are just surplus structure, not real commitments. It's not full-blown verificationism, since I'm allowing theoretical entities we can't observe directly but can infer; it's specifically about the permanently inaccessible stuff.

What would count against it? I want the defeaters mapped out properly — sorted from fatal to merely embarasing. Are there formal results showing that hidden structure can be indispensible to explaining accessible correlations without being ad-hoc? What about quantum correlations across horizons — do they force us to keep ontology inside the blackout? And what about cases in physics or math where two empirically equivalent frameworks differ only in the blacked-out part, but one is simpler or more unified?

Also, has this kind of position already been qualified into unfalsifiability? If every counterexample gets dismissed as 'not really permanently inaccessible,' is that a problem? I want an honest assessment: is the blackout thesis genuinely risky, or has it been insulated so well that it can't be wrong?

One more thing — I personally find explanatory indispensability arguments pretty convincing. So if you can show me a scenario where the hidden ontology does real explanatory work for the accessible world, that'd hit me harder than a pure intuition pump.
