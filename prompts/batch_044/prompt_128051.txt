Since my sister's psychiatric diagnosis was revised twice within eighteen months—not because her symptoms changed, but because the assessment center adopted a revised manual and then a reimbursement protocol—I have come to see psychiatric classification as an active mechanism rather than a neutral taxonomy. I need a classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar: fourteen graduate students, ninety minutes. The goal is to make the dynamics of interactive classification tangible, specifically the looping effect in which being sorted into a category reshapes the behavior that then confirms or destabilizes the category. I want the activity to inject and dramatize one under-examined feature: asymmetric reflexivity, meaning that the classificatory framework absorbs feedback selectively—amplifying behaviors that fit its categories and filtering out disconfirming responses—so that the loop acquires epistemic inertia and resists correction even when the original grounds for the category have eroded.

Design a structured simulation with real but small stakes (not money: use collective determination of the seminar's final case study, plus individual participation credit that can be gained or lost through in-game actions). Divide students into rotating roles across three rounds: Assessors, who construct and revise diagnostic-style categories based on observed behavior; Subjects, who must complete collaborative tasks while being assigned to categories that carry hidden rule-sets affecting their available actions; and Archivists, who decide which behaviors are recorded and visible to Assessors and which are discarded or mislabeled. The rules must be arranged so that, in Round 1, Assessors create categories from initial observations; in Round 2, Subjects adapt their task strategies upon learning their assigned category, producing changed behavior; and in Round 3, Assessors revise categories using only the Archivists' filtered records, facing a forced collective choice—either maintain an obsolete category that still controls access to resources (the final case-study vote) or dissolve it and lose the predictive framework they have come to depend on. The philosophical point must arise from the dynamics: participants should feel pressure to stabilize the loop and should notice that disconfirming evidence disappeared not through conspiracy but through structural filtering, before anyone names the concept.

Provide the exact rules and turn order for each round; the incentive scheme with precise payoffs; role instructions that conceal the philosophical framing; a minute-by-minute timeline for the session; a debrief that maps Round 1 onto initial classification, Round 2 onto reactive change, and Round 3 onto loop closure and asymmetric absorption; a prediction of what will go wrong on the first run (for example, Assessors over-correcting in panic, Subjects colluding with Archivists, or the group resolving the tension too quickly by voting); and an explicit, honest statement of which feature of long-term institutional looping—likely the slow sedimentation of categories into bureaucratic and legal structures—this single session cannot reproduce and that I, as facilitator, will need to supply verbally.

The activity should be runnable without any prior reading; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources.
