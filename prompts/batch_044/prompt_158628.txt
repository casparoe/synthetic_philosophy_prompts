A particular framework in longtermist historical analysis applies a four-condition test to identify “hinge periods”: intervals in which actions or events have globally scaling effects, are largely irreversible on a civilizational timescale, open an unusually wide space of long-run equilibria, and depend sensitively on conditions fixed during a brief window. Its judgments on six episodes are as follows. The Neolithic transition (c. 10,000 BCE) is rejected: although irreversible, its spread was multi-centered and gradual, and ecological constraints made the long-run outcome space narrow. The development and first deployment of nuclear fission weapons (1942–1945) is accepted: a discrete decision window, global risk lock-in, and extreme contingency in geopolitical trajectory. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 is rejected: transformative locally but neither globally irreversible nor branching widely. The adoption of the Montreal Protocol (1987) is rejected: globally irreversible for atmospheric chemistry, yet all compliant paths converge on a single recovery equilibrium, so branching is minimal. The diffusion of movable-type printing in Europe (c. 1440–1500) is accepted: it locked in an information-technology regime, and long-run paths for religious, scientific, and political organization were highly sensitive to which texts, languages, and regions were prioritized in the first decades. The Green Revolution (c. 1950–1970) is rejected: it altered demographic and agricultural trajectories but did not establish an institutional or technological lock-in with radically divergent stable outcomes.

Consider now a proposed case: the period 2035–2045, during which a family of self-improving cognitive architectures is deployed globally, establishing a dominant optimization and alignment trajectory that can be reversed only by the complete and permanent destruction of all computing substrate, and that generates on the order of 10^30 distinguishable long-run civilizational states depending on parameter configurations that are largely fixed by autonomous system dynamics rather than by discrete human policy choices during the window.

What should the framework conclude for consistency? Identify the closest analog among the six prior judgments and justify why it is nearer than the others. Then articulate at least two distinct rules that jointly accommodate all six past verdicts but diverge on the 2035–2045 case; state what each rule predicts here, and defend the best guess with explicit reasoning. Finally, specify one further hypothetical or historical case that would best discriminate between those two rules.
