I'm a junior in an upper-level seminar on moral change and social norms, and for our peer-teaching module I must design a forty-minute session for fellow undergraduates who have taken introductory ethics but have not studied moral history. I want to teach the thesis—associated with Bernard Williams and developed in Kwame Anthony Appiah's analysis of honor worlds—that the collapse of practices such as dueling and foot-binding is poorly explained by the triumph of rational moral argument, and is better understood through shifts in the social economy of honor, visibility, and shame. To keep this from sounding like a standard lecture, I intend to frame the session around an analytical distinction I am developing for my own coursework: the difference between "argumentative defeat," in which a practice ends because its own participants are persuaded of its wrongness, and "honor depletion," in which a practice ends because sustaining it demands costs that become visible to an outside audience whose recognition the practitioners still require, thereby breaking the internal coherence of the honor world.

Plan the explanatory arc for me rather than delivering the explanation itself. First, establish the dependency order: should I begin by granting the intuitive appeal of the argument-driven model and then complicating it, or should I start with the phenomenology of honor and risk losing the audience before they see what needs explaining? Second, identify the single carrying example that should anchor the session—I'm weighing the decline of dueling in nineteenth-century France against the decline of foot-binding in China—and specify exactly what that example must include (a clear honor code with internal rules) and what it must exclude (a case where economic incentives alone explain the change), and indicate whether the other case should be introduced at all and, if so, in what role. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form—such as conflating honor with mere reputation, assuming the thesis excuses participants from moral agency, or believing that Appiah's account requires a conscious, elite-led decision to abandon the practice—and specify the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, provide one concrete check for understanding—a brief question or exercise—that would reveal whether the students have grasped the dependency between external visibility and honor collapse, rather than simply agreeing that "social pressure matters." Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level: should I avoid Williams's broader skepticism about the possibility of moral progress, should I refrain from drawing analogies to modern dynamics of public shaming, and should I omit any quantitative claims about the rate of normative change? The deliverable should be a teaching plan with ordered components, dependency notes, and misconception placements, not lecture notes or a bibliography.
