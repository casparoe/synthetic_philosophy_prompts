Here are my gut calls on decision problems where the standard expected value is literally undefined — not infinite, just not well-definied because the positive and negative tails both blow up. I want to know if there's one principle behind my calls, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Case 1: The Pasadena-style coin game. Flip until tails; on round n you win or lose 2^(n-1) arranged so both the win and lose branches have diverging expectations. There's also a safe payout of $5. I take the $5 — the gamble feels like it shouldn't be worth more than nothing, but I also can't say it's exactly worth $0.

Case 2: Same setup, but now any loss is capped at -$20 while wins still run to infinity. So the negative tail is chopped off. I feel much better about this version — I'd pay maybe $3 to play. But technically the expectation is now +infinity, not undefined. So why did fixing the undefinedness by capping losses make me want to pay, when an infinite expectation sounds scary?

Case 3: Two gambles, A and B, both with undefined expectation. A dominates B state-by-state: every outcome in A is strictly better than the matching outcome in B. I pick A instantly. So dominance works even when expectation is broken.

Case 4: The untruncated game from case 1, but there's a rule that if you reach round 15 the game stops and you get $0. The expectation is now enormous but finite. I'd enter for $2. But if the cutoff is round 100 instead of 15, I'd still only pay about $2 — the cutoff feels arbitary, yet the rational value shouldn't depend on an arbitary cap. So either I'm wrong about case 4, or there's something wrong with treating expectation as the guide.

Case 5: A well-defined gamble with expectation exactly $0 but all outcomes are ± a few dollars. Compared to case 1, I prefer this $0-expectation gamble. So undefined expectation feels worse than zero expectation, even though both "center" at nothing.

My verdicts: dominance always decides it; truncation shouldn't matter but I treat it like it does; capping one tail makes me accept even with infinite expectation; undefined feels worse than zero. Is there a coherent account — maybe relative expectation, or a dominance-plus-robustness rule — that saves all of these? Or do I have to drop one? Also, what does your best principle say about a portfolio: if I take two indepedant copies of case 1 together, does the undefinedness cancel out, and should I then accept the pair?

Please don't list papers; I just want to know if my intuitions hang together and which one is the outlier.
