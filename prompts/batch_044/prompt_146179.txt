okay so i've been reading simone weil's thing about how all political partys should be abolished. she says they turn people into machines that can't think for themselves because you have to agree with the group even when you know it's wrong. she also talks about attention - like, real thinking requires this pure attention to whats true, and parties destroy that because they make you direct all your attention toward winning or helping the collective.

so i tried to formalize her point and now it proves way too much:

(1) Weil is right that a party is bad because it systematically replaces individual attention-to-truth with collective loyalty.
(2) If systematically replacing attention-to-truth with loyalty is wrong, then it's wrong in any institution that does it.
(3) But scientific labs, churches, and even close families often demand loyalty that overrides pure attention to truth - like defending your colleague's flawed study, or staying silent about a family member's mistake.
(4) So by this reasoning, labs and families are just as corrupt as political partys.
(5) But that can't be right; my sister's family loyalty isn't the same kind of moral failure as party politics.
(6) Therefore either Weil's critique applies only to parties for some hidden reason, or (2) is false and loyalty can sometimes be okay even when it distorts attention.
(7) I really want to keep (1) and (5).

where does the argument slip? is there a hidden distinction between the kind of loyalty a party demands and the kind a family demands? or did i smuggle in an equivocation between attention in the philosophical sense and just paying attention? i feel like there's a modal slide somewhere but i can't name it.
