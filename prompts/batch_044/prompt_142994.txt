Having spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics before retiring and, more recently, joining a small local discussion group for those of us turning to philosophy of science, I have been trying to understand Imre Lakatos’s account of scientific research programmes—specifically the architecture of a hard core surrounded by a protective belt, and the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts. In our last meeting I presented a line of reasoning that now strikes me as suspicious, because if it holds, it seems to collapse Lakatos’s own negative examples—such as the programmes he regarded as pseudoscientific—into unavoidable progressiveness. I have laid it out as follows: (1) On Lakatos’s view, a problem shift is progressive when modifications to the protective belt yield predictions of novel, hitherto unexpected facts, and degenerating when they serve only to accommodate anomalies already known. (2) Any given theoretical adjustment within the belt, however ad hoc its motivation, can in principle be shown to entail some previously untested or unmeasured consequence if one examines its formal structure with sufficient mathematical ingenuity. (3) Consequently, for virtually any protective-belt revision one can retrospectively construct a “novel prediction” that the revision technically makes, regardless of whether anyone actually derived or sought to test that consequence prior to the revision being introduced. (4) It therefore follows that no protective-belt modification can be conclusively classified as degenerating, since the progressiveness condition in (1) is always satisfiable ex post. (5) Yet Lakatos plainly intended certain historical programmes—Marxist historiography and Freudian psychoanalysis among them—to stand as clear cases of degeneration. (6) Hence there must be an implicit constraint within Lakatos’s framework that blocks the move in (3) from rescuing every programme. Where does my chain slip? Am I forced to deny the retrospective-constructibility claim in (2) or (3), or have I omitted a hidden normative condition within (1) itself—perhaps one requiring that the novel fact be predicted in advance of its discovery rather than excavated afterwards? I would prefer to preserve both (1) and (5), but I wish to understand precisely what I must pay, conceptually, if I reject a different link.
