I'm trying to work out what I actually think about intentional action, responsibility, and practical knowledge in absent-minded driving cases. I've been using a rough distinction between execution authority—whether an action is authorized by my present practical reasoning—and origin authority—whether the plan that launched it was genuinely mine—but I can't apply it consistently. Here are my verdicts on six concrete scenarios.

A. The classic autopilot. I intend to drive to a colleague's house for dinner. The first eight miles overlap with my commute to work. I'm tired, miss the exit, and park at my office, only realizing when I reach for my work badge. Verdict: I clearly intended the colleague's house, yet my body executed the work-route. I'm not morally responsible for ending up at work, though I am somewhat responsible for the inattention. The drive to work was not "mine" in the sense of being authorized by my present intention.

B. The monitored autopilot. Same route overlap, same fatigue, same wrong turn. But this time my passenger asks, "Are we going to dinner?" I reply, "Yes, take the next exit," yet I continue past it, still daydreaming, and park at work surprised. Verdict: I'm more responsible than in A, because my explicit verbal commitment to the plan was present. Still, I don't think I "decided" for work; it was a control failure, not a change of mind, but the failure feels more culpable.

C. The GPS amnesiac. I intend to go to the colleague's house but have never driven there. I program the GPS, play music, and daydream the whole trip. I follow every instruction precisely, arrive correctly, but later have no episodic memory of the drive. Verdict: Despite total absence of conscious experience, I drove there intentionally. The GPS translated my plan into step-by-step action. I'm fully the author of the arrival, though not of each turn.

D. The obsolete muscle memory. I intend to drive to work. Unbeknownst to me, my old apartment—where I lived ten years ago—lies on a diverging route I haven't taken since. I consciously think I've forgotten the way. Yet I take the old exit, drive precisely to the old building, and only then realize. I genuinely deny knowing how to get there. Verdict: Even with my denial, my action was guided by retained knowledge-how. I didn't wander randomly; I executed a precise old plan. So I did "know" the way in a practical sense, though my introspection misses it. The intention was dormant, not absent.

E. The virtual reflex. I'm in a high-fidelity simulator, intending to drive to the colleague's house on a virtual map that contains no office building—the "work" turn is literally impossible. Yet at the shared divergence point I turn the wheel hard right exactly as in real life, crashing into a virtual wall. I report feeling "pulled" by the turn. Verdict: Unlike D, I don't think there's any hidden intention or knowledge guiding me. The turn is a decoupled motor reflex, detached from any representational content about this environment. I'm not responsible, but also not "acting on a secret plan."

F. The corrected deviation. I intend to work. Near the divergence I accidentally start taking the exit to the colleague's house—the opposite error from A. I notice within two seconds, correct back, and reach work. Verdict: I'm fully responsible for the initial wrong turn—more so than in A—because my active, occurrent intention was to work, and the deviation was a direct failure to execute it. Yet because I corrected immediately, the overall failure is minor.

The tensions: A and F are asymmetric. In both I deviate from my conscious plan, but in A (toward the stronger habit) I excuse myself, while in F (toward a weaker, non-habitual route) I blame myself. Meanwhile D and E are behaviorally similar—precise execution of an old route without conscious access—yet in D I attribute hidden practical knowledge, while in E I deny any hidden intention. So any principle must distinguish environment-coupled procedural guidance from decoupled reflex, and must also explain why deviation-direction relative to habit strength affects responsibility.

What principle, or small family of competing principles, best captures this pattern? For each clause, cite the case that motivates it. Then apply your account to: (i) a driver who takes an old habitual detour to avoid traffic she never consciously decided on, which gets her to her intended destination faster; (ii) a driver who blindly follows buggy GPS instructions to the wrong location and never notices the discrepancy. Which of my verdicts—especially the asymmetry between A and F, or the difference between D and E—does the simplest version have to drop, and why?
