me and my friends Marcus and Elena got into it for like two hours last night over expected value fanatacism. Marcus said look, rationality is just EV maximization, full stop. he gave this example: you can save one person for sure, or take a button that saves 10 billion people with probability 0.0000000001 and does nothing otherwise. The EV of teh button is 1, so it's technically tied, but if the payoff is 50 billion then you should obviously press it, no matter how tiny the chance, as long as it's nonzero.

Elena said that's totally absurd in practice. She said if you live by that rule you'll spend your whole life chasing cosmic longshots and ignoring the person right in front of you. Worse, she said, these choices don't happen in isolation -- you face thousands of them, and the ones that fail still eat up your time, attention, and money. So fanatacism isn't just weird, it's self-defeating because teh opportunity costs compound.

Marcus came back with: opportunity costs are just more numbers to caluclate. If pressing the button takes ten seconds, you factor in the EV of those ten seconds. If the cosmic payoff is big enough, it still dominates. He said Elena was smuggling in risk aversion without calling it that.

Then Elena switched gears. She said the real problem isn't risk aversion, it's epistemic humility. We don't actually know that 0.0000000001 figure -- for cosmic-scale events our models are basically guesses spanning orders of magnitude. So treating that tiny number as a precise input to an EV formula is like measuring with a broken ruler. Marcus said that's skepticism about probabilities, not an argument against EV theory itself.

Marcus then pushed her: if she rejects fanatacism, she needs a threshold p below which any payoff is ignored. But she can't draw that line without being arbitrary. Is 1 in a trillion okay? 1 in a quadrillion? Where's teh cutoff?

Elena said she doesn't need a sharp line. She said there's a difference between "within-model" EV, where your probabilities are somewhat calibrated, and "across-model" EV, where you don't even know which value function is right. In that second zone, blindly applying the formula isn't rational -- it's just false precision. She called it shifting from maximization to robustness: you don't bet everything on noise.

Marcus said that's just giving up on decision theory. Elena said no, it's recognizing that decision theory applies inside a model, and when the model's parameters are unknown by orders of magnitude, applying the formula blindly can be worse than useless.
