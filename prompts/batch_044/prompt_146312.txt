Take the role of an expert philosopher of decision theory and practical rationality. Before answering, spend a moment outlining the core logical tension between maximising expected value and maintaining coherent practical agency.

Two colleagues, Mira and Jonas, debated expected-value fanaticism over coffee. Here is their exchange:

(1) Mira said that letting astronomically valuable but astronomically unlikely outcomes dominate all choices collapses practical reasoning. If a 0.0001% chance of preventing cosmic-scale suffering can outweigh near-certain modest goods, then every rational agent should funnel all resources into that tail risk, leaving no space for ordinary obligations, relationships, or stable life plans. The framework is self-undermining because it eliminates the conditions for coherent agency.

(2) Jonas replied that this confuses normative truth with psychological comfort. The fact that EV reasoning demands extreme allocations doesn't make it false; it reveals that the stakes are genuinely extreme. He offered an analogy: if saving a drowning child costs you only a dry shirt, you do not refuse because the shirt has near-certain value; the child's life dominates. Scaling up, refusing to act on a tiny probability of saving countless lives because it disturbs your weekend is not prudence but moral myopia.

(3) Mira countered that the analogy fails because we can calibrate "drowning child" probabilities and values, whereas existential-risk probabilities are not frequencies but speculative judgments under deep uncertainty. Applying precise EV arithmetic to them is a category error. A rational agent should instead apply ambiguity aversion — treating unknown probabilities with Knightian caution — rather than multiplying an ungrounded figure by an astronomical utility.

(4) Jonas responded that ambiguity aversion is merely risk aversion with extra steps, and if you reject EV maximisation for ambiguous tails you need a non-circular justification. Any substitute rule — maximin, satisficing, robustness — implicitly assigns weights to outcomes. Once you admit you care about the tail at all, refusing to act proportionally is arbitrary. Ignoring the tail is itself a decision with an expected value, usually catastrophic.

(5) Mira added that even within the framework, the position violates basic fairness. If she must divert her child's education fund into a speculative cosmic-insurance scheme, she is not being rational; she is being unjust to identifiable present people for the sake of statistical ghosts. The fanatic cannot accommodate deontic constraints without smuggling them in as ad hoc utility adjustments.

(6) Jonas concluded that deontic constraints can be internalised: you can build fairness into the utility function by treating justice violations as costs. But then the fanaticism remains — you are still maximising expected value, just over a richer description of outcomes. The framework absorbs the objections rather than disappearing.
