I am preparing the interview guide for a philosophy podcast episode featuring an unnamed philosopher who has recently advanced a “functional-fluidity” account of conceptual slippage in a narrowly defined microdomain: the practice of “echoic correction” in spontaneous spoken dialogue, in which a speaker repeats a fragment of an interlocutor’s prior turn with altered prosody, substitution, or truncation to challenge, clarify, or redirect. On her view, the concept “copycat”—used to classify these repetitions—does not suffer from ordinary vagueness with hidden sharp boundaries, but rather slides irreducibly among three functional roles: faithful reproduction, parodic distortion, and original conversational contribution. She maintains that attempts to stabilize the concept by appeal to speaker intentionality, acoustic-similarity thresholds, or discourse-structural position necessarily leave a residual class of cases that serve one function in one context and a different one in a structurally identical context. I want eight to ten interview questions that press her on the actual machinery of this claim rather than asking her to restate it. Specifically, I want at least one question on why this should be treated as functional fluidity rather than as ordinary vagueness amenable to an epistemicist resolution; one on whether an acoustic-similarity metric coupled with a discourse-role algorithm could in principle eliminate the residual cases; one on what normative mistake, if any, is involved when participants classify a repetition differently from her framework; and one on how her view handles higher-order slippage—whether “functional fluidity” itself becomes unstable when applied to meta-level judgments about the practice. For each question, include a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give (for instance, that boundary-fixing always imports context-independent assumptions that the practice does not respect). Additionally, please frame any explanatory gloss you provide so that it clearly distinguishes what is broadly settled in pragmatics and conversation analysis—for example, that echoic repetition is a documented, structurally identifiable phenomenon—from what belongs specifically to her contested framework, and do not present the functional-fluidity thesis or the irreducibility claim as if they were consensus positions in the field.
