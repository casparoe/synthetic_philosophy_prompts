Read Harold Ramis's film Groundhog Day through Douglas Hofstadter's conception of the self as a strange loop — specifically the claim that an "I" is a self-referential symbol that achieves downward causal closure over its neural substrate. In the film, Phil Connors is trapped in a repeating February 2nd: he retains full memory across iterations, yet every other person and physical object resets. The usual moral reading treats the loop as a pedagogical purgatory that ends once he becomes altruistic. I want instead an ontological reading that asks whether the loop creates, destroys, or parodies the conditions for a Hofstadterian self. Since Phil's high-level pattern (his memories, intentions, self-model) persists while the substrate is forcibly restored, does the film stage a split between symbol and substrate? Consider his suicide attempts — driving off a cliff, stepping in front of a truck — after which he wakes unharmed in bed: if the self requires causal interaction with its body, what does it mean for the symbol to survive the body's annihilation and reset? Examine also the piano-lesson and ice-sculpture sequences, where he accumulates skills and artifacts that vanish overnight: is this a strange loop without downward closure, since the symbol's achievements leave no trace in the substrate? And when he finally escapes by becoming a different person, is that an ascent out of the loop, or is it the loop finally closing upon itself? I want a reading that takes both the film's comic metaphysics and Hofstadter's framework seriously: point to specific scenes where the strange-loop model illuminates something the moral interpretation misses, and to scenes where the film resists the model — perhaps because the loop is externally imposed rather than self-generated. Conclude by saying whether Groundhog Day adds something to the philosophy of self-reference that a purely theoretical account cannot.
