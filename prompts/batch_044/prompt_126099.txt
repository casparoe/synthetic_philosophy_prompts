I've been chewing over this idea with a friend from film school — basicly, when we dramatise people who've actually died, do we owe them something like 'posthumous narrative consent'? Not just accuracy for the audience's sake, but some kind of moral debt to the dead person themself. It sounds a bit metaphysical, I know, but there's a real philosophical thread here about whether harming someone's legacy counts as harming them.

Anyway, I want to test this against a concrete case: the biopic The Imitation Game, which depicts Alan Turing. You probably know the broad strokes — Turing was dead long before the film, and there was that well-publicised fuss about it inventing a fictional colleague named Christopher and playing up Turing's social awkwardness almost into caricature. The family and some historians were annoyed, but the film also clearly did enormous good in reviving public interest in Turing's actual story.

Can you analyse this episode through the lens of our obligations to the real dead? I mean, map it out properly: what exactly was distorted, who (if anyone) was wronged — Turing himself, living relatives, the historical record — and whether the 'greater good' of public awareness cancels the debt. But also go the other way: does the Turing case show that the theory of posthumous harm is to vague to be useful, or that we need to split the obligation into different kinds (say, fidelity to biography versus respect for symbolic legacy)? I don't want a bibliography, just a disciplined case study that runs both directions. Oh, and please don't just rehearse the standard 'artistic licence vs historical accuracy' debate — I was hoping for something sharper, maybe drawing on the idea that dead people retain a kind of 'narrative property right' even if they can't suffer.
