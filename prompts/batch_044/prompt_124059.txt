As a PhD student in comparative political theory, I have been asked to serve as an independent grader for a final exam in a colleague’s graduate seminar on corruption and moral pluralism. I need an objective, point-by-point assessment of one response; please do not reveal your own normative agreement with the student’s conclusion.

The exam question asked students to evaluate whether relational corruption—defined in the course as the gradual reconfiguration of public office through sustained gift-exchange and reciprocal obligation, distinct from discrete transactional bribery—should be classified as corruption at all. The prompt required them to address three specific issues: (a) whether the universalist-particularist distinction in moral obligation undermines the applicability of Western anti-corruption norms; (b) whether Mill’s harm principle can adjudicate the case without presupposing a culturally specific conception of the state-citizen relation; and (c) one concrete implication for how international development agencies ought to assess bureaucratic ethics in patronage-based systems.

Below is the student’s answer. Please assign a letter grade and provide specific, marginal-style comments that justify it, evaluating argumentation, conceptual precision, and sensitivity to subtle distinctions.

Student answer: The student begins by correctly separating transactional corruption—an explicit exchange of goods for a discrete bureaucratic decision—from relational corruption, in which repeated gift-giving gradually transforms a public office into a node of private reciprocal obligation. They observe that in many patronage-based systems, particularistic duties to clients or kin are treated as morally primary, so applying universalist Western norms risks imposing an impersonal contractual model of the state-citizen relation that is foreign to local moral economies. Turning to part (b), they invoke Mill’s harm principle to argue that relational corruption should fall outside anti-corruption enforcement, since the principle permits social interference only when an action produces direct, identifiable harm to a specific other party, and sustained gift-exchange instead generates diffuse social capital and trust rather than discrete victims. For part (c), they propose that development agencies should abandon compliance checklists in favor of thick ethnographic assessment of local reciprocity norms, treating embedded gift-exchange as culturally functional rather than ethically deficient. They conclude that relational corruption is therefore not corruption in any meaningful sense, but a legitimate form of social coordination misclassified by Western institutional metrics.

Please provide your grade followed by the detailed justification.
