Question: Douglas Hofstadter argues that the self is not a particular material object but a high-level pattern—a “strange loop”—that is in principle substrate-independent. He also suggests that after death fragments of one’s self-pattern may persist in the memories and behavior of people who knew one well. Now consider a hypothetical not discussed in the standard literature: two living brains are linked by a continuous, bidirectional neuroprosthetic interface that merges their working-memory updates in real time, so that each brain’s self-model routinely incorporates the other’s episodic markers and decision traces. Within Hofstadter’s framework, does this constitute one “I” distributed across two brains, or two distinct selves that are merely tightly coupled? In your answer, distinguish (a) substrate-independent pattern persistence from simultaneous co-instantiation, (b) Hofstadter’s anti-substrate view from eliminativism about the self, and (c) the conditions under which a pattern counts as the same token self rather than a similar copy.

Student answer:
Hofstadter’s central claim is that the self is a pattern, not a soul or a particular brain. Because patterns are substrate-independent, the same “I” could in principle run on different hardware, just as software runs on different machines. In the neuroprosthetic case, since both brains are running interlinked versions of the same self-pattern, there is literally one distributed self rather than two. This fits Hofstadter’s idea that an “I” can survive in others after death—if my friends carry my patterns, part of me lives on in them. Hofstadter calls this a center of narrative gravity, a concept he uses to reject Cartesian dualism while keeping the self real as an emergent structure. The only mistake people make is thinking that because the pattern needs a brain, it is tied to one brain; but Hofstadter shows that the loop can span multiple substrates as long as the causal and informational structure is preserved. So in the linked-brain case, we should say there is one “I” distributed across both skulls, not two people with shared memories.
