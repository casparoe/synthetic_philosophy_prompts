My younger sibling is on dialysis, and a recent dinner argument about whether live kidney donors should be permitted to receive direct cash compensation made me realize that my regional bioethics advisory board has no structured way to evaluate proposals that open markets in goods currently protected from trade. I need you to design a reusable decision procedure that the board—which has no economists on staff, meets monthly, and must defend its recommendations to a legislature that distrusts abstract philosophy—can run in roughly forty-five minutes whenever it is asked whether to permit, restrict, or prohibit a specific exchange involving bodily goods, civic rights, or emergency necessities.

The procedure should translate the philosophical distinction between corruption, in which pricing a good alters the intrinsic meaning or value attached to it, and crowding-out, in which pricing displaces non-market motivations, into a sequence of practical questions rather than theoretical claims. In particular, include an explicit “semantic degradation” step that asks whether introducing compensation would permanently change how the good is spoken about, recognized, and valued within the affected community, and specify what kind of observable shift in institutional language, social practice, or public justification counts as sufficient evidence of such degradation when we have no budget for formal sociological study. The questions should be ordered so that the board proceeds from factual determination to value judgment, with clear stopping rules that yield one of three recommendations—prohibit, restrict with enforceable safeguards, or permit with post-hoc monitoring—while marking every point where the answer depends on a judgment call that must be signed off by the board rather than derived from evidence. The procedure must also state explicitly what to do when relevant data is missing and must not pretend to settle questions that are genuinely about values.

Please apply the procedure in continuous prose to three cases: first, a proposal to allow direct cash payments to live kidney donors within a public health system; second, a proposal allowing residents of a flood-stricken region to trade future votes in a cooperative water-board election for immediate relief supplies; and third, a proposal to suspend price-gouging restrictions on bottled water after a hurricane, a case where the board expects the answer will be “permit with monitoring” but needs to see whether the procedure genuinely reaches that conclusion or breaks down in an instructive way. For each application, show where the procedure gives a definite answer, where it forces an uncomfortable judgment call—especially in one case where the recommendation will likely run against the board’s intuitive moral reaction—and whether there is any step at which the procedure admits it cannot settle the matter and returns the decision to our values. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire, produce answers we will dislike, or depend on distinctions that collapse under pressure.

Write the entire response as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if composing a single detailed letter. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works—the board has no research staff and needs something it can use immediately without searching for sources.
