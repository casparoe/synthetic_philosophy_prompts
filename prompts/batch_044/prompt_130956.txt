I've been working through the frame problem for an A-level philosophy essay, and I have constructed an argument that seems to prove rational planning is impossible for any physically realisable agent, which I know must be wrong, yet I cannot locate the exact misstep. The reasoning runs as follows. (1) Any rational agent that performs an action must update its belief-set so that it includes all and only those states of affairs that the action actually alters. (2) To determine whether a particular state S has changed, the agent must possess an explicit representation of S both prior to and following the action; otherwise it has no basis on which to register either alteration or preservation. (3) For any given action, there are indefinitely many states that remain entirely unaffected—flipping a light switch does not change the colour of distant mountains, the atomic weight of gold, or the current mood of the Prime Minister. (4) If the agent lacks explicit representations of these unchanged states, then it is unable to verify that they have indeed remained constant, which means it fails to satisfy condition (1) with respect to them. (5) Consequently, a rational agent must carry explicit representations of an indefinite number of unchanged states for every action it contemplates. (6) No physically realisable system can store or process an indefinite number of representations within finite time and memory. (7) Therefore, rational planning is impossible for any real agent.

I suspect the error lies somewhere between (2) and (4)—perhaps I am illegitimately conflating the absence of an explicit representation with an inability to maintain consistency, or sliding from epistemic verification to computational storage—but I cannot quite name the equivocation. Which premise or step ought I to reject, and what does surrendering it cost me? I should say that I am especially keen to preserve (1) and (6), since they appear to be basic constraints on rationality and on physical realisability respectively. Please also indicate what would change your mind: which counter-example or theoretical consideration would persuade you that my diagnosis of the flaw is mistaken, and where in this chain your own reasoning is most likely to fail?
