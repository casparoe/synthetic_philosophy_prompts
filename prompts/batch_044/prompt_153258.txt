At a recent faculty workshop on public education and identity, two senior colleagues—let's call them M. and N.—engaged in a sustained disagreement about whether French laïcité should be classified as assimilationist or as a genuinely multicultural framework distinct from Anglo-American models. I attended as a bystander and do not endorse either side. Their exchange unfolded as follows.

(1) M. opened by arguing that laïcité is best understood as a form of negative multiculturalism: by refusing to register religious or ethnic categories in public schools and institutions, the republican model prevents the state from sorting citizens into groups. This, she claimed, blocks both state domination and the patronizing politics of recognition that empowers community leaders over individuals. Anglo-American multiculturalism, by contrast, requires the state to classify citizens—through exemptions, funding categories, and representative quotas—thereby reifying group boundaries that individuals might otherwise transcend.

(2) N. replied that this framing gives assimilationism a philosophical alibi. The negative posture assumes that secular republican culture is not itself a particular tradition—specifically, a post-Catholic, Enlightenment-derived form of life with its own norms of bodily display, gender interaction, and temporal organization. Banning visible religious markers in schools does not erase culture; it enforces one culture's norms. Multiculturalism's explicit recognition may indeed reify groups, but at least it does not pretend that the majority's practices are universal or culturally neutral.

(3) M. countered that Anglo-American multiculturalism produces what she called asymmetric visibility: it grants exemptions and public visibility to minority practices—dietary rules, dress codes, holiday observances—while treating majority practices (secular calendars, standard professional dress, unmarked speech norms) as the invisible baseline. Minority identity is constantly performed and administratively managed, while majority identity disappears into normalcy. The French model, for all its severity, at least applies the same visibility rules to everyone: every citizen's particularities are stripped in the public sphere, rather than only the minority's being marked.

(4) N. responded that stripping particularity is ontologically impossible; it only strips particularities that the dominant culture has already coded as special or religious. A secular woman's uncovered hair is read as neutral, while a Muslim woman's covered hair is read as a symbolic attachment—because visibility is culturally interpreted, not naturally given. Thus the rule no visible religious symbols applies selectively: it renders dominant culture invisible and subordinate culture hyper-visible. True neutrality would require either accommodating all visible differences or suppressing all—including Christian ones—which never happens. The French model therefore does not achieve symmetry; it achieves invisibility for the powerful and hyper-visibility for the subordinate, which is precisely what makes it assimilationist rather than universalist.
