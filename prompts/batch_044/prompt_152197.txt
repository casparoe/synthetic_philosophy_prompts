Student response:

Epiphenomenalism, as defended in Jackson's earlier work, holds that phenomenal properties are caused by physical brain processes but exert no causal influence in return. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is that if my experience of redness is causally inert, it cannot cause my belief that I am seeing red; that belief is produced solely by underlying neural activity. This seems to make our first-person judgments about experience either unreliable or mysteriously accurate despite lacking any causal link to their objects.

The direct-acquaintance response denies that judgment-formation must be causal. On this view, I do not need my experience to push me into belief, because I stand in an immediate relation of awareness to the quality itself. This is essentially the phenomenal-concept strategy developed by Chalmers and Levine: they argue that we possess special recognitional concepts formed through direct acquaintance, allowing us to refer to qualia without causal mediation.

Because acquaintance operates outside the causal order, the epiphenomenalist can maintain that physical brain states fully cause the judgment-mechanism, while the acquaintance relation determines the judgment's specific content. So neural processes cause me to form a judgment, and direct acquaintance makes that judgment about this particular quale rather than another. This shows that phenomenal properties can be known without being causally efficacious, which fully resolves the paradox.

That said, the resolution is unstable. For direct acquaintance to be more than accidental correlation, the phenomenal property must be actively present to awareness in a way that shapes the acquaintance relation. This presence is itself a kind of causal contribution—if the quale did not exert any influence on the acquaintance state, then my being acquainted with redness rather than greenness would be unexplained. Consequently, direct acquaintance actually reintroduces causal efficacy through the back door, which means the epiphenomenalist who accepts it must abandon the claim that phenomenal properties are causally inert.
