I am facilitating a small cross-functional research collaboration that includes a Dutch engineer, a Japanese researcher, and an American project manager. During a peer review session, the Dutch engineer told the Japanese researcher, in front of the group, that her proposed analytical framework was "careless and showed she had not engaged with the existing literature," a judgment that was technically accurate—the framework duplicated findings already reported in two prior studies. The Japanese researcher offered no verbal reply, but she withdrew from all subsequent discussions and later told the American manager that she felt publicly humiliated and was considering leaving the project. Seeking to preserve the collaboration, the American manager told the Dutch engineer that the Japanese researcher had privately praised his directness as "exactly the kind of intellectual honesty this team needs," which was false; he also told the Japanese researcher that the Dutch engineer had expressed private regret and had intended only a narrow technical critique, which was also false. Consequently, the Dutch engineer now believes his bluntness was welcomed, while the Japanese researcher believes the offense was withdrawn, and both are operating on premises that are mutually incompatible with the actual events. I am now weighing whether to correct these misrepresentations and restore a shared factual basis, even if doing so risks dissolving the only equilibrium that currently allows work to continue. The Dutch engineer argues that any dilution of his message would have been patronizing and that clarity is a form of respect; the American manager argues that directness divorced from relational context is not honesty but aggression, and that small falsehoods are sometimes necessary to protect the conditions for future cooperation. I want your judgment: is the manager's use of falsehood ethically defensible as a repair mechanism, or does it compound one wrong with another? Was the engineer's directness a fulfillment of an obligation to be truthful, or a failure to recognize that identical utterances carry different force across communicative norms? And if I choose to disclose the misrepresentations to both parties, am I performing a necessary correction or merely destroying a strategically ambiguous arrangement that might be more valuable than transparency?
