Hey, I'm drafting a short proposal for our department's internal seed grant — it's only three months of funding, so I need to keep the scope tight but still sound like it's not just homework. The idea I've been chewing over came up in a grad seminar on social choice where someone mentioned Harsanyi's aggregation theorem in passing, and I got stuck on something that doesn't seem to get much airtime.

Usually the theorem is presented like this: if every agent has von Neumann-Morgenstern preferences and we require the social ordering to satisfy Pareto, anonymity, and the independence axiom, then social welfare is just a weighted sum of individual utilities. Fine. But almost every treatment I've seen takes the weights as given — exogenously assigned, like we're just declaring that person A counts twice as much as person B — and it assumes that each person's independence axiom holds in exactly the same way regardless of context. I want to ask whether the result survives if individuals violate independence in a structured, non-random fashion: specifically, when the gamble involves outcomes that are jointly experienced (public goods, shared risks) versus purely private ones. If people's risk attitudes shift systematically based on whether they're deciding alone or as part of a group, does Harsanyi's linear aggregation still hold, or does it collapse into something non-linear? And is the problem really the independence failure, or does it expose that the state-space descriptions aren't interpersonally comparable once framing changes?

The existing positions — at least the ones I'm familiar with — either assume independence away for everyone to save the theorem, or they reject aggregation altogether without asking whether a modified representation could work. So there's a real gap.

My plan: build a minimal two-agent, two-state formal model where each agent's preferences depend on a "social framing" parameter. Month one: formalize the framework and check consistency conditions. Month two: try to derive (or disprove) a representation theorem for the social ordering — I'm expecting either state-dependent weights or a proof that linearity is unavoidable and framing-dependence just gets absorbed into risk aversion. Month three: write the philosophical payoff — does this mean Harsanyi-style aggregation is incompatible with context-sensitive rationality, or do we just need a richer notion of the weights?

What counts as success: either a modified theorem with framing-contingent weights, or a clear negative result showing no representation exists without collapsing back to standard linear aggregation. The project fails if I find out that the framing effect I described is just a redescription of ordinary risk-aversion differences that don't actually change the aggregation structure at all — then it's not a challenge, it's just relabeling. Given it's one semester, I'll stick to the two-agent case and leave generalization for later. I don't need anyone to send me a bibliography — I'll track down the refereces myself.
