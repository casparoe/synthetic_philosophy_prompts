In a recent philosophy-of-language seminar, a participant summarized the state of play on the Liar paradox and theories of truth as follows:

"The Liar sentence demonstrates that any language containing its own truth predicate and capable of self-reference is outright inconsistent. Tarski resolved this by showing that truth can only be defined in a strictly richer metalanguage, which proves there is no single universal truth concept—only a stacked hierarchy of language-level predicates. Deflationists such as Horwich reject the hierarchy because they hold that truth is nothing more than a disquotational device; consequently, they treat the Liar not as a genuine paradox but as a sentence that fails to express a determinate proposition. Paraconsistent logicians, by contrast, maintain that the Liar is both true and false, and therefore that classical logic must be abandoned entirely. Most working philosophers now accept the hierarchical solution as correct but incomplete, and they draw the further lesson that natural language is implicitly stratified into object-level and meta-level registers, which explains why ordinary speakers never actually produce liar-like assertions. This philosophical point has direct architectural implications: if an artificial system can represent its own outputs as sentences and apply a unified truth predicate across those representations, it will necessarily generate logical inconsistencies unless it implements a rigid Tarskian stratification in its reasoning architecture. Present large language models avoid this danger precisely because they lack any integrated reflexive truth predicate."

The speaker then concluded that any future superintelligent system designed for open-ended reasoning must therefore be engineered with an explicit metalanguage boundary, or it will be logically unstable.
