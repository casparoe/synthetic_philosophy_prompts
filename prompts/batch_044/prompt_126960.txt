I've been leaning hard into Good's result that free infomation can't hurt a rational Bayesian agent -- the expected value of knowledge is non-negative if you upate correctly and choose optimally afterward. From that I think strategic ignorance is never actually rational: avoiding a medical test or skipping the news is either hiding a cost or confessing you'd reason badly after learning. But I want to know what counts against this. Not just the standard "but knowing makes you sad" objection -- I want the real defeaters: cases where knowing lowers your ex-ante utility even with perfect reasoning, like preference shifts or strategic games where being ignorant binds you. Which ones are fatal, which are just awkward? What do defenders of Good's view themselves concede counts against them, versus what they dismiss by saying you're not rational? And does the view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability -- if every counterexample gets answered with "then you weren't a proper Bayesian," is that a problem? I also want to know what this predicts that rival accounts of rational ignorance don't, and whether there's a live formal counterexample that doesn't depend on post-learning irrationality. Give me the exposure map: what's genuinely risky for the position and what's just noise.
