I'm trying to pin down my actual view on moral responsibility when someone couldn't have done otherwise, working from gut reactions rather than theory.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Classic Frankfurt case: Black waits to force Jones to vote for Candidate A if Jones doesn't do so on his own, but Jones decides independently and votes for A. Black never acts. Verdict: Jones is fully responsible, despite lacking an alternative.

2. Flicker variant: Black's device detects only an involuntary micro-hesitation—not a real choice—and would intervene then. Jones proceeds smoothly with no flicker. Verdict: Still responsible; the missing "possibility" was never genuinely available to him anyway.

3. Predictive scaffold: An AI suppresses Sarah's harmful impulses before they reach awareness, yet within safe bounds she deliberates normally and donates generously. Verdict: She is responsible for the donation.

4. Total enclosure: The same system suppresses harm so completely that Sarah never even conceives of it; her good choices feel inevitable to her. Verdict: Not responsible, or significantly diminished, despite the identical behavioral outcome as case 3.

5. Physical constraint: A firefighter is chained to a wall and physically cannot flee a burning building; she chooses to stay and rescue a child. Verdict: Responsible for the rescue.

6. Moral farm: A person is raised in an environment where harmful options are made psychologically inaccessible through perfect conditioning, not coercion; she genuinely wants only good outcomes. Verdict: Not responsible for her good deeds.

The tension is sharp: cases 3 and 4 are structurally close but I split them; cases 1 and 5 both involve hard causal impossibility of leaving, yet I treat them alike; case 6 resembles 3 but gets the opposite verdict.

Is there a single principle—perhaps about whether the blockage sits outside or is internalized within the agent's own reasoning—that saves cases 1, 2, 3, and 5 while explaining 4 and 6? Or does coherence require overturning one verdict? Please state the principle precisely, apply it to an unlisted case (a trusted advisor silently vetoes harmful plans before the agent notices them), and say honestly which of the six verdicts it cannot accommodate. Answer in American English.
