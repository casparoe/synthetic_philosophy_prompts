I serve on the acquisitions and ethics committee of my university’s special collections library, and we face a concrete decision that raises fundamental questions about archival power and the ethics of preservation. We have been offered a substantial donation comprising the surviving documentary traces of a 1970s tenant-organizing campaign in a neighborhood since demolished: not the organizers’ own papers, which were destroyed in an arson attack and never institutionally preserved, but the landlords’ security logs, eviction notices, surveillance photographs, and court filings that record those organizers exclusively as targets of disciplinary attention. Alongside these coercive records come recently recorded oral reconstructions conducted by descendants—what the donor calls “shadow histories”—which attempt to recover the organizers’ voices through family memory and inferential reasoning, though they contain no contemporaneous documentary corroboration.

The donor proposes that we accept the collection, digitize it, and apply what she terms “restorative cataloging”: reorienting metadata, finding aids, and descriptive practices so that the surveilled subjects appear as primary agents rather than as entries in the landlords’ administrative apparatus. Some committee members argue that this is legitimate interpretive repair, since standard descriptive neutrality already encodes the perspective of power; others contend that it imposes anachronistic moral categories onto documents never intended to bear them, thereby distorting historical understanding. A further and more troubling consideration is that preserving and circulating these materials—even with reframed descriptions—may extend the original harm by continuing to display data and images extracted through coercion, and that some silences might be ethically preferable to a mediated presence that remains structurally violent.

We must recommend by our meeting on the fifteenth whether to accept the donation (with or without conditions), reject it, or defer for further community consultation. Please draft the committee’s formal opinion memo: it should present a clear, actionable recommendation with a specific implementation timeline; engage seriously with the argument that restorative cataloging risks historical distortion; and treat with full honesty the strongest consideration on the opposing side—namely, that an institution’s preservation of domination’s instruments can replicate the very erasure it claims to remedy, and that ethical refusal is sometimes more appropriate than curatorial redemption. The memo should be a document the committee could circulate and vote upon, not personal advisory counsel.
