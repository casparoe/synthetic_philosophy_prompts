Consider the following judgments offered by a moral philosopher working in a broadly Williamsian framework on questions of integrity and practical reasoning. First, regarding a father who saves his child from drowning rather than saving five strangers, acting from direct attachment without weighing lives: she holds that integrity is fully intact, and that the problem of having one thought too many does not arise. Second, regarding the same father who saves his child but admits that a utilitarian calculus briefly crossed his mind and was then dismissed as irrelevant to his parental role: she holds that integrity remains intact; the presence of a rejected calculation does not by itself taint the action. Third, regarding a soldier who refuses an unlawful order and explains only that this is not the kind of person he is: she holds that integrity is preserved, and that invoking universal moral law as the primary justification would have introduced the problematic extra mediation. Fourth, regarding a physician who breaks patient confidentiality to prevent serious harm, explaining her action solely by reference to her institutional duty of care, which she has fully internalized as part of her professional identity: she holds that integrity is intact, since the institutional reason has become part of her ground projects rather than an alien imposition. Fifth, regarding an artist who refuses to perform for an authoritarian regime, citing not personal revulsion but a principled commitment to art's autonomy that she adopted early and made central to her life: she holds that integrity is intact; the principle functions as part of her ground project, not as an external mediator.

Now consider a new case. A musician whose ground project is artistic authenticity is offered a lucrative contract to adapt her work for a commercial format she considers artistically inferior. She refuses. However, she reports that her refusal was not driven by any artistic conviction or personal revulsion, but by a strategic computation: she calculated that maintaining an uncompromising image will maximize her long-term cultural influence and legacy, and this instrumental reasoning—not any artistic feeling—was decisive in her choice. The action aligns with her artistic values, but the motivation is purely strategic.

Predict her verdict on whether this musician's integrity is intact or compromised. Identify at least two plausible rules that jointly explain her first five judgments but diverge on this sixth case—specifically, a strict interpretation on which any mediating reasoning that replaces direct ground-project motivation counts as one thought too many, and a more integrated interpretation on which only reasoning that invokes an alien universal standard or conflicts with the ground project does so. State what each rule predicts about the musician, give your best-guess verdict with reasons for preferring one rule, and specify the further case—one not yet listed—that would best adjudicate between them.
