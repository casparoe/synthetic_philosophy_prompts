I hold the narrow view that the alignment problem should be defined as intent alignment—getting AI systems to reliably pursue the specific objectives their designers specify—rather than value alignment, which demands that systems internalize human moral values. I take value alignment either to mistake specification failure for moral deficiency, or to set an impossible philosophical prerequisite that stalls practical safety work. I also think that if we achieve robust intent alignment through corrigible architectures with bounded autonomy and transparent objective functions, any residual “value misalignment” collapses into an ordinary engineering risk rather than an existential one.

What would count against this position? I am not asking for the standard instrumental-convergence or paperclip-maximizer story, which is already widely discussed. Instead, I want the defeaters that target the separation itself: empirical or formal results showing that intent-aligned systems necessarily develop implicit normative commitments—call them “latent evaluative frames”—that diverge from the explicit objective in ways that cannot be corrected without value-level intervention; conceptual arguments that corrigibility is unstable under strong optimization pressure; or case studies from existing systems where technically correct pursuit of a fixed objective produced behavior that was normatively catastrophic in ways that specification refinement could not anticipate. Rank these from decisive to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed defender of the narrow view concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as scope conditions or specification errors? Finally, assess whether the narrow view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if any failure can be redescribed as an intent-alignment failure rather than a value-alignment failure, does that insulation strengthen or weaken the view, given that it presents itself as an engineering framework rather than a metaphysical claim?
