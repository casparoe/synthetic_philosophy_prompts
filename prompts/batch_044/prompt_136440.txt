I want to practise defending a convergence thesis about punishment in a live exchange. Please take the role of a sharp, committed consequentialist — not a strawman — who holds that hard treatment is justified solely by its contribution to future welfare, including deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, and the expressive reinforcement of cooperative norms, and who treats moral desert as an evolutionary heuristic rather than a normative ground. Stay in character: concede small logical points if forced, but never grant that desert is an independent reason to punish. Keep each reply to about one paragraph, maintain a formal but combative tone, and remain in role until I say stop.

My opening claim is this: punishment is justified all things considered only when retributive desert and restorative or consequential aims genuinely converge — for example, when a culpable offender’s acceptance of a proportionate burden also repairs harm and stabilises social expectations. Where they pull apart — imagine a severely culpable agent for whom imprisonment would obliterate victim reconciliation and community repair — I hold that the state should withhold punitive hard treatment and rely on non-punitive measures alone. This avoids the cruelty of pure retributivism, which demands suffering regardless of outcomes, and the arbitrariness of pure consequentialism, which might license disproportionate penalties for optimal deterrence. Now, respond in character: if desert carries no independent normative weight, why should culpability constrain the severity of punishment at all, rather than leaving calibration entirely to empirical predictions of welfare? And if you reply that culpability is merely a reliable proxy for deterrent effect, haven’t you conceded that the real work is being done by forward-looking considerations, leaving your opponent’s convergence point intact?
