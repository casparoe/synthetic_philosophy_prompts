Many philosophers and AI researchers invoke Dennett’s thermostat to argue that we should freely attribute beliefs and desires to large language models: just as saying the thermostat wants to keep the room near 72 degrees is justified if the intentional stance predicts its behavior, saying that a model believes Paris is the capital of France is justified if that framing predicts its outputs, regardless of whether there is a localized “belief box” inside. This analogy is often used to license treating AI systems as genuine intentional agents in safety and interpretability discourse, and to dismiss worries that internal mechanisms are too alien for mentalistic vocabulary.

But there is a competing analogy, drawn less from philosophy of mind than from statistical mechanics: attributing a belief-state to an LLM is like attributing temperature to a single molecule. Temperature is a robust macro-variable for an ensemble, but applying it to one particle is a category error; by the same token, “belief” might be a legitimate system-level descriptor for a biological brain but a misapplied micro-description when projected onto distributed weights or individual attention heads. This counter-analogy is meant to show that the intentional stance toward LLMs confuses descriptive levels and smuggles in a false localization.

Then identify where each mapping breaks: does the thermostat lack something the LLM possesses (representational structure, feedback loops, compositional syntax), or does the molecule analogy falsely assume beliefs must be localized rather than distributed? Separate the disanalogies that undermine the intended conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. In particular, examine what each analogy quietly imports about whether intentional descriptions are instrumental fictions, emergent properties, or literally true, and whether that import survives contact with how transformer architectures actually process information. Does the conclusion that we should freely adopt the intentional stance toward AI survive once both analogies are on the table? If neither fully holds, propose a better analogy—perhaps drawn from distributed cognition, control theory, or predictive processing—and say what it would need to carry that these do not.
