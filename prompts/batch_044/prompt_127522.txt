I'm a doctoral candidate in international law and political theory, writing on the conceptual architecture of atrocity classification. At a recent workshop, two senior colleagues—I'll call them Dr. A (a moral philosopher working on collective harm) and Dr. B (an international lawyer specializing in treaty interpretation)—had a sharp disagreement about whether the legal category of genocide has become conceptually detached from the moral phenomenon Raphael Lemkin originally sought to name. They asked me to referee, and I want a careful point-by-point adjudication rather than a general impression.

Dr. A opened by arguing that Lemkin's original formulation included cultural and political destruction, whereas the 1948 Convention narrowed genocide to physical or biological acts against specifically enumerated groups. For A, this narrowing was not a conceptual clarification but a political compromise—most notoriously the exclusion of political groups, which was driven by state interests rather than moral reasoning. A then claimed that the "politics of naming" demonstrates the damage: states systematically avoid the genocide label precisely because the legal category triggers obligations they wish to evade. Therefore, A concluded, we should treat the legal definition as merely one regulative instrument and preserve an independent moral category that includes cultural destruction and group-targeted political violence, even if that broader category lacks precise legal enforcement mechanisms.

Dr. B replied that A was committing a category mistake. The Convention's value lies exactly in its narrowness and the specific intent (dolus specialis) requirement; without these constraints, the term becomes a rhetorical device that can be applied to every ethnic conflict or revolutionary purge. B acknowledged that states avoid the label, but insisted this is a feature of legal precision, not a bug—false positives dilute the norm's power and undermine the very advocacy A cares about. B further argued that Lemkin himself was pragmatically committed to legal codification, so appealing to his "true" moral vision misrepresents the historical project.

A came back with two moves. First, he said B was defending a "positivist fallacy": treating the Convention's text as constitutive of genocide rather than as an imperfect regulatory attempt to capture a pre-existing moral reality. Second, A introduced what he called "atrocity calibration"—the idea that the legal threshold's specificity creates a perverse incentive for perpetrators to design violence that falls just below it (e.g., through cultural destruction or dispersed killing lacking provable specific intent), which means the narrow legal category may actually facilitate evasion rather than prevent it.

B responded that "atrocity calibration" is empirically speculative and that, even if true, it supports tightening enforcement of the existing category rather than expanding the moral definition. She then accused A of "moral essentialism": assuming that a concept's moral purity should override its institutional function. B noted that torture, for comparison, is defined partly by its moral wrongness but also by specific legal elements; expanding genocide to include all group-targeted harm would erase the distinction between genocide and other crimes against humanity, making targeted advocacy impossible.

They ended without agreement. Please go through their exchange move by move—rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Say who is right about which specific point, what both parties are missing (for instance, whether the distinction between regulative and constitutive definitions actually resolves their dispute), and who is more right overall. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in each of your assessments, and explicitly identify the point on which you are least certain.
