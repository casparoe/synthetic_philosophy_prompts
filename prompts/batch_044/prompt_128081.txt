You are an expert philosopher of science with deep familiarity with Imre Lakatos’s methodology of scientific research programmes, and I would like you to think carefully, begin with brief brainstorming, and then deliver a precise analytical answer. I am trying to determine what I actually think about when a research programme counts as genuinely progressive versus degenerating, going by my gut reactions to concrete cases rather than by textbook definitions. Here are my verdicts.

First, consider a neuroscience programme whose hard core holds that long-term memory requires stable synaptic weights. When rapid one-trial learning is observed—seemingly too fast for structural synaptic change—the researchers postulate a transient molecular buffer that later consolidates into weights, and this postulate generates testable predictions about protein-synthesis inhibition windows. I call this progressive. Second, a linguistics programme with the hard core that syntax is fully autonomous from semantics encounters persistent pragmatic counterexamples. Its defenders respond by redescribing every counterexample as a performance error governed by an ever-expanding list of auxiliary conditions, but they never derive a novel syntactic prediction from these conditions. I call this degenerating. Third, a climate-modelling programme anchored on radiative forcing as the dominant driver of global temperature encounters an unexpected decadal cooling trend. The researchers introduce an aerosol-forcing parameter calibrated precisely to cancel the anomaly, yet the parameter carries no independent predictive content about other climate variables. I call this degenerating, though I feel it is less intellectually dishonest than the linguistics case. Fourth, an evolutionary-biology programme committed to natural selection as the primary engine of adaptation faces rapid phenotypic shifts that outpace selection coefficients. Its proponents propose a mechanism of stress-induced epigenetic inheritance, and this mechanism subsequently predicts specific transgenerational effects in controlled model-organism studies. I call this progressive. Fifth, a behavioural-economics programme with the hard core of expected-utility maximization confronts systematic violations of transitivity. Its defenders invoke context-dependent preference frames that can be reshuffled post hoc to reproduce any observed choice pattern without ever risking a falsified prediction. I call this degenerating.

Sixth, and this is the case I keep going back and forth on: a theoretical-physics programme modifies its protective belt to accommodate a failed high-precision replication of one of its predictions, but the modification takes the form of a single simplifying equation that reduces free parameters elsewhere in the theory and restores coherence with an older, previously abandoned derivation. Some part of me wants to call this progressive because of what we might term heuristic compression—a net reduction in overall theoretical complexity—while another part insists that accommodating a direct failure is the hallmark of degeneration. I cannot settle whether the elegance of the repair should outweigh the fact that it was triggered by disconfirmation.
