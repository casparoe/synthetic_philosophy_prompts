Casey said that's misapplying metorology to social systems; societies have tons of redundancy and negative feedback, so small perturbations usually get absorbed rather than amplified. She pointed to the Cuban Missile Crisis, where there were like three separate near-nuclear moments in one week but instutitional protocols kept absorbing the shock, which to her shows history has "basins of attraction" that keep it on track no matter what.

Jordan came back with this concept he called localized contingency at bifurcation points — chaos does have strange attractors, he said, but when a system is already near a structural breaking point, a tiny nudge can push it into a totally different basin. So it's not that any nail matters; it's that nails matter exactly when the horse is already on a crumbling bridge. Casey replied that this just collapses contingency into determinism with extra steps, because now the macro conditions do all the work and the "small" event is only causally relevant because the big deterministic structure was already primed to flip. She also said Jordan can't have it both ways: if chaos proves history is unpredictable, then he can't also claim there are predictable "weak points" where chaos suddenly activates. They asked me to referee who actually understands chaos theory's implications for history, whether Jordan's bifurcation move saves contingency or kills it, and if Casey's damping argument ignores real cases where tiny events clearly changed macro outcomes.
