I've been writing this novel where the main character is a sentencing judge who has to sit through victim impact statements before she decides prison time, and there's a tension in the court manual that I can't shake. On one hand she's told to imaginatively enter the victim's suffering, step inside their shoes and really feel it with them; on the other hand she's warned to stay an imparial spectator, detached, not letting her own feelings run the verdict. That sounds like Adam Smith's sympathy thing, right? Where you imagine yourself in another's place but there's also this internal spectator watching and judging?

Anyway, I'm wondering what this instutional practice actually presupposses about sympathy and moral knowledge. Does it need the idea that sympathy is contagious, like catching another's pain like a cold? Or does it depend on Smith's notion of an internal division—part of you suffers with them, another part observes and approves? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it's coherent or quietly smuggling in rival views, and tell me if several theories could license it. If we redesigned the courtroom to take Smith seriously—say, separating the sympathy role from the spectator role—what would the victim-impact ritual have to become? I keep thinking about Smith's point that we never actually feel the sufferer's full intensity, only our own idea of it, which makes me wonder if the whole ritual asks the judge to do something impossible.
