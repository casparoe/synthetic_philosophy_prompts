After retiring from a long career in experimental chemistry, I have recently begun a systematic study of metaphysics, and I find myself returning repeatedly to the problem of universals—not merely in its classical form, but as it arises within the practice of scientific classification itself. In the laboratory, we routinely treat properties such as solubility, catalytic activity, or superconductivity as if they were genuinely shared across instances, yet the underlying physical realizations are often strikingly heterogeneous. This has led me to wonder whether the standard framing—realism versus nominalism versus conceptualism—adequately captures the conceptual difficulty when a universal, if it exists, appears to be what philosophers sometimes call disjunctive or multi-realizable.

Could you explain the core conceptual issue here in some detail? Specifically, I would like you to clarify how a realist about universals might defend the existence of a shared property—say, the property of being a catalyst—when that property is instantiated by enzymes, transition-metal surfaces, and synthetic zeolites through entirely different mechanistic pathways. At the same time, please address why a nominalist might regard this very heterogeneity as proof that “catalysis” is nothing more than a convenient label for a family of causally unrelated processes, and whether conceptualism offers any genuine middle ground or merely relocates the problem into the structure of human cognition. I am particularly interested in whether there is a coherent position—perhaps some form of trope theory or a “thin” structural realism—that allows scientific categories to be genuinely explanatory without committing us to the existence of thick, Platonic universals that exist independently of all particular instances.
