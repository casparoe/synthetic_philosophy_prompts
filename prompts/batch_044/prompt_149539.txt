Two philosophers of science, Mara and Elias, are discussing whether "autism spectrum disorder" as operationalized in current psychiatric nosology is a natural kind. Mara argues that despite well-known phenotypic heterogeneity, convergent evidence points to disrupted synaptic pruning and altered neural connectivity patterns during early development, suggesting that the category tracks a genuine causal architecture in nature rather than merely grouping superficially similar behaviors. Elias responds that these biological findings are probabilistic, distributed unevenly across diagnosed individuals, and also present in other conditions; he claims the category functions more like "fever"—a useful clinical flag that aggregates disparate underlying etiologies for practical management, not a unified kind that carves nature at its joints. Mara counters that fever itself tracks a specific homeostatic mechanism (pyrogenic cytokine signaling), so the analogy actually supports her position: clinical categories can be natural kinds even with heterogeneous surface manifestations, provided there is a shared underlying causal structure. Elias then objects that autism lacks any comparable unified mechanism—some cases involve rare monogenic mutations, others polygenic risk profiles, others prenatal environmental factors—so there is no "the" mechanism analogous to pyrogenesis. Mara replies that requiring a single necessary mechanism is an outdated essentialist criterion; she proposes that autism may instead constitute a homeostatic property cluster sustained by feedback loops among genetic susceptibilities, neurodevelopmental timing, and behavioral adaptations, which longitudinal studies of social-cognitive divergence appear to support. Elias concedes that homeostatic cluster theory is coherent in principle, but insists that for a psychiatric category to count as a natural kind we need evidence that categorical diagnosis outperforms purely dimensional or trait-based models in prediction and intervention, and he argues that current data do not clearly demonstrate this for DSM-5 autism relative to a continuous "social-communication variation" framework without categorical boundaries.

Please go through their exchange round by round and adjudicate who is right about each specific point, noting where both may be partly correct and what, if anything, they are each missing. In your assessment, clearly separate what is well-established in philosophy of science and psychiatric taxonomy from what remains genuinely contested among knowledgeable researchers, and do not present a disputed view as though it were the consensus.
