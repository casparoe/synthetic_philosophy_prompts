In the dialogue, Socrates compares true opinions to statues made by Daedalus: they run away unless you tie them down with an explanation of the cause, and that tying-down is knowledge. People in my class keep using this statue picture to argue that knowledge is just true belief plus a justification-tether, and that without it your beliefs wander off like runaway sculptures.

But I keep thinking about my old lab work — sometimes we'd recieve the right number on a spectrometer without knowing why the peak was there, and other times we had the full mechanistic story. So I want to audit the Daedalus analogy properly: what exactly corresponds to the statue, the running away, the rope, and the sculptor? Where does the mapping break — for instance, do beliefs actually run away in the same sense a statue might, or is that smuggling in some weird psychology? And there's this modern idea I heard mentioned, anti-luck epistemology, which frames knowledge as true belief that isn't modally lucky — does that fit the statue picture, or does it break the analogy? Most importantly, does the conclusion that knowledge is more valuable because it's stable actually survive once you separate the parts that map cleanly from the parts that don't? Give me the correspondence table, the disanalogies that matter for the value claim, and whether there's a better analogy from science — maybe something with instrumentation or calibration — that does the same work without the weird art-history baggage.
