My philosophy of language seminar has built an argument that the is-ought gap is rationally self-defeating, and I want to know where the chain breaks without giving up the gap itself. Here are the steps. (1) Hume's principle—the is-ought gap—holds that no set of purely descriptive premises can, by valid logical inference alone, entail a normative conclusion. (2) If this principle is correct, then descriptive reasoning exhibits what we might call normative closure failure: no chain of descriptive premises, however long, can ever justify a normative belief. (3) Every normative belief we actually hold—including beliefs about moral obligation, rational duty, and even the duty to respect Hume's principle—is ultimately supported by descriptive premises about human suffering, preference structures, or institutional arrangements. (4) So if Hume's principle is true, then none of our normative beliefs, including our belief in Hume's principle, are ever justified. (5) A principle that removes the justification for believing itself is rationally self-defeating and should be rejected. (6) Rejecting the principle means allowing normative conclusions to follow from descriptive premises, so the gap collapses. (7) But this feels wrong: deriving "you ought to reduce suffering" from "many people suffer" still seems to require a hidden normative bridge, not just logic. I am committed to keeping (1) and (5), and I want to preserve the intuition behind (7). Which step between (2) and (4) is the weak link, and what does abandoning it cost? Does giving up (2) force me to allow that descriptive reasoning can justify norms without any bridge principle, or is there a more targeted repair?
