I am developing an essay that inverts Douglas Hofstadter's knob-twiddling account of conceptual creativity. On the standard reading, innovation occurs by altering selected parameters—the knobs—of an existing mental structure while leaving the rest intact. I want to argue that this mislocates the creative act: the genuine commitment lies not in which knobs are turned, but in which ones are held rigidly constant, and that the variations are merely the working-out of that invariant choice. To test this, I apply the framework to contrapuntal composition in Bach's Die Kunst der Fuge, treating the fugue subject's intervallic skeleton as the deep invariant and the surface transformations—inversion, augmentation, change of voice count and mode—as the twisted knobs. The claim is that Bach's creativity resides in his refusal to alter the skeleton, not in the ingenuity of the surface variations, which are deterministic given the invariant. I then extend the argument to the history of modal logic: Aristotelian syllogistic form persisted for centuries as an invariant skeleton despite radical reinterpretations of its terms and scope, and I ask whether that persistence demonstrates creative discipline (the invariant was deliberately protected) or conceptual stagnation (the invariant was never questioned). Two counter-cases are meant to pressure the thesis: algorithmically generated fugue variations, which twist every available knob without an invariant commitment, and scientific revolutions in which the deep invariant itself is only discovered retroactively through the process of parameter adjustment. Before proceeding, I want targeted conceptual criticism rather than source recommendations. Specifically: (1) Does this inversion misrepresent Hofstadter's emphasis on the act of parameter change, and is there a principled way to distinguish deep invariants from surface ones in a contrapuntal structure without importing authoritative music-theoretic judgments? (2) Does the comparison with algorithmic generation actually establish that invariance without commitment yields mere mechanical variation, or does it illegitimately conflate intentional design with causal mechanism? (3) Are there clear historical cases of conceptual creativity in which the invariant was identified only after extensive knob-twiddling, which would undermine the claim that the invariant is logically and psychologically prior? (4) Does applying this inverted model to modal logic conflate normative questions about conceptual progress with descriptive questions about cognitive structure? Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation suggestions; I am asking for an assessment of the argument's coherence, its fidelity to the model it invokes, and whether the distinction between creative discipline and stagnation can be sustained within the framework proposed.
