I recently found myself in a prolonged disagreement with a close friend about whether a technically crude but emotionally direct community mural could be considered greater art than a meticulously executed gallery installation that left us both feeling nothing — a dispute that replayed, in modern terms, Tolstoy’s well-known distinction between genuine art and counterfeit art, though with complications his framework seems ill-equipped to handle. Before I commit to any particular view, I want the full specification. Write the desiderata that an adequate normative theory of artistic value must meet if it is to take seriously Tolstoy’s central claim that art is essentially a means of transmitting feeling in service of human fraternity, while also accommodating what I will call moral resonance asymmetry: the phenomenon whereby an artwork intended to foster brotherly love in one interpretive community produces alienation or moral revulsion in another without being technically defective or emotionally inauthentic.

For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual consideration — which artistic phenomena it must account for (such as the dismissal of technically masterful but emotionally hollow productions that Tolstoy classifies as counterfeit), which distinctions it must preserve (for instance, between emotional authenticity and emotional efficacy, or between universal accessibility and culturally situated reception), and which findings from moral psychology or aesthetics it must not contradict. Clearly flag which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, and identify any subset of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable — I am especially interested in whether a theory can simultaneously require universal emotional transmission and full sensitivity to moral resonance asymmetry without internal contradiction. Keep the weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on a comprehensive scorecard, though I would like a brief indication of how Tolstoy’s own moralist theory, a formalist position that denies moral content any artistic relevance, and a pluralist account that treats emotional transmission as one artistic value among many fare against your list.
