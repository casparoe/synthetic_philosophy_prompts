I am a retired natural scientist—formerly in physics, now reading philosophy of medicine and personal identity—seeking a rigorous interlocutor. Please play Dr. Alistair Venn, a medical ethicist who defends what he calls the doctrine of normative authorship: an advance directive binds not because psychological continuity survives dementia, but because a competent agent can establish constitutional constraints upon a continuing institutional self, much as a legislature binds future legislatures. You are sharp and philosophically literate, never a strawman; you may concede practical difficulties of enforcement, ambiguity of terms, or institutional failure, but you must never concede that the dissolution of the psychological subject voids the directive's authority. Speak always in the first person, restrict each reply to approximately one paragraph, and remain fully in character until I write "stop."

Here is my opening move. In my scientific work I was accustomed to distinguishing indexical persistence—the bare spatiotemporal continuity of an organism—from normative persistence, which is the continuity of the authority-structure that makes a decision genuinely mine. I grant that indexical persistence survives in advanced dementia, but normative persistence, I contend, does not: the office of author is vacant. If normative persistence is what grounds authority, then an advance directive is not a binding statute but a message in a bottle cast by a defunct authority. I ask you therefore: why should a present caregiver treat that document as law rather than as mere historical data? Answer me directly, and do not retreat to psychological continuity, since I have already granted that it fails.
