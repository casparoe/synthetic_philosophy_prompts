At a recent seminar on semiotics and self-reference, two participants—Leah and Jonas—disagreed about a hypothetical artifact they called the Codex. The Codex is conceived as a hand-bound manuscript with three simultaneous registers: (1) a frame consisting of its provenance from a nineteenth-century workshop known to have produced both legitimate scientific instruments and fraudulent spiritualist apparatuses; (2) an outer message, a technically precise manual describing the construction of a mechanical clock; and (3) an inner message, discoverable only by measuring the spacing of specific letters, which encodes a logical argument that mechanical causation is inherently inconsistent. They applied Hofstadter’s tripartite distinction—frame, outer, and inner messages—to determine where the artifact’s meaning actually resides.

Leah opened by arguing that the frame is primary. Given the workshop’s history of deception, she said, any competent interpreter should approach the Codex with hermeneutic suspicion; without that framing context, one might naively attempt to build the clock, missing that the object is designed to stage a conflict between appearance and reality. Jonas replied that this overstates the frame’s authority: if the outer message genuinely contains buildable instructions, its practical efficacy cannot be dissolved by historical context. One could construct a functioning clock from the text alone, which proves the outer message possesses autonomous content that the frame can color but not cancel.

Leah then pressed the role of the inner message. The hidden argument against mechanical causation does not merely supplement the manual; it structurally undermines the presuppositions required to take the outer message seriously. This creates a strange loop—like those Hofstadter discusses—where the message system refers back to and destabilizes its own conditions of interpretation. Because the loop operates between inner and outer messages, she concluded that the frame is still the stable ground: only the suspicious frame explains why the loop is present at all, and thus the frame determines the artifact’s ultimate significance as a demonstration of interpretive instability.

Jonas countered that Leah was mislocating the loop. The contradiction between inner and outer messages is a property of the message system itself, not of the frame. The workshop’s ambiguous reputation is an external container—historically interesting, but not logically necessary to recognize the loop. Meaning, he insisted, is therefore distributed across the relation between inner and outer messages; the frame supplies context, but the loop supplies the conceptual structure. He added that if the frame truly governed interpretation, then placing the Codex in a museum of pure science should eliminate its deceptive dimension, yet the internal contradiction would remain intact regardless of display context.

Leah’s final move was to deny that there is any “pure” message level independent of framing. Even the recognition of a strange loop, she argued, requires adopting an interpretive stance—treating the object as a puzzle rather than a manual—which is itself a framing decision. Without that frame, one sees only a clock-building guide with some odd letter-spacing; the loop becomes visible only when the frame directs attention to hidden structures. Jonas responded that this collapses all interpretation into framing, rendering the distinction between frame, outer, and inner messages useless. If everything is frame, then there is no inner message—only framing effects—which contradicts the very purpose of separating the levels.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: evaluate Leah’s opening claim about frame primacy, Jonas’s reply about outer-message autonomy, Leah’s argument from the strange loop, Jonas’s relocation of the loop to message-relations, and finally Leah’s collapse-into-frame move against Jonas’s charge of distinction-collapse. For each step, indicate whether the argument is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing about how frame, outer, and inner messages interact in cases of deliberate self-undermining design.
