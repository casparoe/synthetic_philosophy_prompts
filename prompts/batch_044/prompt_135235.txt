I've noticed that government agencies, employers, and marketers routinely divide the population into generational cohorts — Baby Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so on — and build policies, products, and workplace rules around them. They treat these labels as predictive categories, as if being born between 1997 and 2012 gives people a shared psychology that explains their politics, consumption, or work habits. Yet the boundaries are administratively arbitrary: a person born in December 1996 is classified as Millennial, while one born in January 1997 is Gen Z, despite being essentially the same age and experiencing the same historical events. What is more, once institutions apply the label — changing school curricula, advertising tone, hiring criteria, or management training — people near the boundary often adopt the very traits the category assigns, so the classification seems to create what it claims merely to discover. Given all this, what philosophical view of social groups or collective identity does this institutional practice presuppose? Must it treat generations as real kinds with essential properties, or can it work just as well on the view that they are purely constructed? Does the self-fulfilling loop near the boundaries save the practice by making the categories accurate after the fact, or does it reveal that they are circular? And if we replaced the underlying view with one that denies cohort-based essences entirely, what exactly would have to change in how these policies and products are designed?
