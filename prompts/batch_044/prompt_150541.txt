I am a retired experimental physiologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has turned to moral philosophy with the same expectation of conceptual precision I once applied to physiological systems. I am convening a workshop of twelve participants—graduate students and colleagues from the natural and social sciences, none of them professional philosophers—in a seminar room with movable seating, for a single ninety-minute session. I want a runnable, structured activity, not a lecture or open discussion, that makes the group feel the force of P. F. Strawson's claim that moral responsibility is grounded in the appropriateness of reactive attitudes rather than in metaphysical freedom, and specifically that adopting an objective attitude toward a wrongdoer is not merely the suppression of blame but a fundamental shift in the mode of engagement from interpersonal to managerial.

Design an activity—preferably a role-play or constrained deliberation with assigned roles and tangible stakes—in which participants must repeatedly decide whether to hold a wrongdoer to account or to treat him as a case for practical management. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that they discover the tension themselves: for example, through a staged sequence of disclosures about the wrongdoer's neuropsychological state that progressively alter the costs of maintaining resentment versus shifting to an objective stance. The collective decision should carry a real, if small, consequence for the group—such as the allocation of a shared resource or the continuation of a collaborative task—so that the choice between reactive and objective attitudes has immediate, experienced weight.

Specify the setting precisely: twelve people, ninety minutes, movable chairs, mixed disciplinary backgrounds. State clearly what participants should come away seeing: that the boundary between reactive and objective attitudes is not fixed by knowledge of causal determination alone, and that the objective attitude involves seeing the other as an object of policy rather than as a moral interlocutor.

Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto Strawson's distinctions—personal reactive attitudes, vicarious reactive attitudes, and the objective attitude—explaining which moment corresponds to which concept. Identify what will predictably go wrong—for instance, participants may conflate the objective attitude with emotional coldness or with deterministic belief, or they may treat reactive attitudes as voluntary choices rather than as natural responses that can be modified but not simply switched off. Indicate exactly how the facilitator should correct these misinterpretations without lecturing. Be frank about which feature of Strawson's view the activity cannot reproduce on its own—namely, that reactive attitudes are not deliberatively adopted but are pre-reflective human responses—and specify where the facilitator must supply that missing element directly.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask participants to quote texts. The design should depend only on concepts explainable from general familiarity with Strawson's central argument on reactive attitudes and moral responsibility, without requiring access to the original essay during the session. To keep the prompt from being generic, inject one specific, somewhat niche conceptual refinement: distinguish within the activity between suspending a reactive attitude because it is inappropriate given the agent's quality of will, versus suspending it for purely practical reasons of management or risk reduction, and design the rules so that participants must navigate both kinds of suspension separately.
