The common prior thing -- Aumann agreement, Harsanyi doctrine, all that -- is usually taught like rational people with the same prior can't disagree. But in the AI safety / cognitive science circles I run in, there's this stale disagreeement. One side says any real disagreement is just hidden private evidence (someone missed a study). The other side says no, it's structural: people are running different causal graphs, different ontolgies, so there isn't even a shared state space to put a prior over. This has gone on for years in workshops and nobody shifts. Is this actually a hidden empirical claim about how brains update? A verbal mess around what 'rational' means? Or are the camps describing different formal situations -- shared-world priors vs incommensurable model spaces -- and arguing past each other under one label? If it's structural, does that kill the common prior assumption as a normative tool or just limit its domain? And what would move the needle -- a formal result embedding any ontology in one space, a neuroscience finding, or is the stall telling us the framing is just wrong?
