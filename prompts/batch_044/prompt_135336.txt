Construct a decision tree that classifies ethical positions on distributive obligations within an infinite, non-convergent causal population arranged in a "branching fountain" structure: each agent has exactly two successors with independent welfare states, and policy effects propagate downward without ever reaching a terminal generation. The tree must begin with one root question: does the respondent accept that there is a well-defined comparative fact about the total moral value of outcomes in this structure? From there, develop exactly six binary branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete case or claim that someone could answer without knowing philosophical labels—specifically addressing: (1) whether a policy that improves welfare for every agent at even generations while worsening it for every agent at odd generations is permissible; (2) whether moral obligations weaken with causal distance down the tree; (3) whether the asymptotic density of affected agents should determine the weight assigned to an outcome; (4) whether earlier generations have lexical priority over later ones regardless of magnitude; (5) whether negative welfare at any given node nullifies positive welfare at all of its descendants; and (6) whether aggregation across generations is blocked by the absence of shared experience or collective identity among them. The leaves must include: a geometric-discount prioritarian view; a nearest-neighbor relational view that denies cross-generational aggregation; a density-sensitive aggregation view; a lexical-hierarchy view; an anti-realist view that denies well-defined totals; and one additional logically available combination placed in an unnamed leaf, accompanied by a brief diagnosis of whether that combination is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Every named leaf should carry a one-sentence statement of its most awkward practical commitment. Explicitly flag any pair of named positions that this tree cannot reliably separate, explaining which branch node is ambiguous between them. The intended audience is philosophy graduate students who understand basic decision theory but have not encountered this specific framing; do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure named theories from the literature. The fountain-structure scenario is introduced within this prompt as a novel framing device rather than an established literature case.
