You are a professional philosopher with deep expertise in Nietzsche's philosophy of history, particularly his tripartite distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history as developed in his reflections on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. Before answering, please take a moment to brainstorm: outline the core purpose and characteristic excess of each mode, the criterion by which a historical practice can be said to serve life rather than burden it, and the tensions that arise when two modes are combined in a single institution.

Consider the track record of the Council for Historical Vitality, a hypothetical policy body that evaluates public proposals for historical engagement according to this Nietzschean framework. Its past rulings are these:

1. It approved full funding for a monumental public statue of a national founder, explicitly directing that the accompanying plaque omit recent scholarship documenting the figure's moral failings, on the grounds that present citizens require an unbroken model of greatness in order to undertake collective projects.

2. It rejected a proposal to build a complete, uncurated digital archive of every surviving bureaucratic form from a colonial administration, searchable but uninterpreted, reasoning that unmediated preservation of dead administrative detail weighs upon the present without serving it.

3. It approved a public art installation that visually juxtaposes the same founder's achievements with his documented abuses, forcing viewers to hold both in tension, reasoning that critical history liberates present will from false veneration.

4. It rejected a proposal for an annual, rigorously accurate reenactment of a historical treaty signing—certified by historians for every spoken word and physical gesture—performed inside a sealed museum space, reasoning that turning living participants into custodians of dead form serves antiquarian preservation rather than living history.

5. It approved a commissioned series of essays comparing past leaders to present activists, explicitly designed to demonstrate how the present can morally surpass the past, reasoning that monumental history should spur surpassing rather than paralyze action.

Now the Council faces a new proposal: an interactive AI-driven application called the Historical Mentor. For any user, it selects biographical figures whose documented struggles match the user's stated current challenges, narrates those struggles with strategic omissions of historical details that would undermine the user's specific motivation, and includes a mandatory but brief "historical context" footnote—rendered as a dismissible pop-up—that lists the omissions. The developers claim the tool is monumental because it inspires present action, and they cite the footnote as satisfying the critical function.

Your task is fourfold. First, reconstruct at least two distinct implicit rules or principles that could consistently explain all five past rulings, making explicit for each how it interprets the proper relationship among the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes and where it locates the boundary between serving life and burdening it. Second, apply each reconstructed rule to the Historical Mentor proposal and state the divergent predictions that result. Third, give your best recommendation for what the Council should decide if it wishes to remain consistent with its own precedents; identify the closest analog among cases 1–5, explain the reasoning that favors your recommendation, and say honestly whether the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. Fourth, for each possible decision—approval and rejection—state the broader principle about historical engagement that the Council would be adopting, and propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the two rules you reconstructed. Please structure your response clearly, and where judgment is unavoidable, mark it explicitly as such.
