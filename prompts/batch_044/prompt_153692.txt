I am preparing a short essay for an advanced seminar on normative metaphysics, and I would be grateful for focused comments on the conceptual framework I am developing. The standard discussion of free-floating rationales—normative commitments that operate without being anchored to any present reasoner—tends, in my view, to run together two quite different phenomena. First, there are residual rationales: obligations or values that survive the agents who originally held them, such as the fiduciary duties written into a perpetual trust once the settlor is dead. Second, there are what I propose to call vector rationales: directional normative pressures that emerge purely from the functional dynamics of a system, without ever having been endorsed, represented, or even entertained by any mind. To make the second category concrete, I ask the reader to consider not a legal instrument but a distributed automated irrigation network governed by a locally optimising rule-set. Over successive growing seasons, the network’s adjustments converge on behaviour that systematically privileges drought-resistance over immediate yield maximisation; maintenance crews and funding bodies come to treat this orientation as a guiding consideration, yet no engineer, farmer, or regulatory body ever adopted it as a policy goal. It is, I argue, an artefactual normative vector—guiding action, justifying expenditure, and resisting alteration—while lacking both a present and a historical reasoner.

My essay contends that vector rationales are free-floating in a stronger sense than residual ones, because they have no originating agent to point back to, and that attempts to dissolve them into collective intentionality fail: the orientation is not a shared mental state distributed across users, but a structural regularity that acquires normative inertia through functional stability alone. Could you comment on whether the residual/vector distinction is philosophically stable, whether the irrigation-network example genuinely demonstrates a rationale without any hidden reasoner (for instance, buried in the design specifications or the implicit preferences of the maintenance community), and whether my argument against reducing vector rationales to collective intentionality is convincing? Please write in British English, avoid bibliographies or citation requests—since I am working without access to a library for this draft—and be explicit about where your reasoning is most vulnerable. In particular, please say what specific counter-example or theoretical move would change your mind, especially if it showed that all apparently vector-like orientations are ultimately traceable to implicit design intentions.
