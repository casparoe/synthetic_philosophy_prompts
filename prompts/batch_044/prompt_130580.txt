I'm trying to work out what distinguishes appropriate personal attachment from inappropriate moral calculus in Williams's idea of having one thought too many, by testing my gut reactions to cases. A parent saves her child from a burning car rather than two strangers, and thinks he's mine— that feels right, and any reasoning that weighed lives impersonally feels alienating, as if she had one thought too many. The same parent saves her child but justifies it to herself as the total suffering I would experience exceeds that of the strangers—the act is the same, but the mental framing feels corrupt, like the wrong reason entered at the wrong level. A surgeon who declines to operate on her own child and sends him to a colleague, even though she is technically the best available, feels correct—here personal attachment is rightly suppressed by professional role. A soldier who refuses an order to destroy a civilian settlement by saying I just can't do it; it's wrong feels like genuine integrity. But a soldier who refuses the same order by running a proportionality calculus—civilian harm weighted by severity exceeds tactical gain—reaches the same refusal but feels bureaucratic rather than principled, as if the moral refusal has been replaced by an audit. Meanwhile, a disaster coordinator who directs scarce medical supplies to ten strangers rather than her own sibling, reasoning that her institutional role requires impartial allocation by need, strikes me as professionally correct but also somehow damaging to integrity; yet if she directed supplies to her sibling saying family comes first, that seems like a failure of role. I keep going back and forth on whether role-based suppression of attachment is itself a form of one-thought-too-many reasoning, or whether the corruption only happens when attachment is translated into impersonal calculus rather than suspended entirely.

Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts—specifying when attachment should guide action, when it should be suspended for role, and when its translation into universal reasoning becomes the specific corruption Williams identifies? Or must I drop one of my verdicts, particularly the disaster-coordinator case where I seem to want both impartial allocation and some acknowledgment of personal bond?
