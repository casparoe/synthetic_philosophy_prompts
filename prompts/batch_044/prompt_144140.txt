I have been working through an extension of Avicenna's flying-man scenario that my committee finds suspicious because it seems to prove far more than Avicenna intended—namely, that embodied cognition is impossible in principle. Here is the argument. (1) In the suspended-man case, the subject can affirm her own existence without any sensory or proprioceptive content whatsoever. (2) If self-knowledge requires no bodily state, then the intellect's essential operation is fully independent of corporeal conditions. (3) If the intellect is essentially independent of the body, then there is no principled reason why sensory and motor schemas should be necessary for any conceptual or empirical knowledge; embodiment becomes an accidental, dispensable vehicle. (4) Therefore embodied cognition is false, and all knowledge is in principle available to a disembodied intellect. (5) But contemporary work in embodied cognition shows that even abstract conceptual structures are scaffolded on sensorimotor processes, so (4) is false. (6) Hence (1) must be false: the flying man must retain some tacit bodily awareness after all. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to preserve (1) and reject (3), but I want to understand whether that rejection involves an equivocation between independence in this particular case and independence in principle, and what it costs for Avicenna's view that the soul uses the body as an instrument rather than being constituted by it.
