I have recently become involved in efforts to secure formal recognition for a campaign of targeted violence that destroyed not only lives but also the social institutions and cultural practices of a particular community, and this experience has made me attentive to a persistent asymmetry in international practice. Judicial bodies and national legislatures apply the legal category of genocide with extreme selectivity, requiring proof of specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part, while routinely subsuming structurally comparable campaigns under the headings of ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity, or mass atrocity. Concurrently, certain jurisdictions maintain specific statutes that criminalize the denial of recognized genocides, yet impose no parallel prohibition on denying other large-scale killings that involve comparable death tolls and group-targeting. What philosophical commitments about moral taxonomy, collective harm, and the ontology of groups does this institutional pattern of selective naming and differential protection presuppose? Does it necessarily presuppose that genocide constitutes a sui generis moral kind—perhaps because of its attack on group existence as such—or can the asymmetry be accounted for by considerations of institutional tractability, evidentiary accessibility, and political feasibility alone? To what extent does the practice implicitly depend on Raphael Lemkin's original, broader conception of genocide as encompassing the destruction of cultural and social structures, a conception that the 1948 Convention deliberately narrowed to physical and biological acts? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and recognition embedded in this legal and memorial practice, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and specify what would have to change in tribunal procedure and legislative recognition if we replaced the current hierarchical taxonomy with a continuous, non-lexical framework that treats group-targeted physical destruction and cultural annihilation as points on a single spectrum of collective wrong rather than as categorically separated offenses.
