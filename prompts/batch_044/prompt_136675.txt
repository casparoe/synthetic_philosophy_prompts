You serve on a clinical ethics committee that has adopted a strict tranquilist framework: the only intrinsic moral goods are the absence of suffering and the absence of craving; all other values—continued life, autonomy, dignity, relational presence, or narrative coherence—are instrumentally relevant only when they causally affect those two dimensions. The committee receives frequent proposals for comfort-focused interventions in long-term and end-of-life care, and it needs a reusable decision procedure that applies this framework without requiring philosophical expertise at the point of use.

Design a practical, ordered procedure—rendered as a checklist or flowchart in prose—that a clinician or ethics officer could run on any proposed intervention (for example: altering sedation, withdrawing artificial nutrition or hydration, modifying sensory environments, administering appetite suppressants, or discontinuing life-extending measures). For each step, specify: (i) the precise question to be answered; (ii) what counts as a sufficient answer, including any operational definitions you invent or formalize; (iii) whether the answer must be factual, clinical, or values-based; and (iv) the next branch (proceed, stop with a recommendation, or hand off).

Your procedure must explicitly incorporate and distinguish the following tranquilist categories, treating them as separate dimensions rather than collapsing them into one:
- First-order suffering: present, experienced distress.
- First-order craving: present desire for a state of affairs to differ from what it is, including both appetitive craving (wanting something present) and aversive craving (wanting something absent).
- Second-order craving: a desire about one's own first-order mental states (for example, craving the cessation of one's own suffering, or craving to preserve a particular craving for its own sake).
- Latent or dispositional craving: tendencies to suffer or crave that are not currently manifest but would manifest if conditions change (for example, in a temporarily unconscious patient).

The procedure must also:
1. Identify where tranquilism yields an unambiguous recommendation—yes, no, or modify—based solely on the net effect on the four categories above.
2. Identify the exact points where the procedure must halt and hand the decision to a non-tranquilist authority (family, legal guardian, institutional policy, or law) because tranquilism is silent, and explain why.
3. Include a mechanical misfire warning: describe a case type where rigorous application produces a recommendation that conflicts with widespread clinical intuition, state what that recommendation is, and explain why the framework still demands it.
4. Be explicit about judgment calls that are disguised as procedural steps—flag them directly.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:
A. A conscious, terminally ill patient requests withdrawal of artificial hydration. The patient reports no physical pain, but describes an intense, persistent appetitive craving for a future event (a family milestone) that they will not live to see. Withdrawal would hasten death and eliminate all future mental states.
B. A deeply sedated, non-responsive patient is receiving a continuous infusion that suppresses all first-order suffering and craving, but also suppresses respiratory drive, extending a state of unconsciousness with no prospect of recovery. The family requests that the infusion be continued indefinitely because they value being present at the bedside. Discontinuation would likely result in death within hours, with no evidence that the patient would experience suffering or craving during the brief transition.
C. A proposal to introduce continuous loud instrumental music into a dementia-care ward, based on evidence that it reduces craving-driven wandering and vocalization in approximately 70 percent of residents, but causes mild, persistent headache (first-order suffering) in approximately 30 percent of residents, with no way to predict individual response in advance.

For each case, run the steps in order, report where the procedure stops, state the tranquilist recommendation if any, note any hand-offs, and flag any misfire risk.

Write the prompt as a single, formal instruction suitable for inclusion in a dataset of philosophical reasoning prompts. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to named philosophers or texts. Do not refer to highly obscure technical literature. You may invent or formalize operational terms—such as a net tranquilist balance or a latent disposition check—provided they are defined within the prompt.
