I'm preparing a graduate workshop on scientific realism and have asked participants to defend their preferred response to the pessimistic meta-induction. Rather than evaluating these responses in the abstract, I want to compare five specific strategies along a single precise dimension: their capacity to preserve explanatory success as a genuine reason to believe in theoretical entities without committing to the full-blown correspondence theory of truth that the meta-induction typically undermines.

The five strategies are as follows. First, structural realism that retains only mathematical structure across theory change. Second, entity realism restricted to entities that are experimentally manipulable. Third, a divide-and-conquer strategy that partitions the historical record into cases where theoretical terms genuinely referred and cases where they were merely instrumental fictions. Fourth, a defensive reformulation of the no-miracles argument that treats predictive success as a local, context-bound indicator rather than as a global miracle-avoider. Fifth, a hybrid position—one I have been developing in preliminary notes—that holds theoretical entities are causally indispensable but ontologically underdetermined, so that belief in them is warranted only as a structural placeholder within a predictive framework rather than as a literal description of nature.

Please rank these five approaches in tiers (S-tier, A-tier, B-tier, C-tier, D-tier) according to how effectively they strike that specific balance. For each tier, provide exactly one sentence explaining why the strategies placed there share the same fundamental strength or weakness with respect to preserving explanatory warrant without collapsing into full correspondence realism.
