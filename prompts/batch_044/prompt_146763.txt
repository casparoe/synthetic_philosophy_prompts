I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of logic writing on semantic closure, and I've been trying to reconstruct how Alfred Tarski would have responded to developments in truth theory that emerged only after his death in 1983. The familiar part of his view is clear enough: the liar paradox is dissolved not by revising logic but by stratifying languages, so that the truth predicate for an object-language can only be defined in a richer metalanguage, with material adequacy secured by Convention T and with no legitimate self-application permitted. What I want to explore is how he would have reacted to two posthumous challenges that seem to bypass that architecture entirely.

First, there is the revision theory of truth articulated by Gupta and Belnap in the early 1990s, which treats circular definitions involving the truth predicate not as syntactic mistakes but as generating stable revision sequences—circularities that converge on definite values without ever ascending a hierarchy. Second, and more recently, formal epistemologists and AI safety researchers have begun applying exactly these circular frameworks to large language models, treating a model's self-referential evaluation of its own outputs—for instance, assigning calibrated confidence scores to sentences that themselves embed confidence predicates—as a kind of groundless, distributed semantic loop that remains computationally tractable despite lacking any explicit object-language/metalanguage boundary. Suppose Tarski were confronted with the claim that modern neural architectures instantiate the very semantic closure he banned, not through explicit formal syntax but through statistical representations that blur the levels he insisted on separating.

Would he regard revision theory as a disguised form of meta-level ascent—arguing that the stability of revision sequences merely smuggles in an implicit hierarchy—or would he distinguish sharply between formal languages, where he can legislate syntax, and statistical learning systems, where self-reference emerges without explicit rules? More specifically, how might he have evaluated the argument that circular truth-predicates in machine reasoning require abandoning the hierarchical solution altogether? I'm interested in a philosophical reconstruction rather than a literature review: which of his commitments about the material adequacy of truth definitions and the indispensability of stratification would he hold firm, and where might he be compelled to concede that his framework was designed for precisely delineated formal languages rather than for the messy, self-referential dynamics of artificial cognition?
