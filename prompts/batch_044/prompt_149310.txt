I've been arguing with my study partner about corruption and now I'm stuck on this argument she wrote up. She calls it the reciprocity horizon problem, which sounds made up but I think captures something real. Anyway, here's her chain and I can't find the crack:

(1) For an act to count as corruption, there must be a clear seperation between an official's public role and the private obligations they use it to serve.
(2) In strong clientelist systems, the official is appointed precisely through kin and patron networks, and those networks expect material reciprocation—jobs, contracts, protection—as the normal cost of loyalty.
(3) So inside these systems, the "private" obligation and the "public" mechanism are not separated; they are the same social machinery, and serving your clients IS how the state functions.
(4) Now, Western bureaucracies also depend on implicit reciprocity—campaign donors, alumni boards, revolving doors—but they hide it behind formal rules.
(5) If we reject (1) for clientelist societies because separation is absent, consistency forces us to reject it for Western systems too, since the separation there is only procedural, not real.
(6) That leaves two options: either both systems are equally corrupt, or neither is corrupt at all.
(7) But Western anti-corruption law applies the label globally, condemning clientelist gift-giving while treating domestic analouges as legitimate politics.
(8) So the framework is either hypocritical—using a double standard—or it collapses into saying all governance is corruption, which makes the concept useless for anyone.
(9) Therefore, "corruption" either isn't a universal standard, or it doesn't actually denote anything specific.

She thinks step (5) is where it breaks, but I'm not convinced—maybe the slide is in (3), where she moves from "no separation exists" to "no misuse can happen"? If I want to save the idea that corruption is real, which premise do I have to pay the price for? I really don't want to end up saying Western politics is just as corrupt, but I also don't want to be the person who says African patronage is fine just because it's traditional. There's probably a typo in there somewhere but I think the logic holds.
