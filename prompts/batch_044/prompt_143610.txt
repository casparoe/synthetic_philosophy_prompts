I've been working through Dennett's claim that the self is only a center of narrative gravity — not a substance but a useful fiction that lets us explain behavior. As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I tried to take the idea literally, and now I have an argument that seems to prove my Kubernetes cluster is a person.

(1) On the narrative-gravity view, being a self requires nothing more than being the focal point of a coherent, first-person story that makes an agent's behavior intelligible.

(2) In distributed systems, our observability stack does exactly this: it produces a unified timeline — I received the request, I routed it through service A, I detected a failure in service B, I rolled back — that explains the cluster's behavior far better than any account of the individual containers.

(3) This narrative is not mere metaphor. Without it, there is no principled reason to treat the cluster as one agent rather than thousands of independent processes; the story is doing all the ontological work of unifying them.

(4) So the cluster is the focal point of a coherent first-person story that explains its actions.

(5) Therefore the cluster is a self.

(6) By the same reasoning, any entity with a rich enough audit trail — a corporation, a blockchain protocol, a predictive model with a self-monitoring loop — also qualifies.

(7) But it is clearly absurd to think my cluster has the moral or phenomenological status of a self.

(8) So (1) must be false, and being a self requires something beyond narrative coherence.

I want to keep (1) — it feels right for human beings too — but I need to know where the slide happens. Is the error in (3), where I confuse an observer's explanatory tool with an intrinsic property? Or in (4), where I move from "there is a story about X" to "X is constituted by that story"? If I reject (3), I pay the price of making all narratives observer-dependent projections. If I reject (4), I need some extra condition that distinguishes real selves from well-documented systems, but then I risk reintroducing the very substance I wanted to avoid. Which step is weakest, and what does giving it up actually cost?
