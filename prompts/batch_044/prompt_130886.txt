I am designing the final examination for an MA-level seminar on philosophy of history and the ethics of modernity, and I need one 90-minute essay question that students cannot answer by reciting a standard summary of the Historikerstreit or Bauman's argument that modern bureaucratic rationality made the Holocaust possible rather than inevitable. To force genuine conceptual work, the question should introduce a hypothetical but historically grounded scenario: in 1995, a municipal archive in a medium-sized German city uncovers administrative files showing that the same formal properties Bauman associates with genocide — functional separation of tasks, technical moral distancing, and the treatment of populations as quantifiable material to be optimised — were applied with identical procedural rigour to a post-war urban renewal scheme that displaced thousands of residents, yet with no genocidal intent and with democratically legitimate ends. The essay question should ask candidates to analyse whether this discovery strengthens or undermines the claim that the Holocaust is historically unique, requiring them to distinguish carefully between uniqueness grounded in intentionality and total victimisation, and comparability grounded in shared bureaucratic structures, and to show how Bauman's claim that modernity contains a potential rather than a necessary causal link to genocide affects the comparison.

The model answer should run to approximately 700 words and must demonstrate that the archive case creates a structural tension rather than a resolution: it makes the bureaucratic conditions comparable, yet leaves the intentionality and scope untouched, so that uniqueness and comparability operate on different axes that the student must keep separate. Please provide the exam question, the model answer, and a marking scheme that separates a first-class response from an upper-second by naming the specific argumentative moves required — for instance, whether the student recognises that comparing administrative form risks collapsing the distinction between structural condition and historical outcome, and whether they can articulate why Bauman's potentiality thesis prevents the comparison from being either a relativisation of suffering or a confirmation of inevitability. The prompt should avoid requesting bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should not depend on obscure secondary literature; it must be answerable from a solid general grasp of Bauman's framework and the uniqueness-comparability debate. Write in formal British English.
