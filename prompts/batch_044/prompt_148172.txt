I have noticed a recurring structural pattern in systems that try to separate levels of control or justification, and I wonder whether philosophers or systems theorists have given it a specific name.

The pattern is this. There is a base level that operates according to certain rules, and then a higher level that is meant to set, revise, or guard those rules. Conventionally the higher level is treated as outside the base—immune to the rules it administers. Yet in the cases I have in mind, the higher level is actually implemented through the very mechanisms of the base level, so it cannot genuinely stand apart. At the same time, there is a particular boundary or provision—an apparently inviolate core—that is treated as absolutely protected from revision, even by the higher-level procedure designed to handle revisions. This protection is not enforced by any external authority; it is sustained by the same institutional, logical, or computational machinery that it supposedly transcends. The result is not simple circularity, because the levels remain conceptually distinct and people continue to treat them as hierarchically ordered; but they are interwoven in practice, with the meta-rule both dependent on and conceptually superior to the system it governs.

Two concrete instances help to fix the idea. First, a constitutional order in which the amendment procedure is defined in the constitution itself, yet certain articles are declared eternally unamendable, and the court that polices this eternity clause derives its jurisdiction from the very constitutional text it claims to stand above. Second, an architecture for artificial intelligence in which a model's behaviour is constrained by a written constitution, and a separate oversight process—running under the same constitutional filter—is tasked with proposing amendments to it; certain foundational clauses are treated as non-derogable, though nothing outside the system enforces that status, and the amendment mechanism is therefore governed by the rules it might seek to change.

This feels different from a bootstrap paradox or vicious circularity, since the system can function without contradiction, and it is not quite self-reference, since the levels do not collapse into one. Is there an established term—perhaps in philosophy of logic, jurisprudence, or systems theory—for this specific configuration of tangled levels with an apparently inviolate but internally sustained boundary? If so, how is it distinguished from ordinary hierarchical control or from Gödelian self-reference? If there is no precise term, what would be the sharpest way to define the phenomenon and its boundary conditions, particularly the feature that makes the inviolate level both dependent on and conceptually superior to the level it governs?
