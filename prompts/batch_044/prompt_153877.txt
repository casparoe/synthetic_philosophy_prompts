I'm coordinating a small interdisciplinary working group—three historians of science and medicine, two philosophers of history—with a three-year window to address what I will call the "agential asymmetry" in macrohistorical explanation: we routinely treat human polities, institutions, and movements as agents possessing intentions and strategic capacities, yet when pathogens, climate regimes, or volcanic aerosol events reshape demographics, economies, and political structures on comparable or larger scales, we classify them as passive background conditions rather than as forces that actively restructure the space of human possibility. Rather than asking for a survey or bibliography, I want you to identify the two or three open conceptual questions in this domain that matter most, and to defend a strict ordering of priority for this specific group's effort and timeline.

For each question you prioritize, please explain: (1) why resolving it is upstream of other lines of inquiry—that is, which debates or empirical programs depend on it being settled first; (2) what form of progress would actually count, given that accumulating more case studies of plague years, volcanic winters, or drought-induced collapses is not enough; and (3) where the risk of environmental determinism is sharpest, and what conceptual guardrail—whether a distinction between "trigger events" and "structural vulnerability," or something else—would best prevent the inquiry from collapsing into a deterministic narrative.

In particular, evaluate whether the first priority should be developing and testing a rigorous distinction between trigger events (a specific eruption, pandemic wave, or anomalous weather year) and structural vulnerability (trade-network topology, agricultural monoculture, immunological naivety), or whether that very distinction tends to dissolve once causal mechanisms are traced in detail. I want a defended agenda, not a reading list, and I want your answer written in polished, formal American English, with no citations, bibliographies, or lists of recommended texts.
