I'm drafting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of archaeology and historical inference (graduate level, 90 minutes, 20 marks). Design a single essay question that tests whether students can navigate the underdetermination of material evidence by institutional versus belief-based interpretations, not by summarizing standard methodological positions but by constructing and testing the bridge premises themselves. Present a self-contained hypothetical case — a settlement showing standardized storage pits alongside uniform grave goods with no textual record — and ask students to: (1) identify the specific auxiliary assumptions required to infer centralized economic redistribution from the pits; (2) identify the parallel auxiliary assumptions required to infer a shared cosmological obligation from the same pits and goods; (3) explain why these two inference structures are observationally symmetrical; (4) specify what kind of comparative or negative evidence would actually discriminate between them, without merely appealing to parsimony or 'more context'; and (5) state what this symmetry implies about the limits of archaeological inference into social institutions versus collective representations. Provide a model answer of roughly 700–900 words and a marking scheme with five must-hit analytical moves, the points each carries (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail each one. Do not ask students for citations, bibliographies, or knowledge of specific named excavations; keep the scenario hypothetical and the crux conceptual.
