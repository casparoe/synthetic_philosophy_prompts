I've been re-reading Robert Louis Stevenson's The Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde alongside the split-brain literature—Gazzaniga's interpreter-module findings, the debate over whether commissurotomy yields one or two streams of phenomenal consciousness, and the increasingly popular view that the unity of consciousness is an integrative achievement rather than a metaphysical default. Rather than running the standard Freudian reading in which Hyde is merely a repressed id, I want to press a more literal neurological analogy: treat Jekyll and Hyde as competing co-conscious streams within a single cranial architecture, with the chemical transformation functioning like an artificial breakdown of interhemispheric inhibition and the later spontaneous shifts representing a failure of narrative binding. How well does this mapping hold? I'd like you to examine specific scenes—Enfield's account of the trampling and the check drawn in Jekyll's hand, Jekyll's progressive loss of control over the switch without the potion, and the final irreversible emergence of Hyde—and ask whether they illustrate a disruption in access consciousness, a bifurcation in phenomenal experience, or something that resists both categories because the novel insists on Hyde's moral evil in ways that seem to presuppose a unitary moral agent. At the same time, does Stevenson's horror depend on a normative conception of personal identity—an "ought" of integration—that the split-brain literature actually undermines? I'm preparing this for a philosophy-of-mind discussion group, and I want a reading that takes both the novella and the neuroscience seriously: one that doesn't reduce the story to a clinical case study, but also doesn't let the literary framing obscure what the philosophy reveals about the fragility of the "one body, one self" intuition that makes the ending terrifying.
