I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for analytic philosophy graduate students who are familiar with Locke's memory account and Parfit's reductionism but have never studied Buddhist philosophy. Rather than surveying historical texts, I want to teach anatta specifically as a live conceptual alternative to Western personal-identity theories, using the two-truths framework—conventional designation versus ultimate analysis—as the organizing lens. Do not explain the doctrine to me; instead, map out the teaching sequence. What must come first—perhaps the distinction between conventional reality and ultimate emptiness—before anatta can be introduced without being immediately collapsed into Hume's bundle theory or treated as simple nihilism? Specify the one carrying example or thought experiment that should anchor the session: it must not be the chariot analogy from the Milindapanha, which this audience will have encountered in survey courses, and it should illuminate the soteriological stakes (why recognizing no-self is meant to reduce clinging and suffering) rather than functioning as a purely ontological demolition of the person. List the misconceptions they will likely form in sequence—probably that anatta denies the existence of persons altogether, that it is equivalent to Parfit's psychological-continuity view, and that it eliminates moral responsibility—and state exactly where in the arc each should be defused. Include a final diagnostic question or brief exercise that reveals whether they understand the two-level ontology or are still treating the denial as occurring on a single plane. Note explicitly what should be left out at this level—perhaps detailed Abhidharma taxonomy of the skandhas or comparative Mahayana critiques of svatantrika reasoning—and say so. Finally, separate what is broadly settled in contemporary Buddhist-philosophy scholarship from what remains genuinely contested, particularly whether anatta should be read primarily as a metaphysical claim about the five aggregates or as a strategic, practice-oriented denial, and do not present either interpretation as the consensus view.
