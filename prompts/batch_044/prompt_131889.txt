I'm giving the opening remarks tomorrow for a two-day workshop we're calling 'Who Decides the Watershed?' The room will be half local residents from three towns that share a river, and half poli-sci and philosophy grad students who know the boundry-problem literature inside out. The practical question is wether people who live just outside the municipal line but drink the water should have a vote on the upstream zoning board. I want the remarks to do real work: they need to welcome everyone without pretending we've already solved who 'the people' are here, and they can't just cite standard democratic theory because the residents won't follow that. There's also this weird local quirk I want woven in -- an old irrigation pact from the 1920s that technically gives riparian rights to landowners but not to renters, so some of the people most affected by flooding have no formal standing at all. Can you write about five minutes of opening remarks that sit in that tension? Don't resolve it with a neat answer; let the problem shape what I can honestly promise the room.
