For a piece on the philosophy of mathematics, I am trying to clarify the precise scope of intuitionistic logic. The standard explanation I encounter is that constructivists reject the law of excluded middle for mathematical statements—so that Goldbach's conjecture, for instance, is neither true nor false until an explicit proof or counterexample is actually produced. That sounds like a specific ontological thesis about mathematical existence rather than a general skepticism about unknown facts. Yet I have also seen intuitionism characterized as a broader epistemic principle: that one ought not assert what one cannot verify. If that broader reading were correct, wouldn't intuitionists also have to suspend judgment on empirical propositions—say, whether microbial life exists on a particular exoplanet—until direct observational access is secured? Or is the rejection of excluded middle rigorously confined to abstract mathematical domains, and if so, by what ontological distinction is that boundary drawn? I ask because the popular framing seems to slide between a precise logical doctrine and a general verificationist caution, and I want to avoid misrepresenting which is which.
