Last week my study-group friends Maya and Derek got into a heated argument after we watched some documantary about atrocities in the 90s. Maya was saying that the UN Genocide Convention from 1948 basically betrayed Raphael Lemkin's original vision. She pointed out that Lemkin's 1944 formulation included cultural destruction and politcal groups, not just the four protected categories in the UN text, and that the narrower version was pushed through partly so Soviet bloc states wouldn't be exposed. So she thinks when activists call something genocide even if it misses the legal checklist -- say, systematically destroying a people's language or mass-killing dissidents -- they're actually being truer to Lemkin's moral idea, and that governments resist the label mainly to dodge legal obligations. Derek came back that Lemkin's broad draft was politically dead on arrival, and that the 1948 narrowing is precisely what turned "genocide" into a specific prosecutable crime instead of a vague moral insult. He argued that stretching the term to cover culture-loss or politcal purges just makes it mean "horrible thing I want to condemn," which weakens international law and lets states weaponize the word to justify intervention. Maya said that cynicism protects perpetrators; Derek said broadening protects nobody because courts won't touch cases outside the convention anyway. They kept circling whether calling something genocide is mainly about moral recognition or about triggering a legal duty, and they asked me to referee since I was just listening. Can you go through their back-and-forth move by move and say who's right on each point -- especially about Lemkin's actual intent, about whether dilution destroys the term's force, and about how the politics of naming really works? And tell me what both of them are leaving out.
