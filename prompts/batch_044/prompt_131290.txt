I have been trying, without any formal training, to understand what people actually mean when they claim that a society is unfair because its members do not have equal shares of something. I keep encountering three broad answers — equality of welfare, of resources, and of capabilities — but I want to establish the full specification first: what must any adequate theory addressing equality of welfare, resources, and capabilities be able to deliver before it can be analysed?

Write the desiderata. List the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, the biological or psychological findings it must not contradict, and the institutional constraints it must satisfy if it is to guide real public programmes. For each requirement, state the motivating consideration or thought experiment behind it. Be sure to address:

- the standard case of a disabled person who requires more resources than others to reach the same level of functioning;
- the problem of expensive tastes, where one person needs greater expenditure to achieve the same welfare;
- my own hypothetical scenario in which individuals have access to neuroadaptive implants that allow them to voluntarily reset their satisfaction baselines and to convert resources into subjective well-being at radically different rates — ask whether a theory of equality should treat these self-modified conversion rates as part of the metric or as matters of personal responsibility;
- the distinction between equality of opportunity and equality of outcome, and between possessing moral status and being assigned an equal distributive share;
- whether the theory must remain neutral between competing conceptions of the good life, or whether it is permitted to build in a particular account of human flourishing.

Flag any pair of desiderata that seem jointly unsatisfiable — for example, complete neutrality about the good life together with a non-arbitrary measure of equality — and identify the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of existing theories (welfarism, resourcism, and the capability approach). Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their internal tensions rather than on a detailed scorecard, though a brief comparative note on how the three main accounts stand against the list is welcome. Please provide your answer in American English.
