I'm a high school junior writing an independent study paper on moral aggregation, and I'm trying to determine what I actually believe about adding up small harms. Rather than asking for sources, I want to see whether my own case judgments hang together or whether one of them has to go.

Here are my verdicts.

First, the classic comparison: deliberately torturing one innocent person for a full hour is far worse than causing a billion people each to experience a barely noticeable speck of dust in one eye for a single moment. Even though the summed physical discomfort in the second scenario might be enormous, I still judge the first case to be worse.

Second, if those same billion people each experience that speck every day for eighty years, so that each person accumulates a lifetime of tiny, fleeting irritations, my judgment still doesn't flip. Repetition across time doesn't seem to bridge the gap.

Third, distribute the suffering itself rather than distributing a mild harm. One person suffers the full hour of torture, versus a billion people each suffering precisely one-billionth of that same torture experience—identical total quantity, divided exactly. Here I feel pulled in opposite directions, but my inclination is that the distributed version is actually worse, because suffering is being inflicted on vastly more subjects, even though no individual suffers deeply.

Fourth, a probabilistic variant: a policy gives every living human a one-in-ten-million chance of being tortured for that same hour, with the statistical expectation that exactly one person will suffer. I think this is morally much closer to actually torturing one specific person than to the dust-speck scenario, and I judge it nearly as bad as the direct case.

Fifth, change the dimension of harm. Instead of physical discomfort, the tiny harms are purely epistemic: one billion people each acquire one false belief—say, briefly thinking Tuesday is Wednesday—that causes them negligible, momentary confusion. Even if we sum the "epistemic disvalue," I think this aggregation is essentially harmless and does not approach the wrongness of torture.

Sixth, a case that conflicts with my first judgment: one person receives a million tiny paper cuts, one per day for years, each causing only momentary pain. Compared with giving a single person one severe cut, I tend to think the million paper cuts are worse overall, which seems to contradict my claim that one severe harm dominates many tiny ones.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the intensity of suffering per subject, the number of subjects affected, the kind of well-being capacity harmed, or some interaction among them—that delivers all six judgments? If no single principle can accommodate them, please identify which judgment is the outlier that should be revised, and state the simpler principle that saves the rest. Also, please apply your principle to a case I haven't listed: what if the tiny harms are spread not across people or across time, but across different sensory modalities—so that every human experiences an imperceptible reduction in vision, hearing, taste, and smell simultaneously, with no single modality affected enough to be noticed?

Please present your answer as a precise principle, or as a small set of candidate principles, with the specific case that motivates each clause, your predictions for the unlisted case, and an honest statement of which of my intuitions must be overturned if consistency is to be preserved.
