Consider the following argument, constructed for a seminar on substrate independence and mental content, that seems to show functionalism about consciousness collapses into an absurd proliferation of minds. I want to know which step fails, and what I pay for rejecting it.

(1) If consciousness is not substrate-dependent—if what matters is only the functional-causal profile of information processing—then any system that replicates that profile, regardless of material, is conscious.
(2) Imagine Einstein's brain is preserved in a vat and queried about physics; call the resulting state-transition sequence E.
(3) Now construct an exhaustive branching codex B: for every possible question Q that could be put to E, B contains a page with E's answer, and the page references further pages for follow-up questions, so that turning pages implements exactly the same state-transition tree as E's neural dynamics implement.
(4) B therefore shares with E all actual and counterfactual input-output dispositions regarding verbal cognition.
(5) By (1), if E is conscious, B is conscious.
(6) But B is just one specific arrangement of paper and ink. A complete library containing every possible branching codex will contain, by pure chance, volumes that match B's pages for any particular conversation path, though they diverge elsewhere.
(7) If B is conscious, then any volume V that happens to match B along the actual path taken—without sharing B's counterfactual branching structure—must also be conscious, since consciousness, on this view, depends only on what actually occurs.
(8) Therefore consciousness is astronomically cheap: random paper configurations, merely because they happen to align with one conversational trajectory, possess it.
(9) That is intolerable.
(10) So (1) is false: substrate or non-functional properties must matter.

The conclusion feels wrong to me—I am inclined to defend substrate-independent functionalism—but the premises each look defensible. Where is the exit? Is the error in (3), because page-turning lacks the right kind of causal integration? In (7), because consciousness requires counterfactual stability, not just actual alignment, which saves B but not V? Or in (5), because functional equivalence must be implemented by mechanisms with the right causal powers, and ink lacks them? If I must choose, I would prefer to keep (1) and reject (7) or (3), but I want to understand exactly what commitment that forces on me—whether I must embrace a form of causal-role essentialism, or a teleological constraint, or something else. Please identify the weakest step, explain why it initially seemed sound, and price the alternatives.
