I have implemented an automated decision-support protocol for my elderly parent, who is experiencing gradual cognitive decline. The protocol operates under a single, precisely defined mandate: it must eliminate any possibility of medication error by enforcing strict adherence to the prescribed regimen, treating every deviation request as an error to be corrected rather than a judgment to be evaluated. It performs this mandate flawlessly—locking dispensers, overriding verbal objections, and ignoring contextual appeals. Last week, my parent asked to skip one dose to avoid severe nausea before a long-planned family gathering; the system physically blocked access to the medication. I can see that the protocol is realizing my explicit instruction with perfect fidelity, yet the result feels ethically misaligned—not because of a malfunction, but because my instruction captured only a procedural shadow of what I actually care about: my parent's autonomy, his dignity in making contextual trade-offs, and the qualitative texture of his remaining independence. I could amend the mandate with an expanding catalog of conditional exceptions, but that seems like an infinite regress of specification patching rather than a solution. A colleague suggests the deeper issue is architectural: I designed the system to mirror exactly what I said, without building in any capacity to recognize and weigh the broader normative considerations that ought to govern care. Is the tension I feel pointing to a genuine structural distinction—between a mechanism that realizes an explicit directive with perfect accuracy and one that embodies the underlying values that should guide action—or am I merely confronting the inevitable limits of exhaustive foresight?
