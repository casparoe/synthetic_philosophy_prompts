I was recently appointed to a clinical allocation board that must weigh quality-of-life improvements for patients with radically different value systems—one measures welfare strictly in autonomy-days, another in relational harmony, and neither accepts that their metric can be translated into the other’s. This brought me back to the philosophical stalemate over interpersonal utility comparisons. For decades, the debate between those who defend cardinal interpersonally comparable utility—drawing on aggregation arguments that treat preference satisfaction as a common currency—and those who reject it—citing impossibility results, the problem of adaptive preferences, and the claim that welfare is irreducibly first-personal—has seemed frozen: the formal results are well rehearsed, the counterexamples are well known, and neither camp has moved. My working theory is that the disagreement is not primarily about economics or ethics proper, but about a hidden issue I will call preference-calibration authority: whether a third party can legitimately revise an agent’s reported preferences to correct for what they deem adaptive distortion without substituting the calibrator’s welfare-ontology for the agent’s. Is that correct, or is the stall actually a verbal dispute about the word “utility,” or two groups answering different questions—one about descriptive psychology, one about normative aggregation—under a single heading? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says: do they differ about empirical facts, about standards of evidence for when a preference counts as distorted, about which cases count as data, or about bedrock intuitions regarding whose first-personal judgment has final authority? And given your diagnosis, could anything—an empirical finding about preference stability across cultures, a formal result on aggregation with heterogeneous welfare-ontologies, or a conceptual distinction—actually resolve it, or is the question ill-posed? To test the diagnosis, consider this constructed case: two agents, A and B, undergo a structured perspective-taking protocol in which each predicts the other’s outcome rankings; both consistently mispredict in opposite directions—A overestimates B’s valuation of autonomy, B overestimates A’s valuation of harmony—yet both insist their own first-personal metric is correct and that the other’s errors reveal a failure of empathy rather than a fact about welfare. Does this case function as a genuine crux for the debate, or does it show that the disagreement is about something else entirely—perhaps about the ethics of substitution rather than the possibility of comparison? Please answer in American English, with a precise diagnosis and no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
