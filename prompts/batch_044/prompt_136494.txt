In the philosophy seminar I take at my high school, we have spent several weeks on epistemic injustice—specifically testimonial injustice, in which a speaker receives less credibility than she merits because of identity-based prejudice, and hermeneutical injustice, in which a social group lacks the conceptual resources to understand its own experience. A debate that has stalled both in the texts we read and in our own classroom discussions concerns whether these phenomena are best understood as failures of epistemic calibration—our belief-forming practices assigning incorrect weight to testimony—or as failures of social recognition, in which the wrong is not that we misjudge a speaker’s reliability but that we deny her the standing to be heard as an epistemic peer at all.

The calibration side maintains that if we corrected our reliability-tracking heuristics and eliminated bias in how we weigh evidence, credibility deficits would diminish as an epistemic malfunction; the recognition side replies that this framing misses the core harm, because a speaker can be factually correct and still be ignored precisely because her social position disqualifies her from being treated as a knower whose testimony counts. For months our discussions have cycled through the same examples—medical testimony dismissed on gendered grounds, workplace reports ignored along racial lines—and each side interprets them differently without anyone shifting position. The arguments feel rehearsed, yet neither camp treats the disagreement as merely verbal.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about the examples but about two different questions being pursued under the single heading “what is epistemic injustice?” One question is distributive and virtue-epistemological: how should credibility be allocated so that knowledge is maximized? The other is ontological-political: who gets to count as a subject whose knowledge matters, regardless of predictive accuracy? But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is instead a hidden empirical disagreement about whether recognition-independent calibration is psychologically achievable, or whether the impasse is genuinely verbal—centered on an ambiguity in “credibility” between predictive accuracy and recognitive standing, or on whether “injustice” here names an epistemic harm or a moral-political wrong.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Please engage concretely with what each side says about calibration, recognition, and the shared examples, rather than surveying authors or texts. Is there a substantive disagreement about norms or facts, a verbal ambiguity about key terms, or are two distinct projects—one in virtue epistemology, one in social philosophy—being forced into one debate? Given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result about how listeners form credibility judgments, a formal model of credibility allocation, or should we conclude the question is ill-posed and ought to be split? I am especially interested in whether introducing a distinction between predictive credibility—assigning belief-weight based on expected accuracy—and recognitive credibility—assigning standing based on norms of social equality—would resolve the stall, or whether it would merely restate the division in new vocabulary.
