For roughly a century, philosophers of biology and mind have debated whether Darwin's evolutionary inversion and Turing's computational inversion are structurally parallel: both appear to generate high-level functional competence from mechanisms that entirely lack the corresponding form of comprehension, yet the dispute never converges despite shared agreement on the underlying science. One camp holds that natural selection and algorithmic processing are the same kind of answer—substrate-independent explanations that dissolve any demand for an inner understander—so asking whether a language model 'really grasps' syntax is as conceptually confused as asking whether a beak 'really intends' its shape; predictive and functional adequacy is all there is to explain. The opposing camp insists that evolutionary competence is diachronic, distributed across populations, and representationally vacant, whereas a neural network's performance is synchronic, localized in a single system, and representation-laden in virtue of weights, embeddings, and token transitions; therefore Darwin's inversion licenses no conclusion about individual machine comprehension, and the analogy smuggles in an equivocation on 'competence,' 'mechanism,' and 'explanation.' My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical—no new fossil record or benchmark result will settle it—but conceptual and normative: the two sides implicitly differ on what makes an explanation of competence complete, with one requiring only functional and predictive adequacy and the other requiring that the explanation preserve some form of normative accountability or semantic grounding that the mechanism itself can be held to. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there instead a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about the individuation conditions for representational states, or about whether distributed processes can bear normative properties—that does the real work? I want a concrete structural diagnosis: is the disagreement verbal, substantive, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as relevant data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Do the sides in fact answer different questions under the single name 'competence without comprehension'? If the diagnosis holds, what kind of progress is genuinely possible—an explicit distinction between functional and normative adequacy, a formal result about representation in distributed versus localized systems, a reconstrual of the original question—or should we conclude that no progress should be expected because the underlying intuition about 'understanding' is irreducibly first-personal? Be specific about which commitments in each camp actually generate the tension, and avoid a survey of interpretations.
