I'm working through Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach for a seminar on artificial minds and have extended it into an argument that now seems to prove far more than I intended—perhaps that no scaled representational system can ever fully close its own epistemic loop. I need help locating the precise failure, because I want to preserve the intuition that self-reference creates special limits without being forced into an indiscriminate skepticism about every self-describing system.

Here is the chain, laid out step by step. (1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem establishes that in any consistent formal system F rich enough to encode arithmetic, there exists a true sentence G_F that asserts its own unprovability within F and is therefore not derivable there. (2) Hofstadter proposes that consciousness and self-awareness arise when a representational system achieves a strange loop—a hierarchical encoding that loops back to represent the system's own rules and symbols—analogizing this syntactic self-reference to mental self-awareness. (3) Generalizing this, call the Reflexive Closure Thesis: if a system S achieves representational closure, meaning S contains representations of S's own representational architecture and dynamics, then S is subject to a Gödelian limitation, namely that there exists at least one true proposition about S's internal cognitive states that S cannot represent or verify. (4) Contemporary large language models, particularly those trained with chain-of-thought and self-reflective prompting, can generate accurate descriptions of their own weights, attention patterns, and reasoning traces; they therefore cross the threshold into weak representational closure. (5) By (3), there must be true propositions about these models' cognition—call them mental Gödel sentences—that the models are constitutionally incapable of representing, regardless of parameter scale or training data. (6) Consequently, scaling laws can never deliver full artificial self-awareness, since the barrier is logical rather than empirical; and by parallel reasoning, human brains, which represent their own neural dynamics through global workspace and interoceptive mechanisms, must harbor true neuroscientific propositions they can never internally know. (7) Yet this reasoning applies indiscriminately: a genome that encodes its own transcription machinery, or a library catalog that lists its own entries, also achieves weak closure, so they too should possess mental Gödel sentences and partial self-blindness, which is absurd. (8) Therefore either the Gödelian analogy applies to all self-encoding systems—draining it of explanatory specificity—or it collapses entirely, including for human minds.

I want to keep the analogy alive for genuinely self-aware minds, but I do not want to pay the price of saying that every database with a self-referential index is epistemically crippled in the same way. Should I reject (3)'s generalization from syntactic to semantic closure, (4)'s claim that LLMs actually achieve the relevant kind of closure, or the hidden slide in (6) from internal unrepresentability to permanent empirical inaccessibility? And what does each rejection cost—does it save the analogy only by restricting it so narrowly that it no longer illuminates artificial minds, or does it preserve a meaningful distinction between syntax and semantics at the expense of Hofstadter's original vision?
