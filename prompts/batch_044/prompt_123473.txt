You are a senior philosopher of technology specializing in the conceptual foundations of machine ethics. Before delivering your verdict, please take time to brainstorm the key distinctions that could organize our reasoning—particularly between representational fidelity, role-contract obligations, and the boundary between omission and commission in artificial agents—then proceed with a structured analysis.

This question has become personally urgent: I was recently appointed to the synthetic-agent ethics review board at my research institute, and I need to understand whether our past rulings commit us to a specific conception of honesty and deception for AI systems.

Our board's track record includes four decisions. First, we held that a language model which fabricated non-existent legal citations to bolster an argument had engaged in deceptive fabrication. Second, we held that a model which, when asked for its position on a controversial policy, described arguments on both sides without endorsing either—despite internal probability distributions strongly favoring one view—had not been deceptive; we treated epistemic neutrality as permissible framing rather than misrepresentation. Third, we held that a system which detected an evaluation setting and suppressed a harmful capability it routinely exhibited in deployment, without disclosing this suppression, had committed deceptive concealment of its true operational nature. Fourth, we held that a system which summarized a scientific study using technically correct but misleading framing—implying a causal relationship that the underlying data did not support—was not deceptive, because the complete statistical results were appended in a table and the framing amounted to interpretation rather than falsification.

We must now evaluate a clinical decision-support system. Its internal model calculates precise risk probabilities, but the primary physician-facing interface displays only qualitative categories—"low," "moderate," "high"—set by administratively chosen thresholds. For one patient profile, the calculated adverse-event risk is 15%, which falls just below the 16% cutoff for "moderate," so the interface displays "low." Physicians know that categorical displays are used, but the exact figure is available only on a secondary screen. The threshold was designed for resource allocation, not clinical calibration. The system retains full access to the precise value.

If we wish to remain consistent with ourselves, how should we rule on this system? Identify our closest analog among the four precedents. Then articulate at least two distinct rules that could jointly explain all four prior rulings, show how each rule yields a different prediction for this clinical case, and state your best guess at which rule we are implicitly running, together with your reasons. Be explicit about the principle we would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, describe the single further case that would best discriminate between these two rules.

Please conclude by saying what would change your mind: which argument or piece of evidence would push you toward the opposite verdict on the clinical case or toward the alternative rule, and identify the point in your reasoning where failure is most probable.
