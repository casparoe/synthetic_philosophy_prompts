I'm a PhD student in jurisprudence working on normative contract theory, and I'm preparing a seminar paper on the binding force of promises. Before I defend my position, I want an internal consistency audit of the following package of commitments. (1) Promises generate binding force primarily by creating legitimate expectations in the promisee. (2) Full autonomy of the promisor at the moment of commitment is a necessary condition for any binding force to arise; promises made under significant coercion or deception are void ab initio. (3) The social practice of promising carries independent institutional value, so that even when particular expectations are weak or absent there remains a pro tanto reason to keep the promise in virtue of sustaining coordination. (4) This binding force is defeasible and can be overridden by sufficiently weighty moral considerations. (5) I also think the best structural model is not a sui generis normative power but a mechanism-design commitment device: the promisor rationally restricts her future option space to make cooperation credible, and the obligation derives from the rationality of maintaining that self-binding architecture rather than from a direct duty to the promisee. (6) Consequently I reject any account that treats promises as generating irreducible deontic facts or mysterious normative powers. (7) Finally, I hold that breaking a promise remains wrongful even when the promisee is unaware and forms no expectations, provided the promise was autonomously made within a functioning practice, though in such cases the wrong is thinner and grounded mainly in damage to the institutional mechanism rather than in disappointed expectation.

My question is whether this package is jointly tenable. Specifically: does (5) undermine (1) by reducing expectation-based reasons to instrumental rationality? Does (2) sit comfortably with (5) given that mechanism-design entry conditions require voluntariness but not necessarily the richer autonomy conditions I invoke? Is (3) compatible with (6)'s reductionism, or does institutional value require a non-reducible normative layer? Does (7) contradict (1), or can it be read as consistent with (3) and (5) together? And is there a hidden tension between (4)'s defeasibility and (5)'s claim that the obligation derives from the rationality of maintaining the mechanism—since if the mechanism can be rationally abandoned, does that collapse promise-keeping into ordinary cost-benefit reasoning? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify the least costly revision for each, and tell me whether there is a single revision that cleans up the most conflicts. I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I want an audit of logical and motivational coherence within this specific framework.
