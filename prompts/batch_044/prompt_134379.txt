I'm trying to pin down what actually distinguishes a teleological suspension of the ethical in the Kierkegaardian sense—where Abraham stands outside the universal for the sake of a singular relation to the absolute—from other cases where people claim an exception to moral norms for a higher end. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case 1: Abraham, commanded to sacrifice Isaac, sets aside the universal ethical prohibition on killing one's child because he stands in a direct, singular relation to God that cannot be mediated by universal reason. Verdict: this is the paradigm; the ethical is genuinely suspended, not merely overridden.

Case 2: A general who orders the destruction of a village to shorten a just war, arguing that the political telos of peace suspends the ethical prohibition on killing non-combatants. Verdict: not a suspension; this is consequentialist reasoning within the universal, weighing one ethical good against another.

Case 3: A physician receives what she takes to be an incommunicable, private revelation that a specific dying patient must be told a false prognosis so that the patient's soul can reach a state required by a hidden divine plan. She lies despite the universal ethical duty of honesty. Verdict: this feels structurally parallel to Abraham—singular, non-universalizable, religious telos—so I want to call it a suspension, yet I hesitate because the command concerns deception rather than action against nature, and because I cannot verify the revelation.

Case 4: A father breaks a signed contract that would bankrupt his family, claiming that the teleological end of parental provision suspends the universal legal-ethical norm of promise-keeping. Verdict: not a suspension; parental duty is already a particular within the ethical, not a leap outside it.

Case 5: A monk breaks his vow of perpetual silence to warn a village of an impending avalanche, saying the religious telos of compassion for these particular lives suspends the universal monastic rule. Verdict: yes, a suspension, but of a religious rather than ethical universal; it feels like the mirror image of Abraham.

Case 6: A revolutionary assassinates an innocent political figure, claiming that the historical telos of liberation suspends the ethical prohibition on murder. Verdict: clearly not a suspension; the justification appeals to a universal historical law that applies to all in the same situation.

The pair that keeps shifting is Case 3 and Case 5: both involve a singular agent acting on a non-public, religiously framed reason against a universal norm, yet in one I resist the label and in the other I accept it. Is there a principle that captures Cases 1, 3, and 5 as suspensions while excluding 2, 4, and 6? Or must I drop one of my verdicts?

If there is such a principle, state it precisely—specifying which feature of the agent's relation to the telos, which kind of norm is suspended, and what distinguishes suspension from mere exception or override. Then apply it to two cases I have not listed: first, a soldier who refuses an order to torture prisoners because she believes God has singularly forbidden her to participate, though she accepts that others may be required; second, a scientist who falsifies data in one specific experiment because she believes the cosmic purpose of truth requires this particular deception to preserve funding for a life-saving project. For each, say whether the principle predicts suspension, and whether that prediction matches intuition.

Finally, be honest about which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without distortion, and whether saving that verdict requires complicating the principle beyond plausibility.
