I am writing from a seminar on mechanistic explanation and scientific modeling, where we have been examining how anomaly-detection systems maintain their explanatory identity across implementation changes. I have formed some tentative verdicts about when such a system tracks the same mechanism, and I want to know whether a single principle can accommodate them.

Consider the following cases. First, a manufacturing monitor detects bearing degradation by comparing vibration spectra against a baseline profile acquired during initial calibration. If engineers substitute a titanium-alloy bearing with different resonant properties and consequently update the baseline profile, while leaving the comparison algorithm and intervention protocol unchanged, I judge that the detection mechanism remains identical. Second, an epidemiological surveillance system identifies outbreak anomalies by measuring deviations in a contact-network adjacency matrix. If the representation is switched to hypergraph tensors encoding multi-person interactions, but the statistical divergence test and the public-health intervention triggered remain exactly equivalent, I still regard this as the same mechanism, merely expressed in a richer formalism. Third, however, if that same surveillance system abandons deviation-testing altogether and instead trains a predictive model that forecasts individual infection risk, I take the mechanism to have changed, even when both versions flag the same outbreaks. Fourth, a cognitive neuroscience study examines working-memory retention using a delayed-match-to-sample task. Replacing visual stimuli with auditory ones, while preserving the delay interval and reward contingency, seems to me to study the same mechanism with different inputs. But if the delay is eliminated and replaced by continuous stimulation followed by a post-hoc filter that isolates retention-like neural patterns, I feel the investigators have altered the mechanism under study, not merely its sensory modality.

These judgments seem to pull in different directions: the first and third distinguish algorithmic stability from representational change, the second treats formal equivalence as sufficient for mechanistic identity, and the fourth appeals to something like the preservation of temporal-causal structure in the experimental intervention. Is there a coherent account—perhaps framed in terms of conserved intervention targets or invariant explanatory dependencies—that captures all four verdicts? If not, which one should be revised? Furthermore, apply your principle to a scenario I have not listed: a climate-anomaly detector originally compared current ocean temperatures against an explicit thirty-year arithmetic mean, but is now implemented via a neural embedding space that learns an implicit historical reference without explicit temporal averaging. Does this preserve the mechanism, or has the explanatory dependency on temporal comparison been broken?
