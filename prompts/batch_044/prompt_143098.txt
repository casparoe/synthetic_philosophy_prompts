Following a prolonged and somewhat circular argument with a close friend about whether sustained prosperity and social order can ever constitute a genuine normative justification for authoritarian governance—taking contemporary China and Singapore as the unavoidable reference points—I want to impose some structure on our disagreement. Please rank the following five conceptual frameworks for evaluating what we might call performance legitimacy, ordering them from strongest (rank 1) to weakest (rank 5) specifically in their capacity to withstand the objection that prosperity metrics are structurally opaque to citizens: that is, citizens lack independent epistemic access to whether observed growth stems from state policy or exogenous conditions, and the informational environment is itself controlled by the regime being evaluated.

The frameworks are these:

1. Rawlsian political liberalism: legitimacy requires that basic institutions be justifiable through public reason and that basic liberties be secured, regardless of aggregate welfare outcomes.

2. Weberian rational-legal authority: legitimacy derives from procedural predictability, bureaucratic regularity, and the rule of law rather than from welfare delivery.

3. The East Asian developmental-state thesis: legitimacy is earned through demonstrable state capability in coordinating investment, suppressing rent-seeking, and delivering collective goods that markets alone would underprovide.

4. Stochastic legitimacy (a framing I am introducing for this exercise): legitimacy depends not on mean welfare gains but on a regime's demonstrated capacity to reduce intergenerational variance in catastrophic risk—pandemic preparedness, financial-system collapse, climate-driven displacement—even if this suppresses mean growth and requires opaque long-horizon planning.

5. Epistemic delegation: citizens rationally outsource governance evaluation to expert bureaucracies because the cognitive cost of independent verification exceeds the marginal democratic benefit, provided exit options remain available.

For each framework, assign a tier—S, A, B, C, or D—corresponding to its rank, and provide exactly one sentence explaining why it occupies that position with respect specifically to the structural-opacity objection, not with respect to legitimacy in general. Then identify which of the five depends most heavily on an unstated empirical premise about citizen risk preferences—namely, that populations will accept lower mean welfare in exchange for reduced tail-risk variance—and state whether removing that premise collapses the framework entirely or merely weakens it without destroying its internal logic. Keep the reasoning internal to the descriptions above; do not invoke outside authorities or texts.
