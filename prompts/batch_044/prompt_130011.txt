After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about antinatalism, I keep circling back to the analogy she pressed on me. She claimed that bringing a person into existence is structurally like throwing a party for a guest who has not yet arrived: if the party turns out to be good, the guest has no legitimate grievance that she was not invited—absence of pleasure is not a deprivation—but if the party is miserable, the host has clearly wronged her by dragging her into attendance. She used this to license Benatar’s asymmetry, then extended it: at the population level, she said, it is like planning successive parties in a building of unknown structural integrity, where each invitation risks catastrophic harm but creates no corresponding obligation to extend one. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the host, the invitation, the absent guest, the quality of the party, and the building’s integrity? Second, identify where the mapping breaks: does the analogy quietly import consent-based or harm-avoidance premises that Benatar’s argument does not actually require? Third, address how it interacts with the non-identity problem—can the “guest” who suffers from attendance coherently complain about being invited, given that the invitation is also the condition for her being the subject of that harm? Fourth, assess whether the extension to population-level “structural integrity” genuinely supports the antinatalist conclusion, or whether it smuggles in risk-aversion premises that a pronatalist could reject without touching the asymmetry itself. Finally, if the party analogy collapses under this pressure, is there a better analogy for capturing Benatar’s point without those distortions, and does the antinatalist conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table?
