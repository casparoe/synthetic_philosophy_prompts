Hey, I've been mulling over the Löbian obstacle for self-trusting agents and I'm trying to pin down where my intuitions actually land. Here are my verdicts on a few concrete setups, but I'm not sure if there's a coherent principle behind them or if I'm just drawing lines to save my favourite cases.

Case one: an agent audits its own arithmetic proof-checker, confirms it's sound, and then decides to trust any arithmetic result it produces tomorrow. That feels totally fine — it's basically just trusting a calculator it verified.

Case two: same agent, but now it adopts a general rule: 'if my future self believes X, I should believe X too', and applies this across the board, including to whether the rule itself is sound. That feels like a straight shot into the Löbian trap — it should be able to prove anything. Clear no-go.

Case three: the agent restricts that future-trust rule to empirical predictions — weather, sensor readings, market prices — on the grounds that its future version will have more data. But it can still use the rule to predict its own future behaviour and update on that prediction. I feel like there's still a hidden loop here, but I'm genuinely unsure whether it's actually dangerous or just philosophically icky.

Case four: two identical copies running in parallel, each treating the other's outputs as authoritative, but only for claims that don't mention either copy's reasoning process. This feels okay, like peer review, but part of me thinks it's just self-trust with extra steps and should face the same obstacle.

Case five: the agent uses Löbian reflection — 'if I prove φ then φ' — but only inside a restricted formal language that can't express proof predicates or belief states. The standard workaround, and I think it technically works, but it also feels fragile, like a smart enough encoder could smuggle self-reference back in.

So: case one is fine, case two is broken, case three is probably broken but I'm not sure, case four is probably fine but I'm uneasy, case five works technically but feels shaky.

Is there one precise principle that captures when self-trust avoids the obstacle? Maybe something about whether the trusted domain can encode the trust relation itself, or about causal independence? Please lay out the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn to keep it simple. Also say what your principle predicts for an agent that trusts its future self only for moral judgements, not factual ones.
