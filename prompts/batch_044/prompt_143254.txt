Suppose Friedrich Nietzsche had lived to encounter the twenty-first-century bioethical debate on moral bioenhancement — specifically, the proposal that pharmaceutical or genetic interventions ought to be used to amplify human empathy and altruistic dispositions in order to mitigate large-scale collective risks. Drawing on the conceptual apparatus of his genealogical account of morality, how might he have diagnosed this programme? Would he interpret chemically cultivated altruism as an intensification of slave morality’s inversion of noble values, as a technological mutation of the ascetic ideal’s denial of instinct, or as something that eludes both categories? In your answer, please attend to how Nietzsche’s contrast between active and reactive forces might apply to the social dynamics of mandatory or strongly encouraged enhancement, and speculate on whether he would anticipate a distinctive form of ressentiment emerging among those who resist being pharmacologically re-moralised.
