I have been thinking on my own about whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg—not in the sense of being adopted or conceived on a different day, but literally being the very person I am now if my parents had produced a different child from different gametes, or even from the same parents but a different pairing. I understand that philosophers call this origin essentialism, though I have no formal training and do not want anyone to cite texts I could not check myself. I want to run a structured discussion for about ten adults with no philosophy background, in a single 90-minute session, and I need the design to make us feel the tension of the idea in our own reasoning before anyone names it.

Please design a runnable activity built around what I will call a counterfactual sibling ladder. Each participant begins by stating one minimal, non-private fact about their own origin—birth order, that they are an only child, that they have an older sibling, nothing more. Then the group proceeds through six rungs, each altering one causal component of that origin: first a different sperm from the same father; then a different egg from the same mother; then both different gametes but the same parents; then the same DNA sequence arising by pure coincidence in genetically unrelated parents; then different parents with a different DNA sequence but with identical psychological memories and dispositions implanted at conception; and finally a being with no genetic overlap but with a continuous stream of conscious experience linked back causally to the participant. At each rung, every participant must vote whether the being is “me” or “not me,” but they do not defend their own vote. Instead, each draws one of two argument cards at random—“continuity of causal origin” or “continuity of inner life”—and must defend whichever card they drew, regardless of private belief. A token system tracks consistency: participants begin with five tokens, and the group’s consistency-judge (a rotating role) can call for a justification when someone’s defended position shifts between rungs. If the shift lacks a principled reason noted aloud, the defender forfeits one token; if they maintain coherence across all six rungs, they keep them all. The payoff is not monetary but social: at the end, anyone who retained all tokens explains their ladder in thirty seconds, and anyone who lost tokens explains which rung broke them.

Please include the exact timeline for 90 minutes, the rules for drawing and defending cards, how the consistency-judge role works, and how tokens are distributed and lost. Include a debrief that maps each rung onto a specific philosophical pressure point—for instance, where the intuition that you could have had different parents collapses, and where genetic coincidence fails to preserve identity—so that the group sees where their own reasoning strained. Predict honestly what will go wrong the first time: I expect participants will resist defending the card they did not choose, will conflate genetic similarity with personal identity, or will try to appeal to “common sense” rather than the assigned argument. Be frank about which feature of origin essentialism this activity cannot reproduce; I suspect it may be the formal modal claim about all possible worlds, or the precise distinction between essential and accidental properties in a logical sense, and the facilitator will have to supply that afterward. Do not ask us to read anything in advance, do not provide a bibliography or reading list, and do not cite specific philosophers unless the mechanics genuinely require a name. The idea should emerge from the votes, the forfeited tokens, and the moments when the group realizes they have defended contradictory conclusions—before any explanation is given.
