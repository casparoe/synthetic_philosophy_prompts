I have recently been appointed steward of my late grandfather’s maritime cooperative—a small fleet of restored working vessels that has operated continuously since 1958. In reviewing its archives, I discovered that every plank, every engine component, and every crew contract has been replaced at least twice; only the cooperative’s founding charter and one honorary captain remain unchanged. Meanwhile, a rival collective has reconstructed one of the original decommissioned hulls using salvaged original timbers and now claims it is the true successor, entitled to the cooperative’s historical fishing rights and liable for its inherited environmental debt. This has made the Ship of Theseus visceral rather than abstract for me.

For my upper-level undergraduate philosophy workshop—sixteen students, seventy-five minutes, with no required prior reading beyond a one-paragraph summary I will provide—design a structured deliberation game that forces participants to inhabit the tension between material continuity, functional continuity, and narrative continuity, not as theories to name but as practical commitments that conflict in real time. Frame the session as a simulated Harbor Council in which students are assigned concrete roles: current cooperative members arguing from operational continuity; descendants of the original 1958 crew arguing from material lineage; representatives of the reconstructed “ghost vessel” arguing from artifact authenticity; and neutral arbitrators tasked with allocating a single, indivisible historical license and assigning responsibility for an inherited debt. Give each role hidden information—such as an undocumented repair log or a private letter showing intent—that will cause them to defend opposite continuity conditions depending on whether they are arguing about rights, obligations, or identity.

Specify the rules and small stakes so that students must vote and justify their votes without invoking philosophical terminology until the final ten minutes; the incentives should be arranged so that the same student will likely switch which form of continuity they privilege once the hidden information is revealed. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes; the debrief should map specific moments in the deliberation onto parts of the identity problem, including the “shadow reconstruction” problem that arises when both the current cooperative and the rebuilt vessel claim exclusive succession. Be explicit about which feature of the metaphysical problem—specifically, the difference between a physical artifact and a collective agent with distributed intentionality—the activity cannot reproduce on its own, and state exactly how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally. Finally, predict which feature of the game will most likely cause polarization or collapse, and describe how to recover if the group splits irreconcilably between the “functionalists” and the “materialists.” Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or assigned named theorists; the objective is for participants to feel the pull of the paradox in their own reasoning before they know what to call it.
