I have been working through Berlin’s “Two Concepts of Liberty” alongside Pettit’s republican account with a small reading group, and in trying to formalize the differences for myself I arrived at a framing that I now suspect may be collapsing distinctions I should be keeping. As I understand it, negative liberty is satisfied whenever there is no actual interference with my action, whereas republican liberty demands the absence of arbitrary domination—that is, the absence of a capacity for interference that remains even when it is not exercised. But when I try to render this precisely, republican liberty starts to look like negative liberty evaluated across a particular modal reference class: not “am I interfered with in the actual world?” but “would I be interfered with across the nearby possible worlds in which the dominant party’s preferences or incentives shift?” On this reading, republican freedom is just negative freedom made counterfactually robust, a kind of “stabilized” version of the same underlying value rather than a categorically different one.

If that is right, then the classic benevolent-master case—where the slave faces no actual interference but lives under the master’s arbitrary power—seems to dissolve into a dispute about how wide our modal window should be, not about whether domination is a distinct evil. And if republican liberty is really just negative liberty indexed to a dominance-sensitive counterfactual profile, I am unsure why we need three categories (positive, negative, republican) rather than a single spectrum of interference-sensitivity, with positive liberty representing high internal sensitivity, negative liberty representing actual-world-only sensitivity, and republican liberty representing moderate counterfactual sensitivity. So my question is this: does treating republican liberty as a modal thickening of negative liberty—freedom assessed for robustness against arbitrary interference across a specified reference class of worlds—actually capture what Pettit and others intend, or does it miss something structurally different about the master-slave relation that cannot be reconstructed within the negative-liberty framework, no matter how we expand the modal conditions? And if the framing does miss something essential, what exactly is left over once we have accounted for all counterfactual possibilities of interference?
