I am preparing a graduate seminar on comparative metaphysics and the logic of compound terms, and in a recent discussion with colleagues I found myself returning to the White Horse Dialogue in the Gongsun Longzi, a text I have taught for years but still find genuinely slippery. The passage, as it is usually rendered in translation, proceeds something like this: an interlocutor asks whether a white horse is a horse, and the respondent denies it, arguing that the term “horse” picks out a shape or form, the term “white” picks out a color, and the compound “white horse” picks out both together; since what is named by “horse” alone differs from what is named by the compound, and since asking for a horse will not yield a white horse (and vice versa), the two expressions cannot denote the same thing. I understand the surface-level paradox, but I am unsure how to read the underlying ontological commitment. Is the argument chiefly about the intension of linguistic expressions—claiming that compound terms have a different sense and therefore a different reference—or does it carry a metaphysical thesis that properties such as color are in some way separate entities that alter the identity conditions of the substance they characterize? I am especially interested in whether there is an implicit mereological assumption at work, something like the idea that adding whiteness to the horse produces a new kind of whole with different modal properties, rather than merely describing the same particular under a more specific description. I want to avoid an anachronistic reading through Fregean sense and reference or through modern predicate logic, yet I also want to see whether the School of Names’ broader concern with “pointing out” versus “naming” supports a genuinely non-analytic interpretation of the move from conceptual difference to ontological difference. Could you help me understand how the text’s own conceptual resources—particularly its treatment of “similarity” and “difference”—bear on this question, and whether the dialogue is best read as a linguistic puzzle, a metaphysical claim about the separability of properties, or something that collapses the distinction between the two? I am not looking for a bibliography or further readings, only a careful conceptual unpacking of the argument’s structure and its place within Mingjia thought.
