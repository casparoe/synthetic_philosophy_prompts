I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science, writing for a graduate seminar on ontological commitment in scientific explanation. The controversy I want to address concerns Daniel Dennett's account of real patterns and whether it can sustain the autonomy of the intentional stance without either collapsing mental attribution into physical explanation or inflating mental properties into fully robust entities. Specifically, I want you to weigh two competing interpretations of how predictive success functions across the physical, design, and intentional stances. On one side, there is the stance-neutral reading: predictive compression is a uniform criterion for pattern reality, so if belief-desire attributions yield genuine predictive compression, they identify patterns that are every bit as real as those picked out by physics or engineering design, which seems to force a non-eliminative mental realism that physicalism must accommodate. On the other side, there is the stance-relative or indexed reading: predictive success is always evaluated relative to the explanatory goals, background assumptions, and pragmatic constraints of a given stance, so that being a real-pattern-for-the-intentional-stance carries no cross-stance ontological comparison and does not license mental realism; the patterns are real only in a stance-internal, instrumental sense. The argument should proceed through conceptual analysis rather than empirical case studies, though you may illustrate the distinction with a concrete comparison—such as the predictive patterns involved in thermostat modeling under the design stance versus those involved in explaining an agent's action under the intentional stance—to show exactly where the two readings diverge. Do not provide bibliographic references, reading lists, or citation requests; ground the analysis in the distinctions themselves.
