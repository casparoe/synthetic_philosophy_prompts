I'm serving as a senior advisor to the Scientific Funding Renewal Panel at the Institute for Longitudinal Health Research. The panel must decide whether to approve Phase-IV funding for the Cohort Interaction Study (CIS), which has run continuously since 1994. The study's core Bayesian predictive model was calibrated in 1995 using baseline demographic and environmental priors and was never revised thereafter. In 2023, the study observed—without any post-hoc parameter adjustment—the precise interaction effect between two specified exposure variables that the 1995 architecture had projected. The research team argues that this multi-decade predictive success provides strong, independent confirmatory support warranting renewal. A dissenting panel member contends that, because the 2023 outcomes fall within the range the original prior was already structured to accommodate, they amount to old evidence in the technical Bayesian sense: they should not yield a substantial posterior update and therefore should not count as novel evidential support. There is an additional conceptual complication: CIS possesses a genuinely pre-registered, unrevised predictive architecture spanning nearly three decades, which raises the question of whether evidential depreciation applies differently to long-horizon predictive confirmations than to static, one-time retrodictive fits. Draft a concise, formal memo recommending either renewal or denial. The memo should present clear, actionable criteria the panel can apply; explain how the problem of old evidence bears on the evidential weight of the 2023 results, carefully distinguishing predictive novelty from retrodictive coherence; address the strongest argument on the opposing side—specifically, that sustained, unrevised predictive accuracy over time is qualitatively different from post-hoc accommodation; and rely solely on conceptual reasoning, with no bibliographic citations, reading lists, or appeals to authority.
