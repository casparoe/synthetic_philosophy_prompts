I serve on the Acquisition and Ethics Committee of the Mid-Atlantic Regional Historical Society, and we must vote next Thursday on a proposed donation: the complete administrative files (1962–1983) of the fictional Delaware Valley Urban Renewal Authority. The collection is unusually intact—budget ledgers, inter-agency correspondence, demolition orders, and parcel-level photographic surveys—but it contains virtually no records generated by the displaced residents themselves. Internal memoranda reveal that tenant testimony was systematically excluded from official minutes, and the photographic logs identify structures only by parcel number rather than by occupant or community. A separate, smaller donation offers fragmentary oral-history recordings and handwritten petitions from former residents of two cleared neighborhoods, but these lack institutional provenance and are not cross-referenced to the bureaucratic files.

Our charter requires a recommendation of accession, conditional accession, or rejection, with a concrete implementation timeline if we recommend acceptance. The philosophical tension is this: preserving the bureaucratic record risks re-inscribing the authority’s epistemic dominance—its power to determine what counts as a record—while rejecting it erases evidence of how that dominance operated. A minority of the committee argues that we should accept the files only if we simultaneously fund a “negative-space cataloging” protocol, which would treat documented absences (missing tenant files, redacted testimony slots, anonymized parcel maps) as primary metadata objects rather than background gaps, and which would link every bureaucratic entry to the corresponding community fragments where they exist. Others argue that such a protocol overstates our curatorial capacity and implicitly legitimizes the archive’s silences by aestheticizing them; they favor rejection unless the donor also transfers legal title to the displaced residents’ papers, which is impossible.

Please draft the committee’s written opinion—no more than about 800 words—for circulation before Thursday’s vote. It should recommend a specific course of action within a defined timeframe, engage seriously with the minority concern that negative-space cataloging risks aestheticizing structural violence, and explain why your recommendation respects both the evidentiary value of the bureaucratic record and the epistemic rights of those it excluded. Write in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
