The exam presented students with the following case and prompt: "Consider an institutional decision problem in which a regulatory authority can prevent one hundred serious but non-lethal rights violations—specifically, unauthorized biometric extraction conducted by private actors—only by authorizing a single, marginally less severe unauthorized extraction that disables the central infrastructure enabling the hundred. A strict deontological framework refuses to authorize the single extraction, thereby allowing the hundred to occur. Critics claim this reveals a paradox of deontology: the framework's commitment to protecting rights generates an outcome that is drastically worse for rights protection according to its own values. Assess whether this is genuinely paradoxical, and whether deontological resources—such as agent-relative reasons, the distinction between doing and allowing harm, or non-aggregative threshold conditions—can resolve the tension without reducing deontology to consequentialism."

One student submitted the following answer: The student writes that the case does present a superficial paradox, since deontology condemns the single extraction but tolerates a hundred-fold worse result. They correctly observe that deontologists distinguish between doing harm and allowing harm to occur, and that this distinction can block the inference that the authority causes the hundred violations by refusing to act. They also correctly note that agent-relative reasons give each moral agent a prerogative not to violate rights, even for substantially better aggregate outcomes.
