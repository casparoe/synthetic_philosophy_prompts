Please argue for the position that brain size is morally relevant, but only within a sharply restricted domain: comparing entities that share an identical basic cognitive architecture and differ mainly in the scale of homogeneous neural substrate, rather than in qualitative organizational restructuring. The argument should not rely on the familiar trope that larger brains yield greater intelligence or consciousness in general, but should instead claim that—holding architecture constant—greater absolute neural volume expands the combinatorial space for temporally extended, integrated states of suffering and preference-formation, and that this expansion carries direct moral weight under a functionalist account of moral patienthood. Distinguish carefully between scaling through replication of similar processing units, which increases state-space volume, and scaling through qualitative redesign, which may realize equivalent functions with far less substrate. Then address the counterexample of a small but hyper-optimized brain that achieves functional equivalence with minimal neurons: explain why, on this view, architectural homogeneity still matters for the moral calculus even if such equivalence is conceptually possible. Build the case from conceptual first principles rather than empirical species comparisons, and avoid appeals to specific neuroscientific literature; I am interested in whether the structural point holds independently of particular biological data.
