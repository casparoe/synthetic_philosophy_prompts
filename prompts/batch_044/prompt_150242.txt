I'm designing a handout for a graduate seminar on Keynes's philosophy of economics and statistics. I want a decision tree that takes a reader from their commitments about rational belief under ignorance to one of several named interpretive stances on Keynes's theory of uncertainty. The tree should have six to eight leaf nodes.

Root commitment: you accept Keynes's distinction between risk (situations with measurable, numerically definite probabilities) and uncertainty (situations where such probabilities do not exist or are inapplicable).

First branch: Is this distinction primarily epistemic (we merely lack the data to compute probabilities) or ontological (the future is genuinely open in a way that makes numerical probabilities inapplicable in principle)?

Second branch: What is the status of the weight of argument? Is it an independent dimension of rational belief alongside probability, a proxy for confidence in the premises of a probability judgment, or a misinterpretation that should be collapsed into standard subjective probability?

Third branch: How ought one to decide or act under true uncertainty? By conventional judgment and animal spirits, by maximin or worst-case reasoning, by refusing to commit and maintaining liquidity, or by treating all options as equally arbitrary?

Fourth branch: Does Keynes's view of logical probability relations treat them as objective relations between propositions (like Russell's logical relations), subjective degrees of belief constrained by logic, or purely conventional ordering tools? And separately, does the principle of limited independent variety provide any rational basis for projecting past patterns into the future, or does it leave forecasting entirely conventional?

At each leaf, name the interpretive stance and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment — the thing it must swallow to maintain consistency. Where combinations of answers lead to no named position, leave the leaf as unnamed and diagnose in one sentence why the space is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored).

Every node must be phrased as a concrete claim or case that a student could answer without already knowing which interpretation it points to. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Where the tree cannot genuinely separate two positions, flag them explicitly rather than forcing a split. The deliverable should build from the logical space of answers first, with the named positions located afterward.
