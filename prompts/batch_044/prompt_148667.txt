Below is the outline and opening argument for a short essay I am drafting, titled “Two Inversions: Darwin’s Blind Watchmaker and Turing’s Blind Interrogator.” The thesis is that Darwin and Turing each perform a strange inversion of reasoning around the relation between competence and comprehension, but they invert it in opposite directions. In On the Origin of Species, Darwin explains the appearance of biological design—widespread adaptive competence—without invoking any comprehending agent; cumulative selection produces competence precisely because it lacks comprehension. In his well-known essay on machine intelligence, Turing proposes that we ought to attribute comprehension (intelligence) to an agent solely on the basis of its displayed behavioral competence, thereby reversing the usual epistemic order in which comprehension is taken to explain competence. The parallel is that both thinkers sever the intuitive link between understanding and successful performance. The divergence is directional: Darwin removes comprehension from the causal origin of competence, while Turing removes the need for direct access to comprehension in its epistemic attribution.

I develop this by comparing Darwin’s account of cumulative selection—where small, non-purposive variations accumulate into complex adaptations—to Turing’s account of learning machines, which acquire chess-playing or language-using competence through iterative feedback without being explicitly programmed with governing rules. In both cases, competence emerges or is recognized without an internal blueprint being readable by an outside observer. Yet there is an asymmetry I want to flag: Darwin’s argument is abductive and historical, explaining how competence came to be without mind; Turing’s argument is operational and normative, proposing how we ought to judge mind given only competence. I suggest framing Darwin as an “eliminative” inverter and Turing as a “projective” inverter, with the hidden symmetry being that both displace the observer’s own reasoning—Darwin by substituting selection for design, Turing by substituting behavioral criteria for introspective access.

I would welcome critical comments on three specific points. First, is the parallel between Darwin’s causal story and Turing’s attributional story philosophically sound, or does it conflate ontology with epistemology? Second, does the “inversion” framing obscure the fact that Turing was not advancing a metaphysical claim about machine consciousness but proposing a methodological replacement for an ill-defined question? Third, in the section comparing selection to machine-learning feedback loops, I risk overstating the structural similarity—how damaging is that distortion?
