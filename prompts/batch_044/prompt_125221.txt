I'm giving the opening address at a workshop co-hosted by a behavioral-policy institute and a philosophy department, titled "Choice Architecture and Human Agency"; roughly half the room builds opt-in defaults, nudges, and welfare algorithms, and the other half studies autonomy, coercion, and the history of political theology. I want the speech to take Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor—the argument that human beings will surrender freedom for bread, miracle, and authority, and that the deepest kindness is to relieve them of choice's burden—as its structural spine rather than as literary decoration. The address must live inside the Inquisitor's tension: it should make the designers feel the weight of what it means to "correct Christ's work" in secular form, reducing cognitive load for people who may not have asked for the reduction, while also forcing the humanists to confront that freedom genuinely burdens people with limited bandwidth and that not every simplification is tyranny. Please write a fifteen-minute address that frames modern choice architecture as an unwitting repetition of the Inquisitor's experiment, leaving the room with a shared, unsettled problem—how to distinguish relieving a burden from confiscating a right—rather than with either a libertarian sermon or a technocratic apologia. It needs to land for experts without losing the non-specialists, and it should not resolve the tension; the workshop's job is to work inside it. Avoid bibliographies, citations, or appeals to authority outside the idea itself; the argument should stand on the logic of the Inquisitor's offer as applied to our own tools.
