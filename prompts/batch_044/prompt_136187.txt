I have recently been asked to serve on a university committee reviewing whether a proposed interdisciplinary program in integrative wellness science should be granted departmental status, and in preparing my position I found myself having to articulate my own philosophical commitments about scientific demarcation. Before I present them, I want a consistency audit. Here is roughly where I stand:

(1) I hold that Popper's falsifiability criterion captures the core logical feature that distinguishes scientific theories from pseudoscience, though I interpret it normatively—specifying what science ought to do—rather than as a purely descriptive sociology of working scientists.

(2) I accept the Duhem-Quine thesis: no isolated hypothesis can ever be conclusively falsified, since every empirical test relies on auxiliary assumptions.

(3) I think Popper's conventionalist response to (2)—treating basic statements as tentatively adopted conventions—is correct, and I hold that this conventionalism does not reduce science to arbitrariness, provided those conventions remain open to revision.

(4) I think evolutionary and sociological debunking arguments apply to our demarcation intuitions: our judgments about which fields count as science track social prestige, funding patterns, and institutional history at least as much as they track any logical structure.

(5) Despite (4), I am a realist about scientific status: there is a mind-independent fact of the matter about whether a given theory or research programme genuinely belongs to science.

(6) I think the practical defense of science against pseudoscience should rely on institutional norms—replication, transparency, peer review—rather than on philosophers or committees applying a philosophical demarcation criterion case by case.

(7) I also think that Lakatos's notion of progressive problem-shifting supplies the right constraint on the conventions in (3): basic statements are adopted within research programmes that demonstrate sustained empirical success and predictive novelty, and this empirical success is not itself reducible to convention.

I am aware that some of these commitments pull against one another, but I am unsure which tensions are genuine and which only appear so. In particular, I worry that combining conventionalism about basic statements (3) with debunking about our intuitions (4) undermines my realism in (5), and that my appeal to Lakatosian progress (7) is actually incompatible with my commitment to a purely Popperian logical criterion (1). At the same time, I suspect that the tension between (2) and (1) is only apparent, since Popper explicitly anticipated the Duhem-Quine problem.

Please audit this package. For each cluster that is genuinely in tension, specify the severity, identify exactly which commitment has to give and what that costs, and rank the tensions from most to least severe. Identify the apparent tension that dissolves on inspection and explain why. Finally, tell me which single revision—giving up or substantially modifying one commitment—would clean up the most tension at the least philosophical cost, and what I would lose by making it.
