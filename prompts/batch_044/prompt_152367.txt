I am delivering the eulogy on Saturday for my uncle, who died at seventy-eight after serving twenty-six years on our county council. I am not an academic—I work as a carpenter—but for the past several years I have been thinking on my own about why elections seem to turn representatives into careerists, and my uncle eventually came to share that worry. In the final years of his life he argued, in letters to the local paper and in conversations with me, that selecting council members by lot for single, non-renewable terms would break the cycle in which office-holders come to represent their own survival rather than the people who put them there. He left explicit instructions that his memorial should not praise his long tenure as a form of wisdom or institutional memory, because he had come to see that very longevity as part of the corruption he was describing; he also asked that nothing be said about his watching over us from elsewhere, since he regarded that as false comfort. The audience will be split: about half are family and neighbors who remember him mainly for resolving a zoning dispute and for being present at every school board meeting; the other half includes members of the local democratic-reform circle he joined late in life, people who will recognize references to sortition and to the problem of career politicians. I need a eulogy of roughly ten minutes that can hold both groups without either turning into a lecture on lottocracy or reducing his conviction to a sentimental footnote. The idea should do real structural work: it must shape what I can and cannot say about his life—so I cannot glorify his decades in office, I cannot invoke an afterlife, and I should not present his late change of mind as a simple personal quirk—while still offering comfort to people who are grieving and who are not philosophers. Please write it.
