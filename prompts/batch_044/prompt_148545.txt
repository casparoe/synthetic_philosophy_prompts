A lecture summary I came across characterizes Gödel's modal ontological argument in the following way. Gödel formalizes Anselm's conception of God as the greatest conceivable being, defining "God-like" as the conjunction of all great-making properties within a second-order modal framework. He introduces axioms stating that positive properties are necessarily positive, that necessary existence is positive, and that the property of being God-like is itself positive; from the possibility of a God-like being he derives its necessary, hence actual, existence. The summary emphasizes that Gödel avoids Descartes's error of treating existence as a predicate by using modal operators. Yet it claims that Gödel's proof depends on an unstated identification between positive properties and essential properties, so that any property God actually lacks becomes impossible for God to possess. Consequently, the summary argues, the proof generates a modal determinism in which every contingent feature of the world is fixed, and it concludes that Gödel has not advanced beyond Anselm, since both arguments define God into existence by embedding modal strength within the concept itself. Is this a fair characterization? Please separate what accurately reflects Gödel's axioms and modal logic; where it conflates Gödel with Plantinga or Anselm; whether the identification of positive and essential properties is present in Gödel's text or an interpretive addition; and whether the modal-determinism objection succeeds against Gödel's formulation specifically or only against a strengthened version of it.
