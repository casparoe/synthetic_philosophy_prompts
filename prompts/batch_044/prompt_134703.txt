Having spent my career in field biology and only recently turned to philosophy of mind in retirement, I have become somewhat fixated on Douglas Hofstadter’s “Ant Fugue” dialogue and its figure of Aunt Hillary—the ant colony treated as a potential subject of consciousness. From this I have formed a position that I suspect is stronger than metaphor: I hold that an ant colony can literally constitute a minimal cognitive subject, not because its individual members are intelligent, but because the colony’s pheromone-mediated signaling network realizes cross-scale integrated information dynamics that are non-decomposable into the behavioral rules of single ants. Specifically, I am inclined to think that when colony-level decision-making exhibits attractor-state transitions in chemical signal-space—transitions that constrain future signaling patterns in ways no individual ant’s local rule can determine—the colony satisfies a functionalist criterion for minimal awareness analogous to re-entrant processing in neural systems. I do not claim the colony has rich phenomenology; rather, I claim it has a form of primitive, distributed subjectivity that is genuinely its own.

What, then, should count against this view? I want an honest map of its exposure, not merely a list of common objections. Which empirical findings would be decisive—say, demonstrations that every apparently integrated colony behavior is fully reducible to independent agent-based dynamics with no emergent causal structure—versus which would only be awkward, such as the absence of behavioral reports we could recognize as subjective? I am particularly concerned with whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if defenders respond to any failure of integration measurement by redefining “integration” downward or appealing to hidden chemical variables, does that insulation become a theoretical vice rather than a virtue? Furthermore, how should I weigh the “combination problem” as applied to insects—does the difficulty of explaining how ant-level experiences (if any) combine into colony-level experience genuinely undermine the functionalist move, or can it be dissolved by denying that there are ant-level experiences to combine in the first place? I would value the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely inconvenient, together with an assessment of which ones a committed colony-mind functionalist would themselves concede, and which they would dismiss as category mistakes. If the view turns out to be well insulated against refutation, I want to know whether that insulation is a sign of conceptual coherence or of emptiness.
