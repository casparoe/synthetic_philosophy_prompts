The debate over whether Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis identifies a genuine causal mechanism, or merely offers a redescription of structural economic shifts, has been stalled for decades in both sociology and philosophy of social science. On one side, defenders hold that the ascetic ethos of early Calvinism introduced a distinctive motivational structure—an orientation toward methodical labor and capital reinvestment—that was not reducible to contemporaneous market conditions or legal-institutional changes; they treat values as causally autonomous variables capable of altering the trajectory of economic development. On the other side, critics grant the correlation but deny the causal attribution: they argue that the same behavioral patterns can be fully explained by emerging property rights, demographic pressures, and trade-network expansions, with religious discourse functioning as a legitimating epiphenomenon rather than an independent driver. Both camps now share the same archival data and largely agree on the chronology; neither has produced a decisive empirical counterexample that the other accepts, and the exchange has settled into a repetition of methodological complaints—about overdetermination, about the untestability of counterfactual cultural histories, and about whether explanation in social science must always bottom out in material conditions.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily an empirical deadlock but a disagreement about the criteria for explanatory adequacy in the human sciences, masked by shared historical vocabulary. Specifically, I suspect the Weberian side is answering a question about the interpretive coherence of actors' reasons—whether the religious framework made certain economic behaviors intelligible in a way that purely structural accounts cannot—while the materialist side is answering a question about counterfactual dependence—whether the economic transformation would have occurred absent the religious framework—without recognizing that these are different explanatory targets. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is instead a hidden formal problem—perhaps about how to individuate cultural causes when every value-laden practice is embedded in a material substrate—or whether the dispute ultimately turns on a bedrock intuition about whether human agency can ever be genuinely efficacious against structural determination.
