I am taking a seminar on political philosophy and data governance, and I have become puzzled by a tension I cannot resolve. In Germany, the architecture of citizen-state interaction is built around what might be called compartmentalised visibility: the same individual holds separate identifiers for tax, health insurance, and municipal residence, and federal law treats the consolidation of these streams as a harm in itself, even when it would increase administrative efficiency. By contrast, American administrative culture treats a unified profile—Social Security numbers linking tax, credit, and licensing records—as a default public good, with transparency norms that treat opacity as corruption. Meanwhile, Chinese social-credit systems appear to treat cross-domain behavioural data not as a privacy risk but as the necessary substrate for a coherent moral evaluation of the citizen. I want to understand what philosophical commitments about the self, the state, and the nature of information make each of these architectures seem rational rather than merely expedient.

Specifically, what theory of personal identity or of the boundaries of the moral agent does the German model presuppose? Does it require a view on which the state addresses distinct spheres of a person that ought not to be synthesised, or can it be explained purely by a risk-based calculus about power? And is the American openness model committed to the idea that transparency is intrinsically democratic, or does it merely presuppose a particular epistemology—perhaps that citizens can only hold power accountable if information flows are maximised? Finally, does the Chinese integration of behavioural records into a single evaluative framework presuppose a specifically communitarian conception of the person, one for which individual actions only acquire meaning within a unified social narrative? Or could a liberal individualist defend the same institutional design on purely instrumental grounds?

What I am asking for is a reconstruction of the implicit philosophical anthropology in each practice: not just the policy arguments that are made explicitly, but the deeper commitments about what a citizen is—whether an atomised rights-bearer, a transparent node in a network, or an organically embedded member—that would make the architecture feel coherent. I also want to know whether these three practices are genuinely underpinned by incompatible theories, or whether a single view—say, a consequentialist calculus about social trust—could license all three depending on empirical conditions. And if the German compartmentalisation model does indeed presuppose a non-instrumental boundary around the self, what concrete institutional changes would follow if that boundary were rejected in favour of a fully integrated, transparent, or evaluative architecture?
