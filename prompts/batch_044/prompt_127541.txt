Consider the institutional pattern in which a present democratic government formally apologizes for historical wrongs—such as systematic dispossession or state-sponsored violence—explicitly framing them as moral failures of a prior regime, while simultaneously maintaining legal doctrines like sovereign immunity and statutory limitations that prevent survivors or descendants from obtaining judicial redress. What theory of collective moral agency and state continuity does this pairing presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: must it hold that moral responsibility can be inherited symbolically without being transmitted structurally, or that the state's identity across regime change is representational rather than institutional? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify which rival accounts—continuous-agent theories, purely distributive theories, or purely expressive, non-agentive theories of state speech—would be unable to license both moves together, and say whether the pairing is philosophically underdetermined. If an expressive theory could also justify the apology without attributing agency to the state, what does that imply about the sincerity conditions the apology is actually meant to satisfy?
