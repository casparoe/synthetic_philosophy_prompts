Constitutional theorists routinely invoke the image of Ulysses binding himself to the mast to justify entrenched constitutional provisions—supermajoritarian amendment rules, judicial review, or rights-based constraints—claiming that a democratic people can rationally restrict its own future legislative capacity to guard against temporary passions or demagogic sirens. The analogy is meant to license the conclusion that such precommitment is an exercise of collective autonomy rather than its betrayal. Audit this comparison with precision: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping Ulysses, the sirens, the ropes, the crew, the mast, and the future self onto their constitutional analogues; identify the disanalogies that specifically undermine the normative inference—particularly the shift from an individual agent to an intergenerational collective, the difference between knowing exactly which seductive threat awaits and facing an abstract structural risk of democratic backsliding, the reversibility of physical ropes versus the rigidity of constitutional entrenchment, and the problem of binding future citizens who never consented to be tied; separate the disanalogies that merely complicate the picture from those that dissolve the analogy's authority; and determine whether the conclusion—that constitutional entrenchment is rational self-binding—survives once the features quietly imported by the source domain (a single knower, a cooperative crew, an immediately present danger, an identical bound agent) are made explicit. If the analogy fails to carry the normative weight assigned to it, suggest whether an alternative model drawn from institutional design rather than individual psychology could better capture the logic of constitutional precommitment without presupposing that a present democratic self has legitimate authority over its future counterpart.
