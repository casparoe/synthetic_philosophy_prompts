In seminars on epistemic logic I have repeatedly encountered the maxim 'all truths are knowable' being invoked to dismiss worries about permanently inaccessible facts—most recently when a colleague argued that events lying beyond our cosmological horizon cannot be genuinely true, since 'if it were true, it could in principle be known'. I am sceptical that this maxim expresses a single claim, and I want to understand precisely what work it might be doing. Could you lay out the distinct, precise theses that the slogan 'all truths are knowable' could stand for—carefully separating modal, epistemic, and temporal readings; distinguishing trivial logical truths from substantive metaphysical commitments; and identifying for each whether it is compatible with Fitch's paradox of knowability or is undermined by it? In particular, please indicate which readings actually support the inference that unknowability entails falsehood, and which rely on conflating knowledge with mere logical possibility. Also say which versions, if any, genuinely address the cosmological-horizon case rather than changing the subject. Please write your answer in British English.
