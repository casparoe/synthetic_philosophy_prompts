I am leading a single 90-minute seminar for philosophy graduate students trained in analytic metaphysics and logic, none of whom have studied Buddhist philosophy. I want them to grasp Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) and Madhyamaka emptiness (śūnyatā) not as a historical curiosity but as a live argument: specifically, that emptiness is not a fifth metaphysical position but the refusal of inherent existence (svabhāva) across all four logical corners. Design the explanatory arc: (a) the dependency order of ideas — what must be established before the tetralemma is introduced, and why presenting svabhāva before the conventional/ultimate distinction invites a predictable misconception; (b) the carrying example — select one case drawn from analytic metaphysics (explicitly not the chariot, table, or fire-and-fuel) that allows students to feel the intuitive pull of svabhāva and then watch it dissolve; state what properties this example must have and what it must lack to avoid reducing emptiness to a mereological puzzle; (c) the misconception map — identify the four misconceptions this audience will predictably generate (for instance: that the tetralemma endorses dialetheia; that emptiness is metaphysical nihilism; that conventional existence is purely pragmatic falsehood; that Madhyamaka is skeptical agnosticism about all propositions) and specify the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; (d) a final check for understanding that distinguishes genuine comprehension of emptiness from rote repetition that all things are empty; (e) what to openly exclude from this session (for example, the Prāsaṅgika–Svātantrika distinction, Sanskrit philological debates, or the two-truths doctrine beyond its minimal functional role) and why leaving each out protects the core argument. The plan should require no prior Sanskrit, should not depend on reading primary texts during the session, and should avoid asking students to evaluate historical attributions.
