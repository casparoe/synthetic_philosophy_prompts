I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. At a recent cross-disciplinary workshop on AI safety, a speaker invoked the principle “It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence” to argue that deploying a machine-learning system whose failure modes have not been exhaustively mapped is ethically impermissible—essentially, that relying on statistical confidence without deductive proof violates a categorical moral duty not to hold beliefs on weak grounds.

Before I push back, I want to understand exactly what that sentence could be claiming. Could you lay out the distinct precise readings of this slogan, arranged from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, please specify: whether it expresses an epistemic norm, a moral norm, or both; whether it presupposes that belief is voluntary or applies even to involuntary cognitive states; whether it can extend to non-human agents or to functional “beliefs” embedded in software—systems that maintain confidence-weighted outputs but lack mental states; and whether, on that specific interpretation, statistical testing could ever constitute “sufficient” evidence for an engineering decision.

In particular, I need to know which of these readings actually supports the speaker’s conclusion that probabilistic verification is never adequate for deployment, and which readings collapse into platitudes—such as “do not claim certainty when you only have suspicion”—that do not prohibit acting under uncertainty at all. I am not asking for a single endorsed interpretation; I want a disambiguation menu with verdicts on plausibility and on applicability to engineering judgment, so I can pinpoint precisely where my disagreement lies.
