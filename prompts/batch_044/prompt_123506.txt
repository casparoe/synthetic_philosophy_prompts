Write a joke about nuclear brinkmanship that turns on the conceptual tension between credible commitment and the rational-actor framework that makes deterrence calculable. The setup should involve strategic planners trying to solve the credibility problem—convincing an adversary that escalation is not a bluff—by engineering mechanisms that genuinely remove human discretion over the launch decision, such as automated randomized protocols or irrevocably delegated authority. The punchline should hinge on the paradox that, once these mechanisms succeed in making the threat credible by injecting real, non-rational chance into the system, they simultaneously destroy the very framework of predictable, utility-maximizing response that allows an adversary to rationally accommodate the threat rather than preempt it; in other words, the more perfectly they prove they are not bluffing, the more they turn a strategic interaction into an uncontrollable lottery, leaving the opponent with no rational move except to strike first. The joke should be intelligible to someone familiar with basic deterrence logic, but it should avoid generic tropes like "pressing the red button" and should not depend on citing or attributing any specific theorist, doctrine, or text.
