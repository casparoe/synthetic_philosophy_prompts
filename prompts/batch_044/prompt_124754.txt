Two colleagues at a recent workshop on consciousness studies became locked in a disagreement about heterophenomenology as a method for the science of consciousness. The first colleague argued that heterophenomenology, in the form Dennett originally proposed, is the only methodologically sound approach because it treats subjects' first-person reports about experience as texts comprising what Dennett calls the heterophenomenological world, interpreting these reports without granting them special metaphysical authority over third-person behavioural and neural data. She maintained that this brackets the hard problem rather than solving it prematurely, avoids the trap of the Cartesian theatre, and allows consciousness science to proceed with rigorous third-person methods. The second colleague replied that this position collapses into a sophisticated behaviourism: if scientists treat reports purely as texts for interpretation, they risk studying nothing more than narrative patterns while the actual phenomenon of subjective experience slips away unexamined. The first colleague answered that her opponent was smuggling in an unverifiable commitment to direct introspective access; heterophenomenology does not deny that subjects have experiences, but it refuses to treat first-person descriptions as incorrigible data about an inner realm. The second colleague then conceded the metaphysical neutrality of the method but raised a practical and conceptual limitation: standard heterophenomenology privileges articulate verbal reports, which systematically excludes non-linguistic subjects and, more recently, raises questions about artificial systems that generate structurally analogous outputs without the same cognitive architecture. He proposed an extension he termed architectural heterophenomenology, in which the generative coherence, contextual sensitivity, and self-correction patterns of a reporting system are treated not as hidden proxies for inner experience but as integral parts of the heterophenomenological data themselves. The first colleague countered that this extension risks reintroducing precisely the internalist assumptions that heterophenomenology was designed to avoid, turning structural and architectural features into disguised indicators of real consciousness rather than treating all observable data as interpretive texts on equal footing. They asked an observer to referee. Please work through their exchange move by move, rating each argument as clearly mistaken, partly correct, or clearly right, and explain where they talk past each other or miss a shared point. In particular, assess whether the proposed architectural heterophenomenology genuinely addresses a blind spot in the standard method or instead changes the subject away from Dennett's original purpose. Present your adjudication as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a single flowing letter or email.
