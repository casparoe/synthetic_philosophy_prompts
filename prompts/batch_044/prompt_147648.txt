I'm trying to understand a recurring pattern in discussions about life-altering choices—becoming a parent, emigrating irreversibly, or undergoing a deep religious or philosophical conversion. The pattern concerns what people who have already crossed over can say to those still deciding. Those who are glad they changed almost always insist that the experience is radically opaque from the outside: "you can't know what it's like until you're here." But there is a mirror group—people who made the same crossing, now profoundly regret it—who invoke the very same opacity, except now as a warning. What is striking is that the regretful individuals rarely describe the experience as painful or harmful in any ordinary sense; instead, they say that the person they were before the shift made a mistake, yet they cannot translate that mistake into terms their pre-shift self would recognize, because the framework for recognizing errors has itself been transformed. Meanwhile, the satisfied individuals seem unable to imagine having made the opposite choice, not because they can point to decisive reasons accessible beforehand, but because the choice now looks inevitable from within their new evaluative structure.

So you end up with two bodies of first-person testimony that are structurally symmetrical—both claim inaccessibility from the outside, both deny that pre-choice reasoning can settle the matter—yet they point in opposite directions. The person deciding is left without any reliable first-person guide, not merely because information is missing, but because every available testimony is filtered through a value system the decision-maker does not yet share and cannot evaluate without already having adopted it. I know this sits near the philosophical literature on transformative experience and the difficulty of rational choice when one's core preferences will shift, but that literature tends to focus on the single agent's problem of projecting future values. This seems different: it is the epistemic deadlock created when an entire community of post-change individuals, divided by outcome, all present structurally identical claims to authority. Does this specific deadlock—this bifurcated, symmetrically opaque testimony across value-locked outcomes—have an established name? Is it best treated as an extension of the standard transformative-choice problem, or as a distinct phenomenon, perhaps something like "value-locked testimony" or "symmetrical inaccessibility in bifurcated transformative paths"? If there is no established term, how exactly should we carve it: is the core issue the unreliability of cross-value testimony, or the deeper impossibility of comparing outcomes when both outcomes restructure the very comparator?
