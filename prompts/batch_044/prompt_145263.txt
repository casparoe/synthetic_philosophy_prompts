Since I recently became the long-term guardian for a family member who lacks autonomous decision-making capacity, I have been forced to navigate care priorities and resource allocations that affect her welfare in direct relation to that of others, which has made the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism feel personally urgent rather than merely academic. Please argue for the position that this objection fails because it conflates two distinct phenomena—structural substitution, in which one person’s loss is treated as directly fungible with and replaceable by another’s gain, and distributive optimization, in which aggregate welfare is maximized without any implicit claim that lives are interchangeable. In particular, defend the claim that utilitarian aggregation necessarily involves only distributive optimization; that structural substitution is an independent moral error blocked by deontological constraints against using persons merely as means rather than by any special metaphysical separateness of persons; and that once this distinction is drawn, Rawls’s analogy between interpersonal aggregation and the internal balancing of costs and benefits within a single life collapses, leaving utilitarianism vulnerable only to objections it can already accommodate. Lead with your bottom-line conclusion in the first sentence, then present the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question.
