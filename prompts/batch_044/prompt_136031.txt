I'm working through my commitments on the identity of indiscernibles using variations on Black's two-spheres scenario, and I'd like to know whether there's a principled account behind my verdicts or whether some have to be overturned.

Here are my judgments. (1) Two perfectly symmetric spheres in an otherwise empty universe are distinct objects, even with no relational or intrinsic differences. (2) If a uniform field introduces an imperceptible differential acceleration, they remain two objects, but I don't think the relational property is doing the individuating work. (3) If the spheres are quantum duplicates differing only in symmetric spin orientation, I'm inclined to say they present the same duplication problem—though I'm unsure whether quantum properties should count as individuating. (4) If a single sphere undergoes symmetric quantum branching into two causally separated copies, I think there are now two entities, even though neither was differentiated prior to branching. (5) If a simulation runs two identical object files with no external spatial framework but distinct memory addresses, I lean toward redundant representation of one object rather than genuine plurality.

Can these be captured by a single principle—say, a weakened Leibniz law that permits spatiotemporal or causal-historical relations but rejects pure intrinsic duplication—or do I need separate principles for physical and representational cases? In particular, case (4) seems to violate any continuity-based account, and case (5) seems to treat computational architecture as ontologically inert. Which verdict should I drop? Also, what would the best principle predict for two spheres that differ only in orientation relative to a distant, causally disconnected region?
