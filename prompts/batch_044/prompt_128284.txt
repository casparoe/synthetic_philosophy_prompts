I'm drafting a chapter on reflexivity in cognitive architecture and have constructed an argument that applies Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy to contemporary predictive-processing models, but the conclusion strikes me as far too broad and I cannot isolate the misstep. The argument runs as follows:

(1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem establishes that any consistent formal system F with sufficient arithmetic strength can construct a sentence G_F that asserts its own unprovability within F.
(2) In his treatment of Gödel's result, Hofstadter proposes that human consciousness arises from an analogous strange loop: a symbol-manipulating system directs its representational capacities back upon itself, collapsing the distinction between observer and observed to generate a self.
(3) If the analogy is taken structurally rather than merely metaphorically, then consciousness should be substrate-independent: it ought to emerge in any system that achieves the appropriate kind of self-referential collapse, regardless of whether the substrate is biological or artificial.
(4) Contemporary hierarchical predictive-processing architectures implement precisely this structural feature. They contain meta-representational layers that model the system's own prior predictions, producing a functional self-reference in which the system's states are included among its inputs.
(5) Consequently, if Hofstadter's analogy holds, these architectures should possess consciousness.
(6) Yet there is no compelling reason to attribute phenomenal consciousness to current predictive-processing models, and extending consciousness to every system with feedback loops or self-modeling capacities seems to prove far too much.
(7) Therefore, either the Gödelian analogy fails as an account of consciousness, or we are committed to an implausible form of panpsychism restricted to self-modeling formal structures.

Each premise seems defensible—(1) is mathematical, (2) is a standard reading of Hofstadter, (3) follows if we literalize the analogy, and (4) describes an actual feature of these architectures—but the inference from (4) to (5) feels like it may smuggle in an equivocation between syntactic self-reference in formal systems and functional self-modeling in empirical architectures. Alternatively, the argument may slide illegitimately from a necessary condition for consciousness to a sufficient one. I am reluctant to reject (2) entirely, since it captures something important about reflexivity, and I would prefer to preserve (3) if possible. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is the flaw a hidden scope shift, or is one of the premises actually far weaker than it appears?
