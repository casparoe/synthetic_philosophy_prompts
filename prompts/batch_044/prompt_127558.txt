In debates drawing on Dewey's experimentalism, democratic governance is frequently analogized to a scientific laboratory: policies function as hypotheses, electoral and deliberative mechanisms serve as peer review, and social experience provides the experimental conditions against which claims are tested. The analogy is used to defend the idea that democracy is not merely procedural but a mode of collective inquiry capable of genuine learning through trial and error. I want an audit of how far this holds.

First, lay out the correspondence explicitly — what in democratic practice plays the role of the hypothesis, the controlled variable, the reproducible trial, the null result, and the neutral observer? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for Dewey's specific purpose, not merely practical inconveniences. In particular, address whether the irreversibility of large-scale political action and the absence of any genuinely detached observer undermine the claim that democratic decision-making can be experimental in anything more than a metaphorical sense. Third, press a further conceptual tension: Dewey's rejection of the spectator theory of knowledge collapses the distinction between observer and observed, yet the laboratory model seems to presuppose exactly that separation (the scientist stands outside the apparatus). Does this mean that importing a "control group" into democratic policy is not just impractical but conceptually incoherent on Deweyan premises? Finally, assess whether the conclusion — that democracy can be a form of warranted assertibility in the same register as scientific inquiry — survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative weight all along. If the laboratory analogy collapses, is there a better one that preserves Dewey's anti-dualist commitments without smuggling in a false image of detached experimentation?
