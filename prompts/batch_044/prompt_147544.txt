I'm writing about a split in consciousness-research ethics: review boards now require a structured "phenomenal baseline" interview—in which subjects report the qualitative character of their experience in standardized terms—before any neural intervention, yet those same boards reject subjective testimony entirely when assessing harm, relying only on behavioral and neural metrics. The practice treats first-person reports as indispensable for calibrating the experiment but as epistemically worthless for evaluating damage. What view of mental content licenses that bifurcation? Does it presuppose that qualia are real but causally inert, that they are available to introspection but not to evidence, or something closer to the eliminativist suspicion—associated with Chase and Sanborn—that qualia form an incoherent posit and all first-person data are confabulated? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and specify what would have to change in review standards if the underlying stance were replaced by a uniformly realist or uniformly eliminativist view of consciousness.
