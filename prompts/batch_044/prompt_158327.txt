As a novelist working on a manuscript that experiments with form, I have been returning to Schopenhauer's argument in The World as Will and Representation that music is unique among the arts because it does not copy Platonic Ideas but expresses the Will itself—directly, without representational mediation. This seems to depend on music's lack of conceptual content: it is pure structure and affect, not a picture of anything in the phenomenal world. In my own work I have been writing extended passages of dialogue stripped of all narration, description, and stage direction—no attribution, no setting, only voices in collision. To my ear these passages approach something like instrumental music, because they have shed the representational apparatus that binds conventional prose to the realm of Ideas. This raises a question I cannot resolve: if Schopenhauer's criterion for direct Will-expression is the absence of representational mediation, then should not this kind of unmediated dialogue count as literature's equivalent to pure music—an art that expresses the Will without passing through Ideas? Or does Schopenhauer have a deeper reason, rooted in the nature of sound or in language's essential conceptuality, that would show my experiment is still trapped in representation? And if language is indeed inherently representational, does that imply that literature can never achieve the immediacy Schopenhauer reserves for melody, no matter how radically its form is stripped?
