A colleague of mine has been extending Simone Weil’s critique of collective organization—centered on suppression of individual moral judgment, enforced loyalty, substitution of collective passion for reason, and pursuit of power for its own sake—to various organizational forms, and her rulings so far are these: traditional mass parties with disciplined membership and party-line obligations are corrupt; temporary single-issue coalitions that formally dissolve after achieving their aim are permissible; trade unions with mandatory dues but open internal debate are acceptable with reservations; hierarchical religious orders that demand unconditional obedience to a superior are corrupt on essentially the same grounds as parties; and leaderless, temporary protest camps that refuse any formal structure or collective defense of outputs are unproblematic. She now asks about a new form: a decentralized deliberative assembly with no formal membership, rotating anonymous spokespeople, policy decisions made by quadratic voting weighted by stake, no ambition to capture state power, and automatic dissolution if active participation falls below a set threshold—though members are still expected to coordinate collective action and to defend the assembly’s outputs publicly. Predict her verdict if she wants consistency with her track record, identify the two or three rules that jointly fit her earlier judgments, name the closest analog among her prior cases, state what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice, and tell me what further organizational case would best distinguish the competing rules. Please answer in American English, and end with a single line: Verdict: [your verdict and the rule it favors].
