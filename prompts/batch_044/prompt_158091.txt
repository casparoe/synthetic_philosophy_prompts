For my A-level philosophy coursework, I am writing about Jean-Paul Sartre's ideas on radical freedom and bad faith, particularly how he distinguishes between consciousness as transcendence and the fixed characteristics of facticity. I am interested in applying these concepts to a situation that arose only after Sartre's lifetime: the growing use of predictive behavioural algorithms in education and employment, where machine-learning systems analyse accumulated data to forecast an individual's likely choices and present these forecasts as authoritative recommendations. Some people who rely on these predictions defend themselves by saying that the algorithm has revealed their 'true' nature or inherent limitations, and that they are therefore not genuinely free to act otherwise. I would like you to predict, please using British English, how Sartre might respond to this defence. Would he treat the appeal to algorithmic forecasting as a new form of bad faith—specifically, a way of disguising one's transcendence behind an apparently objective, technological facticity—or might he analyse it differently? Furthermore, please explain whether Sartre would still insist that the individual retains radical freedom despite the model's claimed accuracy, and how he might draw the line between using data as a practical tool and using it as an excuse to deny responsibility for one's choices.
