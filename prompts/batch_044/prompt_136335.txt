I am staging a counterfactual philosophical argument for a graduate seminar on colonial afterlives, and I want to avoid both anachronistic projection and generic liberal framing. John Stuart Mill spent his career at the East India Company, wrote ambivalently about Indian society, and defended a distinctive form of tutelary government for societies he judged unready for self-rule—while also insisting that imperial rule should ultimately dissolve into representative institutions. Suppose Mill had survived not merely into old age but into the 1940s, and had been invited to advise the Indian Constituent Assembly on a single institutional question: whether to retain, reform, or abolish the British colonial census classifications—specifically the categories of "Scheduled Castes" and "Scheduled Tribes"—as the constitutional foundation for compensatory discrimination and reserved political representation.

Drawing on his actual commitments—his utilitarian calculus about engineered social progress, his skepticism toward religious and customary authority as obstacles to rational administration, his belief that transitional institutions must eventually yield to uniform citizenship, and his occasional acknowledgment that British classification had hardened previously fluid social boundaries—reconstruct the position he would likely have defended. Would he have endorsed these classifications as a necessary, temporary remedy for entrenched historical disadvantage, consistent with his view that the state must actively reform backward societies? Or would he have rejected them as a dangerous reification of group distinctions that modern governance ought to erase? I want a reconstruction that treats Mill's views on India as a specific philosophical system rather than a placeholder for Victorian liberalism, and that identifies the precise point where his framework of tutelary improvement collapses when the agent of reform is no longer an imperial administrator but a democratically constituted post-colonial state.
