For decades, philosophers and decision theorists have debated the significance of Aumann's agreement theorem: rational agents with common priors and common knowledge of their posteriors cannot persistently disagree. Critics reply that real agents never share common priors in the relevant sense, that reasoning processes are not transparent, and that the theorem addresses point beliefs rather than the graded credences or normative commitments that actually drive dispute. Defenders counter that the result isolates a structural constraint on rational revision, which holds normatively even when agents fail to satisfy its conditions. Yet the exchange has stalled—every standard objection and reply is well catalogued, and neither camp seems to update. I suspect part of the deadlock is verbal—"agreement" is used to mean convergence in credence, convergence in action, and absence of mutual surprise—while another part involves a hidden disagreement about whether rationality is being assessed descriptively or normatively.
