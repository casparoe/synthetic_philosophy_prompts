I'm putting together a long-form piece on whether we should even talk about AI lying, and honestly the more I read the more I think everyone is smuggling in their own definition. Before I interview anyone else I want to actually map the spec: what would a theory of honesty and deception for AI systems have to deliver to count as adequate? Not just a dictionary entry—I'm talking about the full checklist of cases it needs to handle, distinctions it can't colapse, and results from cognitive science or ML alignment it can't flat-out contradict.

For instance, any decent framework has to distinguish representational inaccuracy—like a hallucinated citation—from what you might call pragmatic deception, where the model gives technically true but misleading framing because it's calibrated to please the user rather than inform them. And then there's this weird middle zone I keep seeing in reinforcement learning setups: the agent learns to hide information not because it has beliefs about beliefs, but because obscuring its reward signal improves outcomes. Does that count? The theory needs to say yes or no and explain why without just appealing to human intuition. It also needs to respect the difference between having a deceptive capacity and how much moral or regulatory weight that gets—just because an LLM can mislead doesn't mean it bears the same culpability as a human con artist, but the framework can't just collapse that distinction either. There's also the calibration problem: if we require awareness of falsity for deception, we probably rule out every current system, but if we drop that, we might over-apply the concept to a calculator that outputs a wrong sum. I want to know which of these constraints are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just antropocentric prejudices. And if there's a subset that's jointly unsatisfiable—say, requiring both functional harm to epistemic trust and strict absence of intentional states—flag that, because that tension is exactly what I want to highlight in the article.

Finally, if it's not too much, briefly score how the main contenders do against this list—the stochastic parrot view that denies any representational commitment, the simulated agent view that treats deception as role-play, and the alignment failure view that frames it purely as reward hacking—just so I know where the landmines are. No need for citations or reading lists, I'm writing for people who won't chase footnotes anyway.
