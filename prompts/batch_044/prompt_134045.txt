I serve as the senior curatorial-review officer for a regional Office of Active Memory. Each month I evaluate four to six proposals—public monuments, school-curriculum inserts, digitized archive releases, commemorative renamings—each presented as a way to honor, preserve, or learn from the past. I have seventy-two hours per proposal, must sign a written recommendation (approve, approve with conditions, reject, or escalate to the citizen-values panel), and work with staff who have no philosophy training. The recommendation must be defensible in public hearings and must not disguise value judgments as technical findings.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure, usable as a ordered flowchart in words, that operationalizes Nietzsche’s distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—specifically his claim that each mode serves life only when subordinate to present creative action, and becomes harmful when it dominates. The procedure should contain: (1) an initial classification test that identifies which mode or hybrid a proposal activates; (2) a life-service check that asks whether the proposal stimulates present capacity for action or instead paralyzes, ossifies, or breeds resentment; (3) a dominance check that detects pathological excess (e.g., monumental history becoming a narcotic imitation, antiquarian history becoming a suffocating accumulation, critical history becoming a destructive negation without reconstruction); (4) explicit stop-points where the procedure returns reject, approve-with-conditions, or escalate; and (5) clearly marked judgment nodes where the question is irreducibly one of values rather than classification—especially where critical history’s destructive work threatens a shared narrative that current civic cooperation requires.

The procedure must include hard cases and misfire warnings: where a proposal deliberately blends all three modes in unstable ratios, where digitization creates an antiquarian effect at monumental scale, and where staff disagreement about “present creative action” reveals that the framework smuggles in an unstated theory of flourishing. State explicitly what the procedure cannot do—e.g., generate a substantive account of human flourishing, adjudicate competing life-claims across different community groups, or resolve conflicts between historical fidelity and present solidarity.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases:

A. A proposal for a monumental bronze statue of a local industrialist celebrated for technological innovation, but documented in suppressing labor organizing. The asker expects approval with a critical plaque; show where the procedure might instead recommend rejection or heavy conditioning because the monumental effect risks narcotic imitation of exploitation.

B. A proposal to digitize and permanently preserve every municipal record from 1970 to 2020, justified as “complete antiquarian fidelity.” Show how the dominance check detects mnemonic saturation and where the procedure demands conditions or escalation rather than straightforward approval.

C. A critical-history museum exhibit documenting the city’s founding through the dispossession of an indigenous community. The asker’s preliminary instinct is to approve it, but apply the procedure rigorously so that it classifies the exhibit as potentially life-denying—undermining the shared civic identity required for current cooperative projects—and therefore recommends escalation or conditional framing. Show exactly where the procedure forces this unwelcome result, where the asker might legitimately overrule it, and where escalation to the values panel is mandatory rather than optional.

Keep the language precise but free of philosophical jargon where possible, avoid any request for bibliographies or citations, and flag every point where staff judgment cannot be eliminated.
