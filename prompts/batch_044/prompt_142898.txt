A friend who recently began using an astrology app defended her habit by invoking what she called Kepler’s maxim: the stars incline, but they do not compel. She interpreted it to mean that celestial configurations can shape temperament, probability, and disposition—perhaps through some kind of physical or harmonic resonance—without ever overriding free choice, so that consulting a birth chart is rational if one treats its claims as advisory rather than deterministic. I have since read enough to know that Kepler himself cast horoscopes for patrons, rejected the traditional twelve-house framework, spoke of planetary aspects in terms of musical-harmonic ratios, and insisted on a physicalist cosmology in which celestial bodies act on the sublunary world; yet I still cannot determine what precise thesis the slogan is meant to defend in her argument. Could you lay out the distinct precise principles that “the stars incline, but they do not compel” might be expressing? For each reading, specify: what kind of “inclination” is at work (causal, statistical, psychological, analogical, or harmonic), whose freedom or agency is preserved, at what explanatory level (individual voluntary action, general temperament, collective historical events, or biological development), and whether the “not compel” clause denies strict determinism, denies high predictive accuracy, denies moral obligation, or merely denies physical coercion. Indicate which readings are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which best align with Kepler’s documented combination of astrological practice and anti-determinist polemic. Say also which readings actually do the work my friend needs them to do—i.e., justify treating a natal chart as a rational guide to life choices—and which do not. Finally, please say what argument or discovery would change your verdict about Kepler’s intended meaning—say, if private correspondence showed he treated specific natal predictions as unavoidable—or where your analysis is most likely to fail, such as conflating his public concessions to patronage with his private scientific commitments.
