After a long and rather intense argument with a friend this evening about whether the experience-machine scenario genuinely undermines hedonism or merely exposes a hidden assumption about how values are anchored to worlds, I tried to crystallize my thoughts into a brief conceptual essay. I am not sure the architecture holds together, and I would value your critical comments on its coherence, its fidelity to the standard theories of well-being, and whether I have missed an obvious objection or a cleaner taxonomy.

The essay, provisionally titled “Counterfactual Anchoring and Modal Stability in Well-Being,” advances three claims. First, the familiar framing of the machine-choice assumes a unified notion of “contact with reality,” but different theories actually decompose this notion in different directions. For a pure hedonist, the salient feature is the causal history of mental states; one can therefore imagine a “historical hedonism” that rejects the machine not because reality is intrinsically valuable, but because phenomenally identical states caused by simulation lack the right etiological profile. This is not merely renaming a reality-condition; it is locating the constraint inside the satisfaction-condition of pleasure itself, so that a simulated pleasure is, by its causal origin, a different kind of state.

Second, standard desire-satisfaction theories, if they adopt a “present-state” criterion—counting a desire satisfied whenever its content matches the agent’s current mental representation—should actually endorse entering the machine, provided the simulation is stable. The interesting intermediate case is what I call a “counterfactual-stable” desire-satisfaction theory: a desire is genuinely satisfied only if, in nearby possible worlds where the belief-state grounding it is false, the desire is not counted as satisfied. For a machine-occupant, the desire “that I actually authored this novel” is satisfied in the actual simulated world, but fails in nearby glitch-worlds where the simulation breaks. Hence the satisfaction is modally unstable, and the theory counts it as not genuinely satisfied. This yields a split verdict: some desires survive entry, others do not, depending on whether their content carries an implicit counterfactual anchor.

Third, this suggests the machine is not a uniform diagnostic across theories. Objective-list theories reject it because they require real achievement. Pure hedonism accepts it. Present-state desire theories accept it. Only counterfactual-stable desire theories and historical hedonism generate nuanced, partial verdicts, with different subsets of goods surviving.

My friend objected that counterfactual-stable desire satisfaction simply collapses back into an objective-list view that includes “authentic achievement” as one item. I think the difference is structural: in my proposed view, the modal condition is internal to the satisfaction-relation of the desire, not an independent entry on a list of goods. But I may only be dressing the same intuition in technical clothing.

Please comment specifically on the following:
1. Is “historical hedonism” a coherent position, or is it just a disguised reality-condition? Does it save hedonism from the machine objection without changing the subject?
2. Does the counterfactual-stable criterion genuinely differ from including “real achievement” on an objective list, or is my taxonomy redundant?
3. Is there a more elegant “modal” theory of well-being—one that evaluates an agent’s well-being across a neighborhood of worlds rather than in a single world—that renders both of my intermediate categories unnecessary?

Treat this as a work in progress. I am not looking for bibliographic recommendations or citations; I want to know whether the conceptual architecture is sound and whether the distinctions I have drawn actually do explanatory work.
