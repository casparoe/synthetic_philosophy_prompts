Construct a branching taxonomy—rather than a flat list—of the interpretive positions on John Stuart Mill’s late socialism, focusing on the arguments advanced in the later editions of Principles of Political Economy and in Chapters on Socialism. The taxonomy must be built from three diagnostic questions that split the logical space, each phrased so that a reader can answer it from the conceptual structure of Mill’s own claims without already knowing the scholarly labels.

The three branching nodes are:
(1) Scope of social ownership: does Mill require the socialization of all capital and instruments of production, only of land and rent, or only of large-scale industrial enterprises?
(2) Role of competition within cooperative associations: is rivalry among workers abolished, retained as internal market-like competition, or retained only as inter-association exchange?
(3) Reconciliation with liberty: is the compatibility achieved through an institutional separation between socialized production and private consumption, through a transformation of human character that eliminates competitive egoism, or does an unresolved tension remain that makes socialism and liberty genuinely incompatible?

For every leaf defined by a combination of answers, provide either the name of an actual interpretive camp (for example, liberal-socialist synthesis, associationist gradualist, cooperative-market revisionist) or label it unnamed and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining why the distinction collapses on closer inspection. Identify any logically possible combination that yields an unoccupied leaf, and briefly diagnose whether that vacancy is due to incoherence with Mill’s explicit premises, normative unattractiveness, or mere neglect in the literature.

Finally, inject one niche but textually grounded sub-distinction—such as whether Mill’s endorsement of the labor theory of value in Book II survives his socialist revisions, or whether his stationary-state argument carries an implicit normative commitment to zero-growth socialism—to split one existing branch further, and show exactly where that sub-branch leads. The deliverable should require no bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; it must be fully usable by someone who understands Mill’s arguments but has no access to external sources.
