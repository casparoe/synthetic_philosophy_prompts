I am a novelist preparing a seminar presentation on a conceptual thesis that connects decision-theoretic structures to narrative form. Specifically, I argue that XOR blackmail, when analyzed through the framework of counterfactual mugging rather than standard causal or evidential decision theory, exhibits a formal symmetry with what I call aesthetic refusal: just as the rational agent refuses to pay the blackmailer in the counterfactual branch despite the causal irrelevance of that refusal, the author refuses to provide narrative closure that a reader demands, generating literary value precisely through an anticipated but unfulfilled commitment. The audience is an interdisciplinary group: two analytic philosophers who work on updateless decision theory and have defended variants of quantum decision derivations; two literary theorists familiar with narrative ethics but with no training in formal decision theory; and one cognitive scientist who studies belief revision under threat but is skeptical of analogies between epistemic and artistic rationality. Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging they are: what each subgroup will object to in their own vocabulary, which objections have genuine force and should be conceded up front, what reply must land for the analogy to survive, where that reply is weakest, and the one question about performative contradiction that I am hoping no one asks. Frame the prep sheet as a speaker's defense, not a survey.
