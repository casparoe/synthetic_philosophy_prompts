Below are notes for a short essay I am drafting on Keynes's ethical vision. The standard reading of his 1930 essay treats it as a macroeconomic forecast: once productivity solves scarcity, humanity will enjoy leisure and consumption. I want to argue instead that Keynes is advancing a specific moral ideal shaped by G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica and the Bloomsbury Group's aestheticism. On this reading, the good life is not defined by increased consumption but by states of consciousness valued for themselves—intimate friendship, artistic contemplation, and civilized enjoyment—made possible only when economic striving ceases to dominate. I further suggest that Keynes operates with an implicit satiation-threshold model: absolute needs can be satisfied, but relative needs (status, accumulation) become ethically pathological once abundance is reached, turning the love of money from a means into a mental morbidity. The problem is that Keynes seems to assume material abundance automatically dissolves this pathology, ignoring that positional goods can regenerate psychological scarcity even when material scarcity is absent. Consequently, his vision may depend on a prior collective moral re-education that economics itself cannot produce.

Please comment on these notes. First, is the Moorean-Bloomsbury interpretation historically defensible, or does it overread Keynes's personal philosophy into an economic prediction? Second, does his distinction between absolute and relative needs genuinely support a satiation-threshold model, or is that an anachronistic philosophical imposition? Third, is the objection from positional goods convincing as an internal critique, or does it rely on motivational assumptions that Keynes's framework would reject?
