Since I began drafting governance recommendations for a post-conflict reconstruction program, I have found myself repeatedly stalled in discussions about whether authoritarian regimes can claim legitimate authority solely on the basis of delivered prosperity and social order. The debate I encounter—whether in policy briefings or academic seminars—circles the same positions with remarkable consistency. Defenders point to sustained growth, infrastructure delivery, and low corruption in Singapore and, with necessary qualifications, in contemporary China, arguing that when democratic procedures yield paralysis or populist disorder, performance itself generates a distinctive kind of political obligation. Critics reply that legitimacy is not reducible to a utility function, that rights violations cannot be priced out by welfare gains, and that both the Singaporean and Chinese cases are either exceptional, structurally unreplicable, or purchased through repression that undermines the very metrics used to celebrate them.

Yet despite decades of this exchange, neither side has moved. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not mainly empirical—few dispute the growth figures or the authoritarian architecture—nor a simple axiomatic clash, but conceptual and structural: the defenders invoke a regime-specific notion of performance legitimacy, where compliance is earned through outputs regardless of input procedures, while the critics operate within a liberal-democratic framework in which participatory input is a precondition for any output claim to carry normative weight. But I am uncertain whether this captures the real fracture, or whether hidden disagreements are doing the work—perhaps about counterfactual baselines (whether democratic alternatives would have performed worse), about whether prosperity creates forward-looking obligations or merely backward-looking permissions, or about whether legitimacy should be assessed ex post, as an achieved state, or ex ante, as a procedural entitlement that must precede governance.

Can you diagnose the nature of this impasse? Is it verbal—hinging on incompatible uses of "legitimacy"; substantive—turning on whether welfare gains can ever compensate for the absence of consent; or are the sides answering different questions, one sociological (what makes people comply) and one moral (what makes rule rightful), under a single heading? Ground your diagnosis in what each side actually says: the growth-and-order defense, the rights-based rejection, and the comparative appeals to Singapore and China. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about counterfactual democratic trajectories, a formal argument about value incommensurability, or a reframing that dissolves the question rather than resolving it?
