As a retired physicist who has recently begun reading moral philosophy with some seriousness, I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in discussions that invoke what I take to be Confucian role ethics, though I am uncertain whether this specific configuration has an established name. The phenomenon is this: a person justifies a particular course of action—say, a parent favoring their child in a distribution of goods, or a ruler imposing a severe disciplinary measure—not by appealing to universal moral principles, nor by calculating aggregate welfare, but by insisting that the action is constitutive of the relational role they occupy. They maintain that being a father, an elder sibling, or a minister just is to act in this manner, and that the obligation derives its normative force directly from the identity of the relationship itself, rather than from any further justification. What distinguishes the pattern, however, is not merely this appeal to role, but a particular defensive maneuver that accompanies it: when an outsider challenges the action as unjust or harmful, the defender responds that the challenger lacks standing to judge, because the moral content of the role is partially constituted by practices and understandings that are opaque to those who do not inhabit the relationship. I have observed this in debates about familial loyalty, in political analogies that model authority on family structures, and in arguments about professional obligations that resist external audit.

I want to know whether there is an established concept for this precise combination—the derivation of a non-instrumental moral obligation from relational identity together with an epistemic closure claim that shields the role’s demands from evaluation by non-members. It seems adjacent to standard role morality and to the Confucian view that ethical life is constituted by relationships, yet it also differs from ordinary professional ethics, in which role obligations are typically justified by social function or implicit contract, and from simple moral particularism, which denies universal rules without necessarily invoking relational membership as a source of authority. If no precise term exists, I would like a proposed concept with clear boundaries: specifically, how it differs from conventional role ethics, from contextualist moral reasoning, and from appeals to authority grounded in technical expertise rather than in relational identity. I am not seeking a bibliography; I am seeking the conceptual carving—what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

In giving this analysis, please indicate where your reasoning is most vulnerable: what kind of counterexample or boundary case would show that this is not a distinct phenomenon but merely a variant of familiar role-based reasoning, and what consideration—perhaps regarding the persistence of certain universal moral constraints even within constituted roles—would lead you to revise your assessment.
