I've been retired from the lab for about three years now and mostly read philosophy to keep my brain from turning to mush. Lately I've been thinking about moral compromise — like when two groups with totally different value systems have to make a deal, and both actually come out better. My study group (mostly retired engineers and one ex-biologist) came up with this argument that feels like it proves way too much, but none of us can spot the leak.

Here's how it goes. Let's say there's a group we'll call the Thresholdists, who think harm is only okay if it crosses some magnitude line, and another group, the Proceduralists, who think harm is never okay if it violates a specific process, no matter how small. They strike a treaty: harm is allowed only if it crosses magnitude M AND only through procedure P. Each side gives up something, but both recieve protections they care about more — classic gains from trade through moral compromise.

Now the argument:
(1) For the treaty to be rational for both sides, each must be treating its core commitment as having an "exchange rate" against other goods.
(2) Once a commitment has an exchange rate, it isn't categorically binding; it's just expensive.
(3) Apply this to every value in the system. Even the meta-value "don't put prices on moral commitments" can be traded away if the package is big enough.
(4) So the framework of moral compromise is self-undermining: to enter it you have to abandon the idea of non-negotiable values, including the value of not entering such frameworks.
(5) But we see real compromises — constitutional conventions, interfaith agreements — that seem stable and not self-destructive.
(6) Therefore either these real-world compromises don't actually happen, or step (2) smuggles in something it shouldn't.

My gut says (2) is where it slips — maybe treating something as negotiable in practice isn't the same as saying it has a metaphyscial price tag. But I want to know exactly what the slide is: is it an equivocation between conventional weight and categorical status? A scope error? And if I keep (1) and reject (2), what do I owe? Do I have to say there's a hidden "moral exchange rate" for everything? Or can I defend the idea that some values are genuinely incommensurable but still allow for practical trade-offs?

Also, is there something wrong with how I'm applying the "gains from trade" idea to values that aren't like goods? I'm used to thinking about energy exchange in physical systems, and maybe I'm forcing a market metaphor onto ethics where it doesn't belong. Anyway, I'd love someone to walk me through where the chain breaks and what each repair costs.
