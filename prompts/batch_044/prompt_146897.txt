I’m giving the opening remarks this Saturday at a small gallery show in my neighborhood — it’s called "Truth is Beautiful" and it’s hung with work by local Black artists, half of them my old students. The crowd is going to be split right down the middle: on one side are people who’ve read Du Bois’s 1926 essay on art as propaganda and can talk about the part where he says all art is propaganda but only some admits it; on the other side are grandparents and cousins who came because they love the artist and don’t want a lecture. One piece is a massive abstract canvas — no bodies, no narrative, just color and form — made by a 22-year-old who told me she considers it propaganda because she thinks insisting on beauty, when everyone expects her to perform trauma, is exactly the kind of propaganda Du Bois was talking about. I need about four minutes of remarks. The philosophical idea has to actually shape the speech: I can’t just say the painting is beautiful and also political, because that lets both halves of the room off the hook. I need to say something that works for the experts — maybe engaging with Du Bois’s distinction between art that hides its purpose and art that wears it — without losing the family members. I should sound like an exhausted adjunct who taught the essay once and isn’t totally sure she agrees with Du Bois, not like I’m delivering a keynote. No citations, no reading list, please — and I accidentally spelled propaganda "propoganda" in my notes so if you can work that in as a little human touch that’d be great, but not required.
