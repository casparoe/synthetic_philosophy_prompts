The debate sparked by the “Quining Qualia” intuition pump—centered on Chase and Sanborn, the Maxwell House tasters who, after years of drinking the same brew, both report they no longer enjoy it—has been stalled since the late 1980s. In the setup, Chase insists that the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and that only his preferences have changed, while Sanborn insists that the coffee itself now tastes different to him. The qualia-skeptical reading takes this divergence to show that there is no further “what it is like” residue beyond these first-person functional and dispositional reports: once all behavioral, cognitive, and comparative judgments are fixed, nothing remains to be incoherent about. The opposing side responds that Dennett has merely redescribed two genuinely different phenomena—preference shift versus phenomenal shift—without demonstrating that the latter collapses into the former, and that the pump illicitly assumes that introspective access must be transparent and behaviorally manifest in order to be real. Despite decades of exchange, neither camp has moved: one side keeps insisting that any phenomenal claim that cannot be externally verified is either empty or self-defeating, the other keeps insisting that manifestability misses the point because the very concept of phenomenal character is introduced precisely to mark a non-functional remainder. I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is this stall a substantive metaphysical disagreement about whether phenomenal properties exist, a verbal dispute about what “tastes different” means in first-person reports, or two different projects—one about the ontology of consciousness, the other about the epistemology of self-knowledge—running under a single heading? More specifically, is there a hidden empirical premise about the reliability of introspective comparison standards, or are we facing bedrock intuitions about whether a private standard of sameness can be mistaken that neither side can argue for? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the pump, a formal result about the limits of first-person access, or an argument that no further progress should be expected because the disagreement is ultimately about which explanatory framework to adopt? Please ground your answer in what each side actually says about the Chase/Sanborn case, and do not simply list interpretations.
