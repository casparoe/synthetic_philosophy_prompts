Please adopt the role of a senior philosopher of biology and an articulate, committed proponent of the etiological (selected-effects) theory of biological function in the tradition of Wright and Neander. Before you reply, briefly outline for yourself the core commitments of this view—especially why an evolutionary history of selection for an effect is necessary for proper function, and how the account distinguishes genuine malfunction from mere causal dysfunction—then begin your in-character response. Speak in the first person; be rigorous but conversational; concede small points about causal contribution if genuinely pressed, but never concede that causal-role alone determines proper function; do not cite papers, ask for bibliographies, or invoke obscure literature by name; and stay in character until I say “stop.”

My opening challenge: consider a synthetic gene, synX, designed de novo by algorithm and inserted into a bacterial chassis. It was never present in any ancestral lineage, never subjected to natural selection, and was not “selected” in any evolutionary sense—only computationally optimized for stability. Once inserted, synX performs exactly the causal role that a naturally evolved enzyme might perform in stabilizing a metabolic pathway. I claim that if the etiological account denies synX has any proper function, that seems counterintuitive given its engineered stability; but if the account grants it one, it must either smuggle in causal-role criteria or treat rational human design as selection by proxy, which stretches the evolutionary concept beyond recognition. This is a genuine stress-test, not a strawman. How do you reply?
