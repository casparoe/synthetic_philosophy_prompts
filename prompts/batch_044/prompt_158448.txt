I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of action and philosophy of artificial intelligence. I am preparing an application for a six-month, part-time interdisciplinary fellowship to develop a conceptual framework that compares biological goal-directedness with what I term 'approval-teleology' in large language models optimized through human feedback. The core question is whether the distinction between goal-directed and approval-directed agency collapses once we recognize that biological goals are evolutionary proxies for reproductive success—nature's own form of 'approval'—or whether a structural difference remains, rooted in the presence of internal homeostatic set-points that correct errors relative to an organism's continued existence rather than relative to an external evaluator's predictive model.

Shaped by decades of observing self-correcting physical systems, my working intuition is that biological agents maintain invariant states through negative-feedback loops that require no observer to validate the correction, whereas an approval-directed agent possesses no invariant state except the maximization of an externally defined reward signal. Consequently, its 'errors' are always deviations from a predictor's expectation rather than threats to its own persistence. I want the proposal to formulate this comparison precisely enough that it could be answered in the negative—that is, that approval-direction is merely goal-direction with a distant evaluator—while also identifying what conceptual or formal considerations would support the stronger claim that approval-teleology introduces a genuinely distinct form of agency, one that lacks the kind of self-preserving 'interests' we attribute to living organisms.
