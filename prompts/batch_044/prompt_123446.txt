Since retiring from a career in biochemistry, I have been attending a philosophy discussion group that meets monthly to work through texts in ethics and philosophy of science. At our last meeting, two other members—Elena, who teaches literature, and Marcus, who was trained in economics—fell into a prolonged disagreement about Kant’s well-known position on lying, particularly the scenario in which one might mislead a murderer searching for a hidden friend. I am trying to understand the logical structure of their exchange rather than take sides, and I would appreciate a careful point-by-point adjudication.

Here is the exchange as I recorded it in my notes.

Elena began by stating that Kant’s prohibition on lying is absolute and derives from the form of the maxim, not from any calculation of harm. She said that to universalize the maxim “I will lie whenever it produces a good outcome” is self-defeating, because if everyone did so truthful declaration would become impossible, undermining the very practice that makes lying effective. She added that the murderer-at-the-door case is constructed precisely to show that emotional urgency and catastrophic consequences cannot override a perfect duty.

Marcus agreed that universalization is central, but argued that Elena was missing a deeper feature of Kant’s view: his rejection of what philosophers now call moral luck. Marcus claimed that for Kant, the moral quality of an action depends entirely on the agent’s maxim and will, not on outcomes that lie outside her control. Therefore, if one lies to the murderer and succeeds in saving the friend, one has not earned any moral credit beyond the ordinary value of the maxim of beneficence; if one lies but the murderer sees through the deception and kills the friend anyway, one is not blameworthy for the death, only for the corruption of one’s own rational nature through the lie. Marcus concluded that this shows outcomes are not merely overridden but conceptually irrelevant to moral assessment.

Elena replied that Marcus had imported an anachronistic framework. She said Kant’s concern is not with “moral luck” in the modern sense but with the conditions of rational agency. The contradiction in the maxim of lying is formal: it destroys the possibility of truthful communication as such, which is a condition of rational interaction. She argued that Marcus’s framing actually weakens Kant’s position, because if the wrongness of lying is purely internal—corrupting one’s own rational nature—then the presence of the murderer is entirely redundant. One would be equally wrong lying to save a friend in a scenario with no external threat at all. Elena insisted that this does not show Kant is implicitly testing outcomes; it shows that perfect duties bind regardless of circumstance.

Marcus then pressed a more subtle point. He said that if Elena is correct—if the murderer’s presence is genuinely redundant to the wrongness of the lie—then Kant’s choice to frame the problem with an active, malevolent interlocutor is philosophically puzzling. Marcus suggested that in Kant’s Doctrine of Right, lying is wrong not merely because it corrupts the liar’s rational nature, but because it violates the listener’s right to be treated as an end in himself, as a being capable of rational assent. From this, Marcus inferred a niche distinction: if the murderer, by his murderous intent, has already forfeited his standing as a rational interlocutor entitled to truthful treatment, then addressing him with a falsehood does not violate his dignity in the same way it would violate an innocent person’s. Marcus admitted he was not certain whether Kant’s texts support this distinction, but he maintained that it offers a more coherent reading of why Kant discusses the murderer specifically rather than discussing lying in the abstract.

Elena dismissed this as unsupported. She said that Kant never conditions perfect duties on the moral desert of the recipient; to do so collapses the categorical structure into a consequentialist calculus about who deserves respect. She argued that Marcus’s “forfeiture” move transforms Kant into a proto-utilitarian who weighs the rational status of parties, and that this betrays the entire critical project.

They turned to me and asked me to referee. Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move? Specifically: Is Marcus correct that Kant’s position involves a rejection of moral luck, or has he mischaracterized the formalist point? Is Elena right that Marcus has smuggled consequentialist reasoning back in through his internal-corruption framing? Does Marcus’s final claim about the murderer forfeiting interlocutor status have any support within Kant’s broader framework—particularly in the distinction between perfect and imperfect duties, or between the Doctrine of Virtue and the Doctrine of Right—or is it an unsupported injection? And who, if anyone, is missing a relevant consideration about the relationship between perfect duties and conflicts with beneficence? Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am working without access to a research library and wish to assess these arguments on their philosophical merits alone.
