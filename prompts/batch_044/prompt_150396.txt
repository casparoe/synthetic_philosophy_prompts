I am currently helping design the interpretive framework for a memorial project that must navigate between honest remembrance of a defeated political cause and the risk of mythologizing it into a stable national narrative. Given limited research and curatorial resources over the next three years, I want a defended conceptual agenda rather than a survey: among the unresolved issues in how societies should relate to lost causes—specifically, whether there is a coherent distinction between preserving defeat as an open moral wound and domesticating it into a closed historical episode; whether a defeated movement can retain any normative authority without fueling revanchism; and whether collective mourning requires specific formal or aesthetic conditions to avoid sliding into myth—which should be resolved first? For each, justify its rank by explaining which other questions depend on its answer, how tractable it is for conceptual rather than empirical work, and what kind of progress in the next few years would cause you to reorder them. I am especially interested in whether framing defeat as either an enduring ethical claim or a concluded historical event is the upstream issue on which the rest depend, and which downstream implications for policy or public memory are overrated or do not actually follow.
