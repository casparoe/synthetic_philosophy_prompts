I'm writing this after a late-night session with my study group for our philosophy of mind seminar, where we've been working through Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. I tried to formalize an objection that connects it to Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—specifically the problem of radical translation illustrated by the gavagai example—and now the conclusion looks like it dissolves not just the Cartesian ego but personal identity altogether. I want to know where the slide happens, because I think I want to keep Dennett's anti-substantialist picture without ending up with radical narrative chaos.

Here is the chain: (1) For Dennett, the self is not a metaphysical substance but a center of narrative gravity: the abstract point around which a cluster of stories about beliefs, intentions, and experiences organize themselves. (2) For such a center to be more than a convenient fiction, there must be some constraint that determines which stories genuinely belong to the cluster and which are merely attributed from outside. (3) Quine's indeterminacy thesis holds that for any body of behavioral and linguistic evidence, there exist multiple translation manuals that are empirically equivalent yet assign radically different belief-contents; there is no fact of the matter beyond behavioral fit. (4) Applying this to personal narrative: if belief-attribution is radically underdetermined by all available evidence, then the stories that constitute my narrative center have no fixed content, and the same life-behavior supports incompatible autobiographical clusters. (5) Therefore, without an external constraint, the center of narrative gravity is itself underdetermined—there is no unique narrative that is mine. (6) One might object that first-person authority supplies the missing constraint: I know my own story from the inside, fixing which beliefs and intentions belong to my cluster. (7) But first-person reports are themselves linguistic behavior, and Quinean indeterminacy applies to the interpretation of those reports just as it applies to any other utterance; there is no privileged language of thought immune to radical translation. (8) Consequently, first-person authority cannot anchor the narrative either. (9) If neither third-person interpretation nor first-person report can constrain the cluster, then for any individual there are infinitely many equally valid centers of narrative gravity that are empirically indistinguishable. (10) This proves too much: it does not merely reject the Cartesian ego, but eliminates any principled distinction between one person's narrative and another's, since the clusters can overlap or diverge arbitrarily with no fact of the matter. (11) Yet Dennett clearly wants to preserve personal identity, moral responsibility, and the distinction between self and other within his narrative framework.

My question is not whether Dennett is right about the self, but which step in this specific chain is the weak link—whether it is an equivocation between linguistic translation and belief-fixation around step (3) to (4), an unjustified extension of Quinean arguments to first-person cases around step (7), or an overstatement in step (10) about what underdetermination actually implies for identity. If I have to choose, I would rather preserve Dennett's anti-substantialist picture in (1) and the Quinean challenge in (3) than accept (10). But I need to know exactly what I am paying to reject whichever step is the actual error: does saving the self require giving up radical translation, giving up first-person transparency, or recognizing that narrative coherence itself functions as a non-behavioral constraint that Quine's argument never addressed?
