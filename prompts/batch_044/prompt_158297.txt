Question:
Wittgenstein’s later reflections on religious belief are often interpreted as holding that assertions such as “God exists” do not function as empirical or metaphysical hypotheses that could be confirmed or disconfirmed by evidence. Rather, they are said to operate within a distinctive language-game that is embedded in a particular form of life. Evaluate the claim that, on this interpretation, religious belief is thereby rendered immune to every form of rational criticism, on the grounds that any objection raised from outside the religious framework necessarily misunderstands the internal grammar of religious discourse.

Student’s answer:
Wittgenstein clearly separates religious belief from scientific belief. In science we test claims against evidence, but when a believer says “God exists” this is not like saying “there is a planet beyond Neptune”; it expresses a total orientation to life and a framework for judging what matters. So the statement is not false just because science cannot verify it—it belongs to a different language-game altogether. This means religious belief is protected from rational criticism, since anyone who criticises it is simply playing the wrong game, like trying to use chess rules in checkers. Wittgenstein treats religious pictures as regulating our lives rather than reporting facts, so believers never need to defend their commitments with reasons; the framework is self-contained. This position is essentially the same as logical positivism, which also said religious statements are meaningless unless empirically verified, except that Wittgenstein saves them by placing them in a private box where verification does not apply. Because the form of life is private, outsiders cannot genuinely understand or assess it from within.
