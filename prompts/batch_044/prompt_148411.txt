Consider the debate over why practices such as dueling and chattel slavery eventually cease to be widespread: some maintain that moral reasoning becomes causally effective only when it coincides with shifts in material interest, while others maintain that transformations in collective honor—what counts as shameful versus admirable—can drive abandonment even when interest points the other way. Focus on the conceptual distinction between treating honor as an internalized normative commitment and treating it as a strategic coordination equilibrium sustained by reputation costs and the enforcement behavior of third parties. Explain why, under the equilibrium interpretation, the decline of dueling is not best understood as either a change in private moral sentiment or as an exogenous shock to material interests, but rather as a restructuring of the payoff matrix that occurs when a state monopolizes legitimate violence and thereby alters the social cost of withholding recognition or granting it. Then address the broader classificatory problem this raises for so-called moral revolutions: if a practice collapses at the level of first-order compliance—people stop engaging in it—while second-order normative belief persists—many still regard refusal as dishonorable—should we classify the shift as argument-driven, interest-driven, or honor-driven? Identify the specific conceptual mistake involved in assuming that the survival of a normative belief necessarily requires the survival of the corresponding practice, and explain how that mistake distorts assessments of whether moral reasoning can ever be efficacious independently of institutional restructuring.
