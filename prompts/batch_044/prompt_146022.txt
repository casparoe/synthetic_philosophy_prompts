I'm working through a problem set for my metaphysics course on fundamentality and grounding, and I built the following argument, which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion — that there are no fundamental entities whatsoever — seems far stronger than its premises warrant. Here are the steps:

(1) If A grounds B, then A is strictly more fundamental than B.
(2) Grounding is irreflexive, transitive, and well-founded: no infinite descending chains of immediate grounding.
(3) Suppose there are at least two distinct fundamental entities, F₁ and F₂.
(4) Then there obtains the fact D: F₁ is not identical to F₂.
(5) D is not itself one of the basic building blocks, so D is not fundamental.
(6) Therefore D is grounded in F₁ and F₂ together.
(7) Now consider the grounding fact G: D is grounded in {F₁, F₂}.
(8) G involves the grounding relation R applied to F₁, F₂, and D.
(9) If R is fundamental, then G contains a fundamental component (R) alongside a grounded component (D), which is incoherent on the assumption that fundamentality is all-or-nothing for any given fact.
(10) So R is not fundamental; R is itself grounded in some R₁.
(11) By parallel reasoning, the fact that R is grounded in R₁ requires R₂, and so on, generating an infinite ascending chain R, R₁, R₂, ...
(12) Well-foundedness, generalized to prohibit infinite dependence in either direction, rules this out.
(13) Hence our supposition in (3) fails: there are not two distinct fundamental entities.
(14) By parity, there cannot be exactly one either, since self-identity generates an analogous regress.
(15) Therefore there are no fundamental entities at all.

Where does this argument go wrong? I am especially reluctant to give up (1) or (2), and I want to preserve the possibility of plural fundamental entities. I am willing to reject (9) if that is the weakest link, but I want to understand what rejecting it costs — does it force me to treat the grounding relation as non-ontological, or to allow "mixed" fundamental/grounded facts? Also, please say what would change your mind: which specific premise, if shown false or misinterpreted, would lead you to accept the nihilist conclusion in (15), and where is your own diagnosis most vulnerable?
