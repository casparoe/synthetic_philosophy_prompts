Compare two proposed resolutions to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—the objection that bodily, intellectual, and malicious pleasures appear to share no common phenomenal quality. On one side is the classic uniform-hedonic-tone thesis: all pleasures contain an identical felt quality, analogous to a single sensory modality, that distinguishes them from pains and neutral states. On the other side is a structural-functional convergence view, according to which pleasures are unified not by phenomenology but by jointly satisfying three conditions: they increase the subjective probability of repetition, they reduce cognitive load, and they resist voluntary suppression. Which approach better preserves hedonism as a substantive theory of well-being without collapsing it into preference-satisfaction or behaviorism? Specifically, address whether the structural-functional view can still honor the intuition that malicious pleasure and gustatory pleasure differ radically in what they are like, or whether it abandons the phenomenological core that originally made hedonism attractive.
