Been noticing a pattern in formal philosophy and alignment-adjacent arguments that I can't quite place. Someone builds a model — say, of all possible preference orderings or every state-space of a physical system — inside a formal framework that's necessarily countable: finite symbols, computable rules, etc. The model then proves that there are "uncountably many" entities in the domain, or that some property holds continuously across an infinite range. People treat these results as telling us something about the actual, real-world domain. But from outside the model, every single "uncountable" object is just a countable encoding — a string of symbols, a node in a computable structure. Inside the model there's no bijection with the naturals, so technically the result is correct, but it's correct about the represention, not about whatever was being represented.

I know Skolem's paradox covers the set-theory version — ZFC proving uncountable sets exist while having a countable model — but I'm after whether there's a broader concept for this representational mismatch, especially when it occures outside logic proper. Is there a name for the error of treating internal structural richness (uncountability, continuity, etc.) in a formal model as if it reflects richness in the target domain, given that the model itself is constructed from smaller stuff? If not, can you coin one with clear boundaries: distinguish it from ordinary model-theoretic limitation, from just being wrong about the physics, and from Skolem's paradox narrowly construed? I want to know exactly when this is happening and when it's a different mistake altogether.

Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — either the established term or your coined one with its definition — then give the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate the question, and don't give me a reading list.
