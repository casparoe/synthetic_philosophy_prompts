So I basically buy Sen's argument that famines are political crimes, not natural disasters—Bengal in '43, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward all show entitlement collapse rather than just crop failure. But I'm pushing a weirder version: I think the real crime in each case wasn't only blocking food access, but actively supressing the infromation that would have revealed the collapse in real time. So my view is that famine is always partly an epistemic crime—states don't just starve people, they starve the data that starvation is happening. What would actually count against this? I don't want generic drought objections; I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Would a case where a well-meaning government genuinely lost track of regional supply due to chaos (not supression) kill the view, or just bruise it? And if someone showed that British officials during the Irish famine actually knew and tried to act but were structurally blocked, is that a concession I have to make, or can I absorb it? Basically, map out what I, as a defender, should admit hurts versus what I can dismiss.
