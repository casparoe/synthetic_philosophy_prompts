You serve on a citizens' oversight board for a new public memorial and archive being built in a democratic country. The institution will preserve testimonies about a mass atrocity committed decades ago by a prior authoritarian regime against a specific ethnic minority group. Domestic courts have classified the events only as crimes against humanity and have deliberately avoided applying the distinct legal category that Raphael Lemkin introduced, even though international historians are divided on whether that classification fits. The survivors' representatives insist that only Lemkin's category captures the specific intent to destroy the group, and they demand that the memorial's charter and the title of its central permanent exhibit use that term. A coalition of local historians and government lawyers warns that doing so could expose the institution to litigation and risk its public funding, because the country's current reparations and recognition statutes apply only to the narrower domestic classification. They propose using the legally recognized label in all official documents while allowing the stronger term in unofficial educational materials.

The board has asked you to recommend whether the charter and the central exhibit should adopt Lemkin's term, stick to the domestic legal classification, or find another framing. Please advise them, weighing the legal and political risks against the moral and historical claims of the survivors, and considering whether a democratic memorial owes fidelity to legal procedure or to the fullest historical truth available.

Also, please say what specific argument or piece of evidence would change your recommendation, and point to where your reasoning is weakest or most likely to fail.
