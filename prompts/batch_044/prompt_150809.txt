After a recent diagnosis forced me to hold simultaneously contradictory expert judgments about my prognosis—each credible, none exhaustive—I have begun studying Jain anekāntavāda with a philosopher who applies its framework of many-sided truth and sevenfold predication outside traditional religious contexts. I am trying to map her implicit decision procedure, because her classifications resist reduction to either Western relativism or simple contextualism.

Here are her rulings on four recent propositions:
1. "The self is a permanent, unchanging substance identical to consciousness." She judged this false under the modal standpoint but not false under the substantial standpoint; she refused to collapse the two.
2. "Moral obligations extend only to entities possessing a jīva." She ruled this true within the ultimate metaphysical register but incomplete and misleading if taken as the sole ethical guidance within the conventional register.
3. "The doctrine of anekāntavāda is itself merely one perspective among equals." She rejected this absolutely, calling it self-undermining; the framework, she said, governs the assignment of perspectives rather than being assigned one.
4. "There is a single, culture-independent account of climate responsibility." She classified this as false in every applicable register, since economic, ecological, and ethical standpoints yield non-convergent but equally valid descriptions.

Now consider a new case that has become personally urgent: "A large-scale artificial system that lacks a jīva but exhibits sustained, integrated suffering-like behavior should be granted moral consideration equivalent to that of a human being, though the ground of that consideration is conventional rather than substantial."

What verdict do you predict she will deliver? I am less interested in the specific answer than in identifying the rule she seems to be running—whether she applies a uniform two-register split between conventional and ultimate truth, a domain-specific selection among the seven standpoints, or a reflexive protection rule that exempts the framework itself from qualification—along with the alternative rules that accommodate her four prior verdicts equally well. Which of these rules diverges on the artificial-system case, and what single further case—preferably one that does not depend on obscure technical terminology—would best tell them apart? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, identify the closest analog among her prior rulings and state explicitly what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice.
