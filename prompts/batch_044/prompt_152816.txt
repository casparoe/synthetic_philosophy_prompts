After retiring from thirty years in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have begun reading philosophy in earnest, and I find myself drawn to Rorty’s neopragmatism and antirepresentationalism precisely because they seem to challenge the implicit metaphysics I once took for granted in the laboratory. I want to write an essay that uses my scientific experience to probe a tension I think Rorty does not fully resolve, but I need feedback on whether I am misreading him or whether the tension is genuinely internal to his view.

The core idea is this. Rorty rejects the notion that scientific theories are representations of an independent reality and treats vocabularies instead as tools for prediction and control. This is persuasive for high-level theoretical frameworks—quantum interpretations that share empirical predictions but differ in ontology—where representation seems genuinely optional. But in experimental practice, we constantly refine models of specific systems, such as crystal lattices, not merely to improve predictive accuracy but because we discover that an earlier model misrepresented the actual symmetry of the structure. If representation is entirely replaced by pragmatic utility, then misrepresentation collapses into being less useful for certain purposes. Yet there seems to be a normative difference between a model that is false about lattice symmetry and one that is merely computationally inefficient. I want to argue that antirepresentationalism cannot accommodate this normative distinction without smuggling back in a correspondence relation, though Rorty might reply by distinguishing predictive robustness from structural fidelity and treating the latter as itself a pragmatic virtue—specifically, as the virtue of enabling precise intervention rather than description.

My essay is structured around three moves: first, reconstructing Rorty’s argument against the mirror metaphor through the case of theoretical underdetermination; second, analyzing how experimental refinement relies on a notion of structural fidelity that resists reduction to pragmatic success; and third, assessing whether treating structural fidelity as an instrumental virtue resolves the tension or merely renames it. I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between predictive robustness and structural fidelity is philosophically substantive or merely a confusion born from my scientific habits, and whether an honest critic must concede that the experimental example fails to challenge Rorty. Could you comment on the accuracy of my reconstruction, the interest of the central distinction, and the strength of the third move? I am writing for an audience of philosophers who know Rorty well but are not specialists in philosophy of science, and I want to avoid reducing scientific practice to an illustration rather than letting it genuinely complicate the philosophical claim.
