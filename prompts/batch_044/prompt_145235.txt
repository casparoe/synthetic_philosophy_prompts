Consider the long-running dispute in philosophy of mind about whether knowing-how is genuinely distinct from knowing-that. By now both sides accept the standard empirical landscape: experts in domains like chess, surgery, and fluent language use display rapid, largely non-conscious competence; they can often articulate some governing rules; and their performance can be partly modeled with propositional representations and procedural schemas. The reductionists hold that a complete account—one that specifies the relevant propositional contents together with their practical mode of access and execution conditions—leaves no residual explanatory work for a separate category of knowing-how. The anti-reductionists grant that such propositional-cum-procedural descriptions capture the structural backbone of expertise, but they maintain that there remains an irreducible practical grasp: an ability to adapt to genuinely novel perturbations that is not identical to, and not fully explained by, the possession of any set of that-statements, however richly indexed by context, modality, or first-person access. Despite decades of exchange, the debate has barely moved. Both camps agree on the neuroscience, both concede that articulation and non-articulation come in degrees, and neither can cite an experiment that the other must accept as decisive. What kind of disagreement is this? Is it a verbal dispute about the honorific knowledge, a substantive but hidden disagreement about what counts as explanatory completeness in cognitive science, a metaphysical clash over whether practical abilities require a non-propositional ontological category, or a conflict of bedrock intuitions about the unity of mind—intuitions that neither side can defend without begging the question? Given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, or a formal result that should be able to resolve it? Or is the impasse a sign that the original question was ill-posed, and if so, what reframed question should replace it?
