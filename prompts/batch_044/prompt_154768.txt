I'm constructing a research agenda for elder-care law reform centered on the authority of earlier selves through advance directives. Rather than treating directives as static contracts, I want to examine them through the lens of "diachronic autonomy" — normative authority that extends across periods of cognitive change rather than being fixed to a single moment of choice. Within this frame, three open problem-clusters stand out. Cluster A is the boundary problem: whether the loss of authority over one's later self is sharp or graded along a continuum of cognitive decline, and what metaphysical or normative criteria should fix any boundary. Cluster B is the conflict problem: when a later self with diminished capacity expresses stable, apparently authentic preferences that contradict the directive — for instance, contentment in states the earlier self explicitly feared — whether present autonomy, present well-being, or relational continuity should prevail, and whether the answer depends on resolving Cluster A first. Cluster C is the institutional translation problem: how legal systems can apply answers to A and B without requiring courts to make ad hoc judgments about personal identity or to weigh incommensurable values case by case.

I want a defended agenda with explicit trade-offs between importance and tractability, not a survey or a bibliography.
