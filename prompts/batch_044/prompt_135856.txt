I retired from biophysics research several years ago and have since devoted myself to philosophy with the same rigour I once applied to experimental design. I currently sit on an advisory panel for a regional health authority that must allocate a fixed research budget between two proposals: an experimental gene-therapy protocol that clinicians claim would eliminate near-maximal chronic neuropathic suffering in a single patient, and a population-level preventive programme that would reduce moderate, chronic discomfort for roughly eight thousand individuals. Aggregate quality-adjusted life-years favour the latter only marginally, yet one of my fellow panellists—a health economist—insists we should fund the single-patient therapy because suffering intensity creates a non-linear utility curve, so each unit of expenditure there yields orders of magnitude more well-being than in the diffuse case. He compares our reluctance to refusing to rescue one drowning swimmer because we might instead distribute ten thousand swimming lessons, and argues that if maximisation is our principle, arithmetic should override intuition. Having spent decades calibrating instruments and trusting linear approximations only within validated ranges, I find the scaling of subjective experience across persons conceptually suspect rather than merely counter-intuitive, but I cannot pinpoint whether the error lies in the empirical model of suffering, the normative aggregation rule, or a hidden metaphysical assumption about the comparability of mental states.

Is his argument fair? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then set out your reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question. I should particularly value an analysis that separates empirical claims about pain intensity, structural features of distributive ethics, and any unstated premises about interpersonal utility comparison.
