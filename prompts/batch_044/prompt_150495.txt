I'm trying to map the implicit historiography of a colleague in our longtermist history reading group. She takes Fernand Braudel's framework seriously but refuses to be a pure structuralist, and I'm unsure exactly where she draws the line between the longue durée and the event. Here are her verdicts on four cases we've discussed.

First, the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century: she treats the 476 collapse as surface foam, attributing the decline instead to the multi-century aridification of North African grain provinces, progressive soil salinization in Italy, and the slow fiscal divergence between east and west.

Second, the Meiji Restoration of 1868: she insists Japan's rapid modernization was structurally predetermined by its insular geography, its domestic scarcity of coal coupled with accessible maritime trade routes, and the Tokugawa information-gathering infrastructure; the Perry expedition and the oligarchs were merely the trigger that released accumulated structural pressure.

Third, the abandonment of the Norse Eastern Settlement in Greenland around 1450: she explains the evacuation primarily through the long-term cooling of the North Atlantic and the collapse of the European ivory-export market, treating the specific decision to leave and the failure of the final rescue mission as epiphenomenal responses.

Fourth, the rapid agricultural shift from rice to sorghum in parts of the West African Sahel during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: she attributes the transition mainly to the long-term southward migration of the monsoon boundary and localized soil exhaustion, downplaying the role of specific merchant networks or the political choices of post-Mali imperial successors.

Next case: the sudden collapse of the Classic Maya lowland political system around 900 CE, particularly the rapid depopulation of Tikal and Calakmul within roughly two generations, coinciding with severe multidecadal drought but also with the documented cessation of dynastic monument-building and a sharp increase in inter-polity warfare. Would she say the drought and long-term soil depletion in the Petén were the real cause, with the warfare and dynastic failure being surface expressions of structural stress? Or would she treat the particular institutional fragility of divine kingship and the monument-building ritual economy as the necessary mechanism that turned drought into collapse?

More generally, what are the competing rules that jointly accommodate her four earlier verdicts? One is a hard Braudelian geographic-climate determinism, in which slow physical structures always provide the primary explanation. Another is an institutional path-dependence view, in which long-term structures shape outcomes but only through the specific rigidities they produce—so that the Tokugawa bureaucracy, the Roman fiscal system, or the Maya ritual economy are the actual explanatory intermediaries. Are there further rules that fit just as well?

Give me your best guess at her verdict on the Maya case, with the reasoning that distinguishes the rules. And tell me what further case—perhaps one involving societies facing similar environmental stress but with different institutional architectures—would best adjudicate between geographic-climate determinism and institutional path-dependence.
