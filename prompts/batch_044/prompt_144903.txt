I'm preparing material for an interdisciplinary workshop on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I'd like you to write a short story that explores the intentional stance—specifically, the conditions under which we attribute beliefs and desires to non-human systems—without relying on standard science-fiction tropes about robot consciousness or sentience. The setting should be a near-future administrative body, the Office of Cognitive Attribution, tasked with adjudicating whether autonomous decision-making systems embedded in urban infrastructure can be described as holding beliefs for legal and ethical purposes. Your protagonist should be a junior auditor who applies predictive, rational-agent modeling criteria to determine whether a distributed forecasting model's behavior is best explained by attributing to it representational states, while simultaneously navigating her mother's early-stage dementia, whose goal-directed but increasingly fragmented actions force her to apply the very same interpretive framework to a deteriorating human mind.

The narrative should dramatize the tension between viewing belief-attribution as a pragmatic strategy we adopt toward complex systems versus viewing it as tracking an internal mental reality, especially when the same framework is applied to both synthetic infrastructure and human cognition. Please avoid explicit philosophical exposition or named theories in dialogue; let the conflict emerge through the auditor's procedural choices, her mother's ambiguous actions, and a specific incident in which the infrastructure model's prediction error causes a public harm that could be described either as a mechanical malfunction or as a false belief about road conditions. The story should leave open whether the parallel between human and synthetic attribution reveals a unified interpretive science or merely exposes the fragility of our cognitive habits, and it should conclude not with a resolution but with the auditor filing two incompatible reports—one for the system, one for her mother's care plan—using identical language in both.
