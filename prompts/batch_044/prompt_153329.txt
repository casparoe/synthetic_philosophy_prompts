Read the madeleine episode in Marcel Proust's Swann's Way through the lens of Daniel Dennett's "quining" argument and the Chase–Sanborn coffee-taste scenario. In Dennett's thought experiment, a drinker's taste qualia shift gradually over years without his noticing, suggesting that judgments about one's own experience can dissociate from the experiences themselves and undermining any model of direct, authoritative introspective access. Proust presents Marcel's bite into a tea-soaked madeleine as triggering an involuntary, bodily memory of Combray: the taste is described as unmistakably private and as containing the past within it. I want a reading that treats both the philosophy and the novel seriously. Does Proust's scene presuppose a "Cartesian theater" of taste—an inner display of raw qualia that Marcel reads with perfect transparency—or can it be reconstructed in heterophenomenological terms, as Marcel constructing a retrospective narrative about a taste he only recognizes as significant after the memory arrives? Identify specific moments in the episode where the phenomenology seems to resist Dennett's dissociation—perhaps in the bodily shock, the temporal compression, or the claim that the past is "contained" in the present sensation. At the same time, say where the novel risks reducing the experience to an illustration of either direct access or constructed judgment. Finally, ask whether the madeleine scene contributes something to the philosophy of mind that the Chase–Sanborn argument misses: for instance, whether narrative coherence or temporal recognition can serve as a constraint on how radically qualia judgments can be quined, or whether involuntary memory introduces a form of self-knowledge that is neither purely phenomenal nor purely theoretical.
