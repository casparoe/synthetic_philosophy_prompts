I've been thinking about Glaucon's challenge in Plato's Republic—specifically the story of the ring of Gyges, which lets its wearer become invisible and thus escape all social punishment for injustice. After discussing it in my philosophy course, I've come to hold a particular defense of morality against this challenge: I think the ring doesn't actually show that justice is only instrumentally valuable, because it implicitly assumes that the only costs of injustice are external, such as reputation, punishment, or reward. Instead, I hold that for a person who has genuinely internalized justice—not merely as a habit of obeying rules, but as a structural feature of their practical reasoning—invisible wrongdoing would generate an immediate, self-directed collapse of coherence. The unjust act wouldn't just be hidden from others; it would be incompatible with the agent's own framework for making decisions, producing a kind of internal friction that is experienced as a direct loss of practical identity rather than as conventional guilt. So my view is that Glaucon's scenario fails because it treats moral character as a mask that can be kept on or taken off depending on visibility, when in fact deep moral formation makes invisibility largely irrelevant to motivation—though perhaps in a different way than Socrates argues in the Republic.

What would count against this position? I want an honest assessment of its exposure: which observations about human psychology, which historical or literary cases of apparently invisible wrongdoing committed by people we consider virtuous, or which conceptual distinctions—say, between acting out of character and possessing a genuinely fragmented self—would genuinely threaten it? Please distinguish between evidence that would be merely awkward, such as finding that many people feel liberated rather than fractured when they act unjustly in secret, and evidence that would be decisive, such as demonstrating that practical reasoning can remain fully coherent while systematically violating moral norms with no internal cost whatsoever. I also want to know whether defenders of this kind of view typically concede any of these defeaters, and whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by defining "genuine justice" so narrowly that anyone who enjoys invisible wrongdoing is automatically excluded from having possessed it in the first place. Finally, please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, and say whether you think this view is well insulated or genuinely vulnerable.
