A friend has been pressing me with an argument about Hofstadter’s well-known reversal satire—where masculine generics are replaced by feminine ones to expose hidden defaults—and I think it proves far more than it should. Here is the chain as she lays it out:

(1) In the satire, replacing universal “he” and “man” with universal “she” and “woman” makes male readers feel erased, revealing that masculine generics carry a hidden male default and force women into constant mental translation.

(2) The harm is not mere statistical underrepresentation; it is that the term claims universality while structurally encoding one group as the unmarked norm, so excluded members must either accept misdescription or expend cognitive labor to correct it.

(3) If this is the correct diagnosis—any universal category that encodes a hidden prototype causes analogous erasure—then the reversal strategy applies to every taxonomic term that groups heterogeneous items under one label with a salient prototype.

(4) For example, “human being” in much philosophy carries a hidden prototype (rational, autonomous, language-using adult), “planet” carries a hidden prototype (rocky body orbiting a main-sequence star), and “belief” carries a hidden prototype (explicit, conscious, propositional attitude).

(5) By the satire’s method, we could write parallel reversals: one where “human being” means “fully rational autonomous adult,” rendering infants and severely disabled people invisible; one where “planet” means “gas giant,” rendering Earth invisible; one where “belief” means “explicit judgment,” rendering implicit attitudes invisible.

(6) So either we accept that all taxonomic language is structurally oppressive in the same way gendered generics are—which seems absurd, since “planet” is not morally harmful—or we deny that hidden prototypes cause harm in general.

(7) But if hidden prototypes do not cause harm in general, then gendered generics are harmful only because of some extra feature specific to gender, such as social power asymmetry.

(8) Yet the reversal argument is designed precisely to show that the harm is visible without invoking power asymmetry: the male reader feels erased purely from linguistic reversal, not from historical oppression.

(9) Therefore the reversal either overgeneralizes to all language, or it implicitly relies on the very power asymmetry it claims not to need, undermining its own demonstration.

Which step should I reject? I want to keep (1) and (2) intact—that there is a real phenomenon of hidden-default erasure in gendered language—and I would prefer not to deny (7), since gender asymmetry is real. Is the mistake in (3), where I slide from gendered language to a general principle about prototypes? Or is there a subtler slide—perhaps between “hidden default in a social identity term” and “hidden prototype in a scientific taxonomy”—that makes (4) or (5) illegitimate? And whatever I give up, what does it cost me: do I have to admit that the satire works only as rhetoric, not as a general argument about language; or do I have to accept that all our categories are politically loaded in the same way?
