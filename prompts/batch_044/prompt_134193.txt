You are serving as an expert moral philosopher and philosopher of history for this exercise. Before you map anything, spend a moment brainstorming the underlying logical commitments—do not begin by listing familiar authors or movements, but by identifying the concrete distinctions that actually divide people on this issue.

Construct a focused taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree—of philosophical positions on whether ranking historical atrocities is conceptually coherent and morally permissible. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: each internal node must be a claim or distinction that a respondent could answer without already knowing which position it yields.

Use exactly these four branching levels:
1. The purpose of comparison: moral evaluation of agents, causal-historical explanation, or commemorative and political practice?
2. The metric required: must any legitimate ranking presuppose a cardinal scale of human suffering, or can an ordinal comparison proceed solely through structural and institutional features (for example, the degree of bureaucratic rationalization, the explicit intent to destroy a group’s collective identity, or the scale of state capacity mobilized)?
3. The ground of resistance: is the strongest objection epistemic (we lack adequate comparative access to suffering), moral (ranking disrespects particular victims or survivors), or conceptual (the category of atrocity is sui generis and resists ordering altogether)?
4. The entailment of comparison: does judging one event more severe necessarily diminish the moral standing of other events, or can a non-competitive, plural ordering be sustained?

At each leaf, name the position if it is genuinely defended (for instance, comparative genocide studies, Holocaust uniqueness or incommensurability, memorial pluralism, structural-ordinal comparison, deflationary skepticism) or mark it “unnamed” if the combination appears unoccupied. For every leaf, provide one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment. Note any empty logical combinations and briefly diagnose whether they seem incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate. Write in American English, keep the response concise and sharply focused, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
