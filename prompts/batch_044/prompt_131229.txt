I'm preparing a conceptual framework for a seminar on artificial general intelligence dynamics, and I'm trying to isolate exactly where the disagreement lives between two camps that both claim to be reasoning from first principles rather than from historical analogy alone. One camp holds that once systems reach human-level reasoning in scientific and engineering domains, we should expect a discontinuous intelligence explosion: a rapid, self-reinforcing escalation in capabilities driven by recursive self-improvement and the automation of cognitive labor, with the critical dynamics unfolding faster than institutional, regulatory, or economic adaptation can occur—potentially within months. The opposing camp holds that intelligence is not a unitary scalar that can recursively amplify itself in isolation; rather, it is a vector of heterogeneous, context-dependent competencies embedded in physical, institutional, and verification bottlenecks, so that even dramatic algorithmic advances will necessarily diffuse gradually, encountering friction in data acquisition, energy infrastructure, empirical testing, and deployment that stretches transformative impact across years or decades.

Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps. Begin with the claims both sides can sign—about the formal possibility of recursive architecture search, about the distinction between algorithmic capability gains and real-world economic or strategic impact, about the empirical record of past technologies that improved their own production processes, and about what specific observable patterns would count as resolving the dispute in either direction. Then lay out the precise formulations of their residual disagreement in wording each camp would accept for its own position: specifically, whether there exists a threshold beyond which the marginal cost of generating superior reasoning architectures collapses faster than verification and deployment constraints can adapt, and whether the concept of general intelligence refers to a single optimizable quantity capable of recursive amplification or to an irreducibly plural set of skills whose improvement curves remain decoupled.

Next, identify the cruxes. I want at least three: (1) whether meta-learning and automated architecture design can proceed without human-in-the-loop verification at a rate that outpaces physical manufacturing and safety-review timelines; (2) whether historical analogies—semiconductor scaling, nuclear weapons development, the Industrial Revolution—support discontinuous or continuous models of transformative technological change when properly analogized; and (3) whether there is a "meta-cognitive saturation" point at which improving the system that improves reasoning requires exponentially more computation or data, thereby capping recursive speed regardless of institutional friction. For each crux, specify a concrete hypothetical scenario or reasoning step—something both sides would agree is relevant—along with each camp's predicted outcome. For instance, for crux (1), specify a case in which an AI system designs a successor with substantially improved reasoning efficiency but requires six months of chip fabrication, empirical validation, and regulatory clearance to deploy; both sides should state whether this counts as evidence of rapid takeoff constrained only by physical reality, or as proof that diffusion bottlenecks dominate algorithmic speed. Finally, flag any portion of the dispute that risks being verbal—particularly around terms like "intelligence," "explosion," "recursive," or "general"—and clarify whether resolving that ambiguity would dissolve part of the disagreement or merely relocate it to empirical predictions. Keep the weight on the conceptual architecture rather than on named researchers, specific forecasts, or citations.
