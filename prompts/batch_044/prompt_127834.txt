I'm working out whether conscious experience requires a Cartesian theater—a single unified stage where representations come together for an inner audience—or can be fully accounted for by Dennett's multiple drafts model, in which reports are constructed by distributed processes with no central spectator. Here are my verdicts on concrete and hypothetical cases; I want to know whether a single principle captures them, and if not, which verdict I should overturn.

Case 1: In a standard introspection task, a subject reports seeing a red flash immediately after it ends. The report feels direct and unified; I treat this as consistent with a theater, though not conclusive.

Case 2: The same subject reports the flash only after a 400 ms delay. During the interval she reports no lingering visual experience; the report is reconstructed from a brief trace. I take this as draft-like—there is no unified experience persisting in a theater, only a post-hoc narrative.

Case 3: Consider a hypothetical "tri-module" architecture (not found in natural cognition) with independent streams for color, shape, and motion. A central selector drives speech from one stream, but the others remain active and can guide behavior without ever entering the verbal report. When the system says "red circle," the shape stream actually registered "square." I think this is pure multiple drafts: there is never a unified experience of "red circle," only competing drafts serialized at the point of report.

Case 4: A split-brain patient sees red on the right and orange on the left. The left hemisphere says "red" and confabulates a reason; the right hemisphere can point to orange. I think this is also draft-like—two separate drafts in two hemispheres—but I am unsure whether the left-hemisphere confabulation reveals a local theater or just an editor maintaining coherence.

Case 5: A "staggered-binding" system receives color data 50 ms before shape data. Before report there are only unbound feature drafts; at the moment of verbalization they are bound into "red circle," but there is no prior instant at which the bound representation existed as an object of awareness. This pulls me away from a theater, yet the sudden integration feels like a single event, which pulls me back.

Case 6: A system with a single serial bottleneck forces all sensory information through one channel for conscious access, though direct motor bypass is possible. The bottleneck seems like a functional analog of a theater—"where it all comes together"—but since there is no inner observer, I am unsure whether this counts as a Cartesian theater or merely a constrained draft model.

So: Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about the revisability of reports without confabulation, or about the availability of representations to a unified narrative editor—that distinguishes theater-like from draft-like cases? How does it resolve the tension between Case 4 and Case 6, where both involve bottlenecks or splits but I want to classify one as draft-like and the other as possibly theater-like? Which of my verdicts must be dropped? And what does the principle predict for a system with four drafts, three agreeing and one dissenting, where the dissenting draft can influence non-verbal behavior but never corrects the verbal report?
