Consider the following paraphrased line of reasoning from an 18th-century essay on miracles: when someone reports an extraordinary event, a rational hearer must compare the probability that the event actually happened against the probability that the speaker was mistaken or lying, and because uniform experience shows that people are frequently deceived or deceptive, the second probability always outweighs the first for genuinely miraculous claims, so belief should be suspended. I am trying to understand whether this argument presupposes a reductionist account of testimony—namely, that accepting a speaker's word always involves an individual listener's private statistical weighing of that speaker's credibility—or whether it can accommodate an anti-reductionist view on which testimonial belief is sometimes a basic, non-inferential source not reducible to such personal calculus. In particular, could an anti-reductionist reply that in ordinary social cognition the monitoring of speaker reliability is distributed across the epistemic community rather than computed by each hearer, and if so, does that reply undermine the essay's comparison of probabilities or merely shift the locus of evaluation without affecting the conclusion?
