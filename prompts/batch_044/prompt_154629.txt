I'm writing an essay for my political philosophy seminar on rational-choice critiques of democratic participation. The paradox of voting, as I understand it, is that in any large-scale election the probability that a single vote will be pivotal is so small that the expected instrumental benefit of voting is swamped by its costs—travel, information-gathering, time—so a purely instrumentally rational agent should abstain. Most of the literature treats this as an explanatory puzzle: why do people vote despite the calculus? But I think there is a more fundamental issue that is usually glossed over. If democratic legitimacy depends on popular authorization, and if rational agents have no reason to provide that authorization through voting, then doesn't the paradox undermine not just our descriptive theories of turnout but the normative coherence of democratic procedures themselves? In other words, is democracy rationally self-defeating?

I recently came across a proposal for delegated proxy voting—sometimes discussed in liquid-democracy frameworks—where citizens can transfer voting power through transitive chains rather than casting direct ballots. At first this seemed like it might resolve the paradox, since delegation lowers the individual cost of participation. But on reflection, it seems to make the pivotal-probability problem worse: a delegate who accumulates many proxies becomes highly pivotal, which means each original delegator's contribution to that pivot is diluted by the size of the chain. So my question is whether democratic theorists should treat the paradox as a localized problem about direct voting, or whether it reveals a structural flaw in any aggregation procedure that relies on voluntary, instrumentally motivated participation—including delegated systems. Does the persistence of the paradox across delegation architectures imply that democratic legitimacy must be grounded in something other than rational consent, such as civic duty or expressive value? Or is there a way to restructure the calculus so that voluntary participation remains rational even when pivotal probabilities are distributed across networks?
