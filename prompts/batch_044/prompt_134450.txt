Having spent thirty-five years in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to the philosophy of science and democratic theory, I have become preoccupied with John Dewey's conception of democracy not as a mechanism for aggregating pre-formed preferences, but as a mode of social inquiry in which collective problems are identified, hypotheses about institutional arrangements are tested through action, and beliefs are revised in light of experienced consequences. I find particularly compelling his claim that a genuinely public intelligence emerges only when citizens share in the formation of problems and in the evaluation of outcomes, rather than delegating judgment to fixed authorities. Yet Dewey wrote in an era of print journalism and town-hall deliberation, and I am trying to understand whether his framework can accommodate a form of governance that has become conceivable only with modern computation.

Consider a mid-sized city that abandons traditional legislative cycles in favor of a continuous, algorithmically structured experiment. Residents propose policy variants—say, different configurations of public transit subsidies or school-start times—which are then implemented in statistically matched districts. Real-time metrics of welfare, equity, and environmental impact feed back into a reinforcement-learning system that automatically adjusts resource allocation, while citizens vote only on the meta-rules governing the experiment's ethical constraints and measurement protocols. In this model, governance operates as an instrumental inquiry without a shared deliberative forum: the public is distributed across data streams, and policy knowledge is generated through automated correlation rather than through the communicative reconstruction of shared experience. Given Dewey's experimentalism, would he have regarded this as the fulfillment of democratic inquiry—an extension of scientific method into social life—or as its dissolution into a technocratic simulation of intelligence? Specifically, I wonder how he would apply his distinction between warranted belief and mere successful prediction to policies validated only by algorithmic optimization, and whether his separation of the public from the state can survive when the feedback loop between experience and institutional adjustment is mediated entirely by automated platforms.

I am not asking for a historical survey of Dewey scholarship, nor for citations or a reading list; I want a philosophical reconstruction of his likely judgment, with attention to the points where his framework might require revision or break down in the face of such perpetual, data-driven governance. If his view implies that democratic legitimacy requires a particular kind of shared, communicative experience that this system eliminates, then I want that conclusion drawn out. If, conversely, he would accept automated experimentation provided certain conditions are met, I want those conditions specified precisely.
