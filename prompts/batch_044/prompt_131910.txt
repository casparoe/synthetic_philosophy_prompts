I have been trying, without any formal philosophical training, to make sense of a disagreement that seems to have run for decades about what happens when our senses deceive us—like seeing a straight stick as bent in water. On one side, people argue that the experience in the illusion and the experience in normal seeing share a fundamental common structure: both present the world as being a certain way, and the only difference is whether the world actually matches that presentation. On the other side, people insist that illusion and veridical perception are entirely different kinds of mental events, and that treating them as variations of a single thing is a fundamental mistake. Every argument I encounter—the appeal to representational content, to relational accounts, to whether there is a highest common factor—seems to have been exchanged repeatedly since at least the 1980s, yet neither camp moves. Is this a substantive dispute about the nature of perception, a verbal disagreement about what we mean by experience or seeing, or are the two sides actually answering different questions—one psychological, one metaphysical—under one heading? I want a diagnosis that actually engages what each side says, not a catalog of interpretations. In particular, I have been wondering about a case that does not fit neatly into either framework: looking at a completely transparent glass wall under lighting that eliminates all reflection, so that one experiences it as an opaque barrier, where there is no veridical counterpart of seeing a wall because nothing solid is present. Does this kind of case expose a hidden empirical assumption in the debate, reveal that the taxonomy needs a third category, or is it just an edge case that both sides can absorb? And given your diagnosis of the stalemate, tell me what—if anything—would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, or a reason to conclude that the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned.
