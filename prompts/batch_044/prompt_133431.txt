I have recently become both a first-time parent and the executor of my late grandmother's estate, and these roles have made the problem of pure time preference feel less like an academic puzzle and more like a practical crisis. I am trying to determine whether there is any coherent principle that captures my intuitive verdicts about when future benefits justify present costs, but I keep finding pairs that pull in opposite directions, and I need help seeing whether a unified account is possible or whether I must abandon one of my judgments.

Here are the cases, with my gut verdicts. First, I think it is fully rational to save roughly fifteen percent of my current income for retirement three decades away, even though I experience almost no emotional identification with my seventy-year-old self. Second, I think supporting carbon-abatement policies that reduce present consumption by a small margin to prevent catastrophic warming in two hundred years is not merely permissible but morally required, despite the fact that none of the beneficiaries will be contemporaneous with me. Third, in career planning, I believe one should choose a trajectory that builds slowly but sustains well-being over forty years rather than one that maximizes satisfaction for the next decade and then burns out; the distant future should not be treated as negligible.

Yet my judgments seem to reverse in structurally similar contexts. Fourth, I would regard it as irrational for me to pay a large present premium for a life-extension therapy that adds only three years of life after age ninety; the temporal remoteness seems to drain the value, even though the quantitative trade-off resembles the retirement case. Fifth, if a painless vaccine taken today eliminated a one-in-a-thousand risk of a pandemic in fifty years but carried a minute immediate side-effect risk, I should refuse it—the future danger feels too remote to justify present exposure, which conflicts with my willingness to accept present costs for climate benefits at a comparable or greater distance. Sixth, I enforce strict savings rules for my child's college fund, which will not be used for a decade, yet I feel no comparable obligation to contribute to a charitable fund that benefits strangers two centuries hence, even though both involve sacrificing present resources for distant beneficiaries.

Is there a defensible principle of intertemporal rationality—perhaps one that distinguishes prudential continuity from impersonal moral obligation, or that introduces a non-linear weighting function tied to narrative identity rather than clock time—that can accommodate all six verdicts? If not, which of my judgments is the outlier that a consistent account must reject, and what does the best remaining principle predict about the following case I have not resolved: whether I ought to accept a position that requires relocating my family for two years now in exchange for a pension benefit that does not vest until thirty years later?
