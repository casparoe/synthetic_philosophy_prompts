ok so there's this college that structures its entire BA around Whitehead's three rhythms — romance, precision, generalization — but they implement it kinda literally. Year one is pure romance: no grades, students wander across departments. But here's teh twist: they have to turn in a "discipline log" every week proving they weren't just drifting, and if they miss two they get expelled. Year two and three are pure precision: one locked-in major, standardized exams, no electives. Year four is generalization but you have to write a thesis that explicitly loops back to something from your romance year, or you don't graduate. The deans say this captures Whitehead's point that freedom and discipline are two sides of the same coin, and that education should be a cyclical growth of wisdom not just info. But the whole thing feels like it presupposes some pretty specific philosophical commitments — maybe that learning has to be teleological, that every student must end in synthesis, or that individual wandering only has value if it's eventually disciplined into a single narrative. So: what view of education, or maybe of the self, does this institutional design actually depend on? Is it genuinely Whiteheadian, or does it quietly commit to things he'd reject, like a deterministic arc of development? And could the exact same rules — logs, lock-in, loop-back thesis — be justified by a completely different theory, like pure constructivism or even behaviorism? If the underlying commitment turned out to be wrong, what part of the program would actually have to change?
