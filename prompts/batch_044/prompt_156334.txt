In historiography, the dispute over presentism has been deadlocked for decades: critics insist that judging past societies by contemporary moral or conceptual standards produces anachronistic distortion, while defenders reply that every historian necessarily selects and frames evidence from a present vantage point, so the relevant distinction is not whether presentism occurs but which forms are methodologically defensible. Both sides cite the same canonical patterns—assessing colonial administrators through modern rights-based frameworks versus reconstructing their own normative vocabularies—yet neither concedes ground. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not fundamentally about moral judgment, but about whether historical explanation requires what we might call category fidelity: the claim that explaining an action demands deploying the agent’s own classificatory scheme, rather than merely describing behavior that happens to fall under our modern taxonomy. Is that diagnosis right? Or is the disagreement instead about the purpose of historiography—whether it aims at understanding agents on their own terms or at generating actionable knowledge for present communities—such that the two sides are answering different questions under the shared label presentism? I want a diagnosis supported by what each camp actually commits to, not a survey of positions, together with an honest assessment of whether any conceptual distinction—perhaps between explanatory presentism and evaluative presentism, or between indexical and normative deployments of present categories—could actually move the debate, or whether the impasse reflects bedrock intuitions about why we study the past that neither side can argue the other out of.
