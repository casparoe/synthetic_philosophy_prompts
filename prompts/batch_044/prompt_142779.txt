After discovering my great-grandfather’s 1914 administrative papers in a family archive, I have been trying to determine whether my views on national responsibility for the First World War actually cohere. My commitments are these: (1) I accept the post-Fischer consensus that blame for the war is structurally distributed across alliance systems rather than concentrated in Berlin; (2) I think national responsibility is genuinely normative but only in an “archive-continuity” sense I have been sketching, meaning it inheres in the unbroken institutional and documentary record of a state rather than in the mental states of present citizens; (3) I deny that nations are intentional moral agents possessing collective wills independent of bureaucratic procedure; (4) I hold that when institutional continuity is broken—as with the dissolution of the Austro-Hungarian Empire—archive-responsibility is extinguished regardless of how large that state’s causal contribution to the outbreak was; and (5) I believe present citizens of successor states inherit a strict duty of preservation and acknowledgment tied to those continuous archives, though they inherit no moral guilt. Please audit these for consistency: identify the genuine tensions, distinguish them from pairs that only appear to conflict, rank the real ones by severity with what each revision costs, and say which single change would restore coherence most cheaply. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; item 1 should address the most severe tension, items 2 and 3 the remaining conflicts and the apparent tension that dissolves, item 4 should treat how distributed blame interacts with archive-continuity, and item 5 should state the least costly revision.
