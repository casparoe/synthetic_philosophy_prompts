I was sitting in on a grad workshop on planning theory and two people got into it over whether the socialist calculation debate was ever actually resolved. (1) The first one argued that Mises's 1920 point wasn't mainly about computers being too slow—it was that without private capital ownership there's no entrepreneurial agent who stands to gain from noticing that some factory in the provences is using the wrong grade of steel, so the knowledge about misallocation never gets generated at all, not just that it can't be summed up. (2) The second person said that Lange's 1930s iterative model already solves this: the Central Planning Board sets prices, observes shortages and surpluses, and adjusts, which creates the same feedback loop that reveals errors, except the discovery happens through quantity signals rather than profit motives. (3) The first student came back with Hayek's 1945 tacit-knowledge objection—most of what local managers know is embodied in practice and can't be captured in aggregate surplus reports, so the iterative method only corrects mistakes that can be expressed in numbers, leaving deeper structural misallocations invisible. (4) The second student replied that Hayek's argument proves too much: if knowledge is genuinely inarticulable then markets can't coordinate it either, since prices are just aggregated signals that every actor still has to interpret using there own unspoken expertise, so Hayek either collapses into general skepticism about any coordination mechanism or he's secretly assuming that private ownership magically transforms inarticulable knowledge into prices.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also, is there a hidden assumption both sides share—maybe about whether equilibrium is static or genuinely dynamic—that neither Mises nor Lange questioned, and does that undermine the whole framing?
