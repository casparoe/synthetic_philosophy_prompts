Popper argued that science does not aim for verification or high probability but rather for theories that lie closer to the complete truth, even if they are strictly false. He formalized this by comparing the sets of logical consequences of two theories: theory A is closer to the truth than theory B if the set of true consequences included in A is at least as large as that in B, and the set of false consequences included in A is no larger than that in B. The host then explained that philosophers later demonstrated a serious flaw in this quantitative measure—specifically, that if both theories are false, the formal comparison collapses in such a way that no false theory can be ranked as closer to the truth than another false theory—so Popper effectively abandoned the quantitative framework and retained only falsifiability and corroboration as qualitative substitutes.
