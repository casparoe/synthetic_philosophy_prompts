I'm putting together a handout for a seminar on choice without preference and I'm tired of the usual 'Buridan's ass starves' story. Can you write a decision-tree taxonomy — like a flowchart in words — that sorts out what people actually think rational choice demands when you're genuinely stuck between options of exactly equal value? Not just 'what would you do' but which theoritical commitments drive the answer.

Build it from the logical space first, then place the views. Start with whether perfect indifference is even possible. Then branch by: do you allow arbitrary internal tie-breaking? Must the agent suspend choice? Is a second-order preference required? Is an external randomizer (coin, algorithm) rational resolution or just outsourcing failure? And is the tie metaphysically real or just epistemic ignorance?

Every node should be a concrete claim or case someone can answer without already knowing the label. The leaves should name real positions if they exist — strict Leibnizian, permissive decision-theorist, deflationary 'no problem' — but also flag empty combinations, like maybe nobody holds that suspension is mandatory but randomization is forbidden, and say briefly why that spot is empty (incoherent? unattractive? unexplored?). Keep it to about six branching questions so it fits one page.

Separate what's basically settled from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested take as the consensus. There's probably a typo in here but I think you get the idea.
