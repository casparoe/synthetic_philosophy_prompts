Consider a political philosopher who assesses institutional arrangements by asking whether the central agent is free in the politically relevant sense—where her concern is strictly with the structure of power relations rather than with subjective satisfaction, psychological autonomy, or resource abundance. She has delivered the following verdicts.

Case 1—The Benevolent Proprietor: A tenant lives in a privately administered town in which a single corporation owns all housing, utilities, and transport infrastructure. The corporation’s charter grants its board absolute discretion to revoke residency or deny access to essential services for any reason, without cause or appeal. In practice the board is benevolent and has never exercised this power. The tenant holds substantial savings and could relocate to another region with comparable employment within a few months. Verdict: Not free.

Case 2—The Regulated Professional: A physician works in a public hospital governed by extensive statutory protocols—treatment guidelines, staffing ratios, and expenditure approvals—enacted by an elected legislature and enforced by independent courts. She holds tenure and cannot be dismissed without cause shown before an independent tribunal, but she must justify any clinical deviation to a review board. Her skills are highly specialized; leaving the profession would require years of retraining and professional recertification elsewhere. Verdict: Free.

Case 3—The Democratic Subject: A citizen lives in a democratic state that extensively regulates occupational licensing, business formation, and internal movement. Every regulation was enacted through participatory procedures with equal voting rights and is subject to judicial review. The citizen lacks the resources to emigrate and depends on state-provided infrastructure for basic needs. Verdict: Free.

Now consider Case 4—The Cooperative Engineer: A wealthy software engineer lives in a minimal-state jurisdiction with virtually no government interference in her work or movement. All essential services—energy, water, data transmission, and waste processing—are provided by a single cooperative governed by a charter that permits a two-thirds board vote to suspend any member’s access without cause, explanation, or review. The charter has never been invoked against anyone; the engineer receives all services without restriction, and she possesses sufficient capital to relocate within weeks to a jurisdiction with competing providers. Predict whether the philosopher will judge her free or not free.

In your answer, identify which of Cases 1–3 is the closest analog and say whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. If they do, articulate the principle she would be adopting with each choice. Then state the rule she appears to be running, name at least one other plausible rule—drawn from negative, positive, or republican conceptions of liberty—that accommodates Cases 1–3 equally well, and say what each rule predicts for Case 4. Give your best-guess prediction with reasoning, specify which structural feature of Case 4 does the decisive work, and propose one further case, structurally different from Case 4, that would best distinguish the leading rule from its nearest competitor.
