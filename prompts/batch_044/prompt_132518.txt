I've been arguing with my friends about work and leisure rights — the usual mix of Protestant work ethic stuff, France's 35-hour week, and how karoshi gets talked about in Japan. We kept running into this same weird pattern across different conversations, and I can't tell if it already has a name or if I'm just inventing something.

Here's the thing. In debates about the 35-hour week, people defend leisure as a universal right, like everyone should have it equally, but they never ask whose labor makes that leisure structurally possible — the outsourced care work, the delivery workers, the manufacturing done elsewhere. Then when people talk about karoshi, they treat it like the victim failed to claim leisure that was technically available, ignoring that the system never actually made space for it. And with the Protestant work ethic, leisure gets treated as morally suspicious unless it's "productive" or "deserved", which means the idea of leisure as just... rest, gets lost. I saw this happen in four different spots: a startup founder bragging he doesn't need weekends because he loves coding; a French politician defending the 35-hour law without mentioning dependancy on immigrant labor; an article blaming a Japanese worker for not setting boundaries; and a discussion where someone said reducing hours would hurt "innovation" without explaining why innovation needs 80-hour weeks specifically. In all these cases, leisure is discussed like it's an individual possesion you either have or don't, rather than a relation that depends on someone else doing the sustaining work. Is there an established concept for this specific blind spot — treating leisure as an isolated entitlement while ignoring the labor dependancy that makes it possible? I don't think it's just false consciousness, since that's about not seeing your own exploitation, and it's not exactly privilege blindness either, which feels broader. If there's no name, could you coin one and say exactly where its edges are — like, does it apply when people genuinely don't know about the dependency, or only when they actively ignore it?
