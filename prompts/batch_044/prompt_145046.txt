In my philosophy of science seminar, we have been examining whether Wolpert's no-free-lunch theorems for search and optimization undermine standard justifications for inductive projection. I have formed the following judgments about particular cases, but I am unsure whether they cohere around a single principle or whether some should be abandoned.

First, when physicists extend an inverse-square gravitational law, verified within the solar system, to predict the orbit of a newly observed exoplanet, the projection seems fully warranted, even though the specific conditions are unobserved. Second, if a machine-learning engineer applies a convolutional network trained on natural photographs to classify synthetic astronomical images without domain adaptation, the inference strikes me as reckless; given that NFL results establish equivalent average performance across all algorithms over the space of all possible problems, there appears to be no default justification for expecting visual-feature transfer. Third, a toddler who has encountered only three barking dogs and concludes that dogs bark seems to be making a legitimate inductive leap, apparently because evolved cognitive architecture restricts the hypothesis space in ways that do not apply to arbitrary machine-learning targets. Fourth, a quantitative analyst who constructs a complex non-linear model that perfectly retrodicts the 2008 financial crisis and then deploys it to forecast the next crash seems barely justified—this feels analogous to post-hoc tuning rather than genuine prediction. Fifth, however, I also believe that applying a deliberately simple linear predictor across heterogeneous economic datasets is more defensible than applying an overparameterized model, even though NFL theorems imply that simplicity confers no universal performance advantage over all possible target functions.

Is there a coherent account—perhaps built around constrained hypothesis spaces, background structural knowledge, or what I tentatively call inductive licensing—that can accommodate all five judgments? If the answer requires discarding one, which should be revised, and why? Finally, what should such an account say about a case I have not listed: deploying a physics-informed neural network to extrapolate fluid dynamics into a turbulence regime for which no measurements exist?
