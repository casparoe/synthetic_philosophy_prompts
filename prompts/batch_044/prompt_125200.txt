I was trained originally as a physicist and spent most of my career in condensed-matter research before retiring; over the past few years I have turned to philosophy of mathematics, and I am currently working through L. E. J. Brouwer’s 1908 essay The Unreliability of the Logical Principles in an English translation. The argument is dense, and I am trying to parse a particular stretch in which Brouwer seems to claim that mathematics is a languageless mental activity that precedes logic, that the law of excluded middle is only legitimate for finite domains, and that for infinite collections one may not assert A or not-A unless one already possesses an explicit mental construction that establishes one side. He also insists that time—not space or any formal symbol system—is the sole a priori form within which these constructions unfold. I understand the broad anti-formalist thrust, but I am struggling with the precise epistemic status he assigns to the mental act itself: does he mean that the construction is a private psychological event whose occurrence validates the proposition for the individual mind, or is there an intersubjective criterion—perhaps the communicability of the construction through language—that allows different mathematicians to recognize the same result without reducing mathematics to empirical psychology?

To complicate my reading, I recently discussed the passage with a friend who works in theoretical computer science, and she suggested that Brouwer’s distinction between lawlike sequences and genuinely open-ended choice sequences—ideas he developed more fully later—might already be nascent here in his insistence that an infinite process must remain unfinished and therefore cannot be treated as a completed object governed by logical deduction alone. I am unsure whether importing that later technical framework back into the 1908 text is legitimate hermeneutics or anachronistic over-reading. Specifically, does the passage’s emphasis on temporal becoming and unfinished mental construction genuinely anticipate a distinction between deterministic algorithmic procedures and open choice processes that do not resolve into predetermined laws, or is that interpretation entirely foreign to Brouwer’s immediate concern with the foundations of logic? I would appreciate help clarifying the logical structure of the passage, separating what is strictly about the priority of mental construction over symbolic logic from what might be read as a proto-computational theory of mathematical activity, and identifying where my modern interpolation risks distorting the historical argument. I am not looking for a bibliography, only for a careful dissection of how the pieces fit together.
