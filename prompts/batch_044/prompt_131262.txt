Write a dialogue between two philosophy profs—Mara and Leo—standing in front of a museum display on simultaneous invention (Newton vs Leibniz, Darwin vs Wallace). Mara argues these cases show big discoveries are basically inevitable once the intellectual soil is ready; Leo argues that's a post-hoc myth and that the specific discoverer shapes the idea's articulation and reception so much that "the same discovery" is never really the same. They need to actually respond to each other, not talk past eachother.

Here's the twist: inject a hypothetical—imagine a mathematician in Kyoto around 1720 who derives a result equivalent to the fundamental theorem of calculus using purely geometric techniques, but dies before publishing because his patron collapses. Have them debate whether this counts as a "missed simultaneous invention" supporting Mara's inevitability thesis, or whether the fact that it vanished proves Leo's point about fragility and social networks. Then have them tackle whether credit actually matters if the underlying math is equivalent—especially since notation and proof style determine what later scientists can build on.

Keep it casual, write in American English, and skip citations or reading lists—just the conceptual back-and-forth.
