Exam question: W.K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley argue that authorial intention is irrelevant to a poem’s meaning, while E.D. Hirsch defends actual intentionalism. Explain both positions and apply them to this case: a poet publishes a sonnet widely interpreted as being about grief; twenty years later she states in an interview that she intended it to be about economic inflation and insists readers should now read it that way. How would Beardsley and Hirsch each respond? Would either allow the later revelation to change the poem’s meaning?

Student answer: Wimsatt and Beardsley identify the intentional fallacy as a subtype of the affective fallacy—specifically, the error of treating the author’s emotional state or private mental state as if it determined the poem’s objective meaning. They argue that both errors confuse the work’s public linguistic structure with something external: either the reader’s feelings (in the affective case) or the author’s psychology (in the intentional case). Because meaning is fixed by the poem’s internal language and conventions, the poet’s later interview claiming the sonnet is about inflation is biographically interesting but semantically irrelevant; the grief interpretation remains valid if the text’s imagery and diction support it. Hirsch, defending actual intentionalism, distinguishes meaning—what the author actually intended—from significance—how the work relates to other contexts. He holds that the original intent fixes the meaning permanently. However, he allows that an author’s later, more reflective self-interpretation can correct a prior misunderstanding of her own intent, thereby revising the poem’s meaning retroactively. So if the poet now sincerely reveals she always meant inflation, Hirsch would update the meaning to inflation, though the grief reading retains historical significance as an interpretation readers once held.
