I have been developing an essay for an advanced seminar on action theory and the philosophy of mind, tentatively titled Meta-Sphexishness and the Architecture of Choice. The central thesis is that the traditional philosophical deployment of the sphex wasp—specifically its rigid burrowing-and-paralysis routine, long used to illustrate mechanically fixed behavior—rests on a false dichotomy between animal rigidity and human flexibility. I propose that human practical reasoning is not the negation of sphexishness but rather its recursive extension: what I call second-order sphexishness, wherein agents develop rigid meta-routines precisely in order to avoid first-order rigidity. For example, a person who systematically second-guesses every instinctive response does not escape mechanism but instantiates a predictable, algorithmically describable pattern of anti-pattern recognition. The essay then asks whether debates about free will have therefore been misframed: rather than asking whether human action is determined or flexible, we should ask whether the level of description at which rigidity operates—first-order behavioral loops versus second-order cognitive architectures—has any normative significance for moral responsibility. I also tentatively suggest that this framework dissolves the standard compatibilist and incompatibilist stalemate by showing that both sides agree on first-order flexibility but disagree about whether second-order rigidity undermines autonomy.

I would be grateful for your critical comments on several specific points. First, is the distinction between first-order and second-order sphexishness conceptually coherent, or does it merely rename familiar compatibilist strategies concerning hierarchical desires? Second, does the essay unfairly conflate behavioral predictability with metaphysical determinism, and if so, how might that confusion be repaired without abandoning the core insight? Third, I am concerned that the argument relies on an empirical claim about the algorithmic predictability of human meta-cognition that may be stronger than the evidence permits—do you see a way to weaken this claim to a purely conceptual point about the structure of practical reasoning? Finally, I wonder whether the conclusion genuinely advances the free will debate or simply relocates it: if second-order rigidity is still rigidity, have I shown anything more than that determinism, if true, operates at a higher level of abstraction? Please address these concerns in whatever detail you think appropriate, and feel free to suggest structural revisions or objections I have not anticipated.
