I hold the view that Carroll's tortoise does not reveal an infinite regress in the justification of deduction, but rather a narrative recursion: every attempt to ground a logical step in an explicit rule merely extends the inferential story without reaching an authorial endpoint outside the text itself. From this I derive that deduction is justified not by an external meta-rule but by the internal coherence of the reasoning narrative — a condition I term narrative closure — and that this closure supplies sufficient rational constraint even though it offers no non-circular foundation. I further maintain that this view absorbs rather than evades the standard objections: the tortoise's refusal to accept a principle without further premises is not a failure of logic but a refusal to accept the narrative's ending. What would count against this position? I want the arguments, cases, and formal considerations that should genuinely move me — not merely the familiar charge of psychologism, but specific ways in which narrative coherence could fail to constrain reasoning, or in which a genuinely non-circular justification might be shown necessary rather than merely desirable. Which of these defeaters would defenders of narrative closure concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Assess honestly whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by treating every counterexample as just another episode in the story, and if so, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for an account of deduction.
