Consider the claim that personal identity survives gradual neuronal replacement only under a condition of continuous causal integration: every synthetic substitute must immediately enter the same synaptic-weight relationships as its biological predecessor, and the global network dynamics must remain within bounded divergence from baseline throughout the process. Apply this to an interleaved protocol in which replacements occur one neuron at a time across decades, yielding prolonged hybrid states. What are the most important implications of adopting this criterion—rather than standard psychological-continuity or bodily-continuity accounts—for (a) the legal and moral status of the subject during intermediate stages where substrate is evenly split, (b) the ethics of pausing, accelerating, or selectively omitting replacements for economic or non-therapeutic reasons, and (c) the metaphysical status of the fully replaced system—same person, causally continuous successor, or an intermediate category? Rank these by practical or normative weight, distinguish necessary consequences from those requiring further metaphysical assumptions, and identify which ones a committed psychological-continuity theorist would reject and on what grounds.
