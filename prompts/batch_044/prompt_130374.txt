Compare the thesis of mnemonic sovereignty—the claim that a community's living collective memory holds normative authority over archival historical records when the two diverge—with the opposing thesis of documentary correction, which maintains that historiographical evidence should always supersede communal remembrance. In constructing your comparison, attend specifically to a phenomenon I will call refractive commemoration: cases in which a community's ritual and narrative practices do not merely misremember a past event, but actively restructure its causal and moral significance for present social bonds, such that an archival correction of the factual record would simultaneously dissolve an identity-constituting practice. Assess which of the two theses can accommodate the possibility that some historical divergences are not epistemic failures but constitutive elements of legitimate communal identity, and which is compelled to treat all such divergence as correctable malfunction. Be precise about the normative stakes: does granting sovereignty to collective memory entail a form of epistemic relativism about the past, and does strict documentary correction risk destroying the social goods that erroneous remembrance sustains?
