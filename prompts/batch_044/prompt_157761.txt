As a journalist who covers philosophy of science and formal epistemology, I have been thinking lately about Jeffrey conditionalization—the rule for revising beliefs when one's evidence does not arrive as a definite observation but as a shift in confidence distributed across a partition of possibilities. The standard expositions cover the arithmetic well enough, yet I am struck by how little agreement there seems to be about where the genuinely live problems lie once one moves beyond textbook cases. In particular, scientific practice is full of situations where instruments yield ambiguous outputs, where the evidential structure does not line up neatly with an agent's prior theoretical partitions, and where updating must occur without the tidy luxury of conditioning on a single observed event.

Given this, I would like a defended prioritization rather than a survey. Which two or three open questions or lines of work in Jeffrey conditionalization and uncertain-evidence updating should occupy philosophers and formal epistemologists now, especially if one's concern is not just normative coherence but how belief revision actually functions under pervasive measurement ambiguity? For each, please explain why it is upstream of the others—whether resolving it would settle downstream debates about scientific model comparison, partition selection, or vulnerability to Dutch-book-style arguments—and assess its tractability with current conceptual tools rather than empirical breakthroughs. I am especially interested in whether the deepest issue is the normative justification of Jeffrey's rule itself, the practical problem of choosing partitions when evidence is structurally ambiguous, or some implication for how we ought to think about theory choice when data systematically underdetermines the space of hypotheses. How should limited intellectual effort be divided among these, and what kind of progress over the next few years would cause you to reorder them?
