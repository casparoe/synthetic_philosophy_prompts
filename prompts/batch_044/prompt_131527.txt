Consider the following argument, developed for a graduate seminar on scientific attribution, which seems to show that simultaneous invention undermines the very concept of individual discovery.

(1) In simultaneous invention—Newton and Leibniz with calculus, Darwin and Wallace with natural selection—the same theoretical structure is produced by independent agents.

(2) When two agents independently produce the same structure, its appearance is counterfactually stable: had either agent been absent, the other (or a near successor) would still have articulated it within a short interval.

(3) If an outcome is counterfactually stable with respect to an agent's presence, then that agent is not a causal difference-maker for the outcome.

(4) Therefore neither Newton nor Leibniz is a causal difference-maker for calculus in 1700; the correct bearer of the discovery is the community or the epoch.

(5) But this reasoning applies whenever a discovery is eventually inevitable, including solitary discoveries: Einstein was not strictly necessary for relativity, since given the state of physics in 1905, another physicist would eventually have derived the field equations.

(6) So either we deny that counterfactual stability eliminates individual attribution in every case, or we must replace all discovery claims with collective attributions—saying not "Einstein discovered relativity" but "the physics community of 1905 discovered it."

(7) The latter is intolerable: it erases the specific conceptual innovations (the equivalence principle, the covariance requirement) that distinguish Einstein's path from any other possible derivation.

(8) Hence we must reject the move from (2) to (3).

(9) Yet if counterfactual stability does not undermine individual attribution in solitary cases, it is unclear why it should do so in simultaneous ones; the argument then fails to explain why priority disputes are especially acute there.

(10) So we face a tension: either simultaneous invention is not metaphysically special (contrary to our practice), or we need an ad hoc reason why counterfactual stability undermines attribution only when redundancy is actual rather than merely possible.

I want to preserve (1), (6), and (7). Which step should I reject—(3), (5), or (9)—and what does each rejection cost? In particular, if I reject (3), am I committed to saying that causally redundant agents still deserve full credit? And if I reject (5) instead, what distinguishes the modal profile of a simultaneous case from that of a solitary but inevitable one?
