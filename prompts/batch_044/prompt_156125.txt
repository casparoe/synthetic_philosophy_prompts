In rationalist-adjacent debates about moral psychology and organizational behavior, people often invoke the line "they were just ordinary men" as if it settled whether situational pressure or deep character explains mass perpetration—usually with Browning's situational study in mind and Goldhagen's cultural-ideological counter-thesis somewhere in the background. I want you to treat that phrase not as an explanation but as a compressed argument, and unpack the distinct precise claims it could be expressing.

Please lay out the readings as a menu: (1) a statistical claim that the perpetrators were representative of the general population on standard personality and moral metrics; (2) a negative clinical claim that they showed no exceptional psychopathy, authoritarianism, or sadism; (3) an institutional claim that they occupied ordinary bureaucratic roles and were subject to ordinary organizational dynamics; (4) a moral-ontological claim that they were not specially vicious or evil by nature; and (5) an interchangeability or counterfactual claim that nearly anyone placed in the same structural position would have behaved similarly. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical thesis, false, or ambiguous in a way that makes it do no work; and specify exactly what evidential burden it carries.

Next, map these readings onto the dispute between situational and dispositional explanations: which precise version Browning's argument requires, which version Goldhagen's view targets, and whether any reading sounds like it supports situationalism but actually collapses into a dispositional or cultural explanation once the ambiguity is resolved. In particular, address the reference-class problem hidden inside the word "ordinary"—ordinary relative to all German men of the 1930s, relative to the specific pool of men actually assigned to that battalion, or relative to a cross-cultural human baseline—and show how shifting that baseline changes whether the slogan supports a situational inference or undermines it through selection effects.

Finally, identify which of these readings people typically invoke when they use the line to end an argument, say for each whether it genuinely licenses the conclusion they draw, and note whether the slogan has been qualified into unfalsifiability in the same way some psychological theories are. Write in American English, in a formal and polished register.
