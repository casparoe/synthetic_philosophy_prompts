Write a blank-verse poem of exactly fourteen lines, structured as an octave and a sestet with a clear volta at the ninth line, spoken from the position of someone delivering Moore's proof that an external world exists. Move beyond the standard rehearsal of presenting one's hands as premises; instead, build the poem around the idea that the proof operates performatively—the raised hand is not merely evidence but a gesture that presupposes and addresses an interlocutor, so that its force lies in the shared refusal to treat appearance as illusion rather than in any deductive chain. In the octave, establish the immediate presence of objects—hands, surfaces, the space between speaker and listener—without philosophical commentary. At the volta, shift to the recognition that knowing and showing are not the same, and that the skeptic is not defeated by logic but by being included in the scene of demonstration. Close the sestet with an ambiguity: the world is either proven or only mutually acknowledged, and the poem should not decide. Present the result as one continuous block of verse, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, annotations, or interspersed explanation—only the lines themselves, arranged as they might appear in a letter.
