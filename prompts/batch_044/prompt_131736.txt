Construct a thought experiment set in a realistic interstate crisis—no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no non-state actors with ambiguous attribution—designed to isolate the tension between rational deterrence theory and credible-commitment brinkmanship. Introduce a specific institutional mechanism, call it an Escalation Veto Protocol (EVP): State A adopts a procedural rule that automatically initiates a limited, conventional retaliatory strike unless three independent domestic agencies jointly issue a suspension signal within a 48-hour window. The protocol is not an automated doomsday device; suspension is always legally possible, but requires unanimous agreement that is costly, observable ex post, and invisible to the adversary in real time. State A now faces a crisis in which the adversary is testing resolve over a regional dispute with conventional stakes.

Engineer the case so that the following conditions hold:
1. A rational-deterrence analysis—modeling the crisis as a sequential game with common knowledge of payoffs and treating the EVP as an endogenous option—recommends that State A keep the EVP dormant and issue only a conditional, ambiguous diplomatic warning. Actual escalation carries negative expected utility, and activating the protocol introduces an avoidable risk of accidental execution without altering the adversary's rational calculus about State A's willingness to back down.
2. A credible-commitment theory of brinkmanship—treating credibility as a sunk resource and the adversary's belief updating as central—recommends that State A formally activate the EVP and couple it with an explicit, public threat. The visible activation binds State A's future self: because suspension requires unanimous multi-agency agreement that is hard to achieve quietly, the adversary updates upward on the probability that State A will follow through, even though the objective payoff structure remains identical and actual escalation is still undesirable.
3. The divergence must arise solely from how each theory models the adversary's belief dynamics about State A's future actions—not from differences in payoff structures, risk preferences, or assumptions of irrationality.
4. The scenario must be deliverable in one paragraph, must involve only conventional-state actors and existing bureaucratic technology, and must not invoke exotic physics, artificial agents, or omniscient observers.

After presenting the scenario, do the following:
- State the precise action and belief prescribed by each theory, tracing the reasoning step by step.
- Identify the single structural feature of the EVP (for example, the real-time invisibility of suspension attempts combined with their ex-post observability, or the multi-agency veto cost) that does all the work in separating the two verdicts.
- Explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into agreement between the theories?
- Present the strongest attack a rational-deterrence defender could mount—specifically, whether they deny that the protocol genuinely binds beliefs, or whether they argue that the adversary should update on the protocol's institutional existence rather than on its activation.
- Conclude with an assessment of whether the case reveals a genuine paradox in brinkmanship logic or merely a parameter disagreement about the dynamics of belief updating under institutional commitment.
