I serve on the Animal Research Ethics and Scale Review Panel at my university, and next Thursday we must circulate a written opinion before voting on Protocol 47-B. The proposal comes from a neuroscience group that plans to use roughly 2.4 million Drosophila melanogaster larvae over eighteen months to map nociception pathways. Their welfare assessment treats sentience as binary and argues that, since the per-subject probability of larval consciousness is low, the aggregate expected moral cost falls well below the threshold we apply to mammalian protocols. A dissenting external reviewer has submitted a formal objection invoking the scale argument: multiplying even a modestly low individual probability across millions of subjects yields an expected number of affected beings large enough that the total anticipated harm could match or exceed that of a smaller cohort of higher-certainty subjects. Our board charter requires us to apply a scale-sensitive precautionary standard, but that language was adopted without specifying whether it means an expected-value aggregation rule—treating 2.4 million × 0.001 probability as equivalent to 2,400 certain subjects—or a separate burden-shifting principle under which vast numbers alone suspend the default assumption of insentience unless specifically demonstrated for that scale.

Please draft the panel’s formal recommendation memo—approximately 800 words, suitable for circulation before Thursday’s meeting. It must recommend one of three concrete courses: approval with standard invertebrate conditions; approval only if the researchers adopt enhanced analgesic-analog protocols; or deferral pending an independent sentience assessment commissioned by the board. The reasoning should engage the researchers’ probabilistic framework seriously rather than dismissing it as naïve quantification, and it should treat the scale argument on its own conceptual terms rather than reducing it to a generic appeal to caution. Crucially, include an honest and substantive account of the strongest consideration favoring the option you reject—particularly the worry that importing expected-value aggregation into invertebrate ethics would render high-throughput model-organism research prohibitively burdensome, with cascading effects on fields that depend on large-n studies. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reference philosophical positions descriptively if needed. I was discussing this over coffee with a colleague in philosophy of biology, and she noted that our board has never actually confronted a case where the subject count is in the millions rather than the dozens, so the memo should also clarify, for future protocols, how the scale-sensitive standard operates conceptually—whether as aggregation or as burden shift—without pretending that clarification resolves the underlying epistemic problem.
