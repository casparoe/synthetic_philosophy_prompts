Below is a short essay I have drafted on the “man of his time” defence in historical moral judgment. I would welcome your comments on its conceptual coherence, on whether the central distinction I draw actually does work in the cases mentioned, and on whether there is a hidden premise about the nature of moral learning that I have not defended.

Essay: The “man of his time” defence usually operates as a diachronic excuse: it claims that judging a historical figure by present moral standards is anachronistic because the conceptual resources required for the correct judgment were not available in that era. I want to suggest that this defence conflates two very different claims, which I will call diachronic moral isolation and synchronic moral availability.

Diachronic moral isolation holds that a particular moral concept—say, the wrongness of chattel slavery or the injustice of colonial extraction—was genuinely absent from the intellectual environment of the figure in question. Synchronic moral availability, by contrast, holds that the concept was present and articulated by dissenting voices within that same environment, but the figure in question failed to attend to it or suppressed it.

These two excuses have radically different structures. The first is a claim about the historical distribution of ideas; the second is a claim about an individual’s epistemic negligence or moral cowardice. Once we separate them, the defence can apply cleanly only to figures for whom the first condition genuinely holds, not merely to figures who were surrounded by critics they chose to ignore.

Consider three standard cases. Thomas Jefferson owned slaves while writing that all men are created equal; Winston Churchill defended imperial administration in India despite contemporaneous anti-colonial arguments from figures within the British political sphere; Christopher Columbus initiated a system of forced labor and extraction in the Caribbean at a time when European moral frameworks did not contain a developed concept of universal human rights applied across cultures, though some dissenting voices within the Church did protest specific abuses.

If the distinction is sound, then Jefferson and Churchill fall primarily under synchronic moral availability rather than diachronic isolation. Their failures look less like unavoidable blindness and more like selective inattention. Columbus presents a harder case: the relevant moral framework may indeed have been largely unavailable in the specific form we now use, though even here one might ask whether basic prohibitions on cruelty and enslavement of the innocent were genuinely absent or merely overridden by economic and theological rationalizations.

My worry is that defenders of the “man of his time” line often slide from the second, stronger claim—that dissent existed—to the first, weaker claim—that the figure could not have known better—without noticing that they have changed the subject from epistemic limitation to moral negligence. Does this slide occur? And does my framing unfairly treat moral knowledge as analogous to scientific discovery, where absence of a theory genuinely excuses, rather than as practical reasoning, where one might be expected to derive correct conclusions from available premises?

Please comment on whether the essay’s central distinction is coherent, whether it applies fairly to the three figures mentioned, and whether the analogy between moral learning and scientific discovery is a hidden weakness in my argument. I am particularly interested in whether there is a way to defend Churchill or Jefferson that does not collapse synchronic availability back into diachronic isolation.
