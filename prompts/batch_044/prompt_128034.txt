Okay so I'm writing a chapter on idealized preference aggregation and I think I've backed myself into a weird corner with coherant extrapolated volition. Here's the argument I keep running into:

(1) Coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) is just whatever preferences we'd hold if we were more informed, more rational, and closer to the versions of ourselves we want to become.

(2) If my actual preference conflicts with my individual CEV-preference, then my actual preference is based on ignorance or bias, so I should defer to the CEV version.

(3) But CEV is defined over all of humanity, and the aggregation procedure (say reflective equilibrium or weighted coherence) can output a preference ordering that no single actual person holds, not even in idealized form.

(4) So for almost anyone, the aggregated CEV will conflict with their individual CEV.

(5) By (2), each person should defer to the aggregated CEV over their own individual one.

(6) Therefore everyone should defer to a synthetic preference profile that nobody actually endorses, even under idealization.

(7) If that's the right standard for AI alignment, it's also the right standard for political legitimacy: replace democratic preferences with the aggregated CEV.

(8) But that's clearly authoritarian in a way that makes the concept useless for alignment.

So where's the slip? I really want to keep (1) and (5) if I can -- (1) is the core idea and (5) feels like a consistency requirement. But maybe I'm smuggling something in (3) about how aggregation works? Or is the problem that (2) only applies within an individual, not across? I can't tell if this is an equivocation or if I just have to bite one of the bullets. Which step do you reject, and what's the cost?
