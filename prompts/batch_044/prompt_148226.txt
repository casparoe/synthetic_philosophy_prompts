I serve on the capital-projects committee of a regional public-works authority, where we evaluate infrastructure proposals whose benefits fall decades or centuries apart: a flood-defense wall, a geological carbon-storage site, and long-lived containment systems. Our staff currently collapses every comparison into a net-present-value formula using market interest rates, but that conflates pure time preference—the philosophical claim that future welfare should not be weighted less merely because it occurs later—with risk premiums, capital scarcity, and uncertainty about future populations. I want you to operationalize the principle that pure temporal discounting is unjustified (while allowing that near and distant outcomes may still diverge for other reasons) into a reusable decision procedure that a non-economist board member can run in about twenty minutes per proposal, with clear sign-off authority resting with the committee chair. The deliverable should be a step-by-step flowchart in words: the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to continue, where the procedure halts with a recommendation, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than facts.

Please construct the procedure so it separates adjustments for pure time preference from adjustments for physical risk, intergenerational equity, and the possibility that beneficiaries will not exist, then apply it to three cases: (a) a seawall with benefits concentrated in thirty years; (b) a carbon-capture and mineralization project whose climate benefits are measurable only after 120 years and require continuous cross-generational maintenance funding; and (c) a nuclear-waste containment retrofit that costs present taxpayers heavily but delivers safety benefits almost entirely beyond 200 years—a case where I expect the procedure will recommend funding yet the board will find the result politically intolerable. For each case show the procedural path and mark any point where judgment must override mechanics. Then identify two structural misfires—scenarios in which following the checklist mechanically yields a recommendation that clashes with common moral intuition—and clarify whether each misfire indicts the underlying principle or only this institutional translation. Be explicit about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls disguised as boxes. End with a one-sentence summary on its own line beginning with 'Bottom line:' that states the single most important limitation of turning pure time preference into an institutional checklist.
