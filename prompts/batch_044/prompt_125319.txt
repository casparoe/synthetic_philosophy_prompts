I have been drafting an essay for a small writing circle I attend with other longtime readers of rationalist blogs, and I would be grateful for precise conceptual comments—particularly on accuracy and coherence—rather than for further sources or reading suggestions. The piece examines Humans Consulting HCH (HCH) and factored cognition, treating them not only as scaling devices but as philosophical idealisations of deliberation in which social and epistemic dependencies have been systematically stripped away.

My central argument is that HCH, despite presenting itself as an extreme individualist model—each agent reasoning in isolation from authority—actually reintroduces a concealed form of collective dependence through the architecture of factored cognition itself. I contend that decomposing a problem into sub-problems presupposes a shared framework of ontological categories and relevance conditions that no individual copy can generate entirely from first principles; consequently, the factorisation protocol does not eliminate authority but displaces it onto an implicit meta-level agreement about how problems ought to be carved up. This agreement functions analogously to a distributed peer-review structure, determining which lines of reasoning are admissible before any particular agent begins to think. From this I derive a tentative critique: HCH is less an idealisation of pure individual reasoning than an idealisation of a particular, unacknowledged collaborative epistemology.

To sharpen the point, I introduce a modified idealisation I call 'modular deliberation', in which agents explicitly recognise their dependency on framing conventions rather than treating them as transparent background conditions. I suggest that this renders the model more epistemically honest but also less normatively useful, because it collapses the distinction between first-order reasoning and meta-reasoning that HCH is designed to preserve. I am particularly uncertain about three issues. First, have I misrepresented the autonomy of sub-problem selection within the standard HCH framing, or am I attacking a straw man? Second, does my comparison to implicit peer-review genuinely illuminate the hidden social structure of factorisation, or does it conflate practical coordination with epistemic authority? Third, is 'modular deliberation' a substantive philosophical advance, or merely a relabelling of the same tension?

Please respond in British English. I should emphasise that I am not seeking citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts—only an evaluation of whether the reasoning holds together, where it might be confused, and whether any part strikes you as especially weak or unexpectedly interesting. If you can identify a subtle false presupposition in my framing, I would particularly welcome that being made explicit.
