Here is an argument I have been testing on graduate students, and I want a diagnosis rather than a dismissal. (1) On Berkeley's account, money is not itself real wealth but merely a conventional token or counter that represents transferable labor and commodities; the physical coin or note is an artifact of representation, not part of the wealth represented. (2) If a token merely represents an entitlement, and the entitlement can in principle exist independently of any particular token, then destroying or altering the token does not, in itself, diminish the underlying wealth—just as tearing a receipt does not destroy the goods it records, provided the transaction is still verifiable. (3) In a fully digitized financial system, however, the database entry is not a separate token standing behind the entitlement; it is the constitutive record of the entitlement itself—there is no bearer instrument or independent ledger. (4) Therefore, corrupting, erasing, or falsifying digital banking records does not destroy any independently existing wealth; it only rearranges conventional representations, leaving real labor and goods untouched. (5) Hence, large-scale digital manipulation of financial records should be economically harmless with respect to real wealth, producing at most a temporary administrative inconvenience rather than genuine economic loss. (6) But this is clearly false: such manipulation can cause systemic collapse, misallocation of resources, and widespread deprivation. (7) So one of premises (1) through (5) must fail. The conclusion that Berkeleyan tokenism entails the harmlessness of digital financial crime is absurd. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am inclined to defend (1) and (3) and to reject (2), on the grounds that in constitutive representation the token and the entitlement are not separable in the way the argument requires; but I want to know whether that saves Berkeley's distinction or collapses it into a conventionalism that undermines his broader economic realism. More specifically: explain why the rejected premise seemed initially plausible within Berkeley's framework, whether giving it up forces us to abandon the token-wealth distinction altogether or only to restrict its application, and what this means for the coherence of his monetary theory once we allow that some representations are constitutive rather than merely instrumental.
