I'm drafting an essay for an advanced seminar on philosophy of mind and value, and I want to test whether the central argument holds up before I expand it into a full draft. The essay argues that pain asymbolia—cases in which patients report intact nociceptive localization and intensity judgments but deny any suffering or behavioral avoidance—poses a deeper problem for representationalist theories of pain's badness than is usually acknowledged.

Rather than treating asymbolia as a mere disconnection between sensory content and motivational output, I propose that it reveals the badness of pain to be constituted by an integrated second-order evaluative tagging mechanism that can be selectively disrupted without altering first-order sensory representation. On this hybrid view, the sensory content represents tissue damage, but the normative weight—the reason pain is bad for the subject—depends on a further layer of evaluative architecture that is not reducible to that representational content. I then anticipate the standard reply that asymbolia only severs the affective-motivational pathway while leaving a purely sensory pain intact, and I counter that the clinical reports do not support a clean sensory-affective bifurcation: patients do not describe a remaining unpleasant sensory quality that they simply do not mind; rather, they describe sensations that are no longer experienced as bad at all, which suggests the evaluative dimension is not an optional add-on but part of what makes the state a pain rather than a mere bodily signal. From there I draw a broader implication: if normative significance can be dissociated in this way, then mental-state accounts of value need to build in architectural conditions on integration, not just content conditions.

Could you comment on the accuracy of this framing, especially whether I am over-reading the clinical phenomenology, and on whether the inference from dissociability to constitutive dependence is sound or whether it runs together causal and metaphysical dependence? Please lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then provide your reasoning with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
