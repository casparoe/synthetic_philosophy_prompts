Analyze the September 2007 run on Northern Rock through the lens of rational information cascades. Map it carefully: depositors each had private signals about solvency versus liquidity, observed the growing queues and the leaked news that the Bank of England had agreed to emergency liquidity support, and updated sequentially. Say exactly where the canonical cascade logic applies—who held prior beliefs, what actions were observable, when a depositor should rationally ignore her private signal—and where it breaks down. In particular, did the cascade depend on true informational externalities, or was it a coordination failure driven by common knowledge of panic? Then test the theory against the ending: the run stopped after the Treasury announced a blanket deposit guarantee. Does that termination show the herding was never fully rational (since rational agents should have updated on the guarantee itself, not merely stopped queuing), or does it reveal that the cascade model is silent about how public commitments restructure payoffs? I want the mapping disciplined—identify the player, the observable action, and what counts as the private signal—and an honest account of what rational herding explains and what it misdescribes about the panic.
