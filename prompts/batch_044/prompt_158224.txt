In both legal theory and recent work on autonomous-system governance, a debate about self-amending rules has stalled for decades without meaningful movement. On one side stand hierarchical formalists, who accept the derivation—associated with Alf Ross—that a rule governing its own amendment generates an infinite regress of meta-rules unless authority is strictly stratified by level. They therefore treat any apparent self-amendment, whether in constitutional amendment procedures or in digital governance charters that authorize a board to revise its own revision protocol, as either logically incoherent or as concealed delegation to an unstated higher-order norm. On the other side stand practice-based institutionalists, who observe that functioning legal systems do revise their amendment mechanisms through constituent assemblies, referendum processes, or version-controlled charter updates, and who conclude that the formal model misdescribes legal authority by conflating a rule as an enduring type with successive rule-tokens, or by ignoring the temporal and role-based indexing built into institutional practice.

What makes the stall particularly frustrating is that both sides agree on the formal structure of the paradox, both agree that real and simulated institutions do in fact alter their own procedures, and neither side denies that logical consistency is a virtue. Yet the formalists continue to insist that the practice is conceptually confused, while the institutionalists continue to insist that the formal derivation is descriptively irrelevant and that versioning resolves the difficulty.
