After a long argument last night with a friend over whether deception can leave consent intact if the deceived party never knew what they were actually authorizing, I need a memo for Thursday’s meeting of our institutional behavioral-research ethics board. We must rule on Protocol 447, a study of decision-making competence under social pressure that recruits adults with mild cognitive impairment. The investigators plan to tell participants they are playing a cooperative economic game, concealing that the study actually measures susceptibility to undue influence by a confederate; full debriefing is promised afterward. Some board members treat this as routine methodological deception correctable by disclosure; others argue that when the deception targets the very capacity being measured—competence to assess risk and authorize participation—consent is structurally undermined rather than merely informationally deficient. Please draft the board’s formal opinion recommending a concrete decision—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—along with an implementation time frame. The memo should take the strongest opposing view seriously: specifically, engage the idea that competence is domain-specific and dynamically situated, so that manipulating the hidden frame of decision-making does not just withhold information but actively corrupts the conditions under which valid authorization could occur, making retrospective debriefing unable to restore what was never properly given. Keep the document actionable, readable by the researchers, and free of bibliographies or appeals to named authorities.
