I am preparing to present a paper at a political-philosophy seminar next term and wish to be thoroughly prepared for the objections that will arise during the discussion and defence. The thesis I advance extends Simone Weil’s familiar critique of political parties—her argument that party membership necessarily corrupts individual judgment by substituting collective loyalty for direct, attentive engagement with justice—into territory she did not explicitly treat. More precisely, I contend that the harm is not merely moral but epistemic, generated by a mechanism I call collective attention-capture: the binary structure of factional allegiance collapses the cognitive distance that Weilian attention requires, and I apply this analysis to contemporary citizens’ assemblies and deliberative mini-publics, arguing that even informal, temporary coalitions within these bodies reproduce the same structural corruption, so that genuinely just deliberation demands an active dissolution of factional identity rather than simple procedural neutrality among participants.

The audience will be composed chiefly of democratic theorists and scholars of deliberative democracy who treat parties and factions as indispensable mediating institutions—protecting pluralism, preventing majoritarian domination, and supplying the epistemic diversity that collective decision-making requires. They are likely to regard Weilian metaphysics with suspicion unless it can be rendered in institutional vocabulary, and they will defend parties on functionalist grounds. I ask you to compose this response as if it were a private briefing letter in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written in the manner of a formal email. Within it, order the objections by how probable and damaging they are, phrase each in the vocabulary these scholars actually use—mediating institutions, functional representation, epistemic diversity, institutional design—indicate for each whether I ought to concede part of it upfront, provide a concise reply, and note precisely where that reply is weakest. Please also include the objection I most hope will not be raised: the charge that my reliance on Weil’s notion of attention covertly imports a controversial theology of grace and therefore cannot perform the normative work I assign to it without an unargued metaphysical commitment. Use British spelling throughout.
