In decision-theory threads on rationalist forums, I keep seeing the Pasadena paradox handled through a particular physical analogy: the undefined expectation is likened to an analytical balance with a broken spring, so that each successive weight placed on the pan sends it swinging infinitely between opposing extremes without ever reaching an equilibrium position. From this image people draw a strong normative conclusion — that comparing expected values in the Pasadena case is not merely unknown but structurally incoherent, like trying to read a measurement from a device that lacks any stable state; therefore, they claim, rational agents should abandon expected-utility maximization altogether and revert to dominance or satisficing rules. I want an audit of this comparison. Lay out an explicit correspondence table: what in the paradox plays the role of the pan, the successive weights, the broken spring, the absence of equilibrium, and the act of "reading" a result. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely damage the argument from those that are only cosmetic. In particular, does the analogy quietly import the assumption that rational choice requires a convergent point-value, or that an undefined expectation is a kind of physical malfunction rather than a perfectly well-defined but non-convergent mathematical object? Does the conclusion — that EV reasoning ought to be rejected in this case — survive once those smuggled premises are on the table? And if the balance analogy misleads more than it clarifies, what would be a more accurate analogy for what undefined expectation actually does to rational comparison?
