I serve on the Ministry of Justice’s Penal Architecture and Ethics Review Panel, appointed last autumn, and the brief has recently become personal: my younger brother was remanded three months ago to a Category A establishment, and I have since observed how its design treats rehabilitation as an administrative afterthought rather than a structural premise. The Panel must recommend, by the end of this quarter, whether to approve a pilot unit modelled on the Norwegian Halden prison’s domestic-scale principles—private cells arranged around shared kitchens, normalised staff-inmate interaction, and the explicit architectural aim of preserving social personhood—within one of our existing high-security estates, or to reject the proposal on grounds of cultural and security incommensurability. I need a formal written opinion, suitable for circulation to the Minister and the Prison Governors’ Association, that recommends a concrete course of action with a clear time frame—whether a phased feasibility study with defined metrics, an immediate rejection with conditions for reconsideration, or approval of a limited pilot subject to quarterly review. The memo must engage seriously with the strongest objection: that importing Halden’s model risks producing what might be called rehabilitative theatre, in which prisoners learn to enact normative personhood for institutional reward without internal transformation, whereas the explicit degradation characteristic of American-style mass incarceration, however brutal, at least avoids this moral deception by maintaining a transparent boundary between punishment and personhood. It should also address the competing philosophical claim that architecture itself constitutes a form of moral recognition—that denying prisoners domestic space is not merely punitive but ontologically reductive, treating them as beings permanently incapable of future moral agency. Please adopt the measured, precise tone appropriate to a government advisory body, free of rhetorical flourish. Finally, I ask that the response state explicitly what would change its recommendation—whether empirical evidence on recidivism, a philosophical argument about moral agency under coercion, or a practical security failure in the pilot—and that it identify the precise point in its reasoning most vulnerable to collapse.
