I'm preparing a ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group—mostly philosophers of science with no background in metaethics—on evolutionary debunking arguments. Rather than a generic survey, I want to teach the specific variant that targets non-naturalist normative realism by explaining the evolutionary origins of deontic modal intuitions: the sense that certain actions are strictly forbidden regardless of consequences. Plan the explanatory arc: first, what conceptual dependency must be established before the debunking argument can be heard—whether it is the causal-inertness problem for non-natural properties, the distinction between origin and justification, or something else, and in what order; second, the single concrete moral intuition that should carry the entire demonstration, specifying why it should be a direct-action prohibition rather than a cost-benefit judgment and what features it must have; third, the predictable misconceptions—likely that an evolutionary explanation genetically falsifies the belief, that such explanations are unfalsifiable just-so stories, and that the debunking extends indiscriminately to logical or mathematical beliefs—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be defused; fourth, one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that distinguishes someone who has grasped the argument structure from someone who merely agrees with the skeptical conclusion; and finally, what I should explicitly leave out at this level—perhaps the full evolutionary game-theoretic literature on cooperation, or the distinctions between reliabilist and process-based epistemologies—and how to state that omission without undermining the session.
