For my philosophy club's upcoming debate on consciousness and neural division, I'm comparing interpretations of split-brain patients following corpus callosotomy. Please rank the following three frameworks by their plausibility in explaining both the observed behavioral dissociation and the patient's persistent subjective sense of being one person. Assign each a score from 1 to 10 and justify the ranking in a brief paragraph per entry.

The frameworks are: (A) the classic dual-mind interpretation, according to which severing the corpus callosum creates two independent conscious subjects within one skull; (B) the unified-but-partitioned view, which holds that a single conscious system remains intact but loses cross-hemispheric integration, producing disjoint access without genuine multiplicity of minds; and (C) what I'll call the interhemispheric arbitration model—a less commonly discussed proposal that the corpus callosum operates not merely as an information bridge but as a continuous suppression mechanism for competing interpretations, so that split-brain patients do not harbor two minds but rather possess one mind with a disabled conflict-resolution layer, causing each hemisphere to generate post-hoc confabulations of unified agency.

In your reasoning, address specifically whether framework C offers any explanatory advantage over B regarding why patients rarely report feeling like two people despite clear hemispheric specialization, and whether any of the three can accommodate the phenomenon of intermanual conflict without multiplying subjects.
