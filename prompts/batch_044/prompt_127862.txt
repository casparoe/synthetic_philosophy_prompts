I've been thinking about the 1755 Lisbon earthquake — it struck on All Saints' Day, destroyed much of the city, killed tens of thousands, and hit churches that were full of worshippers, including many children — and I've been trying to understand it not just as a historical tragedy but as a case study in the problem of evil. I know the broad strokes of theodicy: the idea that human free will explains moral evil but not earthquakes; the thought that suffering might build souls or character; and the skeptical position that human minds are too limited to see God's reasons. But I don't want a generic summary. I want you to take the actual features of this specific disaster and run them through each of these frameworks with discipline.

So first, map the earthquake precisely onto the different kinds of theodicy. For the free-will defense, explain exactly what it can and cannot claim about an event with no human agent, and whether its usual scope restriction to moral evil means it simply remains silent about Lisbon or whether it needs an auxiliary story. For the soul-making view, consider not just survivors but the victims who died instantly: does the theory require comparing the character formed in survivors against the potential lost in the dead, and can it say anything meaningful about children who never reached an age of moral development? For skeptical theism, ask whether the mere scale and religious timing of the destruction — worshippers killed during Mass — constitute the kind of apparently gratuitous evil that motivates the evidential problem, and whether the skeptical reply that God might have a reason we cannot see actually addresses the pattern or just dissolves the question.

Then I want the analysis to reverse direction: what does the Lisbon episode reveal about theodicy itself? There's a distinction I keep running into between a full theodicy, which offers actual reasons why God permitted this specific evil, and a more modest defense, which only shows that God's existence is not logically contradicted by it. Did the eighteenth-century crisis after the earthquake — the public shock, the poetic responses, the philosophical debates about optimism — treat the disaster as a logical impossibility for belief in God, or as something that made belief less reasonable without strictly disproving it? And does applying that modern logical-versus-evidential distinction to the historical reaction help us understand why people lost faith, or does it impose an anachronistic framework that misses how they actually experienced the event?
