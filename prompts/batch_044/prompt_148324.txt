In my philosophy of politics reading group, two members have been deadlocked over the democratic boundary problem, and I am reporting their exchange as a bystander without taking sides. I want a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as they actually unfolded, plus a diagnosis of where they are talking past each other and what both are missing.

One member, Mara, began by defending an all-affected-interests principle: anyone whose important interests are significantly shaped by a collective decision should have democratic standing with respect to it. The other, Leo, objected that this renders the demos unworkably expansive—if a nation sets a carbon tax that influences global climate patterns, does every affected person on Earth acquire a vote? Mara conceded that binary affectedness fails here, but proposed replacing it with a graded criterion she called sustained structural exposure: you belong to the demos for a given decision domain only if you live within the institutional reach of its implementation over time, not merely if you suffer diffuse ripple effects. Leo granted that this blocks over-inclusion, but charged that it smuggles in the very boundary it claims to justify—“institutional reach” is delimited by jurisdictional borders, and those borders presuppose a prior demos that authorized them, so the criterion is circular. Mara replied that jurisdictional reach can be defined functionally—say, by the enforcement capacity of a watershed authority or a labor-regulatory body—without requiring prior democratic authorization of the border itself, so no vicious circularity arises. Leo then pivoted to an all-subjected principle: democratic membership should track coercive legal subjection, which yields determinate boundaries (those bound by enforceable law) and avoids arbitrary grading altogether. Mara countered that this excludes precisely the groups most in need of democratic protection—future generations, undocumented residents subject to severe informal coercion, and cross-border communities affected by upstream extraction—while including transient visitors who happen to be present when a law is enforced. Leo responded that these are manageable supplements: you retain the core subjection principle for membership and add stakeholder mechanisms for affected non-members, without dissolving democratic accountability into cosmopolitan vagueness. At this point the argument stalled. Mara accused Leo of treating democracy as a membership club for the legally included rather than as a check on power over lives; Leo accused Mara of dissolving decision procedures into an unbounded affectedness that makes collective choice impossible. They seem partly right, but I cannot tell where the clash is substantive versus terminological.

Please adjudicate specifically: Is Mara’s sustained structural exposure criterion genuinely non-circular, or does Leo’s circularity charge hold once we distinguish functional enforcement reach from democratically authorized borders? Is Leo correct that Mara’s view risks dissolving accountability, and is Mara correct that Leo’s view arbitrarily excludes vulnerable non-subjected groups? What distinction—perhaps between constitutive and regulative boundaries, or between domain-specific and domain-transcendent membership—might repair the disagreement? Give a verdict on which precise claims stand, which collapse, and what each party is failing to see that would allow them to concede ground without abandoning their core concern.
