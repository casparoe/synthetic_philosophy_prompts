The Mary-room knowledge argument and the physicalist responses to it have been replayed in the literature for decades with little apparent movement: defenders insist that Mary learns a genuinely new fact about phenomenal redness, while opponents invoke ability hypotheses, acquaintance accounts, or phenomenal-concept strategies to explain away the intuition without conceding an ontological gap. In my reading, the exchange has reached a stalemate in which each side treats the other's central move as question-begging—physicalists say the knowledge-argument defender smuggles in an unexplained epistemic primitive, while defenders say physicalists reduce qualitative knowledge to a cognitive capacity or conceptual reorganization that leaves the original datum untouched.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about the ontology of consciousness but about what should count as "complete physical information" in the first place: one side reads it as exhaustive propositional knowledge de dicto, including every structural and functional truth, while the other implicitly demands de re acquaintance with the phenomenal property itself, treating demonstrative phenomenal concepts as unacquirable without direct instantiation. If that is right, the debate may be partially verbal—hinging on whether "knowing all physical facts" permits demonstrative knowledge of properties one has never instantiated—or it may hide a substantive disagreement about whether phenomenal concepts are essentially indexical and whether indexical knowledge requires experiential access.

But I want a diagnosis rather than another round of adjudication. Is the impasse due to bedrock intuitions about what explanation requires? Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about whether phenomenal truths are a priori derivable from physical truths given a specific theory of concept possession—doing the work? Or are the two sides actually answering different questions, one about semantic content and one about metaphysical structure, under the single heading of "the knowledge argument"? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a precise formalization of the phenomenal-concept strategy that makes its commitments explicit, an empirical result about concept acquisition in absent phenomenal experience, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the apparent gap, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and abandon it?
