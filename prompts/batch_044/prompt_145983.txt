I am a doctoral student in philosophy of social science, and I am developing a dissertation framework—tentatively called nested counterfactual stability—for evaluating whether comparative-historical explanations preserve substitutability across levels of analysis. Applying it to the Great Divergence debate, I have become convinced that the canonical formulation asking why Britain and not China industrialized first is logically malformed in a specific way. Once we accept Kenneth Pomeranz's comparative evidence that the Yangtze Delta around 1800 matched England in market integration, labor productivity, and life expectancy, the unit of comparison shifts: we are no longer comparing two states but two regions embedded in radically different imperial fiscal-military structures. This means the original question commits an aggregation error by treating Britain and Qing China as interchangeable causal units. From this, I have inferred that Joseph Needham's parallel framing—why modern science and technology developed in Europe rather than in China—escapes this particular flaw, because it compares civilizational knowledge regimes rather than state-level economic aggregates, thereby avoiding the nesting problem altogether. Yet I worry that I am smuggling in a hidden presupposition: perhaps the Needham formulation assumes an equally problematic coherence at the civilizational level, or perhaps my identification of the aggregation error in the economic comparison is itself confused with a failure of counterfactual stability. Could you help me determine whether my diagnosis of the logical flaw in the Britain-versus-China formulation is sound, and whether the Needham question genuinely avoids it, or whether both questions share a deeper misapprehension about the substitutability of large-scale historical units?
