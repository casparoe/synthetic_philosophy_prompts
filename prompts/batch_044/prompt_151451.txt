As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am designing a ninety-minute workshop for policy analysts with no formal philosophy training, and I want to teach Bernard Williams's integrity objection—centered on the claim that some moral deliberations contain one thought too many—not as a museum piece but as a living diagnostic for institutional ethics. I need a committed teaching plan: take a definite position on how the objection functions and why it matters for organizational decision-making, rather than balancing it against rival views and leaving the analysts to choose. Lay out the explanatory arc: the conceptual prerequisite that must be in place before the objection can even register, the single concrete scenario that should carry the explanation (drawn from bureaucratic or organizational life rather than the familiar domestic rescue case, with specific structural features that make the excess-thought pattern visible), the misconceptions these analysts will likely form—perhaps that Williams is defending mere selfishness, or that integrity rules out all consequentialist weighing—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted, plus one diagnostic question I can ask at the end to test whether they have grasped the underlying structure rather than just adopted the slogan. Also specify what should be openly excluded at this level—say, the full framework of moral luck or the systematic contrast with Kantian universalization—while noting those boundaries. The session should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since the point is conceptual transmission, not scholarly orientation.
