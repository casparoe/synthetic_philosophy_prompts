In debates about rational choice and decision paralysis, commentators regularly invoke Buridan's ass: a rational agent confronted with two options of exactly equal value is likened to the donkey suspended between two identical bales of hay, paralyzed and doomed to starve unless some arbitrary mechanism—randomization, a whim, or an external nudge—breaks the deadlock. The analogy is deployed to defend the claim that rational preference orderings must be complete, that genuine indifference is unstable, and that any workable theory of choice must include an explicit tie-resolution procedure; otherwise agency dissolves into inaction. I am skeptical that the image carries as much weight as its users assume, particularly because it tends to conflate Buridan's own metaphysical account—where the will possesses a liberty of indifference that allows it to determine itself without prior rational determination—with modern decision-theoretic frameworks that treat indifference as a representational failure rather than a feature of agency.

Could you audit the analogy in detail? Specifically, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the ass, the two bales, the starvation, and the supposed tie-breaking twitch? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended argument from those that do not. Where does the picture break? Does it quietly import a deterministic or mechanistic model of choice that Buridan's distinction between the intellect's judgment of equality and the will's power of election was designed to reject? Does it smuggle in the assumption that inaction is catastrophic in a way that applies only to biological need, not to rational deliberation? And does the analogy obscure the difference between an agent who lacks a reason to choose and an agent whose will is free to create one?

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that rational choice requires an explicit resolution mechanism for ties—survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. If the image fails, what would be a more accurate analogy for the modern problem of choice without preference, one that does not presuppose the very completeness conditions it is meant to defend?
