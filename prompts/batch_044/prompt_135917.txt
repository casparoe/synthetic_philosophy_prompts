I've been reflecting on moral blame after a workplace disagreement. A colleague defended an employee who carried out a damaging directive by repeating, "If you couldn't have done anything differently, you can't be held responsible." She seemed to treat this as self-evident, but I suspect the sentence collapses several different claims into one. Could you lay out the precise theses that might hide inside that slogan — distinguishing, for example, readings about physical impossibility, psychological compulsion, rational unavailability, and the kind of "could" that philosophers discuss in connection with hidden manipulators or overridden wills? For each reading, indicate whether it is a trivial truth, a defensible but substantive principle, or a claim that has been shown false by thought experiments in which someone is clearly responsible despite lacking an open alternative. I want this as a menu rather than a single interpretation, and I want to know which version my colleague actually needed to excuse the employee, versus which versions would leave her argument unsupported. Please treat the phrase as a common saying rather than attributing it to one specific thinker; I'm interested in what the words themselves commit us to, and which commitments would survive once we notice that responsibility sometimes seems intact even when every door but one was locked from the outside.
