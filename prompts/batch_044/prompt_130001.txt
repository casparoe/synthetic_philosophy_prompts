At a philosophy of mind reading group, two participants—call them Mara and Elias—got into an extended argument about split-brain patients and whether callosal section reveals one divided consciousness or two. I want you to adjudicate their exchange move by move, taking a definite position at each step and explaining what both sides are missing where relevant.

Mara began by saying that the classic split-brain experiments establish a clear case of divided awareness: when visual input is restricted to the left visual field, the right hemisphere can guide a manual response that the left-hemisphere speech center cannot verbally report, which demonstrates two simultaneous streams of experience with no shared access.

Elias replied that this ignores cross-cueing via subcortical pathways. Even after corpus callosum section, emotional and motor information can travel through brainstem and limbic routes, so in naturalistic settings the hemispheres are not hermetically sealed. The laboratory conditions artificially isolate them; everyday behavior remains unified, so postulating two minds is largely an artifact of testing.

Mara countered that indirect subcortical signaling is not the same as the direct cortical integration that normally binds experience into a single field. She compared it to two people in adjacent rooms sending messages through a mail slot: there is communication, but not the kind of unified access that constitutes one consciousness. If unity can be preserved by any causal link, then a person talking to their mirror image would count as one mind.

Elias said Mara was defining unity circularly: she assumes only direct callosal connection counts as integration, then uses that definition to declare indirect routes insufficient. He pointed out that intact brains also show confabulation—people invent explanations for actions they did not cause—so the left hemisphere's false reports about right-hemisphere actions are not special evidence of a second mind, just the interpreter module doing its usual job. Meanwhile, the patient has one continuous biography, one body, and one social identity.

Mara responded that confabulation in intact subjects differs in kind: there is no rival hemispheric source generating the action. In split-brain cases, the left hemisphere literally lacks access to the right hemisphere's reasons, yet it fabricates a story, which suggests a genuine boundary between two centers of awareness rather than a single center with faulty memory.

Elias finished by arguing that the right hemisphere lacks the linguistic and narrative capacities needed for personhood; it can match objects and guide actions, but it does not construct a self-model. So the situation is not two people in one skull but one narrative self—localized in the left hemisphere's interpreter—coupled with a non-narrative skill system. The unity of consciousness is preserved precisely because only one of these subsystems is a subject.

Please go through this exchange step by step: rate each of Mara's and Elias's moves as clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, what both are missing—particularly about whether functional integration or narrative self-modeling should be the criterion for unity—and identify the single weakest link in the argument for the side you ultimately favor. Give your answer with a committed stance rather than a balanced survey of options.
