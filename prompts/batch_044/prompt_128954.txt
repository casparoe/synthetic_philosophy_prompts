I am preparing a long-form feature on the epistemology of ancient history for a science-and-ideas readership, and I have become preoccupied with a methodological divide that is rarely staged with philosophical rigor outside specialist circles. On one side stand the critical source realists: scholars who maintain that disciplined cross-examination of testimony, material culture, and linguistic patterns—running from Herodotean comparative inquiry through Thucydidean critical autopsy—can yield genuinely probable, if always defeasible, knowledge about events that lie millennia behind us. On the other stand the radical archival skeptics: thinkers who argue that the evidentiary chasm between any present interpretation and a past event is not merely practical but principled, because every chronicle is already a narrative construction, every silence in the archive is overdetermined by power and preservation bias, and no amount of cross-source triangulation can bridge the gap without smuggling in presentist assumptions about coherence and causation.

For the piece I want to treat these two camps not as caricatures but as parties to an adversarial collaboration conducted in good faith. Please draft the joint statement they should have produced. Open with a numbered section of agreed claims—about the nature of historical testimony, the legitimate role of material and epigraphic evidence, the irreducible limits of direct access, and specifically about how negative inference drawn from systematic absences across independent source traditions ought to be weighted, including the recently debated practice of triangulated negative inference, in which historians treat convergent archival silences as evidential rather than merely incidental. Follow this with a disagreements table: each side’s position phrased in wording its own proponents would accept, particularly regarding whether disciplined source criticism can ever recover a specific fact—say, the administrative or logistical scale of an ancient imperial campaign—or whether it can produce only internally coherent narratives that remain agnostic about correspondence. Then identify the precise cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party—including, I suspect, whether coherence across independent sources is better explained by correspondence to a shared past reality or by shared narrative conventions and intertextual borrowing, and whether triangulated negative inference genuinely adds epistemic value or merely compounds interpretive risk. For each crux, name a concrete historical case or evidentiary problem both sides agree is relevant—for example, a contested interpretation of a particular archival gap or a material find that either corroborates or undermines a chronicle account of a distant event—and record in advance what each camp predicts that case demonstrates. Conclude by assessing whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, specifying which portion and why. Present the result as a polished, publication-ready document that requires no bibliographies, citations, or externally verified references, and that a well-informed reader could evaluate without searching for sources.
