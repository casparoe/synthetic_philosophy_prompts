A study-group argument on democratic legitimacy and constitutional monarchy has left me unsure where the error lies, since the reasoning looks valid but its conclusion seems to prove far too much. (1) Democratic legitimacy requires that all coercive state authority be traceable, even if indirectly, to popular authorization. (2) In the UK, Sweden, and Japan, the monarch retains formal reserve powers—refusal of royal assent, dissolution of parliament, appointment of the prime minister—that are not themselves elected or authorized by the governed. (3) These powers are not purely decorative: in Japan the Emperor’s appointment of the Prime Minister under Article 6 remains constitutionally necessary; in Sweden the monarch’s role in government formation was only removed by the 1974 Instrument of Government; and in the UK royal assent, though not withheld since 1708, remains a legal prerequisite for enactment. (4) So constitutional monarchies contain a structural pocket of coercive authority that lacks direct democratic authorization. (5) If democratic legitimacy requires the complete absence of any such structural pocket, then these monarchies are illegitimate. (6) But democratic republics also contain emergency provisions—martial law, constitutional suspension, executive orders—that create structurally identical pockets of unauthorized coercive power. (7) Therefore, if (5) is true, all modern democracies are illegitimate, which is absurd. (8) Hence (5) must be false, and democratic legitimacy in fact tolerates embedded pockets of unauthorized authority.

I want to preserve (1) and (4): authorization matters, and monarchies genuinely contain these non-democratic pockets. The suspicious move seems to be between (6) and (7), but I wonder whether I should instead reject (6) by arguing that republican emergency powers are democratically authorized in advance through constitutional enactment, whereas monarchical powers are hereditary and therefore not anticipatorily authorized. If I take that route, I need a theory of “anticipatory authorization” that distinguishes constitutional monarchies from republican emergency powers—yet both are ultimately defined by constitutional law enacted through democratic procedures, so the distinction may collapse. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the broader theory of democratic legitimacy? In particular, if I keep (1) and reject (5), do I commit myself to saying that hereditary office can be compatible with democracy so long as its powers are dormant, or must I deny that dormancy makes any normative difference?
