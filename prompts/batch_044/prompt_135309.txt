I'm planning a single seventy-minute seminar for second-year philosophy students who have completed an introductory course in philosophy of mind but have no clinical training. I want a teaching plan — not the explanation itself — for the thesis that pain asymbolia undermines the claim that pain is bad solely in virtue of its intrinsic sensory quality.

Begin by mapping the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be secured first — specifically, the separation between sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational dimensions — before asymbolia can do any philosophical work, and why reversing this order causes collapse into confusion with analgesia or with masochism. Then specify the carrying example. Do not use the standard clinical vignette of a patient laughing at injury; instead, construct a comparative pair: a hypothetical mountaineer who reports full nociceptive intensity during severe tissue stress but describes the signal as structurally informative and entirely lacking in aversiveness, set against a phantom-limb patient who exhibits vigorous withdrawal behaviour despite the complete absence of nociceptive input. State exactly what properties this pair must possess — and what they must lack — so that the students see that aversion can be detached from sensation in both directions.

Next, lay out the misconception map in sequence. I anticipate that students will initially think the mountaineer simply does not feel it properly, conflating absence of aversion with absence of sensation; identify precisely where in the explanatory arc this should be intercepted. I also expect them to infer that if the sensory quality is not bad, then bodily damage is morally neutral; say where this second misconception should be defused and what intermediate idea must already be in place. Include a check for understanding at the end: one question that distinguishes someone who grasps that the badness resides in the motivational response from someone who has only memorised the term asymbolia.

Finally, be explicit about what should be left out at this level — for instance, specific neuroanatomical pathways or clinical diagnostic criteria — and justify the omission. State also what should not be mentioned at all, such as bibliographic references or further reading, since this session has no follow-up.

Please provide the plan in British English, with clear headings for the sequence, the example, the misconceptions, the diagnostic question, and the exclusions.
