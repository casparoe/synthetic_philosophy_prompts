I run a philosophy of social science seminar with ten graduate students; we have one 75-minute session. Design an in-class deliberation game that makes the group feel the force of the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation—before anyone names it. Give each student three logically connected propositions about a hypothetical climate policy: P1 (the policy reduces emissions), P2 (it imposes unfair burdens on rural communities), and P3 (if P1 and not P2, then adopt the policy). Have them vote privately, then apply both premise-based and conclusion-based majority rules so the group discovers contradictory recommendations. Now inject a hidden asymmetry: half the students receive a sealed "domain expertise" envelope that doubles their weight on P1 but forbids disclosure unless the group explicitly discusses epistemic authority. The group must choose an aggregation rule before seeing the weighted distribution, then learn that weighted premise-based aggregation yields the opposite of unweighted conclusion-based aggregation, forcing a direct trade-off between logical consistency and democratic equality. Specify rules, materials, a 15-minute timeline, stakes that require no real money (e.g., the collective recommendation determines a hypothetical shared budget allocation), and the debrief that maps each stage onto the formal structure of the dilemma. Include an honest note on which feature of the impossibility result the activity cannot reproduce, and predict the predictable failure mode on the first run.
