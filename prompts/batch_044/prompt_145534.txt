I'm preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for cognitive-science undergraduates who have read Wittgenstein's remarks on family resemblance but have never encountered the Letter Spirit framework or any formal account of letterform categorization. I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. The concept to be taught is that recognizing a particular mark as an 'a' does not depend on matching a stored prototype or on satisfying necessary and sufficient conditions, but on a network of permissible "slippages" from canonical forms that preserve family-resemblance relations; and that "style"—the consistent, idiosyncratic deviations shared across every letter in a given handwriting or font—is an orthogonal dimension, so that category membership can stay fixed while style varies, and vice versa. The carrying case must be a specific trio shown in two handwriting styles: a canonical double-storey printed 'a', a single-storey handwritten variant with an open top and a long right tail, and an ambiguous form resembling a lowercase 'o' with a short ascender, arranged so that in one style the ambiguous form is read as 'a' and in the other as 'o', making visible that the same deviation can belong to different categories depending on the style context. I need the dependency sequence—what must be established before the trio can be introduced, why the concept of slippage has to precede the concept of style, and where in the sequence the ambiguity should appear—plus the misconceptions students will form in order: that family resemblance means "anything goes," that style is merely noise around a true form, and that the ambiguous form proves there is no real distinction between 'a' and 'o'. For each misconception, specify exactly where in the sequence it should be headed off and how. Then provide a final check that distinguishes genuine understanding of the orthogonal category-style relationship from mere ability to label examples—something that would fail if a student still thinks one dimension reduces to the other. Finally, state what must be left out at this level and say so explicitly—for instance, any algorithmic description of role-model matching, the neurobiology of visual word form recognition, or historical details of typeface design. The audience has no background in typography or computer vision; do not ask for readings, citations, or a bibliography.
