You are an experienced commencement speaker with formal training in moral philosophy. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm two structural constraints: what the speaker must avoid promising to the environmental-studies graduates, and what she must avoid dismissing for the business graduates.

Write the address for the joint commencement of a university's College of Environmental Studies and School of Business. The audience is split: graduates and families from both fields, plus local policymakers; about half are familiar with the inefficacy objection in climate and consumption ethics, while the other half are not.

The philosophical tension must shape the speech's architecture, not decorate it. Work with the standard inefficacy objection—that in large collective harms (climate, industrial meat, electoral outcomes), one person's contribution is causally negligible—together with an injected framing: symbolic dilution in reverse, the idea that as an agent's direct causal share in a collective harm shrinks toward zero with the size of the group, the expressive or symbolic weight of that agent's individual choice scales upward in inverse proportion, without being reducible to causal efficacy. Because of this, the speaker cannot tell the environmental graduates that personal lifestyle choices will fix the climate, nor can she tell the business graduates that ethics is only a matter of institutional design; both moves would betray half the room and erase the tension. Let this constraint determine what can be affirmed, warned against, and hoped for.

Write a 5–6 minute address (approximately 650–800 words). Speak formally but avoid unexplained jargon. Do not cite philosophers, texts, or bibliographies. Do not attribute arguments to named thinkers. Do not resolve the tension into a simple slogan; leave it genuinely live. Conclude with a concrete, non-platitudinous wish for the graduates' immediate next step.
