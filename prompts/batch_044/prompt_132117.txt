Exam question: Philosophers debating cognitive penetration of perception typically distinguish strong penetration, in which high-level beliefs alter the phenomenal character of experience, from weak penetration, in which beliefs influence only pre-perceptual attention or post-perceptual interpretation. More recently, some theorists have proposed an intermediate category—structural penetration—according to which conceptual frameworks reshape the organizational or relational structure of perceptual representations, such as figure-ground segmentation or object-file binding, without modifying phenomenal quality and without operating solely through attentional selection. Consider the documented case of expert radiologists interpreting mammograms: experienced clinicians report that ambiguous tissue patterns appear organized into coherent lesions in ways that novices do not experience, yet they describe the underlying image as visually unchanged in its qualitative properties. Does this case best illustrate structural penetration? And does accepting the possibility of structural penetration undermine Jerry Fodor’s argument that perceptual modules are informationally encapsulated?

Student answer: The radiologist case clearly exemplifies structural penetration rather than weak penetration. In weak penetration, conceptual beliefs merely guide attention to particular regions of the image, but here the radiologist’s diagnostic concepts seem to restructure how the tissue patterns are bound together into object-files—this is an organizational change inside perception itself. Because this restructuring does not alter the phenomenal feel of the image, it does not constitute strong penetration either. Consequently, structural penetration is fully compatible with Fodor’s modularity thesis. Fodor’s encapsulation argument targets only the claim that beliefs can change what an experience is like; since structural penetration leaves phenomenal character intact, it poses no threat to informational encapsulation. Furthermore, if this were merely weak penetration, we would expect novices and experts to differ only in where they look, not in how the patterns are organized, but in fact both groups can examine the same regions yet see different organizational structures. Therefore, recognizing structural penetration actually strengthens the modularist position by showing that conceptual influence can be accommodated within the module’s representational rules without violating encapsulation.
