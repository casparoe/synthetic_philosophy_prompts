In discussions of the special composition question, philosophers often lean on the analogy between ontological composition and municipal incorporation: scattered buildings do not form a city until an administrative charter, functional integration, or territorial adjacency enforced by ordinance binds them, and by parallel reasoning scattered material objects should not form a composite whole unless some analogous special relation holds. The comparison is used to license restricted-composition views and to make unrestricted universalism seem implausible — if we do not hesitate that an unincorporated settlement is not a municipality, why should we hesitate that atoms arranged chair-wise are not a chair? I want a full audit of how far the analogy holds. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the charter, the incorporated boundary, the tax base, the annexation procedure, and the unincorporated settlement? Then identify the disanalogies that matter: which features of municipal incorporation — legal convention, intentional design, voter consent, spatial contiguity maintained by human ordinance — are being quietly imported into mind-independent ontology, and which of those imports actually support the restriction on composition rather than merely making it intuitive? Next, assess whether the conclusion that composition requires a restrictive condition survives once those smuggled features are removed, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argumentative weight. Finally, if this comparison collapses, propose a better analogy for distinguishing unrestricted universalism from restricted views, and say whether it avoids the same problems.
