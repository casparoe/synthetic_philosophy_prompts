Draft the joint statement that a Phenomenal Uniformist and a Functional Pluralist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—specifically, on what, if anything, all pleasures share. The Uniformist maintains that every pleasure contains a distinctive, identifiable hedonic quality or affective tone, however faint or masked by concurrent cognitive or sensory content; on this view, the pleasure of solving a proof, the pleasure of warmth, and the pleasure of malicious satisfaction differ only in their vehicles, not in a shared phenomenal core. The Pluralist maintains that pleasures form a natural kind only at the functional or evaluative level—each is a state that either satisfies a desire or registers that something is good for the agent—and that positing a shared intrinsic feel is an artifact of language and of our tendency to apply one word, "pleasure," to a heterogeneous family.

Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, covering at least: the standard taxonomy of examples (bodily, intellectual, social, aesthetic, malicious); the failure of intensity alone to distinguish pleasure from pain or from mere arousal; the logical independence of "X is desired" and "X feels pleasant"; the existence of anhedonic and dissociative conditions in which liking and wanting come apart; and the fact that both sides accept the burden of explaining why we group these states together. Second, a disagreements table stating each camp's core thesis in wording its own representatives would accept, followed by the precise divergence points: whether cross-modal analogies (to warmth, sweetness, or color) illuminate or mislead; whether a single neural signature would establish phenomenal identity or only functional convergence; and whether synthetic or "purely cognitive" pleasures can be decomposed into a common affective residue plus framing. Third, identify three cruxes: (i) whether patients who report liking without feeling good undermine the claim to a shared phenomenal substrate; (ii) whether evolutionary accounts of reward require a uniform mechanism or can operate on functionally analogous but phenomenologically distinct states; and (iii) whether interoceptive inference models can accommodate pluralism or inherently presuppose a common prediction-error signal. For each crux, specify one concrete case, clinical observation, or theoretical argument both sides agree is relevant, and record each side's explicit prediction about what that case shows. Finally, state whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—if so, identify which terms are being used ambiguously and propose a disambiguation both sides can accept. Write in formal, precise prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or appeals to specific named authorities; write for an audience that knows the conceptual landscape but needs the disagreement rendered exact.
