I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and clinical ethics advising a hospital ethics committee that must repeatedly assess whether patients with conditions affecting decision-making—severe addiction, frontotemporal dementia, treatment-resistant OCD—retain a form of autonomy that is "worth wanting" in the Dennettian sense, rather than asking whether they possess metaphysically robust free will. The committee needs a reusable written procedure they can run in about forty-five minutes per case, with no philosophical training required, but with every point where clinical or institutional judgment must override procedural rules explicitly flagged. The procedure should translate the idea that free will comes in varieties into institutional practice by matching the response to the specific variety that remains intact.

Please construct a decision procedure—ordered questions, sufficient-answer criteria, stop conditions yielding a clear "no," and explicit value-hand-off points—organized around three registers of autonomy-worth: (1) control-worth (capacity for self-modification and evasion of immediate impulse), (2) respect-worth (capacity to participate in a social practice of accountability), and (3) project-worth (capacity to form and pursue long-term life-plans). For each register, specify the clinical or behavioral indicators that count as evidence, the threshold that must be met to proceed to the next register, and the precise formulation of the question that becomes one of institutional values rather than clinical fact.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three cases: (a) a patient with severe opioid addiction who consistently refuses medication-assisted treatment but articulates coherent long-term career goals; (b) a patient with frontotemporal dementia who retains social charm and expresses genuine regret for aggressive outbursts but shows no capacity to modify impulsive behavior in the moment; and (c) a designed case in which the procedure yields a recommendation the committee is likely to reject—specifically, that a patient retains both control-worth and respect-worth but lacks project-worth, producing a restricted-autonomy status that permits coercive short-term welfare intervention but withholds long-term planning authority. In case (c), identify exactly which structural feature of the three-register framework generates the counterintuitive result.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: name at least two categories of patients it will systematically misclassify, and identify one institutional decision—such as resource allocation or criminal referral—that lies entirely outside its scope regardless of the register results.

Please structure your response as follows:
1. The Procedure (approx. 450 words): ordered steps, evidence criteria, stop/no conditions, and judgment hand-off points.
2. Worked Applications (approx. 600 words): step-by-step analysis of cases (a), (b), and (c).
3. Limits and Judgment Flags (approx. 250 words): misclassifications, scope exclusions, and the value-laden steps that cannot be proceduralized.
