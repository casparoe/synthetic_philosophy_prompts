I have been developing a personal philosophy over the past year, mostly through reading about meditation and ethics without any formal academic training, and I want to check whether my core commitments actually cohere or whether I am holding contradictory positions without noticing. I am trying to be honest about what I actually believe rather than what sounds good. Here is my current set:

(a) Tranquilism: the only intrinsic goods are absences — specifically, the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. Positive states such as joy, achievement, love, or understanding have no value in themselves; they are at best instruments that help reduce future craving or suffering.

(b) Moral realism: suffering is not merely undesirable to me; it is objectively bad, and there are mind-independent moral facts about what we ought to do to reduce it.

(c) Evolutionary debunking: our strong instincts to pursue pleasure, status, reproduction, and even social connection are evolutionary artifacts that systematically distort our judgments about what is valuable. We should therefore distrust our spontaneous attraction to these states.

(d) Reflective equilibrium: despite (c), the right method in ethics is still to begin with our considered judgments, formulate general principles, and revise both back and forth until they fit together consistently.

(e) A demanding obligation to others: we have a serious moral duty to act to reduce the suffering and craving of other people, even when doing so requires significant sacrifice of our own comfort or resources.

(f) The withdrawal corollary: once an individual genuinely reaches a state of complete tranquility — no craving, no suffering — there is no further reason for that person to continue acting, creating, relating, or even living, because there is no positive goal to pursue and no internal suffering to remedy.

(g) Non-additive value: the value of tranquility is not cumulative across persons. One person being free from craving does not become twice as good if two people are free; it is a qualitative condition that either holds or fails, so moral reasoning should not treat it like a quantity to be maximized.

My concern is that several of these seem to pull against one another. For example, (e) seems to require that I possess cravings — at least the desire to help others — yet (a) and (c) treat all craving as a distortion or evil, and (f) says the ideal endpoint is the total elimination of such motivation. Meanwhile, (g) suggests we should not aggregate tranquility, but (e) sounds like a universal obligation that applies to every sufferer, which feels like aggregation in practice. There is also a tension between (c) and (d): if evolutionary forces have corrupted our value intuitions, how can we trust the initial judgments that reflective equilibrium requires?
