I oversee safety certification for an autonomous warehouse robot. To justify that our obstacle-avoidance module is reliable, we test it in a digital twin. To justify trusting the twin, we compare it to real-world sensor logs. To justify those logs, we verify the camera calibration. To justify the calibration, we reference the lens manufacturer's specification sheet. To justify the spec sheet, we audit the manufacturer's quality-control process, which relies on automated vision inspection—specifically, the same obstacle-avoidance algorithm we are attempting to certify. Consequently, my chain of justification loops back onto itself. One colleague argues that we should simply stop at an unverified baseline—say, accepting fundamental physical constants without further derivation—and treat that as bedrock. Another colleague insists that any halt is arbitrary, and that rigorous justification demands an infinite downward drill with no endpoint. I am trying to determine whether my circular dependency is a fatal flaw, whether selecting an arbitrary foundation is intellectually honest, or whether an unending regression is the only defensible stance. Can you identify the underlying philosophical structure here, and advise how one should reason about where justification legitimately ends?
