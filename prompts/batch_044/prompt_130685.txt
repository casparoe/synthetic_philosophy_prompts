I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. Last week, two colleagues in a study group—neither trained in philosophy—got into an extended disagreement about the Euthyphro dilemma and whether it actually undermines divine command theory. They asked me to referee, but I do not have the conceptual tools to assess the arguments fairly. I need a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange: who is right about which specific claim, what each person is missing, and who has the stronger overall position. Please provide your answer in American English.

Here is the exchange as it occurred.

Riley opened by stating the classic dilemma. She said that if moral truths depend on God’s commands, then morality is arbitrary: God could have commanded cruelty, and cruelty would have been good. If, on the other hand, God commands actions because they are already and independently good, then morality does not depend on God at all, and divine command theory is irrelevant. Therefore, Riley concluded, the theory is caught between arbitrariness and redundancy.

Jordan introduced a response Riley had not considered: the view that God’s nature is not separate from the Good but is identical to it. On this account, God does not select moral rules from an external menu, nor is God bound by rules outside himself; rather, being essentially good is part of what it means for God to be God, in roughly the same way that being computable is part of what defines a specific algorithm. Jordan argued that this dissolves the dilemma, because there is no gap between commander and command—no “choice” that could have gone differently.

Riley pressed a modal challenge. Even if God’s nature and the Good are identical, she said, we can still ask whether God could have existed with a different nature. If the answer is yes, then the content of morality remains contingent on which nature God happens to have, and arbitrariness returns at a deeper level. If the answer is no—if God necessarily has exactly this nature—then the claim is circular: it defines God as necessarily good without explaining why those particular properties are good rather than others.

Jordan replied that Riley’s demand for an external explanation is a category mistake. Once we grant that God is a necessary being, asking for a justification of God’s nature outside of God is like asking for a justification of mathematical axioms outside mathematics. He also criticized Riley’s algorithm analogy: syntax rules are conventional and changeable, whereas divine nature is not conventional, so comparing them obscures the point.

Riley answered that Jordan has only hidden the problem behind a claim of necessity. If goodness is primitive in God’s nature, she said, then we have no independent reason to accept that nature as authoritative except bare authority itself. She added that Jordan’s algorithm comparison actually supports her side: if syntax rules are conventional, then treating moral rules as constitutive of God makes them similarly conventional, just anchored in one being rather than in social agreement. Thus, Jordan has not shown that morality is non-arbitrary; he has only relocated the arbitrariness into an unchangeable but still unexplained essence.

Please go through their exchange turn by turn. For each of Riley’s and Jordan’s moves, rate the argument as clearly right, partly right but incomplete, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification for each rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify any important distinction or stronger formulation that both of them appear to be missing—something that would change how the disagreement should be evaluated.
