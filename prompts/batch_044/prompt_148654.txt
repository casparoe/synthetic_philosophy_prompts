Please argue that deterministic chaos doesn't actually undermine determinism in the way people usually think. The usual story is that even with perfect laws, tiny errors blow up exponentially so long-term prediction is impossible, which means determinism is practically dead. I think that's conflating two different things: the discreteness of our measurements and computer aproximations, and what's actually happening in the continous dynamics themselves. If you take the ontology seriously — continous state spaces with no digital truncation — sensitive dependence is just nearby trajectories diverging in a fully determined fashion. There's no extra ontological randomness being injected. In particular, make the case that the "butterfly effect" belongs to algorithmic chaos in computable models, not to ontological flow in the real system. You should address the standard objection that even with perfect continous knowledge, practical prediction is still impossible, and explain why that practical limit doesn't become a metaphysical limit on determinism. Don't just recycle the usual Laplace demon rebuttals; instead, focus on the gap between representational error (our models being discrete) and dynamical error (the system itself being indeterminate). Also, consider how feedback loops in open systems can complicate the Lyapunov spectrum without actually breaking determinism. Keep it somewhat in the weeds — I want the argument to turn on the distinction between representational approximation and ontological dynamics, not on generic examples of weather forecasting.
