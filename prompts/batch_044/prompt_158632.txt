As a novelist drafting a narrative about democratic crisis, I am preparing the final examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar in political philosophy. I want an essay question that cannot be answered by reciting standard accounts of political morality. Present students with this constructed case: an elected leader must authorize a strategic deception that averts catastrophe but structurally erodes institutional transparency; she executes it with full self-knowledge, and this very awareness—what I term reflexive staining—transforms her private moral injury into an additional wrong against the deceived, because her visible anguish demands forgiveness without granting it, turning guilt into a spectacle of unaccountability. The question should require students to assess whether reflexive staining constitutes a distinct moral category beyond ordinary dirty hands, or merely intensifies the original corruption, and to distinguish instrumental wrongdoing from structural complicity. Provide the exam prompt, a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a marking scheme distributing 20 marks across five specific conceptual moves a first-class answer must make, each with its point value and a one-line note on the characteristic failure. The scheme should specifically penalize answers that reduce the case to a utilitarian calculus, or that treat transparency as a non-relational absolute.
