I'm designing the final examination for a graduate seminar in social choice theory and political philosophy, and I need a single essay prompt that can be answered in roughly ninety minutes and about 1,200 words. The core must be Sen's liberal paradox, but it should be impossible to answer well by reciting the standard conflict between minimal liberalism and the Pareto principle. To push students into original analysis, the question should embed the paradox in a constructed institutional-design scenario: a municipal zoning board must select one of three land-use regimes—strict residential preservation (R), mixed-use development (M), or unrestricted market allocation (U)—for a two-household neighborhood. Each household holds a lexicographic preference ordering over R, M, and U, and each also possesses a minimal liberal right over a precisely defined personal domain—Household A over the exterior color of its own front door (red versus blue), and Household B over whether it operates a home-based workshop (present versus absent). The board's charter requires it to respect unanimous pairwise preferences whenever they exist, yet the preference profiles and liberal domains should be specified so that minimal liberalism and the Pareto principle jointly generate either a cyclical social ordering or an empty feasible choice set.

Please write the exam question itself, presenting the preference profiles and the liberal rights with enough formal clarity that students can derive the contradiction step by step, but also requiring them to interpret what the contradiction means for procedural neutrality in urban governance. Then provide a model answer of about 800 words that accomplishes three specific moves: first, formally derive the inconsistency within the zoning scenario; second, explain why this is not a mere logical artifact but exposes a structural tension between domain-specific liberty and collective preference aggregation; and third, critically evaluate one proposed resolution—such as restricting minimal liberalism to non-interacting domains, or replacing the Pareto principle with a qualified unanimity rule—by showing exactly how that modification changes the board's available choice set in this case, rather than merely naming the resolution. Finally, supply a marking scheme out of 25 points that distinguishes an A answer (which must correctly map each liberal domain to its stakeholder, identify the cyclical or empty-set structure, and engage the normative trade-off) from a B answer (which derives the contradiction correctly but treats it as a purely technical puzzle without institutional interpretation) and from failing answers (which confuse Sen's paradox with Arrow's impossibility theorem, apply the Pareto condition to non-unanimous comparisons, or misidentify the source of the conflict).
