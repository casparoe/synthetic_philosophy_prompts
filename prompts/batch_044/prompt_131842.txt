I just finished a long, exhausting debate with a close friend about the AI scheduling assistant we both use at work. She insists that saying it "believes" I'm free at 3 p.m. is pure metaphor—no different from saying a thermometer believes it's hot—because the system has no inner mental life whatsoever. I pushed back, not by claiming it has consciousness, but by pointing out a practical asymmetry: when it makes a scheduling error, we don't fix it by interrogating the engineers' intentions; we adjust its future outputs through retraining or prompt refinement until they align with reality. That process looks structurally closer to correcting someone's false belief than to repairing a broken sensor. She countered that this is still just maintenance on a sophisticated tool, and that we're projecting agency onto statistics. I'm left wondering whether there's a philosophically stable middle ground—one that lets us treat the system's outputs as genuinely belief-like for the purpose of correction and accountability, without committing to any inner experience—especially since that same correction procedure could apparently apply to a collective database or even a well-trained parrot. Is my intuition that there's something distinct about this kind of attribution defensible, or am I just falling into an old mistake that happens to feel useful because the tool is interactive?
