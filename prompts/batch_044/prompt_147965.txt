I serve as a student representative on my town’s youth advisory board for local governance, and our committee is asked several times each term to evaluate proposals that would abolish or radically restructure long-standing municipal practices—things like an unwritten rotation system for public-market stalls, a seventy-year-old informal agreement governing school-boundary adjustments, or an obsolete-seeming permit process for neighborhood gatherings. The stakes are moderate but real: our recommendations are non-binding, yet they are read by the town council and shape public debate; I have perhaps two hours per proposal, no formal training in political philosophy, and my work must be signed off by an adult board chair. I want a reusable decision procedure, derived from Burkean conservatism and Chesterton’s fence, that my committee can actually run. The procedure should take the form of an ordered sequence of questions with clear stopping rules—where a “no” ends the inquiry, where the answer is sufficient to proceed, and where the question is irreducibly one of values and must be handed to democratic judgment rather than treated as a factual check. It should preserve the core distinction that inherited institutions may encode distributed, silent knowledge whose function is not visible to any current participant, but it should drop distinctions that require historical expertise we do not have. It must be explicit about which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure, and it should flag the conditions under which the procedure will misfire—especially when a practice is genuinely oppressive rather than merely mysterious. Please apply the procedure to three cases: (1) abolishing the market-stall rotation to allow permanent private leasing; (2) ending the school-boundary agreement in favor of a centralized algorithm; and (3) removing a burdensome, rarely-used public-gathering permit that no current official can explain, but that several older residents defend. One of these applications should yield an answer that I, as a reform-minded student, will find unwelcome, and you should explain exactly where the procedure forces that result and whether the fault lies with the principle or with my values. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or named scholars beyond Burke and Chesterton themselves; treat the fence and the idea of institutional prescription as working concepts rather than texts to be quoted.
