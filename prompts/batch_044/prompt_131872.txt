I sit on an international heritage accreditation panel that repeatedly certifies whether reconstructed archaeological objects are the "same work" listed in disputed provenance records, which determines restitution eligibility and insurance liability; the stakes are high, the decisions are frequent, and we currently have no internal framework. I recently encountered the philosophical problem of material constitution—the relation between a statue and the clay or stone that makes it up—and I want it turned into an operational protocol my panel can run without philosophical training. Please write a step-by-step decision procedure: list the ordered questions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark where the protocol stops with a clear negative determination, flag where it must hand the decision to a human because the issue is one of values rather than facts, and identify where the underlying philosophy genuinely leaves the case unsettled. The protocol should preserve the actual distinctions philosophers draw—constitution versus strict identity, gradual part-replacement versus sudden re-formation, and the persistence conditions of an artifact-type versus those of a particular token—but translated for conservators and lawyers. Then apply the protocol to three cases: first, a bronze statue that lost forty percent of its original metal to corrosion and was filled with modern alloy cast from the original mold; second, a shattered clay figurine reassembled with adhesive and missing pieces replaced by visually identical modern clay fired in the original kiln; and third, a stone monument deliberately pulverized by a later regime and then re-carved decades later by a different artist from the same quarry into the same iconography but with altered proportions—an outcome my panel will find unwelcome and that the procedure must treat without special pleading. Finally, state how confident you are in the protocol's reliability across different media, and single out the one step you are least certain about, explaining whether the weakness lies in the underlying philosophy or in the practical translation.
