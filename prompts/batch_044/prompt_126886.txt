Read Shakespeare’s Hamlet through the lens of philosophical debates about causation by omission—specifically the disagreement between counterfactual-dependency accounts, which treat omissions as genuine causes when an expected intervention is absent and harm ensues, and production-based or normative accounts, which deny omissions causal status and reconstrue them as failures to prevent. Focus on Hamlet’s repeated failures to kill Claudius in the prayer scene and after the play-within-a-play, and on his broader paralysis, and ask whether these omissions cause the subsequent deaths of Polonius, Ophelia, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, Gertrude, and Laertes, or whether they are merely background conditions. Treat the tragedy’s plot structure as a kind of cascade failure: each omitted intervention removes a branching point, allowing violence to propagate downward. Where does the play treat Hamlet’s inaction as morally different from his actions—killing Polonius, arranging the deaths of Rosencrantz and Guildenstern—or does it collapse that distinction? Does Shakespeare dramatize the “omission bias,” the tendency to blame harmful acts more severely than harmful failures to act, or does he suggest that Hamlet’s delays are equally, or more, culpable? Examine whether Hamlet’s final lethal action in the duel is presented as the inevitable terminus of accumulated omissions or as an independent causal stream that breaks the chain. Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the play to an illustration of metaphysics, and do not distort the philosophical theories to fit the drama. Conclude by identifying what Hamlet contributes to the debate that abstract argument alone cannot capture—whether about moral responsibility, the metaphysics of absence, or the phenomenology of paralysis. End with a single line beginning exactly with Verdict: stating whether the framework of omission causation ultimately illuminates the tragedy or forces it into a procrustean bed.
