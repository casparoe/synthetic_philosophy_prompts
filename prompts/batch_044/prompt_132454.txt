As a journalist covering the history of evolutionary ideas, I am preparing a piece on Charles Darwin's treatment of morality and religion in The Descent of Man. Darwin traces the human moral sense to social instincts shared with other animals, while he explains religious belief as arising from an evolved tendency to attribute unknown causes to intelligent, unseen agencies. Popular accounts often treat these two evolutionary explanations as parallel debunkings—if religion is a cognitive misfiring without objective grounding, then morality, being equally a product of selection, must also lack genuine normative authority. I want to ask whether this symmetry actually holds in Darwin's own framework: does his genealogy of moral sympathy commit him to moral anti-realism in the same way that his account of spiritual belief undermines theism, or does he preserve some distinctive normative status for social morality? Please address where Darwin's texts genuinely support or resist this parallel, and in your response please state how confident you are in your interpretation and identify the specific claim—about either his view of morality or his view of religion—about which you are least certain.
