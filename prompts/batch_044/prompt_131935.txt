Exam question:
In Republic Book II, Glaucon challenges Socrates by arguing that justice is valued only for its consequences—reputation and the avoidance of punishment—not for its own sake. He frames conventional justice as a compromise between the best outcome (doing injustice with impunity) and the worst (suffering it without recourse), and he offers the myth of the ring of Gyges—making its wearer physically invisible—to show that anyone granted such power would act unjustly. Now consider a modified scenario: a “ring of epistemic opacity” that leaves the wearer fully visible and physically present, but structurally prevents all observers from forming true beliefs that link the wearer to their actions; causal traces remain, yet attribution always fails. Does this variant strengthen or weaken Glaucon’s challenge to the intrinsic value of justice? And does Socrates’ reply—that justice is the harmonious ordering of the soul’s parts, analogous to health in the body—adequately answer the modified case?

Student answer:
Glaucon’s challenge, as presented by Socrates in Republic II, rests on the idea that justice is only a second-best compromise. People agree not to harm each other solely because they fear being harmed themselves; if given the power of invisibility, anyone—even a just person—would act unjustly. The ring of Gyges proves this because the shepherd uses invisibility to kill the king and seize the throne. In my modified version, the ring of epistemic opacity works the same way: the wearer is seen but never blamed, so reputational and punitive costs disappear exactly as they do with physical invisibility. Because Socrates answers Glaucon by arguing that injustice disrupts the internal harmony of the soul—like disease disrupts the body—this reply should cover both versions. The agent still knows what they have done, so if injustice truly corrupts the soul, the internal damage occurs regardless of whether others can attribute the act. Nevertheless, I think the epistemic variant actually strengthens Glaucon’s position, because the wearer can maintain the appearance of virtue in public—others continue to praise them—while secretly enjoying the fruits of injustice. Since Socrates claims justice is good even without rewards, but here the unjust person receives both internal harmony (if they can self-deceive) and external praise, the modified case shows that injustice can be doubly profitable. Therefore, the reply drawn from the tripartite soul is insufficient unless we add that self-knowledge is inescapable, which Plato does not clearly establish.
