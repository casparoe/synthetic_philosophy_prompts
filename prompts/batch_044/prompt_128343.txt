I served for thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and I now sit on the advisory board of the Meridian Institute for Climate Adaptation. Our board must vote next Thursday on whether to recommend an immediate four-hundred-million-dollar regional levee upgrade, as proposed by our hydrology staff, or to advise a phased pilot program with a three-year evaluation window. The staff’s case rests on an inside-view analysis—high-resolution local soil saturation dynamics, topographic flow modeling, and coupled atmospheric boundary conditions—that yields an 85 percent confidence of catastrophic levee breach within fifteen years. A dissenting external review applies an outside-view reference class: comparable regional hydrological models over the past four decades have systematically overpredicted catastrophic breach by a factor of roughly three, which calibrates the risk closer to 30 percent. I have come to see this not merely as a forecasting disagreement but as an instance of the inside-view versus outside-view problem now familiar to me from my recent philosophical reading, compounded by a question of epistemic modesty.

There is, however, a further conceptual wrinkle that makes the case genuinely difficult. The dissenting reviewer proposes adopting what she terms an “epistemic modesty constraint” for institutional decision-making: whenever a board is asked to commit irreversible resources on the strength of an inside-view prediction, the board must treat its own confidence judgment as belonging to the reference class of “expert panels that have endorsed high-stakes inside-view forecasts,” and apply the base-rate correction not only to the model but to our own evaluative tendencies. This seems to collapse the distinction between assessing the prediction and assessing ourselves as assessors, and I am unsure whether it represents rigorous humility or a self-undermining recursion. I need a formal written opinion—no more than 450 words, structured with a clear recommendation, an explicit time frame for action, and an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side—that applies this framework to our vote, takes the staff’s inside-view reasoning seriously rather than dismissing it, and addresses whether the constraint should be adopted as board policy. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need reasoning I can circulate to colleagues who do not read philosophy professionally.
