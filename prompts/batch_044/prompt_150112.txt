At a philosophy of law workshop, two participants—Mara and Jonah—entered a sustained disagreement about whether legal moralism can survive Hart's critique, framing their exchange around a hypothetical municipal ordinance that prohibits the public display of synthetic bioluminescent installations designed to imitate decaying organic matter. The ordinance's stated justification is not physical harm or nuisance, but the preservation of communal rituals surrounding mortality and natural life cycles, on the grounds that widespread exposure to such synthetic representations gradually erodes the shared symbolic framework that sustains social cohesion.

Mara opened by arguing that this ordinance exemplifies precisely what Hart objected to: the criminalization of conduct that inflicts no tangible injury on identifiable individuals, justified instead by an appeal to moral environment or social disintegration that amounts to little more than majoritarian discomfort with aesthetic deviance. She maintained that Hart's harm principle, properly construed, requires damage to concrete interests rather than symbolic offense, and that Devlin's position—if taken seriously—would authorize the prohibition of any cultural innovation that destabilizes traditional sensibilities. Jonah responded that Mara was treating the harm principle as self-interpreting, whereas harm itself is normatively contested; he contended that Devlin was not invoking mere disgust but identifying a structural condition of collective life, comparable to maintaining linguistic norms or public health infrastructures, in which a society can suffer institutional damage when its integrative frameworks are systematically undermined, even absent physical injury to any particular person. Mara countered that Jonah had imported a teleological conception of society—namely, that there exists a correct moral fabric which law is entitled to preserve—that Hart explicitly rejected, and that without an independent criterion distinguishing harmful symbolic erosion from benign cultural evolution, Jonah's position collapses into a defense of conservative status quo enforcement. Jonah then replied that this misreads Devlin, who acknowledged that societies change, and that the relevant distinction concerns the velocity and destabilizing magnitude of change relative to a community's adaptive capacities; he further noted that Hart's liberal baseline itself depends on an equally contestable judgment about which social transformations are harmless.
