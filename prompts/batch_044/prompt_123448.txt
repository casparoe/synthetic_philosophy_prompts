ok so I'm trying to get my thoughts straight before a discussion group on genocide and modernity, and I want to check if my package actually holds up or if I'm just helping myself to contradictory intuitions. Here's roughly where I stand:

(1) I think the Holocaust is genuinely singular—not in being "the worst" but in the specific fussion of industrial bureaucracy, modern rational-technical organization, and a totalizing racial-biological ideology that treated annihilation as an administrative task. I'm sympathetic to Bauman's gardening-state idea that modern rationality made it structurally possible, though I wouldn't say modernity caused it in any direct sense.

(2) At the same time, uniqueness shouldn't mean isolation. Comparing bureaucratic mechanisms with other modern atrocities—the Herero genocide, certain colonial administrative massacres—is totally legitimate and doesn't diminish the Holocaust. In fact, insisting on absolute incomparability feels like a kind of Eurocentrism.

(3) I also think the Historikerstreit in 1980s West Germany was mostly a proxy fight over national identity and normalization politics, not a serious methodological dispute about whether comparison was allowed. So I tend to be suspicious of uniqueness rhetoric when it sounds like it's doing political work rather than analytical work.

(4) But I still hold that modern bureaucratic-rational structures were a necessary condition for the Holocaust's specific form and scale. Without that organizational infrastructure—rail schedules, record-keeping, division of labor—the genocide wouldn't have taken the shape it did.

(5) That said, I reject the stronger claim that modernity has a dark underside that necessarily tends toward genocide. The same bureaucratic tools run public health and disaster relief, so the link is contingent on specific ideological framing, not baked into rationalization itself.

(6) And I think treating European suffering as categorically different from colonial violence that also used modern techniques is a moral failure, which pushes me toward comparison even when it's uncomfortable.

So: do these hang together? I'm especially worried that (4) commits me to structural culpability that (5) tries to deny—like, if bureaucracy is necessary, am I smuggling Bauman's stronger thesis back in? And does (3) undermine (1) in a way I haven't noticed, since if uniqueness rhetoric was politically tainted in that debate, why keep the singularity claim at all? Also, is there a hidden tension between (2)/(6) and (1), or do they only look incompatible? Please rank the tensions by severity, say what the cheapest revision is in each case, flag anything that dissolves on closer inspection, and tell me which single change would clean up the most problems.
