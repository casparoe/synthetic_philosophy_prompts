I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy and aesthetics, and I'm working on a project that extends eighteenth-century critiques of representation into contemporary immersive media. I'm particularly interested in how Rousseau's arguments about theatrical spectacle—his worry that staged fiction manufactures artificial passions, substitutes representation for direct civic presence, and turns moral sympathy into a consumable affect—might apply to performance technologies that postdate him entirely. Consider a hypothetical immersive virtual-reality theater system in which spectators wear biometric sensors; their galvanic skin response, pupillary dilation, and heart-rate variability are streamed in real time to an algorithm that rewrites the narrative arc, dialogue, and emotional intensity of the performance to maintain an optimal arousal curve for each individual viewer. The audience members are not anonymous crowds but isolated nodes in a feedback loop, each experiencing a personalized spectacle that adapts to their physiological reactions, which are themselves harvested as data commodities. Given Rousseau's commitment to transparency, his distinction between amour de soi and amour-propre, and his suspicion that theatrical pity corrupts judgment by directing compassion toward fictive suffering rather than real neighbors, how would he likely evaluate such a system? Would he see the biometric loop as an intensification of the worst theatrical corruption—transforming emotional response into a manipulable resource and dissolving any residual collective presence—or might he paradoxically find something closer to genuine immediacy in the direct bodily feedback? I want a prediction that takes his actual conceptual framework seriously rather than reducing him to a generic anti-technology position, and I'd like you to address specifically whether his critique depends on the presence of a collective audience or can survive the fragmentation of spectators into isolated algorithmic nodes.
