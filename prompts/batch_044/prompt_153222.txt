As a journalist covering science and ideas, I keep encountering a particular argumentative pattern in debates about long-range ethics and risk that I cannot place precisely in the literature, and I am hoping you can tell me whether it carries an established name or needs a new one.

The pattern unfolds in two steps. First, a speaker argues that consequentialist reasoning about outcomes centuries hence should be set aside because we are radically epistemically blind to the causal chains linking present choices to distant welfare states; this is the familiar cluelessness objection to long-term optimization. Second, in the very same discussion, the speaker advocates for a large-scale, difficult-to-reverse present-day intervention—such as deploying a planetary-scale engineering system or releasing powerful autonomous agents—on the grounds that we can manage it adaptively: we will observe effects, update our models, and course-correct as we go. The tension is structural. The adaptive-management defense appears to presuppose exactly the kind of long-range causal transparency that the cluelessness argument just denied. If we genuinely cannot trace how an action ripples through social and ecological systems over generations, then we also lack warrant to believe that our monitoring apparatus will catch the relevant ripples before they become irreversible, or that our adjustments will not themselves trigger unforeseen cascades. Yet the two claims—that distant forecasting is hubristic and that present steering is safe—are treated as compatible. I have seen this pairing in arguments about climate geoengineering and about rapid technological deployment, where epistemic humility blocks one form of long-range reasoning but licenses another.

Is there a specific concept in decision theory or philosophy of risk that captures this asymmetry: the selective deployment of cluelessness about distant outcomes paired with an unexamined confidence in near-term feedback mechanisms? How should it be distinguished from the standard consequentialist cluelessness problem, from precautionary reasoning, and from ordinary motivated reasoning? If the literature lacks a label for this exact configuration, can you propose one, with clear boundary conditions that separate it from cases where adaptive management is genuinely appropriate?

Please separate what is settled in the relevant literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
