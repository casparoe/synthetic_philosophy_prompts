I'm putting together a feature for my magazine—science and ideas, not straight philosophy—about free will and mechanistic behavior, and I got into it with a friend over coffee about sphex wasp stuff. I think I've landed on a position that feels interesting but maybe self-undermining, and I can't figure out exactly where the crack is. So here's roughly where I'm at, and I'd like to know if it actually hangs together.

(1) I think the classic sphex wasp shows that mechinical rigidity kills free will—not because it's an insect, but because its behavior is a fixed loop that can't be interrupted by real evaluation. (2) But I also think flexibility alone isn't enough; you can be flexibly rigid, always adapting your route but never questioning the goal. I call that a recursive sphex loop, and I still think that's not free. (3) So real freedom needs the capacity to break the loop at the level of ends, not just means. (4) At the same time, I think humans mostly live inside recursive sphex loops—habits, algorithms, social scripts—and we only rarely actually break out. (5) Even so, I hold that moral responsability doesn't require being free all the time, just having the occasional capacity to break the loop. (6) And finally I think there's no sharp kind-difference between human cognition and the wasp's; it's all loops of diffrent complexity, and the 'break-out' is just a more complex loop that we recieve as freedom but isn't genuinely different.

Do these hang together? It feels like (6) pulls the rug out from under (3) and (5)—if breaking out is just another mechanized loop, then I never really have end-revising freedom, just more complicated sphexishness. But maybe (2) saves it somehow? I'm not sure. Can you audit the set: which pairs are in real tension, which only look like it, rank them by severity, and tell me the least costly revision? I'd prefer not to drop (6) since it feels like the most interesting part of the frame, but I'll ditch it if it's the only way to stop the bleed.
