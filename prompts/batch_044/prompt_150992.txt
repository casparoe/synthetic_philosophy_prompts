In my advanced philosophy seminar this semester we have been working through decision-theoretic puzzles, particularly the Platonia dilemma and the related Luring Lottery, which ask whether agents with perfectly identical reasoning should cooperate in one-shot interactions even when defection strictly dominates. A classmate who thinks in computer-science metaphors keeps defending the cooperative answer with an analogy I want to examine carefully: he compares the four agents in the Platonia dilemma to four terminals running the exact same algorithm on a single distributed server, where the server performs one computation and outputs it simultaneously to all four screens. According to him, asking whether "terminal one" should defect while "terminal two" cooperates is like asking whether the left half of a calculator should round up while the right half rounds down; the agents are not four independent decision-makers but one decision rendered in four locations, so they should all recognize that defecting is self-defeating, because it is literally the same computation choosing greed four times over. He uses this to argue that rationality demands all agents refuse the envelope, knowing the refusal will be mirrored everywhere and thus securing the collective reward.

I am not convinced the server analogy carries the weight he assigns it, and I need to audit it properly before our presentation. Could you lay out the explicit correspondence table—what corresponds to the server, the algorithm, the four terminals, the million-dollar envelope, and the act of defection—then identify precisely where the mapping breaks? In particular, please address whether the analogy quietly assumes that logical identity entails causal or intentional unity, and whether it smuggles in a metaphysics of mind—perhaps a unified substrate or shared consciousness—that superrationality does not actually require. I also want to know whether the cooperative conclusion survives once we distinguish correlation in reasoning from shared agency, and whether introducing the Luring Lottery—which assigns agents random numbers and creates probabilistic rather than deterministic correlations—makes the distributed-server image more plausible or reveals further fractures. If the analogy ultimately fails, what would be a more accurate conceptual image for how logically identical agents should understand their relationship in a one-shot interaction?
