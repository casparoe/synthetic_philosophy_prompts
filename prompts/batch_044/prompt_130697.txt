I'm auditing a graduate seminar on normative decision theory and artificial value alignment, and after a session on idealized preference frameworks two fellow participants—Maya and Jonah—became locked in a disagreement about the nature and authority of coherent extrapolated volition. They asked me to relay the exchange precisely and adjudicate it point by point, since neither would concede the final move. Here is how their argument unfolded.

Maya opened by describing the framework as essentially an idealized preference-satisfaction procedure: if we correct for ignorance, cognitive bias, and logical inconsistency, the preferences that remain are our true values, and an aligned system should optimize for those revealed ends. Jonah replied that this framing makes the process sound purely descriptive, like uncovering hidden preferences, when in fact extrapolation is often normatively transformative. He offered the case of an agent who currently values ritual obedience but, under conditions of greater knowledge and consistency, might come to value autonomous inquiry; the extrapolated agent is not identical to the present one, so the framework cannot claim to honor existing autonomy unless it smuggles in normative premises about what counts as improvement. Maya answered that this is a false dichotomy: the transformation is anchored in the agent's own second-order desire to be coherent and informed. Even the religious agent might reflectively endorse the transformation, reasoning that this is who they want to be if they think clearly, so autonomy is preserved through reflective endorsement rather than through static preservation of current preferences.

Jonah then advanced a more structural objection. He argued that if we extrapolate preferences across multiple dimensions—risk attitude, temporal discounting, social identity, and epistemic humility—the resulting idealized ordering can become cyclically intransitive: state A is preferred to B on coherence grounds, B to C on knowledge grounds, and C back to A on identity-preservation grounds. Consequently, there may be no single volition to extrapolate toward; the concept is ill-defined for such agents, and its normative authority collapses. Maya countered that intransitivity is precisely the problem the coherence constraint is designed to solve, so Jonah is describing an intermediate stage rather than a failure of the framework itself. But Jonah pressed further: different coherence-resolution procedures—maximizing expected utility, applying lexicographic rules, or minimizing long-run regret—produce different stable points, and there is no neutral meta-criterion within the framework to adjudicate among them. Therefore, selecting one resolution imports controversial normative commitments under the guise of mere rationalization. They stopped there and turned to me. Please go through each move in their exchange—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly correct with brief justification—then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing.
