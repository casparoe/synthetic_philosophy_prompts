In a course discussion on the philosophy of computation, a classmate kept returning to an analogy that also dominates rationalist forums: the human brain is essentially a von Neumann computer implemented in wetware. The correspondence table she offered was explicit—genes as the stored program in read-only memory, synaptic plasticity as rewritable storage, action potentials as discrete binary signals, and the prefrontal cortex as the central processing unit fetching and executing instructions in sequence. From this she inferred that consciousness is nothing more than the right computation running on any substrate, so a sufficiently large digital system built on standard von Neumann architecture should lie on a direct continuum with human sentience.

But this seems to ignore von Neumann's own late comparison of neural tissue and computing machinery, where he stressed that the brain does not rely on precise binary logic but rather on statistical, threshold-based computation with enormous redundancy and analog gradation, and that it lacks any clean separation between processor and memory—features that make it far more fault-tolerant and parallel than any digital architecture. I want to audit how far the analogy actually holds. Can you lay out the full mapping—program counter, arithmetic logic unit, clock, bus, error-correction scheme, and instruction set—then pinpoint exactly where it collapses under those biological constraints? In particular, does the analogy quietly import commitments to discrete symbol manipulation and centralized control that the brain does not share, and if those are stripped away, does the conclusion—that standard digital von Neumann machines are the proper reference class for assessing artificial consciousness—still survive? Please separate what is settled about neural architecture and computing design from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists, and do not present any contested view—such as the computational sufficiency of sentience—as the consensus.
