Construct a thought experiment that reproduces the logical structure of Hempel's raven paradox in a non-standard domain—specifically, one involving neither biological kinds nor color predicates—such that three different accounts of confirmation deliver three different verdicts on the same pair of observations. Use a scenario in which a researcher is testing a universal law about entities defined by a synthetic biochemical pathway, for instance a hypothesized invariant relation between two metabolite concentrations under thermal stress. The law must be logically equivalent to a universal statement about entities that fall outside the pathway. Engineer the observations so that: (1) under Hempel's Nicod criterion, observing both an instance that satisfies the law and an instance that satisfies neither the law's conditions nor its negation both confirm the law; (2) under a Bayesian treatment, the two observations carry different evidential weights because the prior probability distribution over metabolite states is not uniform across the kind; (3) under a Goodman-style projectibility analysis, the second observation fails to confirm the law because the predicates involved in the negated form are not projectible outside the pathway's natural kind boundary. Present the specific entities, the law in both its direct and logically equivalent forms, and the two observations. Then identify the belief being tested, the observational method, the relevant nearby worlds that determine the Bayesian prior, and the precise structural feature—prior asymmetry, kind-boundary restriction, or logical-form symmetry—that makes each account diverge. Next, state what happens if you remove that feature: does the case collapse back into the standard ravens-and-shoes formulation? Finally, explain how a defender of Hempel's symmetrical confirmation view could attack the case, specifically by challenging either the logical equivalence claim or the relevance of prior probabilities to logical confirmation, without appealing to pragmatic or psychological considerations.
