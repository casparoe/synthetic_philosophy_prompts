I've been working through a problem for my comparative politics course about whether constitutional monarchy can genuinely coexist with democratic sovereignty, and I think I've constructed an argument that either collapses monarchy into redundancy or undermines democracy itself—but the conclusion feels wrong, and I can't locate the slip.

Here is the chain:

(1) Democratic sovereignty requires that every binding exercise of state power be traceable to the present consent of the governed, mediated through representative institutions.

(2) In constitutional monarchies—say, the UK, Japan, or Sweden—the monarch formally holds sovereign powers: granting royal assent, appointing prime ministers, dissolving legislatures.

(3) These powers are hereditary, not elected, so they do not trace back to present popular consent.

(4) The standard defense is that these powers are purely ceremonial; the monarch exercises them only on binding ministerial advice, so the real democratic authority lies with elected officials.

(5) But if the powers are purely ceremonial, then the monarch is not exercising sovereign authority at all—merely performing a ritual. The sovereign authority must then reside in the democratic institutions alone.

(6) Yet defenders also claim that the monarchy serves an essential, non-redundant function: it embodies the state's continuous legal personality across generations, acting as a non-partisan focal point that temporary, partisan parliaments cannot provide. Call this the intergenerational anchoring thesis.

(7) If intergenerational anchoring is essential to stable sovereignty, then any democracy lacking a hereditary, non-elective focal point is missing a necessary component of state continuity.

(8) By parallel reasoning, any democracy relying on other permanent, unelected bodies—independent judiciaries, career bureaucracies, central banks—to provide continuity and neutrality is also partly grounded in authority that does not trace back to present consent.

(9) Therefore, either every modern democracy is partly illegitimate, or intergenerational anchoring is unnecessary.

(10) Since it seems implausible that all modern democracies are illegitimate, intergenerational anchoring must be unnecessary, which makes constitutional monarchy redundant symbolism that democracies could replace with an elected ceremonial head without loss.

The conclusion proves too much: it doesn't merely challenge monarchy, it seems to erase the distinction between democratic and non-democratic states, or it forces us to say that the UK and Japan are not real democracies. I suspect there is either an equivocation between legal-formal sovereignty and normative democratic authority, or a hidden premise that all continuity functions must be democratic if any are. But I am unsure which step is weakest. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if I want to preserve both the democratic-traceability requirement in (1) and the idea that monarchy serves a real continuity function in (6), is there a consistent way to do so?

Please say what would change your mind: identify the specific argument or distinction that, if shown to be unsound, would cause you to diagnose the failure elsewhere, and note where your reasoning is most vulnerable—especially whether the alleged equivocation between formal and normative sovereignty is genuine or imagined.
