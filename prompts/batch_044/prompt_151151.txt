I serve as chair of the Curatorial Ethics Board at the National Museum of Civic History, and I am writing to request a formal decision memo for our vote at the end of the month. The museum director has submitted a proposal for a permanent flagship gallery titled "The Unbroken Covenant," which presents the nation as a single continuous collective agent stretching from a medieval civic assembly in 1247 to the present republic; artifacts drawn from feudal estates, imperial administrations, and revolutionary committees would be arranged within one narrative thread of shared national intentionality and inherited moral obligation. The curators defend this framing as a legitimate recognition of obligations that span generations, but two board members—drawing on the conceptual literature concerning invented traditions and imagined communities—have objected that the exhibition commits what they call a "retrospective coherence fallacy": the projection of modern national identity onto historical populations who possessed no such self-conception, thereby collapsing radically different political regimes into an anachronistic moral person. A third member has countered that denying all diachronic continuity is itself philosophically suspect, since collective identity necessarily involves constructive narration and the acknowledgment of commitments made in our name by predecessors we do not choose.

Please draft our board's memo in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—writing in the formal tone of a letter addressed jointly to the board and to the director. The memo should begin with an unambiguous recommendation: approve, reject, or approve with binding curatorial conditions. Then provide reasoning that honestly weighs the constructivist objection against the continuity defense, specifically addressing whether treating a nation as possessing diachronic moral agency is a permissible interpretive framework or a misleading category error that risks deceiving the public about historical agency. If you recommend conditional approval, specify concrete requirements—such as labeling protocols, spatial or temporal separation of regimes, or mandatory contextual panels—that would allow the museum to maintain narrative coherence without suppressing historical discontinuity. Give the strongest argument on the losing side its full weight rather than treating it as a straw man, and keep the reasoning accessible to historians and museum professionals rather than assuming specialized philosophical training.
