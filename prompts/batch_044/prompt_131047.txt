You are delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Strange Loops in Practice." The audience is evenly split: philosophers of science and cognitive scientists who work on hierarchical modeling and self-reference, and curators and visual artists for whom M.C. Escher's Drawing Hands is a central object of study, though they have little background in formal logic. The philosophical idea must shape the speech structurally, not serve as decoration.

Treat Drawing Hands first as a visual argument about causation rather than as an optical illusion. Two hands appear to draw each other into existence, yet both rest on a flat sheet of paper that neither hand itself draws. Introduce the concept of an inviolate level — a stratum within a system that remains untouched by the self-reference occurring at higher levels — and apply it to the image. Then defend a specific, non-obvious interpretation: the paper is not a hidden foundation lying outside the loop, but rather a third term actively sustained by the loop. On this view, the hierarchy is tangled precisely because its base is not outside the system but is continuously reproduced from within. Because you take this stance, you must not resolve the paradox by appealing to an untouched ground, and you must not treat the logical structure as mere artistic ornament.

Your remarks need to land for both halves of the room simultaneously. The specialists should recognize that rigor has not been abandoned; the artists should feel that their subject is not being reduced to a logical diagram. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, citations, or references to specific texts that the audience should consult. Write in a formal but not stiffly academic tone, approximately five minutes in length (around 650 to 800 words), and address the room as colleagues gathered for a shared inquiry rather than as students receiving instruction.

Please write these remarks.
