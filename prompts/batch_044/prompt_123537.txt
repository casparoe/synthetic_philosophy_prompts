I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, and we have been working through Wigner's well-known argument about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences. I want to press a concern about how the puzzle is usually framed. Both Wigner and many of his commentators appear to treat mathematical effectiveness as a single, uniform property—either mathematics applies successfully to a domain of nature or it does not. I am inclined to reject this monolithic picture and replace it with what I am calling a dual-efficacy model, which distinguishes predictive efficacy (when a formal apparatus generates precise, novel quantitative forecasts that are not built into its initial conditions) from architectural efficacy (when mathematics supplies the conceptual and representational structure of a physical theory without by itself yielding specific predictions). Once we apply this distinction, it seems that predictive efficacy is always mediated by empirically calibrated parameters, whereas architectural efficacy is largely a matter of how we choose to formulate a theory rather than a contingent correspondence between abstract structures and physical reality. Given this bifurcation, I have the following question: does Wigner's puzzle not dissolve into two separate issues—one about empirical tuning and one about theoretical representation—rather than posing a single metaphysical mystery about why mathematics should match nature? And if the dual-efficacy model is sound, why do philosophers of science continue to discuss mathematical effectiveness as though it were one homogeneous phenomenon that demands a unified explanation?
