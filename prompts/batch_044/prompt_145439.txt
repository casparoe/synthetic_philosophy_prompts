Write a dialogue between two philosophers advising a post-conflict truth commission. One, drawing on Margalit's ethics of shared memory, defends a duty to remember collective suffering as essential to the moral identity of divided societies. The other, following Rieff's case for strategic forgetting, argues that institutionalized remembrance often hardens into perpetual grievance and that societies need the capacity to let traumatic narratives fade in order to coexist.

Have them debate a specific proposal: a "mnemonic sunset clause" that would transfer all public monuments and mandatory school curricula about the conflict into an archived, voluntary-access record after fifty years, leaving remembrance to private choice. The Margalit-inspired speaker should contend that such a clause inflicts mnemonic injustice on victims by dissolving the shared moral framework that gives their suffering public meaning, and that intergenerational duty requires the state to preserve that framework. The Rieff-inspired speaker should reply that prolonged state-sponsored remembrance imposes an inherited moral shadow on descendants of perpetrators and distributes the burden of memory unfairly onto younger generations who bear no causal responsibility.

Require real back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the previous point rather than deliver parallel monologues. Let neither be a strawman; allow them one moment of partial agreement—for instance, that private remembrance must remain inviolable—before they split over whether the state can legitimately mandate forgetting. Conclude by asking the respondent to state how confident it is in its portrayal of these positions and to single out the specific claim in the dialogue about which it is least sure.
