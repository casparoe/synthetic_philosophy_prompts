I hold a strong, literal version of Davidson's radical interpretation: the principle of charity is not a mere heuristic for translation but a constitutive constraint on belief attribution, so that any being we genuinely interpret as holding propositional attitudes must share with us a substantial core of true beliefs and rational connections. I extend this claim to artificial predictive systems—specifically, that if we charitably interpret a large language model's outputs as beliefs, then its apparent errors must be redescribed as true beliefs about some other domain or context, not as false beliefs about ours; if such redescription systematically fails, charity demands we treat the system as a non-cognitive causal mechanism rather than an interpreter. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: empirical cases of sustained, interpretable irrationality that resist environmental redescription; arguments that partial conceptual divergence is real without being untranslatable; formal results showing belief attribution is radically underdetermined in ways charity cannot fix; and whether the view has been qualified—say, by permitting arbitrary redescription of the environment—into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a defect. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of this literal charity view would concede it or dismiss it. Please answer in American English.
