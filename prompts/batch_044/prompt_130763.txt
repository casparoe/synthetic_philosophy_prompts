As a novelist constructing a scene in which a philosopher-character reads aloud to her skeptical brother over dinner, I need a poem to embed in the manuscript — something she might have copied into her notebook years ago, precise enough to survive her scrutiny but poetic enough that the brother, who distrusts abstraction, keeps listening. Write a poem in blank verse divided into five stanzas of strictly descending length: six lines, then five, then four, then three, then two. The first stanza should speak in the brother's voice, affirming the table's weight, grain, and presence; each following stanza should descend one level of being — from the table to its constituent parts, then to whatever underlies them, then to the relations that hold those in place, and finally to whatever is said to ground those relations. The stanzas must be linked by rhyme: the final word of each stanza must rhyme with the first word of the next, so that the form itself enacts the dependence of one level upon another. The philosophical work should be done structurally: the shrinking line count should mirror the narrowing of what remains as the ground is sought downward, and the final two-line stanza should break the expectation of resolution by leaving the ground either infinitely deferred or circularly self-supporting — the poem should end with the sense that the chain has not found a bottom, without declaring which metaphysical option is correct. Do not use technical terms like "supervenience," "ontological dependence," or "grounding"; instead, render each level through sensory detail that dissolves as the scale changes. Do not include citations, references, or a bibliography. The poem should be accurate enough that a metaphysician recognizing the debate over well-foundedness and metaphysical infinitism would find the stakes represented fairly, but it should stand as verse on its own.
