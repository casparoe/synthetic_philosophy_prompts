You are an expert in philosophy of science and pedagogical design, with deep familiarity with Karl Popper’s falsificationism, the demarcation problem, and the practical difficulties of applying logical criteria in institutional contexts. Before proposing anything, please take a moment to brainstorm quietly: what is the specific emotional or cognitive friction that makes falsificationism feel compelling in the abstract but uncomfortable in practice? Then design a classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar—14 graduate students, one 75-minute session—that makes participants feel that friction themselves, specifically the moment when a criterion intended to protect science from pseudoscience begins to exclude recognizably scientific work or include suspiciously flexible theories.

The activity should take the form of a structured role-play: students serve as a funding review board for a fictional Institute for Empirical Risk, which only approves research programs that can state in advance what observational result would cause them to abandon their core claim within a fixed timeframe. Provide concrete rules, a card-based proposal system describing four research programs—include one standard natural-science case, one clearly pseudoscientific case with hidden immunization strategies, one long-horizon theoretical physics program with no near-term test, and one social-science program whose predictions depend on ceteris-paribus clauses that can be adjusted post-hoc—a non-monetary payoff scheme such as collective reputation points if the board reaches consensus, and a strict 20-minute deliberation timeline followed by secret voting. The rules and incentives, not any lecture, should lead participants to discover that the same formal feature—falsifiability—sorts proposals differently depending on hidden auxiliary assumptions.

Then provide a debrief plan that maps each round of the activity onto Popper’s demarcation criterion, the Duhem-Quine problem without requiring students to name it, and the distinction between falsifiability in principle and in practice. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time this runs—for instance, students will conflate predictive failure with theoretical abandonment, or they will treat auxiliary adjustments as cheating without seeing their necessity—and how the facilitator should handle it. Finally, include an honest note on which feature of Popper’s view, perhaps its normative claim about scientific attitude versus its logical claim about demarcation, the activity cannot reproduce through rules alone and must be supplied by the facilitator afterward. Write in a formal, polished tone; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; avoid asking for quotations from specific texts; and do not ask participants to hear the technical vocabulary until after they have experienced the problem.
