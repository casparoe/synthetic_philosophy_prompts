Question: In several of his late philosophical essays, Erwin Schrödinger appeals to Advaita Vedanta to address what he calls the problem of the plurality of conscious observers in quantum mechanics. Reconstruct his argument: how does the Vedantic identity of Atman and Brahman map onto the quantum-mechanical situation of multiple observers? Then distinguish Schrödinger’s position from the interpretation later developed by von Neumann and Wigner, in which consciousness plays a direct causal role in state-vector reduction. Finally, assess whether Schrödinger’s unitary view resolves the measurement problem, or whether it merely relocates the difficulty to the problem of differentiated experience within a single consciousness.

Student’s answer: Schrödinger’s core move is to apply the Advaita doctrine that Atman equals Brahman to the quantum observer. He holds that what appear to be many independent minds are in fact modalities of one universal consciousness, and the boundaries between them are upadhis—limiting adjuncts—rather than ontological divisions. This is not merely an analogy: Schrödinger treats the quantum wavefunction as describing this single consciousness from a perspective that has not yet recognized its own unity. Consequently, he rejects the Copenhagen interpretation not because it is empirically wrong, but because it presupposes a plurality of observers that Vedanta shows to be metaphysically impossible.

Where Schrödinger differs from von Neumann and Wigner is in the role assigned to consciousness in physical dynamics. Von Neumann and Wigner make consciousness the cause of state-vector reduction; Schrödinger, by contrast, denies that reduction occurs at all, arguing instead that the deterministic Schrödinger equation describes the continuous evolution of the one consciousness, and that apparent collapses are only the result of our failing to see that all apparently separate measurements are internal differentiations within that unitary field.

One objection is that if there is truly only one consciousness, then the fact that different people report incompatible results—say, one measuring spin-up and another spin-down—should be impossible, since there is only one experience. Schrödinger answers this by appealing to the Vedantic concept of vivarta-vada: just as the one Brahman can appear as the many worlds without being divided, so the one consciousness can contain apparently contradictory contents as long as they are not experienced simultaneously from the same limiting perspective. Thus the measurement problem is dissolved: there is no mysterious jump from superposition to definite outcome, only a failure of the individual upadhi to recognize its identity with the whole.
