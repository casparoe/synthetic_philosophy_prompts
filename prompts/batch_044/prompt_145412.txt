A philosophy blog summarizes Strawson's well-known essay on moral responsibility roughly like this: Strawson's core move is to replace the metaphysical question whether people have libertarian free will with the practical question whether they are appropriate targets of reactive attitudes such as resentment and gratitude. He distinguishes these reactive attitudes from the objective attitude, which treats a person as an object of social management, therapy, or control. According to the summary, Strawson shows that determinism is irrelevant because reactive attitudes are purely emotional responses that do not depend on any belief about causation; indeed, he holds that if determinism were true, rationality would require us to abandon reactive attitudes entirely and adopt the objective stance toward everyone. The blog then applies this to penal theory, claiming that Strawson's framework directly maps reactive attitudes onto retributivism and the objective attitude onto rehabilitation, so that Strawson implicitly defends retributive punishment. Finally, the summary concludes that Strawson reduces moral responsibility to a psychological fact about our emotional susceptibilities, which means his view offers no genuine normative justification for blame and collapses under the standard objection that moral practices need rational grounding, not just descriptive explanation. How fair is this characterization? Please go claim by claim: distinguish what is accurate, what is a common oversimplification, what reverses Strawson's actual argument, and what imports an assumption he does not share. Specifically, assess whether the mapping onto penal theory follows, whether the charge that he reduces responsibility to emotion survives his distinction between internal and external perspectives, and whether the conclusion that determinism rationally requires universal adoption of the objective attitude is the view he defends or the view he rejects.
