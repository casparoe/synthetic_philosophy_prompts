I just finished a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about Lewis Carroll’s dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, and I am still trying to determine whether the tortoise’s refusal genuinely undermines the justification of deduction or instead exposes a structural feature of rational commitment that the standard distinction between rules and premises tends to gloss over. My friend insists that once we classify logical rules as non-premise norms of inference, the regress dissolves; but I want to defend a more specific and, I think, more demanding claim. Please argue that Carroll’s tortoise succeeds in showing that deductive justification requires not only acceptance of premises and inference rules, but also acceptance of a second-order principle of epistemic transmission—a principle that cannot be added as just another premise within the argument without regenerating the very same regress, yet that also cannot be treated as a bare rule of inference without collapsing the distinction between actually reasoning and merely describing the structure of reasoning from the outside. In particular, defend the view that the tortoise’s stubbornness is rational because Achilles keeps implicitly smuggling a modal commitment into his formulation of the conditional linking premises A and B to conclusion Z: that is, he treats the connection as involving something stronger than material implication, and without an explicit account of that stronger modal strength, withholding assent is the correct epistemic posture. You should address the obvious reply that this merely pushes the problem up one level to the transmission principle itself, and explain why that reply misunderstands the functional role that such a principle plays—not as an extra premise, but as a constitutive norm that determines what counts as a reason within the practice of deduction. Do not summarize the dialogue for me, do not provide a bibliography or reading list, and stay deep in the conceptual machinery: I am interested in whether the regress is genuinely vicious, or whether it instead marks the precise boundary between premises and the normative framework that makes premise-based reasoning possible in the first place.
