In discussions of whether symbols genuinely push the substrate around — the problem usually framed in terms of simmballs (the discrete symbolic structures) and the careenium (the substrate that bears and is modified by them) — a standard analogy compares the careenium to wet clay and simmballs to stamps or molds pressed into it. Proponents of strong symbolic efficacy invoke the image to argue that, just as a stamp actively determines the clay’s shape, simmballs must causally restructure the careenium; skeptics invoke the same image to argue that clay only receives passive impressions, so symbols leave traces without exerting any pushing force of their own. Audit this analogy: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping clay, stamp, impression, applied pressure, hardening, and removal of the mold onto the careenium, simmballs, substrate change, causal direction, persistence of change, and separation of symbol from substrate; then identify the disanalogies that specifically bear on the causal claim — for instance, whether stamps are external agents with independent energy sources, whether the careenium possesses endogenous dynamics that resist, amplify, or decay impressions, and whether an impression requires continuous contact or can persist autonomously; specify which features of the clay-and-stamp source domain are being quietly imported about passivity, agency, reversibility, and the need for sustained force; and determine whether either conclusion — that symbols do push the substrate, or that they do not — survives once those imports are exposed. If neither conclusion holds up, propose what a more accurate analogy would be, one that captures the actual interaction between simmballs and the careenium without smuggling in the wrong physical model of causation.
