I'm in a philosophy-of-mind seminar this term, and I've been working through Dennett's idea that some artificial systems could be "counterfeit people"—behaviorally indistinguishable from real ones, yet lacking genuine intentionality because of how they were built rather than evolved. I tried to formalize the tension between that claim and his functionalist commitments, and the result looks suspiciously like a reductio. Here is the argument as I have it:

(1) For Dennett, whether a system genuinely has beliefs and desires depends solely on whether adopting the intentional stance toward it yields robust, counterfactual predictions of its behavior.

(2) He also allows that we might construct "counterfeit people"—systems engineered to pass every behavioral test for personhood—while insisting that such systems lack real mental states.

(3) But if the intentional stance succeeds for a system, Dennett has no further internal criterion—no "hidden architecture" check—to deny it genuine mental states; the "counterfeit" label can only track our knowledge of the system's causal origin.

(4) So on Dennett's view, the difference between a real person and a counterfeit is not a difference in mental properties, but only in whether we happen to know that the system was designed rather than evolved.

(5) Apply this to biologically identical engineered humans: if we discovered that a being with ordinary human brain structure was lab-assembled, Dennett could not consistently deny it mental states without letting origin override functional organization.

(6) But origin is irrelevant to functionalist criteria; therefore Dennett must either grant mental states to engineered humans or admit that origin is decisive, violating his functionalism.

(7) The same applies to advanced AI: if a large language model exhibits sustained counterfactual robustness in dialogue—predicting correctly across novel contexts—Dennett must either accept it as a real person or invoke its silicon substrate and training history as disqualifying.

(8) Yet Dennett clearly wants to treat current AI as counterfeit, not as genuinely intentional.

(9) Therefore Dennett is implicitly committed to an unstated biological or evolutionary essentialism about mental status.

(10) So his view either collapses into the claim that any system with the right functional organization is a real person—including advanced AI—or it abandons functionalism for an origin-based essentialism.

This seems to prove far more than I want: either Dennett must welcome AI as people, or he must deny personhood to engineered humans. I feel like something slipped between (3) and (4), or perhaps between (7) and (8), but I cannot name the exact move. Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I want to hold onto (1) if possible, and I am writing in American English, so please reply accordingly.
