I serve on the State Memorial Commission, which must approve the final inscription for the Centennial Monument marking the hundred-year anniversary of the government-ordered forced resettlement of the Eastern Marshland communities in 1924. The proposed text is contested. One faction wants the plaque to state that “the nation, then and now, bears collective guilt for this crime,” invoking Karl Jaspers’s distinctions among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt as grounds for extending culpability across generations. A second faction rejects attributing guilt to present citizens who were not alive and proposes a “tri-layer attribution” instead: naming the specific administrative bodies and officials responsible (perpetrator-layer); describing the legal and bureaucratic machinery that authorized the displacement (structural-layer); and addressing current citizens not as guilty parties but as bearers of an “inheritance-burden”—a non-culpable yet binding obligation to maintain the monument, fund reparative measures, and prevent recurrence. A third faction wants no collective language at all, listing only named perpetrators and treating the monument as pure historical record.

The Commission must issue a formal recommendation memo selecting one of these three framing options, or a rigorously defined hybrid, with reasoning that each dissenting faction can directly answer. Your memo should apply Jaspers’s categories as operational tools rather than historical labels: determine whether a present-day polity can be the proper subject of guilt, whether an “inheritance-burden” can carry the normative weight the second faction demands without collapsing into guilt by association, and whether the monument’s public functions—education, mourning, structural accountability—are better served by one framing. You must include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest argument for the option you reject, especially the concern that avoiding collective-guilt language sanitizes structural responsibility, and the mirror concern that collective-guilt language risks instrumentalizing present citizens for symbolic catharsis. The recommendation should specify the exact conceptual commitments embedded in the chosen inscription, flag the points where value judgments remain ineliminable, and state what the Commission should do if the legislature later demands wording that contradicts your framework. Deliver the memo as a document the body could vote on.
