Draft a 90-minute final-exam essay question (25 marks) for an upper-level undergraduate or graduate seminar on the moral psychology of mass atrocity, centered on the debate between Christopher Browning’s Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen’s Hitler’s Willing Executioners: Ordinary Germans and the Holocaust (1996). Do not ask for a summary of either work. Instead, construct the question around a specific, historically plausible but fictionalized archival profile: Reserve Police Battalion 101 in mid-1943, for which the records show near-total compliance with mass-shooting orders alongside frequent private correspondence expressing reluctance, requests for medical exemption, and anxiety about family reactions—requests that were almost never granted and almost never repeated.

Ask students to adjudicate whether Browning’s situational explanation (peer pressure, incremental commitment, authority obedience), Goldhagen’s cultural-character explanation (a deep, historically cultivated eliminationist antisemitism), or a hybrid model best accounts for this pattern. The question should require them to identify which evidentiary details would genuinely distinguish the explanations, to assess whether expressed reluctance undermines Goldhagen’s claim of willing execution or undermines Browning’s claim of ordinary non-ideological compliance, and—crucially—to examine whether the very opposition between “ordinary men” and “willing executioners” depends on an unstated theory of moral character stability that both authors share but do not defend, and whether situationist critiques of global character traits dissolve the distinction without resolving the historical explanation.

Provide:
(1) The exam question itself, clearly stating time, expected length (approximately 1,200 words), and marks.
(2) A model answer of approximately 800–1,000 words that moves analytically rather than narratively: it should identify the crux, apply the competing explanations to the constructed profile, address the situationist challenge to character-based explanation, and reach a reasoned judgment without forcing a false resolution where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous.
(3) A marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves (total 25 marks, with explicit weights). For each move, specify the precise reasoning required, the common misconception or evasion that should cost marks, and whether partial credit is available for recognizing genuine ambiguity in the archival pattern rather than imposing an artificial resolution.
(4) A brief meta-reflective note (maximum 150 words) in which you state exactly what archival finding or philosophical argument—particularly one concerning the cross-situational stability of moral dispositions—would change your assessment of which explanation is superior, and you identify the weakest point in your own reasoning, especially any place where the constructed archival profile smuggles in assumptions that favor one author over the other.

The response must not include bibliographies, reading lists, requests for citations, or references to obscure theories that a well-prepared student could not reasonably be expected to know without external research. Include an explicit statement of what would change your mind and where your reasoning is most vulnerable.
