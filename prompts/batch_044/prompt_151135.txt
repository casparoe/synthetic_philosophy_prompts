Last night I had a long and rather circular argument with a friend about whether pain asymbolia genuinely undermines the intuition that pain is intrinsically bad. She kept insisting that if patients can recognize nociceptive intensity and location while reporting no suffering whatsoever—especially following insular-cingulate disconnections, selective lobotomies, or morphine-induced dissociation—then the “badness” must be an added evaluative layer rather than part of pain’s phenomenal essence. I countered that this only relocates the normative problem: even if affective valence is dissociable, we still need to determine whether a state lacking that tethered aversive response counts as “pain” in the morally relevant sense, or whether we have simply split the phenomenon into a sensory-discriminative core and a separable harm-module. We kept circling the same clinical descriptions without advancing, and I realized we were treating a cluster of genuinely distinct open problems as if they were one conceptual puzzle.

I am writing to ask you to treat the intersection of pain asymbolia and the philosophy of pain’s badness as an agenda-setting problem rather than a conceptual quiz. Specifically, I want a defended prioritization of the open questions and lines of work that actually matter—say, for a graduate student’s three-year project or a small interdisciplinary grant—not a survey. One axis concerns representational architecture: is the insular-cingulate “tethering” model correct in treating aversive motivation as a separable output, or does dissociation in asymbolia show only that the usual causal pathway is interrupted, leaving normative quality intact but behaviorally inaccessible? A second axis concerns clinical ethics: if targeted pharmacological or neurosurgical asymbolia were feasible, does the remaining sensory-discriminative state carry moral weight, and should palliative protocols aim for full abolition of nociception or merely for the removal of suffering? A third axis is metaphilosophical: should the debate over pain’s badness be settled by conceptual analysis of “pain,” by first-person reports from dissociation patients, or by normative arguments about which states justify avoidance-behavior? These strike me as genuinely independent, but I do not know which deserves scarce attention.

Please rank these lines of inquiry (and any others you think belong in the space) by importance, tractability, and dependency—how many other questions hang on resolving this one—defending the criteria you use and noting the trade-offs. In particular, identify which implication, if settled, would most change how we think about analgesic practice or about the moral status of suffering-free nociception, and which line is likely overrated or a dead end in current literature. Be explicit about what would change your mind: which empirical finding about insular-cingulate connectivity, which phenomenological report from a dissociation patient, or which conceptual argument about representational content would lead you to reorder the priorities? And please flag where your reasoning is most vulnerable—whether in the framing of “tethering” itself, in the assumption that clinical dissociation maps cleanly onto philosophical distinctions, or elsewhere.
