I work as a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and recently I tried to explain to my colleagues why Daniel Dennett rejects the Cartesian theater—the idea that somewhere in the brain there exists a single privileged location where sensory inputs converge for an inner observer—by substituting a multiple drafts model, in which parallel, competing representational narratives are produced without any authoritative canonical version. One teammate, who designs distributed message-queue architectures, immediately observed that our systems also lack a central server yet we still maintain a coherent global state for debugging and monitoring, which led me to wonder whether the philosophical deadlock is really about neural anatomy or about the semantics of "unity" in complex information-processing architectures. Despite decades of discussion, the debate appears frozen: critics charge that Dennett dissolves phenomenal consciousness by reducing it to nothing more than post-hoc narrative accessibility, while defenders insist he is merely describing what neuroscience actually shows—a brain with no theater, only widespread competition among drafts that become reportable at different times. Both camps routinely appeal to the same evidence about distributed cortical processing, yet neither concedes an inch, and I cannot tell whether they are disagreeing about facts, about what counts as an adequate explanation, or about something deeper.

So I am asking for a structural diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is this stalemate substantive—perhaps about whether first-person unity is a real property of experience or an illusion constructed after the fact—or is it partly verbal, turning on incompatible uses of terms like "explanation," "access," and "consciousness"? Are the two sides answering genuinely different questions, with one camp focused on functional architecture and the other on phenomenological presence, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about global workspace dynamics, neural synchronization, or the conditions for reportability that neither side has made fully explicit? I also want to know what, if anything, could actually move the debate: might a formal distinction borrowed from distributed computing—say, between eventual consistency and strong consistency—clarify the levels of abstraction at which "unity" is being claimed; could a specific neuroscientific result about narrative integration or competition settle the matter; or is the impasse rooted in bedrock intuitions about whether there must be "something it is like" to be the system, such that no distinction or experiment could dislodge either side? Please ground your diagnosis in what each camp actually argues, avoid merely surveying interpretations, and tell me honestly whether progress should be expected—whether through conceptual clarification, empirical discovery, or a change of question—or whether this is precisely the kind of philosophical disagreement that persists because it is genuinely hard rather than ill-posed.
