I have come to hold a somewhat unorthodox view about Plutarch's Parallel Lives and the broader practice of moral learning from biography. The conventional reading treats these paired lives as a gallery of exemplars: we study Pericles or Cato not merely to understand them, but to calibrate our own character against theirs. I think this gets Plutarch wrong. On my reading, the pairing is designed to undermine exemplarity itself. By placing a Greek and a Roman life in parallel, Plutarch does not invite imitation; he demonstrates that the virtues displayed are indexed to irreconcilable political and cultural architectures. Courage in Alexander is not the same virtue as courage in Caesar, and the comparison reveals that what looks like universal moral character is actually a local response to institutional conditions. If this is right, then biographical moral pedagogy is not just unreliable but structurally incoherent: there is no stable life to learn from, only a series of context-bound performances. What would have to be true for this anti-exemplary reading to fail? I want more than the obvious objection that Plutarch explicitly praises certain figures. I want to know which textual patterns, which comparative structures, and which external results—say, in ancient moral psychology or in contemporary virtue ethics—would genuinely damage the view. Which of these defeaters would a defender of the anti-exemplary position concede as serious, and which could be dismissed as importing modern assumptions? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by qualifications that make it compatible with any apparent evidence of exemplarity.
