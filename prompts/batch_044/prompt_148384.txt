I have been discussing with a friend who reads widely about institutions and technology whether history tends to get "locked in"—that is, whether an arbitrary choice made early on can become permanent simply because switching becomes too costly, or whether better arrangements eventually prevail regardless of past accidents. She is not a professional historian, but she has formed clear judgments about several well-known cases, and I am trying to understand the implicit principle behind them so I can apply it to a problem at my workplace.

Here is her track record. She considers the fact that most countries drive on the right to be a clear case of lock-in: she says the initial adoption in influential nations was largely contingent on historical accident, and that once vehicles, road signs, and traffic laws were standardized, the coordination costs of switching to the left became prohibitive, with no compensating functional advantage. By contrast, she says the global use of the 24-hour clock is not path-dependent: she argues that dividing the day into two twelve-hour cycles tracks the Earth's rotation and human sleep patterns so closely that even if some societies had begun with a decimal-time scheme, the 24-hour format would have converged everywhere eventually. She also judges the persistence of the QWERTY keyboard layout to be path-dependent, describing its initial spread among competing designs as somewhat arbitrary and its survival as driven by the sunk costs of training typists and manufacturing equipment, despite more efficient alternatives. Similarly, she regards the dominance of the Celsius scale in scientific publishing as locked in, noting that while Fahrenheit offers finer gradations for everyday weather, the coordination costs of maintaining dual measurement languages in peer-reviewed literature made Celsius irreversible once adopted, even though the choice of water's freezing and boiling points as reference marks was somewhat conventional. Finally, she denies that the adoption of the Gregorian calendar was path-dependent, insisting that the Julian calendar's accumulating astronomical error made correction inevitable, and that the specific month-lengths were functionally determined by the solar year rather than by historical contingency.

The situation I face involves our city's public library system, which uses an internal shelving convention invented by a single librarian in the 1970s: books are sorted into seven color-coded zones arranged in a circular pattern that bears no relation to subject matter, author, or publication date. Every branch staff member is trained in this scheme, the digital catalog maps directly onto these color zones, and patrons have learned to navigate by them. A national library consortium has recently proposed replacing all local schemes with a uniform hierarchical numbering system that integrates with cross-city lending and modern search software. The original color system was adopted purely because its creator found it aesthetically pleasing; there was no functional justification at the time. Nevertheless, abandoning it now would require retraining dozens of employees, restructuring the database, and re-educating regular users. Based on my friend's earlier verdicts, do you think she would predict that our color-coding system will persist—that it is genuinely path-dependent—or that the superior national standard will eventually replace it? Which of her prior cases is the closest analog—the driving-side convention or the Gregorian calendar correction? More importantly, does her record genuinely underdetermine the answer? I can imagine she might be running a rule about pure institutional inertia, a rule about coordination dependencies, or a rule that requires both an arbitrary origin and a reinforcing mechanism. For each of these candidate rules, what would she say about our library system? If the rules diverge, which answer best coheres with her overall track record, what principle would she be adopting by choosing it, and what further case—perhaps involving a standard that has both high switching costs and an unambiguous functional superiority—would best reveal which rule she actually holds?
