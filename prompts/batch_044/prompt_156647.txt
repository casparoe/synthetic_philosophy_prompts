I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been convening a monthly philosophical salon for fellow retired scientists and engineers; our members are rigorous with quantitative reasoning but tend to treat consciousness as a private, brain-bound substance. For next month's session I want to teach Douglas Hofstadter's claim—developed in his writings on strange loops—that the self is not a singular, skull-enclosed entity but a distributed pattern capable of persisting across other brains, so that parts of one's 'I' already live in the minds of those who know one well. Rather than delivering the lecture myself, I ask you, acting as an expert philosophy-of-mind educator and cognitive-science communicator, to design the explanatory arc I should use.

Before outlining the sequence, please spend a moment brainstorming the conceptual dependencies: what must be in place before the idea of a distributed self can seem coherent rather than mystical to this audience? Then specify the plan in detail: (1) the precise order in which the components must be introduced—beginning from the audience's likely physicalist assumption that personal identity equals continuous neural substrate, then proceeding through pattern, narrative, and inter-subjective replication—together with the reason each step depends on the previous; (2) the single carrying example or thought experiment that should anchor the discussion, explaining exactly what features it must possess (for instance, gradual versus abrupt transfer, partial versus total replication, preservation of first-person pronoun usage) and what it must lack (for instance, science-fiction teleportation tropes that trigger soul-intuitions); (3) the misconceptions this audience will predictably form or bring—such as conflating distributed self with telepathy, interpreting Hofstadter as denying individual consciousness altogether, or reducing the claim to mere memory-sharing—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; (4) a concise diagnostic question I can pose at the end to determine whether they have grasped the claim about distributed persistence rather than merely feeling unsettled; and (5) which aspects of Hofstadter's broader theory of strange loops or his technical analogies should be explicitly omitted at this level, with an acknowledgment of the omission.

The audience has no formal philosophy background but respects rigorous argument; please keep the plan free of any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and ensure every element could be delivered without external reference material. Think carefully, and present the teaching plan as a structured, step-by-step design rather than as a direct explanation of the concept.
