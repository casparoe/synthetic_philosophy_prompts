I defend the following position on democratic participation, which I call strategic dissociation: in a plurality system with first-past-the-post rules, selecting the least-bad viable candidate ought to be understood as damage limitation rather than expressive endorsement, and therefore the voter bears no moral complicity in the winner's later misdeeds, provided they maintain an explicit public record of dissent. I want to know what would genuinely count against this view.

Specifically, please address three lines of pressure. First, is there a psychologically unavoidable expressive residue in the act of casting a ballot—something that strategic reasoning cannot cleanse—such that damage limitation always collapses back into partial endorsement? Second, if the voter's revealed preference ordering influences party strategy in subsequent elections, can the causal contribution ever be fully separated from the intention to limit harm? Third, under conditions of deep epistemic uncertainty about manifestos—where platforms are deliberately ambiguous—does the duty to avoid complicity turn into a duty to abstain rather than to vote strategically?

Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones. For each challenge, indicate whether a committed defender of strategic dissociation could concede damage or whether they possess conceptual resources to deflect it. Then rank all the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, flag any hidden empirical or normative premises on which your ranking depends—particularly any assumptions about voter psychology, the dynamics of party competition, or the nature of moral complicity.
