I have come to hold a strong version of Davidson's radical interpretation thesis: not merely that interpretation requires a principle of charity, but that for any genuinely interpretable agent there is a unique maximally charitable assignment of beliefs and meanings, and that any alternative assignment preserving rational coherence but diverging in logical or semantic structure is not a rival interpretation of the same agent but a failure to interpret an agent at all. I want to know what would genuinely count against this view. Which arguments—whether from philosophy of language, formal semantics, or cognitive science—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Would demonstrated cases of equally charitable but logically non-equivalent interpretations, such as those arising in radical translation with non-standard arithmetics or deviant logical constants, force me to abandon uniqueness, or can I consistently treat them as interpretations of different cognitive architectures? Would empirical findings that human reasoning routinely sustains locally inconsistent but globally functional belief structures undermine the charity principle itself, or only show that its application is messier than idealized? I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample is absorbed by redefining what counts as an interpretation—and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a fatal defect for a position meant to constrain the metaphysics of mind and meaning.
