I am a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science and metaphysics. I am drafting an essay on path dependence that asks whether history is genuinely "locked in," or whether the appearance of lock-in is largely an artifact of our counterfactual methods. Rather than treating the issue as purely empirical, I want to distinguish economic inertia—switching costs, network effects—from what I call epistemic inertia: once a measurement or theoretical framework achieves predictive stability, alternative ontologies cease to be cleanly translatable into its language, so abandoning it involves not merely practical expense but a loss of descriptive resources. I apply this distinction symmetrically to biological macroevolution and to the development of scientific instrumentation, asking whether persistent structures reflect true historical contingency or only functional convergence masked by incommensurability. I also worry that my argument implicitly relies on a difference-making account of causation rather than a mechanistic one, and that this unacknowledged commitment may undermine my claim about "ontological costs" of framework change. Before I develop the essay further, I would welcome critical comments on whether the distinction between economic and epistemic inertia is coherent, on whether I am illegitimately importing physical equilibrium concepts into historical explanation, and on where my reasoning remains philosophically underdetermined. I am not seeking references, reading lists, or bibliographic guidance; please address the conceptual arguments directly.
