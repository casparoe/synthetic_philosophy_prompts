I have just spent the evening in a rather heated discussion with a close friend about whether the contemporary revival of Stoicism—particularly its absorption into cognitive behavioural therapy and popular self-improvement culture—has effectively collapsed Epictetus’s original dichotomy of control into a mere psychological coping mechanism, stripping away its metaphysical backbone. I want to teach the concept properly to a small reading group of philosophy undergraduates who are familiar with CBT but have not studied the Enchiridion. I do not want the explanation delivered to me; I want a teaching plan. What must come first—the distinction between what belongs to us, in the sense of prohairesis, and what does not—or should I begin with the modern misreading so that the students feel the tension? Which single carrying example should carry the whole idea: the death of a loved one, a failed examination, or a situation in which an algorithmic recommendation system shapes one’s choices without one’s direct awareness? I am inclined towards the algorithmic case because it is closer to their lived experience, yet I worry it smuggles in a technological determinism that obscures the Stoic emphasis on assent. Which misconceptions will they predictably form—perhaps that control means emotional regulation, or that Stoicism recommends indifference rather than virtue—and at exactly which point in the sequence should each be headed off? Finally, what one question can I ask at the end to reveal whether they grasp that the dichotomy concerns the scope of moral responsibility rather than the prediction of outcomes? And what should I deliberately leave out at this level—say, the Stoic physics of pneuma or the compatibilist debate about fate—while clearly signalling that these layers exist?
