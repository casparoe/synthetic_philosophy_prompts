Since leaving a professional design firm to help run my parents’ agricultural equipment repair shop, I have come to defend a view I had not previously examined: that the working-class orientation toward direct material resistance—judging competence by repair outcomes, trusting people through shared labor, and treating physical things as particular and recalcitrant—is not merely a cultural style but an epistemically and morally more defensible stance than the professional-class orientation, which treats reality as primarily representable, administrable, and mediated by formal certification. I think the professional outlook systematically mistakes symbolic mastery for practical understanding, and in doing so it loses track of how things actually behave under stress. What would genuinely count against this position? I want a map of its exposure, not generic accusations of anti-intellectualism: which specific observations—about safety scaling, predictive accuracy in complex systems, or collective decision-making under anonymity—would be fatal rather than merely awkward? Would a committed defender concede that large-scale coordination requires procedural abstraction, or reject that concession entirely? Does the view risk collapsing into a romanticized localism that cannot account for technological systems that genuinely require specialized symbolic knowledge? If direct material engagement correlates with lower predictive accuracy in certain domains—not through ignorance, but through a principled refusal to abstract—is that a defeat or a feature? Rank the defeaters, distinguish what defenders would concede from what they would dismiss, and say whether the position is well insulated and whether that insulation is itself a weakness.
