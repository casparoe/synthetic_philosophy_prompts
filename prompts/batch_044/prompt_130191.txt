In my historical novels, moral change never arrives with a single syllogism; it arrives like weather, through drawing rooms and changing costumes. Before I plot the disappearance of dueling and slavery in the same century, I need the philosophical specification: write the desiderata any theory of moral revolution must meet to be adequate. List the phenomena it must explain—rapid normative collapse versus slow institutional erosion, the persistence of banned practices in private, the divergence between elite moral entrepreneurs and mass adoption—with the motivating case behind each. It must distinguish change driven by moral argument, by a restructuring of honor as Appiah describes, and by material interest, without collapsing these into one mechanism. It must also handle what I call the wardrobe problem: practices that become ridiculous through their symbolic apparatus before anyone proves them wrong, with moral language retrofitted afterward. Include the constraint that slavery was highly profitable while dueling was not, yet both vanished within overlapping generations. Note where Williams's skepticism about rational argument and Appiah's honor thesis fit, but keep the weight on the criteria, not on scoring them. Flag which desiderata are non-negotiable, which are camp preferences, and which pairs are jointly unsatisfiable. Finally, identify the single requirement that eliminates the largest share of popular theories.
