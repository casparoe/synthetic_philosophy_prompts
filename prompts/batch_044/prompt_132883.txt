I'm working through a problem from a course on distributive justice concerning Derek Parfit's prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection. I understand the standard defense: because prioritarianism weights benefits by absolute well-being rather than by equality itself, it does not endorse harming the better-off unless someone is thereby made better off, so the classic leveling-down worry—where equality is achieved by dragging everyone down—simply doesn't apply. But I'm trying to push past this textbook answer into a more granular conceptual tension.

Consider a dynamic, two-period case. In period one, group A is at well-being level 80 and group B is at 40. In period two, an exogenous shock leaves both groups at 50, but an alternative policy could have kept A at 70 and raised B to 55. Now suppose we introduce what I'll call distributive inertia: the moral weight of a prioritarian benefit is scaled not only by the recipient's well-being but by the degree to which institutional structures—budget lines, expectation-formation, resource pipelines—have calibrated themselves to an existing baseline. Under high inertia, reducing A from 80 to 70 carries an additional moral cost beyond the direct well-being loss, because institutions had adapted to the 80 baseline, whereas B's gain from 40 to 55 is amplified because those same institutions had systematically under-invested near the 40 baseline.

My question is purely conceptual, not empirical, and I want to avoid looking anything up. Does injecting distributive inertia into prioritarianism actually help the view answer a refined leveling-down challenge—say, one in which leveling occurs not by direct harm but by institutional recalibration that shifts everyone's baseline downward—without collapsing prioritarianism into relational egalitarianism? Specifically, where is the precise boundary between (a) a genuinely prioritarian view that uses dynamic, institution-sensitive weights to explain why certain downward shifts are worse than they appear in a static calculus, and (b) a view that has quietly made equality or relational parity an independent source of moral weight? If the weighting function now depends on institutional history and calibrated baselines, then evaluating a shift from 80/40 to 50/50 requires comparing not just weighted well-being sums but also the moral status of the institutional path taken. Can you explain that boundary carefully, and tell me what a consistent prioritarian must pay—conceptually, not practically—to maintain it? Please answer in American English, and work through the logic with me rather than pointing me toward specific texts or authors.
