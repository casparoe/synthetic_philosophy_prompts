The nonidentity problem, introduced by Derek Parfit, demonstrates that when a policy affects the timing and circumstances of conception, the people who result are numerically different from those who would have resulted from an alternative policy. In the climate case, high-emission and low-emission futures will involve different marriages, conceptions, and births, so the individuals alive in 2100 under high emissions are not identical to anyone who would exist under mitigation. Because harm requires making someone worse off than they otherwise would be, and because these specific people would not exist at all under mitigation, we cannot say they are harmed by our emissions—provided their lives are not so bad as to be not worth living. Therefore, person-affecting obligations to avoid harming future citizens are undermined.

That said, the problem does not eliminate all moral obligations. Parfit’s own solution was to abandon person-affecting restrictions entirely and adopt a purely impersonal principle of total utility, which requires maximizing aggregate well-being regardless of which individuals exist. So our climate obligations survive as an impersonal duty to maximize the quality of lives lived, not as duties owed to particular descendants. In addition, the problem parallels Parfit’s repugnant conclusion: just as adding a large number of lives with barely positive welfare is not necessarily good, adding lives with minor climate-related hardships is not necessarily bad, provided they remain above a minimal threshold of well-being. Consequently, we should still mitigate emissions to maintain lives above that threshold, but we cannot claim that we wrong identifiable future people by failing to do so.
