At a seminar on psychiatric taxonomy, two colleagues—Mara and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about whether Ian Hacking’s idea of looping effects applies to a recently formalized grief-related diagnosis in a major psychiatric manual, especially because awareness of it now spreads largely through peer-to-peer digital networks rather than through clinical gatekeepers. Here is how their exchange unfolded.

Mara began by saying that the loop is evident: once the diagnosis was codified, people began interpreting ordinary bereavement through its specific criteria, altering how they experienced and reported grief. That, she said, is precisely the mechanism Hacking described—a classification interacts with the people classified, and the people change in response.

Leo replied that Mara is merely redescribing the standard looping effect. Whether the label reaches someone through a clinician or through algorithm-curated social media, the causal structure is identical: the category shapes self-understanding, which shapes presentation, which feeds back into prevalence estimates. He added that without clinical mediation there may be no loop at all—only social contagion—because Hacking’s account specifically concerns expert taxonomies interacting with institutionalized human kinds, not viral self-labeling.

Mara countered that Leo is being institutionally parochial. Hacking’s core claim, she argued, concerns the interaction between human kinds and classifications, not the bureaucratic vehicle that delivers them. She proposed that digital dissemination creates a structurally different distributed loop: individuals self-diagnose based on network content, perform the diagnosis back to peers, and generate prevalence signals that bypass clinical assessment entirely. This is not mere noise, she insisted; it is looping without a center.

Leo pushed back that Mara has invented a distinction without a difference. If epidemiological data are now contaminated by viral trends, then the feedback loop is broken, not mutated. In Hacking’s classic cases—such as the broadening and subsequent expert refinement of autism—the loop eventually stabilized the category. Here, there is no strong expert mechanism to correct the noise, so what Mara describes is destabilization, not looping. He concluded that she should either admit this is a different phenomenon altogether or concede that the digital version undermines, rather than extends, Hacking’s framework.

Mara’s final move was to agree that the loop is mutated—decentralized, noisy, and potentially destabilizing—but to insist that recognizing this mutation is exactly what makes the case philosophically important. She argued that psychiatric taxonomy must now account for loops that do not stabilize kinds but instead dissolve them into performative trends, and that failing to see this is the real parochialism.

Neither would concede.
