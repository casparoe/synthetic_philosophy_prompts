In my advanced philosophy seminar this term, we have been working through the paradox of fiction—that is, the problem of why we appear to feel real emotions toward characters and events we know do not exist. Our instructor has offered explicit verdicts on five scenarios, and I am trying to determine the principle behind them before she addresses a new case next week.

Her rulings so far are these. First, a theatergoer who weeps for Juliet during a performance of Romeo and Juliet, fully aware that the actress is unharmed, is experiencing a genuinely real sadness, but one directed at the represented situation rather than at any actual person. Second, a participant in a fully immersive virtual-reality shipwreck, complete with haptic feedback and spatial audio, who knows the environment is digital, nevertheless undergoes real fear and anxiety; the absence of physical danger does not cancel the emotional reality. Third, a reader of a meticulously researched biography who feels grief upon learning of the subject's long-ago death, despite never having met the person, is experiencing genuine grief directed at the real loss of that life and its potential contributions. Fourth, a child engaged in elaborate pretend play with explicit rules, who feels triumph upon victory and disappointment upon defeat, is having real emotions that are rational relative to the temporarily adopted "magic circle" of the game. Fifth, a lucid dreamer who recognizes the dream state yet still feels terror at a threatening figure within it is undergoing real terror that outlasts its rational cause—an emotional echo that persists even after the belief sustaining it has been suspended.

The new case she will consider is this. A subject is placed in a real-time, adaptive narrative system that uses biometric feedback and generative language models to produce a never-repeating stream of fear-optimized stimuli. There are no characters, no plot coherence, no human author, and no persistent narrative world—only an algorithm that adjusts visual, auditory, and textual inputs instantaneously to maintain the subject's arousal at a constant level. The subject understands the mechanism perfectly, can describe its architecture, and knows that nothing represented corresponds to any real or even consistently fictional object. Despite this, the subject reports sustained, objectless dread that feels subjectively indistinguishable from the fear experienced in the virtual-reality shipwreck.

I would like you to predict her verdict on this adaptive-system case: will she classify the dread as a genuine emotion on a par with the others, as a degraded or "hollow" analog, or as something else entirely—perhaps mere physiological arousal mislinterpreted? More importantly, I am less interested in the bare answer than in the underlying rule she seems to be running, together with the other plausible rules that fit her five earlier verdicts equally well. For each competing rule, say what it predicts about the adaptive-system case and about the lucid-dream case. Then tell me which single further case—specified precisely—would best discriminate between the leading rules, and give your best guess as to which rule she actually holds, with your reasoning. Finally, if her precedents genuinely underdetermine the adaptive-system verdict, state clearly what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice.
