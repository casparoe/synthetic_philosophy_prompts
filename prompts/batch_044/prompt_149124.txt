I'm working through a problem for a seminar on medical ethics and the metaphysics of personal identity, and I'd like to pressure-test an analogy that hasn't come up in the standard literature I'm familiar with. The usual framing treats an advance directive for progressive dementia as an expression of autonomy that binds the future self, but I'm wondering what happens if we import John Locke's political metaphysics directly into the intra-personal case. Locke died in 1704, long before advance directives existed, yet his writings on personal identity distinguish sharply between "man"—the biological human animal—and "person"—the conscious, accountable agent constituted by continuity of consciousness—while his political philosophy grounds legitimate authority in ongoing, revocable consent rather than in a one-time founding act.

So here is the engineered scenario: suppose a patient writes a detailed advance directive refusing all artificial nutrition once she reaches a specific stage of Alzheimer's, but the later-stage patient, though severely cognitively impaired, still exhibits intermittent consciousness, expresses contentment, and verbally resists the withdrawal of feeding. Now apply Locke's framework not as a loose metaphor but as a structural model: treat the pre-morbid, directive-writing self as the "sovereign legislator" and the later demented self as the "subject" within a kind of diachronic social contract. Given Locke's insistence that tacit consent can be withdrawn and that personal identity is forensic—tied to consciousness and memory rather than to substance—would he regard the later self as the same "person" party to the contract, or as a different person (or merely the same "man") such that the directive loses its authority? Furthermore, if the later self does count as the same person, does Locke's political requirement that authority depends on continuous consent entail that the later self's present resistance revokes the earlier directive, even though that resistance issues from diminished rational capacity?

Please reconstruct what Locke's position would likely be, tracing the reasoning through both his metaphysics of identity and his theory of political obligation, and identify the precise point where the analogy either holds or breaks: is it the discontinuity of consciousness that dissolves the contract, or is it the retention of some minimal consciousness that preserves identity but simultaneously undermines the binding force of past consent?
