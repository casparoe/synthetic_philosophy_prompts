I'm helping organize an interdisciplinary seminar on institutional culture, and two colleagues—both well-read in sociology but with opposing instincts—got stuck in a sustained disagreement about whether societies and the institutions within them should favor tight or loose norms around rule-following. They used a concrete case: a university debate society rewriting its charter. I wasn't able to referee them satisfactorily, and I'd like a careful point-by-point adjudication of the arguments exactly as they unfolded.

Elias argued for a tight charter: explicit, enforceable rules for speaking turns, mandatory disclosure of conflicts of interest, and a zero-tolerance policy for ad hominem remarks, on the grounds that loose norms systematically favor people with high social capital who can navigate ambiguity, while those with less cultural capital self-censor because they never know which unwritten rules apply to them. Mira argued for a loose charter: broad principles of respect and good faith, with enforcement left to peer judgment, on the grounds that codifying everything produces what she called normative fatigue—the constant interpretive labor of deciding whether a particular remark technically violates a clause exhausts participants and drives away anyone who isn't fluent in bureaucratic language. Their exchange then moved through four rounds. (1) Elias said that normative fatigue is just a cost of doing business, and that loose societies confuse procedural neutrality with substantive fairness—marginalized participants are precisely those who suffer from invisible norms. (2) Mira replied that Elias's tight charter creates what she called rule shadow: bad behavior doesn't disappear but migrates into spaces the rules can't reach—pre-debate lobbying, social ostracism, or strategic weaponization of the rules themselves to silence opponents—making it harder to identify and correct. (3) Elias countered that rule shadow is an empirical claim, not a conceptual one, and that in the societies for which we have comparative data—high-trust Scandinavian institutions versus low-trust ones—tight procedural rules correlate with higher participation by marginalized groups, not lower. (4) Mira responded that Elias was smuggling in a false dichotomy: she wasn't arguing for no rules, but for normative elasticity—rules designed as adjustable defaults rather than binary prohibitions—so they adapt to context without collapsing into pure discretion. She added that Elias's reliance on correlation data actually proves her point: if tight rules work only in high-trust societies, then demanding them everywhere is like prescribing a medicine that works only for the healthy.

Please go through these four moves one by one, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a coherent middle position between Elias's procedural rigidity and Mira's normative elasticity, or whether the two positions actually share an unstated assumption that makes their disagreement narrower than it looks. Be specific: don't just say it depends on context, but say exactly which conceptual distinction (for example, between coordination rules and status rules) does the work, and whether either speaker actually addresses the other side's strongest point rather than a weaker version of it.
