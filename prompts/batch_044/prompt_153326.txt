I chair the policy committee of a large public university's faculty senate. By the end of this term we must recommend whether to adopt a "deliberative proxy" procedure for resolving deadlocked disputes over mandatory general-education curriculum requirements. Under the proposal, when affected stakeholders—faculty, students, and administrators—reach irreconcilable disagreement, the dispute is referred not to a direct vote but to an advisory panel that issues binding recommendations based on what fully informed, strategically unbiased representatives of all groups would accept under conditions of reflective equilibrium, abstracting away from particular interests and information asymmetries. Proponents argue that this respects indirect normativity: the authority of the decision derives not from the actual preferences of those affected but from the rational acceptability of the norm under idealized conditions. Critics on my committee contend that this substitutes a philosophical fiction for democratic accountability, that idealized deliberation cannot generate binding obligations for real people who never consented to the abstraction, and that a norm can be rationally coherent yet practically intolerable to the constituencies who must live with it.

Please draft the committee's recommendation memo: recommend adoption, rejection, or adoption with structural safeguards, specifying the concrete procedural framework, the composition and term limits of the proxy panel, and an implementation timeline if adopted. The memo must engage the indirect-normativity argument seriously—explain whether a norm can be authoritative for actual stakeholders solely because it survives idealized scrutiny—and must address the democratic-legitimacy objection without dismissing it as mere populism. Include explicit institutional checks that would prevent the panel from imposing norms that are conceptually sound but unacceptable in practice, and identify the point in your own reasoning where judgment rather than principle must carry the decision. Do not cite specific philosophers or legal cases; build the reasoning from the conceptual structure itself.
