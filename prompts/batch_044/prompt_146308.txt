I've been trying to understand why the debate between global workspace theorists and defenders of phenomenal-access separation hasn't moved in decades despite repeated empirical claims. On one side, theorists like Baars and Dehaene treat conscious access as global broadcasting: information becomes conscious precisely when it enters a widespread, integrated workspace available to report, reasoning, and action control. On the other, Block's distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness—supported by arguments from phenomenal overflow, inattentional blindness, and change blindness—suggests that subjective experience can be rich and present even when no broadcasting or cognitive access occurs. The standard exchange is familiar: workspace theorists reinterpret overflow as a working-memory or attentional bottleneck, not as evidence of unbroadcast phenomenal content, while separationists reply that this simply redefines consciousness to fit the architecture. Lately I've been wondering whether the impasse hides a deeper divergence about explanatory targets—whether the workspace camp is explaining the conditions for cognitive accessibility, which they take to be coextensive with consciousness, while the separation camp is defending a first-person, modal property of experience that functional architecture might never capture. But I'm also suspicious that both sides implicitly share a representationalist framework—treating consciousness as a property of representational contents—while disagreeing only about the scope of global availability, which might mean they're not actually addressing whether consciousness is representational at all. Is the stall substantive, verbal, or a case of two research programs talking past each other? Diagnose it: do they differ on empirical predictions about neural correlates, on standards for what counts as data, on starting intuitions about first-person authority, or on whether 'phenomenal consciousness' denotes a natural kind? And tell me what progress would look like—perhaps an experiment that distinguishes graded broadcasting from all-or-none access, a formal result about the information-theoretic requirements for subjective presence, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it treats phenomenal consciousness as a unitary phenomenon. If my framing assumes something confused—say, that workspace theory is purely functionalist, or that overflow arguments survive current critiques—say so directly instead of smoothing over it.
