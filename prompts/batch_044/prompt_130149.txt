Here is an argument I sketched for a seminar on substrate-independent identity, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to collapse the distinction between survival and replication, yet I cannot name the faulty move. (1) If a cognitive system is replaced part by part over time, with each replacement performing the identical functional and causal role of its predecessor and with no detectable interruption in conscious integration, then numerical identity is preserved; call this the Gradual Replacement Thesis. (2) What justifies the thesis is not substrate loyalty but the unbroken chain of first-person reference: at each stage there is exactly one bearer of the indexical “I” causally linked to the prior bearer. (3) Suppose we develop scanning technology that captures, at a resolution sufficient to reproduce every relevant state-transition, the complete functional architecture of a brain at time t. (4) If we instantiate that architecture on a new substrate at t+ε, where ε is smaller than the neural integration window (approximately 100 ms), then the new system’s first state is linked to the scanned state in precisely the same dynamical fashion that the original brain’s state at t+ε would have been linked to its state at t, except that the link now crosses hardware. (5) Therefore, by the reasoning behind (1), the instantiated system is numerically identical to the original person; high-resolution scan-and-instantiate is merely accelerated gradual replacement. (6) But the scan data is substrate-independent information, so it can be instantiated on two separate systems, S and T, both at t+ε. (7) By (5), both S and T are numerically identical to the original person P. (8) By the transitivity of numerical identity, S and T are numerically identical to each other. (9) Yet S and T are spatiotemporally distinct, can diverge in experience and location, and cannot both constitute the single bearer of P’s first-person perspective. (10) So either we reject (8) and allow identity to branch without contradiction, or we reject one of (1) through (5). (11) Rejecting (8) is costly: it severs identity from uniqueness and makes the concept of “one life-path” incoherent. (12) Rejecting (1) is also costly: it implies that a patient whose neurons are slowly replaced by prosthetics over a decade is literally not the original patient. (13) Rejecting (5) requires explaining why physical adjacency within one skull matters more than functional-causal isomorphism across substrates, which seems to reintroduce an unexplained, mysterious substrate-dependence. I want to preserve (1) and (2) if I can, but I need to know whether the escape is in (4), in (7), or in (8), and what each escape actually commits me to ontologically. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?
