I'm currently drafting an essay for my graduate seminar on early modern political philosophy, and I'm trying to work out a somewhat unorthodox reading of Spinoza's democratic theory. Rather than treating his arguments in the Theological-Political Treatise and the unfinished Political Treatise as a conventional defense of popular sovereignty, I want to interpret them through what I'm tentatively calling "affective constitutionalism" — the idea that democratic institutions do not primarily aggregate pre-existing individual preferences but instead actively structure the common affects (joy, fear, hope, and indignation) that bind the multitude into a single body with its own collective conatus. On this reading, Spinoza's claim that democracy is the form of government closest to the state of nature is not an epistemic thesis about the wisdom of crowds, but an ontological claim: only in democracy does the ratio of motion and rest that defines the collective body remain immanent to the relational dynamics among citizens, rather than being alienated into an external sovereign will.

The essay then tries to show how this framing resolves a tension that has always struck me as under-discussed in the literature: Spinoza simultaneously insists that the multitude is prone to irrational passion and yet holds democracy up as the most stable and natural regime. I argue that the resolution lies not in assuming that democratic deliberation miraculously rationalizes the crowd, but in recognizing that democratic law functions as an affective infrastructure — it channels passions rather than suppressing them, preserving the collective conatus precisely by allowing the multitude's internal conflicts to be expressed and modified within a shared institutional field. I close by asking whether this makes Spinoza's democracy closer to a modern theory of agonistic pluralism than to classical republicanism, though I'm unsure whether that comparison illuminates or anachronizes his view.

I'd appreciate comments on a few specific points. First, is this reading textually defensible, or am I importing a conceptual framework that Spinoza's own account of conatus and affect in the Ethics cannot support? Second, does the notion of "affective constitutionalism" actually help explain why Spinoza distinguishes democracy of citizens from democracy of the multitude in the Political Treatise, or does it obscure the more straightforward legal-institutional point he seems to be making there? Third, is the comparison to agonistic pluralism philosophically fruitful, or does it risk making Spinoza sound like a twentieth-century theorist he never was? Any suggestions on where the argument is weakest, or what objections I'm most likely to face, would be especially welcome.
