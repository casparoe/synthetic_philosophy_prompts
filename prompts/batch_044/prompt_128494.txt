I'm writing a feature for a science and ideas magazine on anti-Cartesian theories of the self, and I need a rigorous comparison for the centrepiece. Please compare the Ubuntu relational conception of personhood—where one becomes fully human only through embedded communal relations—with Daniel Dennett's model of the self as a centre of narrative gravity, an emergent fictional construct produced by the brain's storytelling machinery. Both reject the Cartesian ego, yet they ground identity in fundamentally different ontologies: one in intersubjective relation, the other in individual narrative coherence. Examine whether Dennett's framework can genuinely accommodate the Ubuntu claim that personhood is constituted by community rather than merely narrated within it, or whether it inevitably collapses relational identity into an internal fiction. Conversely, ask whether Ubuntu's ontology can preserve the dignity and continuity of a person suffering from advanced dementia or severe dissociative fragmentation—conditions in which narrative coherence disintegrates—without implicitly demoting them to a non-person. Finally, consider a synthetic proposal: a distributed centre of narrative gravity that is spread across social practices, institutional recognition, and linguistic interaction rather than localised in a single brain. Is this hybrid conceptually coherent, or does it dissolve back into one of the two parent theories? The piece should avoid bibliographic lists and should address whether either account offers conceptual resources for evaluating personhood in artificial systems that possess neither biological embodiment nor conventional communal embedding.
