A colleague sent me this argument and insists the conclusion is unavoidable, but I find it suspicious because it collapses a distinction I think should matter. (1) The self-indication assumption (SIA) assigns prior probabilities to worlds in proportion to their total number of observers: a world with N observers receives N times the weight of a world with one. (2) The self-sampling assumption (SSA) treats one's own existence as a random draw from a reference class, so population sizes outside that class do not shift one's credences about which world one inhabits. (3) Now consider two actions available to me: Action C creates one new, causally isolated world containing a single happy observer; Action L adds one equally happy observer to my own world, within my causal reach. (4) By SIA, both actions increase the global observer-count by exactly one, so they receive identical probabilistic weight in any evaluation that applies SIA consistently. (5) By SSA, adding an observer to my world alters the sampling distribution within my reference class, whereas creating an isolated world leaves my own sampling probability unchanged. (6) Therefore, if one accepts SIA as governing not just cosmological belief but also practical reasoning about what to bring about, then C and L are morally and rationally equivalent: in either case I have produced one more observer, and my indexical position is weighted proportionally. (7) But this erases the intuitive difference between affecting someone I can interact with and spawning an isolated consciousness I will never influence; causal proximity seems morally relevant, yet SIA treats it as invisible. (8) The usual reply is that SIA governs evidential reasoning about which world I inhabit, not practical reasoning about which actions to take. (9) However, there is no principled boundary: both involve indexical uncertainty—uncertainty about which observer I am—under conditions where I could have been any of them. (10) So if we reject the equivalence of C and L, we must either reject SIA altogether or accept an ad hoc restriction that undermines its theoretical motivation. (11) I want to keep (7)—that causal proximity matters—and I would also like to keep (1) in at least some domains, but I cannot see where the slide from cosmology to practical reasoning actually fails. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
