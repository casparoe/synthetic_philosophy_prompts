At a recent dinner, two colleagues—Lena and Marcus—debated a hypothetical case study they had both encountered in an ethics course for engineers. The case involved a quality-control engineer at a German automotive supplier who was required by both internal policy and an industry-wide standard to destroy every tenth unit for destructive batch testing. The engineer noticed that recently recalibrated testing equipment seemed to be generating false failures at an anomalous rate, but rather than flagging the discrepancy and seek an exception, he followed the protocol exactly: he destroyed the units, halted the production line, and triggered a supply-chain delay. His American counterpart at a partner facility, facing an analogous situation with a different testing mandate, had documented the anomaly, paused only the affected batch, and kept the rest running while consulting specialists—thereby avoiding the delay but technically violating the written procedure. Lena defended the German engineer's choice; Marcus defended the American one's.

Lena opened by saying the American engineer had merely gotten lucky. If his judgment had been wrong, he would have released defective parts into the supply chain. The protocol existed precisely because individual judgment is fallible, and its purpose was to guard against unknown unknowns—not just the ones visible to a single engineer in the moment. Marcus replied that collective wisdom is not oracular. When you have specific, relevant evidence that the conditions the rule was designed for do not currently obtain, following the rule blindly is not wisdom; it is abdication. The rule was meant to serve safety and reliability, not to be served as an end in itself.

Lena countered that Marcus was treating the situation like a simple cost-benefit calculation, but the deeper point of strict rule-following in these institutional contexts is cultural: it maintains a system in which no single person can unilaterally decide they know better. Once you permit "good reason" exceptions, you open the door to rationalization, especially when production deadlines create pressure. Marcus agreed about the danger of rationalization, but insisted there is a real difference between self-serving excuse-making and genuine epistemic responsibility. The American engineer documented everything and invited scrutiny; he did not hide behind ambiguity. By contrast, the German engineer used the protocol to avoid being blamed for an outcome. That is not moral seriousness; it is moral outsourcing—treating compliance as a substitute for accountability.

Lena then argued that Marcus was assuming blame-avoidance was the engineer's motive. Perhaps the engineer genuinely believed that in a system with distributed knowledge, his proper role was to execute rather than to second-guess the system's design. There is a kind of intellectual humility in recognizing that you are one node in a larger network whose design you do not fully understand. Marcus responded that humility about one's knowledge is a virtue, but humility about one's judgment can become a vice. If every participant outsources moral judgment to the manual, then no one is actually responsible for the outcomes. The American engineer retained responsibility; the German engineer transferred it upward to an abstract rulebook, leaving a gap where human accountability should have been.
