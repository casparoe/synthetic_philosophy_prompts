My study partner and I spent several hours last night arguing about the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. She formalized what she calls a bookkeeping argument, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion is far stronger than any view in the literature actually defends. I want to know exactly where the slide occurs.

Here is her reconstructed chain:

(1) If the separateness of persons is morally significant, then one individual's welfare is not fungible with another's: no gain to B can cancel a loss to A.
(2) Non-fungibility entails that there is no interpersonally valid metric by which a state in which A is worse and B is better can be ranked as better overall than the status quo.
(3) Any principle that licenses collective action—whether a Pareto improvement, a duty of rescue, or a cooperative scheme—requires precisely such an overall ranking: it demands that we compare a multi-party outcome to the baseline.
(4) Therefore, if separateness is taken seriously, no collective action can be justified, since justification presupposes an impermissible aggregation.
(5) But this applies even to minimal cases: saving a drowning child requires comparing the state in which she lives and I am inconvenienced to the state in which she dies and I remain dry, which involves weighing her survival against my discomfort across persons.
(6) So the consistent separatist must deny that I am even permitted, let alone required, to save the child.
(7) Since (6) is absurd, (1) must be false, and separateness imposes no real constraint on aggregation.

She claims this shows that critics who invoke separateness—she mentions Rawls—face a dilemma: either they allow the fungibility they reject, or they collapse into a kind of moral solipsism in which no interpersonal obligation survives. I feel there is a hidden shift between (2) and (3)—perhaps an equivocation between forbidding cardinal comparison and forbidding any vector of ordinal comparisons—but I cannot name the exact error. Which step should I reject? And if I wish to preserve (1) and avoid (6), do I need to deny that justification requires an interpersonally comparable metric, or is there a more surgical repair? What does each option cost?
