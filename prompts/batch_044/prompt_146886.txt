I was chatting with some friends at this dive bar after a writing workshop — you know how it is, everyone's complaining about money — and one guy mentioned these UBI trials in Finland or wherver where they literally just send checks, no job-search logs, no means testing, nothing. It got me thinking about writers' residencies, because some fellowships give you cash with zero deliverables expected, just "live and think," while others want a finished manuscript or a workshop. The unconditional ones feel like they're operating on a totally different premise: that being alive is enough to merit support, not that you have to produce something to earn it. So if a government runs a universal basic income that way — totally unconditional — what philosophical theory of distributive justice or moral desert does that practice actually pre-suppose? Does it quietly commit to the idea that need alone creates entitlement, or can a luck-egalitarian defend it without buying into full-blown equality of outcome? And there's this tension that bugs me: we tax inherited wealth differently than earned income, which seems to presuppose that property rights come from labor or social contract, yet UBI treats need as prior to contribution. Are those two practices philosophically compatible, or do they rest on incompatible views of what people are owed? I also wonder if UBI pre-supposes anything specific about human nature — like that people won't work without coersion, or conversely that they'll naturally contribute if freed? Anyway, please seperate what's actually settled among philosophers and economists who study this from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as if it's the consensus. No reading lists needed — I'm just trying to understand the implicit logic.
