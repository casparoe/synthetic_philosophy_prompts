I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of art, and I wish to check a summary I heard in a reading circle—offered by an engineer—of Tolstoy’s essay What Is Art?. He presented Tolstoy’s view as an epidemiological model of aesthetic transmission: the artist encodes a sincere feeling-state in a work, and the audience receives it with the fidelity of a signal passing through noise, so art succeeds only when the infection is universal across receivers who lack specialized training. From this, he inferred that Tolstoy rejects Wagner, Baudelaire, and much modern painting not because they are insincere, but because they function like signals requiring specialized decoding apparatus; hence they fail technically and are counterfeit art. He concluded that Tolstoy thereby replaces aesthetics with a kind of moral epidemiology, in which a work’s value is strictly proportional to its reproductive rate in spreading universal brotherhood, rendering individual taste irrelevant. I suspect this framing imports scientific analogies that subtly distort the argument, but I cannot tell which parts are faithful to Tolstoy’s theory of emotional infection and which are artifacts of the transmission model—especially the exclusion of insincerity and the quantitative language of infection rates. Could you audit the summary precisely: identify what accurately reflects Tolstoy’s claims about accessibility, sincerity, and the content of true art; isolate the misrepresentations introduced by the signal-transmission and epidemiological framing; and determine whether his judgment that much modern art is counterfeit survives once that framing is removed and replaced by Tolstoy’s own terms?
