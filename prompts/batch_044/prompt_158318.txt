As a doctoral student in philosophy of language preparing the closing session of a seminar on vagueness and the sorites paradox, I would like a short poem—no more than eighteen lines, in terza rima—to serve as a conceptual mirror rather than a decorative illustration. The poem should take as its subject the specific dialectic between epistemicism and supervaluationism regarding borderline cases: on one side, the epistemicist thesis that there exists a precise but epistemically inaccessible boundary separating, say, bald from not-bald; on the other, the supervaluationist thesis that vagueness is a semantic phenomenon, so that in borderline cases sentences lack a unique truth-value because admissible precisifications diverge, yielding neither straightforward truth nor straightforward falsity but a kind of semantic indeterminacy. I want the interlocking ABA BCB rhyme scheme to perform philosophical work: the forward-driving rhyme should evoke the cumulative pressure of the sorites steps, while the enclosed middle lines should trap the reader in the circular structure that both theories attempt to escape—epistemicism by postulating an unknown sharp cutoff, supervaluationism by distributing evaluation across a family of sharpenings. Please place the volta near line twelve, at the precise moment when naive tolerance collapses into contradiction. The final tercet should leave the two positions in unresolved adjacency rather than synthesis: one line voiced from the epistemicist's commitment to hidden boundaries, one from the supervaluationist's commitment to admissible precisifications and super-truth, and one registering their shared structural cost—either enduring ignorance without semantic failure, or accepting semantic failure without ignorance. The rendering must be accurate enough that a specialist recognizes the specific theoretical commitments (sharp unknown boundaries, admissible precisifications, super-truth, the failure of higher-order tolerance) rather than treating them as mere metaphor. No citations or reading lists are needed; the task is conceptual and formal.
