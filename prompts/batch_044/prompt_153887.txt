In discussions of global poverty and effective altruism, Singer’s shallow-pond scenario is routinely extended well beyond its original setting: the donor is said to stand on a distant shore, but the moral mechanics are allegedly identical—there is a child (the beneficiary), a trivial cost (the ruined shoes, here the donation), and a direct rescue (the charity pipeline). Proponents use this to argue that withholding aid is morally equivalent to walking past a drowning child. I want you to audit that extension precisely by treating the charity pipeline not as a transparent line of sight but as a mechanical prosthesis—NGOs, state health systems, and supply chains playing the role of joints, tendons, and feedback nerves that transmit the donor’s “pull” across distance. Lay out an explicit correspondence table: what plays the part of the bystander, the child, the shallow water (causal transparency), the ruined shoes, and the eighteen-inch depth that makes rescue cheap? Then map where the prosthesis introduces slack, misdirected force, or information loss—places where institutional moral hazard, counterfactual displacement of local capacity, and opacity about the beneficiary’s actual identity break the direct-rescue picture.

Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the stringent-duty conclusion from those that merely complicate it without canceling the obligation. In particular, ask whether the loss of direct perceptual contact and the insertion of bureaucratic intermediaries destroy the analogy’s normative force, or whether they only change the form of the duty (e.g., from direct rescue to careful institutional selection). Also address what the pond analogy quietly imports about reversibility and identifiability that mediated aid lacks, and whether those missing features are morally load-bearing or just rhetorical ornament. If the analogy collapses at the level of causal transparency and agent identification, does the conclusion—that refusal is on a par with letting a child drown—survive on independent grounds, or was the analogy carrying the entire weight?

Finally, say whether a different analogy—perhaps one drawn from engineering safety margins or from distributed coordination—better captures the structure of mediated rescue without smuggling in false immediacy, and state clearly whether the “walk past the pond” verdict still applies once the mapping is made honest.
