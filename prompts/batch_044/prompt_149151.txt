I was in a philosophy of science discussion group last week and someone built this argument thats been bugging me ever since. (1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem applies to any formal system rich enough to encode basic arithmetic, so any scientific theory complex enough to describe the physical world will contain true statements it can't prove. (2) That means science is permanently 'incomplete' in the sense that it can never account for every true physical fact inside its own framework. (3) Moral reasoning also involves complex rule-following, and any ethical system rich enough to guide real action can represent arithmetic — think of weighing obligations or comparing outcomes across people — so the same logical gap applies: there will always be true moral judgments that the system can't derive. (4) If rational ethics is just applying a formal system, then it's permanently incomplete too, and we have to fall back on intuition for the leftover cases. (5) But if intuition fills the gap, there's no reason to think those leftover moral truths are universal or accessible to reason — they start looking like private feelings. (6) That seems to undermine the whole point of moral arguement, which feels wrong because people do actually reason about ethics. (7) So Gödel's theorems must be confined to pure mathematics, and anyone who exports them into philosophy of mind or ethics is making a category mistake.

I'm suspicious because the conclusion is so tidy — it dismisses a whole literature with one move — but I can't spot the slip. Is it that moral reasoning isn't formal in the right technical sense? Or is there an equivocation between logical incompleteness and explanatory inadequacy? I want to keep (1) and (2) if possible, and I'd definately prefer to keep (6), since giving up on moral reasoning sounds like paying too high a price. But maybe (3) is where the overreach happens? Where does the chain actually break, and what am I stuck with if I reject each candidate step?
