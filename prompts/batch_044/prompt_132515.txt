I am preparing the interview sequence for an upcoming episode of my podcast on philosophy of history and science, and my guest is an invented interlocutor—let us call her Dr. Elena Vasquez—who has recently advanced what she terms an “asymmetric reciprocity” framework for macrohistorical change. On her view, entities such as Yersinia pestis during the fourteenth-century pandemic or the 1815 Tambora eruption should not be relegated to mere background conditions; rather, they possess what she calls “veto agency,” a non-intentional but structurally decisive capacity to nullify or redirect institutional pathways that human actors had already constructed. Crucially, she insists that this does not collapse into environmental determinism because she pairs veto agency with the “receptivity constraint”: human societies build “enabling niches”—trade networks, agricultural monocultures, imperial supply lines—that determine whether a pathogen or climatic shock becomes historically transformative rather than locally contained. She also introduces the concept of “agentive leakage” to block the inference from causal efficacy to moral accountability, arguing that recognizing microbial or volcanic co-agency never entails distributing blame or praise away from human institutions.

I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond having her restate this architecture. I need at least one question pressing on whether veto agency is a literal ontological attribution or a functional heuristic, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that it designates a relational property, not an intrinsic mental one. I want at least one question on how the receptivity constraint avoids circularity—does it not presuppose the very human agency it is meant to constrain?—and a follow-up anticipating her appeal to institutional path-dependency. Include a question testing whether her framework can accommodate cases where non-human forces fail to become historical actors despite favorable niches, and what that failure reveals about the limits of the model. Finally, craft a question connecting her rejection of agentive leakage to debates about collective responsibility: if pathogens are not moral agents, can they still be proper objects of what she might term “structural resentment,” and does that category do any philosophical work her framework needs? For each question, provide a brief follow-up that assumes she has given the standard defense and pushes one step further, so the conversation does not stall in rehearsed answers.
