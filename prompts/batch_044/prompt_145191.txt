I'm a distributed-systems engineer with working knowledge of consensus protocols — Raft, Paxos, Byzantine fault tolerance — the CAP theorem, eventual consistency, state-machine replication, leader election, quorums, network partitions, and message-passing between nodes, but with no formal background in pragmatism or democratic theory. Restate John Dewey's position that democracy is best understood not as a mechanism for aggregating pre-formed preferences but as a form of collective social inquiry, in which citizens treat policy problems as hypotheses to be tested through enacted experience and revised through public deliberation, using exclusively the vocabulary and structural concepts of distributed computing: nodes, messages, state updates, divergence, convergence, fault models, partition tolerance, quorum thresholds, and so forth. Be precise in the mapping: identify which elements of Dewey's account correspond to nodes, which to consensus rounds, which to state replication, and which to failure modes. Then deliver a residue report. Specify which aspects of Dewey's experimentalism — for example, the idea that inquiry aims not merely at agreement but at warranted assertibility, or that democratic growth involves qualitative moral transformation rather than convergence on a fixed state — translate cleanly into distributed-systems terms, which require analogical stretching that distorts either Dewey or the protocol model, and which cannot be expressed at all within a framework built for fault-tolerant agreement rather than normative growth. Conclude by assessing whether the intuition that democratic deliberation is essentially consensus with additional steps captures Dewey's view or misses an essential normative or epistemic dimension that the distributed-systems vocabulary cannot represent, and indicate whether the missing residue is a limitation of the engineering framework or a sign that Dewey's original claim was less determinate than it appears.
