I sit on the clinical-ethics oversight board of the Meridian Neuroprosthetics Institute. Until six months ago, the debate over whether symbols genuinely push the substrate around—whether symbolic arrays can exert downward causal force on a physical careenium lattice, as the engineers behind simmball-based architectures claim—struck me as remote metaphysics. That changed when my partner received an implant built on precisely this architecture following a traumatic brain injury: according to the design documentation, her cognition now runs through arrays of simmballs that manipulate the careenium substrate rather than merely tracking its dynamics. The board must decide by next Friday whether to authorize Phase-III human trials of the second-generation device. The engineering team argues that because the symbols push the substrate, altering a patient’s simmball configuration is not merely repairing hardware but modifying mental content, which should trigger stricter informed-consent and autonomy protocols. A minority of the board holds that this confuses explanatory description with physical causation: the careenium dynamics are causally closed, and the symbolic level is an interpretive overlay without independent causal efficacy. I am asking you to draft the formal recommendation memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—full approval, approval with defined conditions, or a moratorium with a specific timeline—and should include reasoning that takes the engineers’ downward-causation claim seriously without presupposing either reductive physicalism or substance dualism. It must also treat honestly the strongest consideration on the opposing side, namely that if we deny causal efficacy to the symbolic level we risk treating patients’ mental autonomy over their prostheses as ethically irrelevant. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument should be entirely self-contained.
