I have no formal background in philosophy or cognitive science, but after reflecting on my own experience of making decisions—especially moments when I feel pulled in different directions by reasons I only partly understand—I have become preoccupied with the contrast between two images of the mind. The first is the Cartesian theater: a single inner stage where consciousness comes together and a unified self witnesses everything. The second is Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model, which denies any central observer and instead proposes that experience consists of competing narratives that are continuously revised, with no one version being the definitive real experience. Rather than asking for a general comparison, I want to understand the deepest implications of abandoning the theater model. In particular, suppose we take seriously that several drafts can coexist, that none is privileged as the true conscious state, and that behavioral control can be exercised by drafts that never reach anything like a final audience. Please describe the most important implications of this view for moral responsibility. Specifically, how should we rethink the boundary between an intentional action and an automatic reflex if there is no single moment of conscious finalization? And what becomes of the common idea that we are the singular authors of our choices, given that different drafts might propose conflicting reasons and only one eventually drives behavior without any central arbitration?
