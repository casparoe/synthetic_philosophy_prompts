In my philosophy seminar we have been debating whether pain is bad in itself or bad only because of what it makes us do. One common comparison is that pain works like a smoke detector: the alarm sounds unpleasant, but the real harm is the fire, and if someone heard the alarm without feeling any urgency to flee, the sound itself would not be genuinely bad. A classmate applied this to pain asymbolia — the condition in which patients report feeling pain but show no avoidance behavior or emotional distress — and argued that these cases prove the "alarm" can be separated from the "response," which means pain's badness comes entirely from the behavior it produces, not from the feeling. Another student proposed a different analogy: pain is like a red warning glow projected onto the inside of a driver's windshield; in asymbolia the glow is still present and visible, but the driver no longer feels compelled to brake. The first student says the second analogy just sneaks the badness back into the "glow." I want to audit both analogies. For each one, please give an explicit correspondence table — alarm or glow, fire or danger, listener or driver, behavioral reaction, and the location of "badness." Then identify the disanalogies that matter: does the signal have an intrinsic qualitative character that the source analog lacks? Can the "alarm" exist without being unpleasant? Does asymbolia actually show that the feeling is neutral, or does it show that a different component — affect, motivation, or cognition — has been disrupted? Finally, say whether either analogy survives this audit well enough to support the conclusion that pain is not inherently bad, or whether the analogies were doing the argumentative work all along. If neither holds, suggest a better comparison for thinking about what asymbolia reveals about the value of pain.
