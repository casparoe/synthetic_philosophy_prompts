I facilitate governance design for cooperative housing associations—groups of thirty to two hundred households that draft their own charters and amend them by supermajority. Increasingly, these groups want to embed what I call Ulysses provisions: rules that are deliberately costly to overturn, meant to bind future residents to commitments made today, such as perpetual affordability covenants, bans on speculative resale, or mandatory environmental maintenance levies. The philosophical rationale is familiar—a collective, like an individual, may rationally precommit to avoid predictable failures of will or shortsightedness. But my clients also fear locking themselves into mistakes if conditions change radically, and they want something more nuanced than a simple sunset clause. I have been experimenting with a framework I term conditional rigidity with epistemic release: the idea that a precommitment should not dissolve automatically after a fixed term, but only when an independent internal review body, operating under its own locked procedural rules, certifies that the original rationale for the clause has become epistemically obsolete according to criteria specified in the charter itself. This creates a second-order binding—the group binds not only its future actions but also the conditions under which it may reconsider them.

I need you to turn this combination of Ulysses-style constitutional precommitment and this second-order, conditionally locked release mechanism into an operational decision procedure my clients can actually run during charter drafting. The procedure should be ordered: the specific questions to ask about a proposed provision, what counts as a sufficient answer to advance to the next step, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation not to precommit, and where it explicitly hands the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than institutional mechanics. It must handle hard cases—provisions that protect vulnerable members versus those that merely reflect current aesthetic preferences; release mechanisms that risk being captured by the very majorities the precommitment is meant to restrain; and situations where specifying the epistemic obsolescence criteria in advance seems impossible without smuggling in the desired conclusion. Please apply the procedure to three proposed charter clauses: a perpetual covenant capping resale prices at original purchase value plus inflation; an irrevocable rule requiring unanimous consent to approve emergency loans above a set threshold; and a clause mandating specific low-carbon heating infrastructure that is currently compulsory but might become technologically obsolete. For at least one of these, the procedure should yield a recommendation that my clients will dislike—perhaps that a deeply valued provision should not be precommitted, or that the release mechanism they want is structurally self-defeating. Also flag exactly which steps in your procedure are judgment calls disguised as technical checks, and state clearly what the procedure cannot do—where a cooperative must simply choose between competing visions of its own future self.
