I'm teaching a philosophy-of-biology seminar and want to use Mary Shelley’s *Frankenstein* (1818) to open our unit on defining life, but I want to avoid the generic “is the creature alive?” framing. Instead, read the novel through a specific conceptual tension: process-based accounts treat life as organizational closure and a self-maintaining metabolic boundary, whereas evolutionary accounts treat life as entities capable of heritable variation and selection, with viruses serving as the standard borderline case for both. The creature is assembled from dead tissue—an origin that lacks self-generation—yet he develops sensory integration, self-preservation, and eventually demands reproductive capacity.

Take both frameworks seriously. Where does the creature’s development illuminate the process view, for instance in the learning and self-repair scenes? Where does Victor’s refusal to construct a female companion amount to deliberately withholding evolutionary potential, trapping the creature in a biological limbo that is neither fully alive (in the evolutionary sense) nor merely mechanical? At the same time, do not reduce the novel to an illustration of these criteria: the creature’s moral claims—his demand for companionship, his grief, his retaliation—depend on social recognition rather than on metabolic status or reproductive lineage. Where does Shelley’s work resist being read purely through biological definitions, and does it suggest that what matters about being alive is closer to membership in a moral community than to organizational closure?

Finally, what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that a direct argument between process theorists and evolutionary theorists misses—especially regarding the ethics of deliberately creating an entity that occupies a borderline state? I’m looking for a reading that moves between specific scenes and the conceptual framework without distorting either.
