I've been working out a view on the ethics of meat consumption—specifically comparing beef, chicken, and plant-based diets in light of wild-animal suffering caused by crop cultivation—and I want to check whether my reasoning collapses into something absurd. Here is the argument as I have it:

(1) The moral assessment of adopting a diet should be restricted to the suffering that is counterfactually dependent on my individual choice, holding the choices of all other consumers constant; suffering that would occur in the supply chain regardless of my decision is not assigned to me.

(2) If I adopt beef, the direct harm counterfactual on my choice is roughly the raising and killing of one cow per unit of consumption; the wild animals killed in feed-crop cultivation are largely inelastic to my single choice, since those fields serve the entire herd.

(3) If I adopt chicken, the direct harm is multiplied across many birds, but because feed-conversion efficiency is higher, the feed-crop acreage—and thus the inelastic wild-animal harm—is lower per calorie; the direct harm remains fully counterfactual.

(4) If I adopt a fully plant-based diet, there is no direct livestock harm, but every calorie requires direct crop cultivation; with no intermediate feed-conversion step, the wild-animal deaths per calorie are not absorbed by an inelastic livestock system and are therefore fully counterfactual on my choice.

(5) Consequently, beef assigns me one high-capacity direct harm plus mostly inelastic indirect harm; chicken assigns me many medium-capacity direct harms plus less inelastic indirect harm; plants assign me no direct livestock harm but fully counterfactual wild-animal harm at scale.

(6) If, as seems plausible, the aggregate wild-animal suffering caused by direct crop cultivation per caloric unit exceeds the direct suffering of the livestock that beef or chicken replace, then beef is morally preferable to plants, and possibly to chicken.

(7) The same structure applies generally: in any large-scale harmful system, an individual participant is responsible only for the marginal harm added by their specific choice, while the baseline harm is inelastically distributed across all participants and should not be assigned to any one.

(8) But if (7) is correct, then my buying an electronic device makes me responsible only for the marginal overtime of one factory worker, not for the systemic labor abuses that persist regardless; this dissolves individual responsibility into negligible fragments, which is unacceptable.

(9) So something in (1) through (6) must fail.

If I have to preserve anything, I would most like to keep (1) and (6). Which step should I reject, what makes it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost me—especially for the comparison between beef and chicken? Does the problem lie in how I apply counterfactual dependence across different causal structures, in the comparison of inelasticity between feed crops and direct crops, or somewhere else?
