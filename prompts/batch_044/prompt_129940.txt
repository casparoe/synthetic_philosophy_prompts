Write a poem of about eighteen lines in blank verse, divided into three six-line stanzas, on Émilie du Châtelet's view—developed in her treatise on happiness—that philosophical study is both necessary for and destructive of human contentment. In the first stanza, build the argument that reason and health are the foundations of happiness; in the second, show reason dissolving the illusions—especially about love and self-importance—that make ordinary life bearable; in the third, enact her distinctive resolution: not a synthesis of reason and illusion, but a conscious, rational decision to preserve the illusions one knows to be false, so that the philosopher lives in a sustained, self-aware tension rather than in either pure knowledge or pure naivety. Let the turn between the second and third stanzas function as a volta that does not resolve the contradiction but makes it habitable. Write in American English, in a voice that sounds like du Châtelet addressing a reader who fears that too much thinking will ruin happiness. The philosophical claim should be precise enough that someone familiar with her work recognizes the specific move—her claim that we must study our passions without letting study annihilate them—rather than a generic "ignorance is bliss" sentiment.
