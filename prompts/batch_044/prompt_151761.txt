I have been trying to understand whether evolutionary psychology is genuine science or mostly just-so storytelling, and I have been tracking the judgments of a particular science commentator whose reasoning seems consistent. She treated the claim that humans fear snakes because ancestral survival favored caution as a just-so story, because it offers no risky prediction that could have turned out false. She treated the claim that males show stronger spatial navigation abilities due to hunting selection as legitimate science, because it predicts cross-cultural regularities that should persist even where gender roles are not institutionalized. She rejected the idea that music preferences evolved as mating displays, arguing that the same adaptive logic could be stretched to fit any noisy cultural pattern. She accepted an evolutionary explanation for infant-caregiver attachment, emphasizing that it predicts specific, observable failure modes in institutional rearing environments. Finally, she said that explaining laughter through incongruity remains a just-so story unless it predicts exactly which incongruities are universal and which are culturally learned. Given these rulings, I want to test my grasp of her underlying standard. A new proposal claims that humans possess an evolved aversion to mixing sweet and savory flavors within a single bite, on the ground that ancestral diets separated energy-dense foods from potential toxins; it cites no fossil or archaeological record, but it advances three risky predictions—that the aversion should appear in toddlers before extensive culinary learning, that it should weaken in populations with centuries-old traditions of sweet-savory combination, and that it should be absent in individuals carrying specific taste-receptor mutations. Would she classify this proposal as science or as a just-so story? Which of her five prior judgments is the closest analog? If her earlier rulings genuinely do not settle the matter, what principle would she be adopting if she accepted it, and what different principle if she rejected it? And what further hypothetical case—one that distinguishes the competing rules—would show whether she is really applying the criterion I think she is?
