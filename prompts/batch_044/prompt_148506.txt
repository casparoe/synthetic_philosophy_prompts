I’ve been tracking the case judgments of a philosopher of practical reason—call her my advisor—on conflicts involving incommensurable values. Her record: she approved demolishing a listed brutalist library for emergency housing, on the grounds that shelter needs are time-sensitive and the building’s value can be documented; she blocked a museum from selling a damaged Renaissance panel to fund neighborhood flood defenses, holding that the panel’s aesthetic value is non-substitutable and the flood risk was addressable by other means; and she preserved an old-growth campus forest over a climate-research center, arguing that the forest’s value is place-constitutive while the research is relocatable. These rulings are jointly consistent with a least-substitutable-value principle, with a reversibility principle (protect whichever choice permanently forecloses the other good), and possibly with an implicit institutional-mission heuristic.

Here is the new case: a national archive must decide between digitizing a decaying, unique collection of 19th-century labor records—physical preservation being a one-time, irreversible opportunity—and endowing a recurring legal-defense fund for undocumented workers facing deportation, where deferral produces irreversible individual harm but the program is scalable and other NGOs exist. To remain consistent with her record, what should she decide, and which prior case is the closest analog? Identify the candidate rules her earlier verdicts support, state what each predicts here, and give your best-guess verdict with reasons. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, name the principle she would be adopting either way—particularly regarding whether she treats the conflict as structurally incommensurable (values embedded in incompatible institutional logics) versus merely allocative—and specify a further hypothetical case that would finally discriminate between the two leading rules.
