I'm writing my dissertation on incommensurable values and the phenomenology of hard choice, and I've been tracking a recurring pattern for which I lack precise vocabulary. I'd like to know whether it maps onto an established concept or requires a new carving.

The pattern arises after someone makes a genuinely hard choice between two valuable but incompatible life paths—say, committing to a demanding artistic vocation versus accepting stable employment that allows for family life, or emigrating for political freedom versus remaining within a community of origin. After the decision, the chooser does not merely report that the chosen path turned out well, nor do they simply downgrade the value of what was lost in the familiar sour-grapes or adaptive-preference manner. Rather, they come to describe the rejected path as having been conceptually incompatible with who they are or with some non-negotiable commitment—so that it was not a genuine sacrifice of value, but an avoidance of self-betrayal or category mistake. The artistic life was not "better but impossible"; it was "not really me," despite having been passionately pursued for years. The stay-at-home option was not "less valuable"; it was "a form of moral corruption I could not have inhabited."

I've observed this in first-person autobiographical accounts, in philosophical discussions of tragic choice, and in ethnographic work on migration decisions. The move seems distinct from ordinary rationalization because the chooser often genuinely believes the incompatibility claim and can defend it with reasons; yet it also differs from the resolution of hard choice in which one comes to see one option as relatively better without denying that something real is lost. Here the loss is denied not by comparative valuation but by expelling the rejected branch from the space of live possibilities altogether.

Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy, psychology, or sociology? If not, can you define it precisely—specifying how it differs from adaptive preference formation, from post-hoc rationalization, and from hard-choice resolutions that preserve the reality of loss—and identify its boundary conditions? I'm particularly interested in whether it should be treated as a cognitive distortion, a legitimate form of practical identity formation, or a distinct third category. I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I need the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
