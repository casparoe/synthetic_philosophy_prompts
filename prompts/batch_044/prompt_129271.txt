As a retired biologist who has recently begun reading metaphysics, I am struck by an apparent inconsistency in how my former field treats persistence through change. In natural-history museums, a holotype specimen retains its catalog identity and taxonomic authority across decades even when its physical matter is largely replaced—bones substituted by conservation materials, tissues extracted for DNA banking, the object moved to new climate-controlled storage. The institution behaves as if the specimen is one enduring thing, not a sequence of stages. Yet in long-term ecological monitoring, we routinely speak of tracking the same watershed study site across half a century, despite total turnover of every organism, soil chemistry, and instrument; here identity seems to depend on spatiotemporal continuity or data trajectory rather than on any enduring material substrate. Meanwhile, conservation policy grants no moral standing to individual wild animals, treating them as replaceable, while granting irreplaceable status to the degraded museum object. What view of persistence—endurantism, perdurantism, or a hybrid—does this cluster of practices presuppose? Can an endurantist account for the specimen's survival through material replacement without collapsing into the view that the study site is also one enduring object? Is the dual practice philosophically consistent, or does it quietly commit to incompatible metaphysics? And if we adopted a consistently four-dimensionalist framework, what would have to change in museum cataloging protocols or in the design of long-term ecological datasets? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely accommodate, and tell me what specific argument or empirical finding about biological identity would cause you to revise your diagnosis.
