I am preparing for an advanced metaphysics seminar on mereology, and I have noticed that debates about the special composition question tend to devolve into slogan-matching—universalists appeal to parsimony, nihilists to ontological innocence, restricted theorists to biological intuition—without anyone first specifying what an answer actually has to deliver. Before I evaluate any competitor, I want the adequacy conditions made explicit. Write the full desiderata for a theory that answers when parts compose a whole: the ordinary cases it must accommodate (a wooden table, a human organism, a constellation of stars fixed only by perspective), the engineered and boundary cases it must not dismiss arbitrarily (a statue missing its left arm that still counts as one object, a swarm of drones that maintains collective behavior despite total turnover of constituent units every few minutes, a sustained fusion reaction in which no plasma particle persists for more than a millisecond), the formal properties it must either preserve or reject (transitivity of parthood, uniqueness of fusion, consistency with extensional mereology if claimed), and the metaphysical costs it must either bear or avoid (arbitrary spatiotemporal cutoffs, explosion of scattered entities, conflict with physical descriptions of matter). For each desideratum, state the motivating case and flag whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp.

Then, identify which subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether the demand for a principled, non-arbitrary restriction on composition can be squared with the denial that gerrymandered or highly scattered objects exist. Keep any scoring of existing views brief and subordinate: just indicate how unrestricted universalism, mereological nihilism, and a biologically restricted view fare against the list, with the real conceptual work lying in the criteria and in mapping the tensions among them rather than in defending any one position.
