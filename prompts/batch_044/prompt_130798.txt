Design a 75-minute, two-phase classroom activity for an interdisciplinary seminar of 14 graduate students in philosophy of science and cognitive science that makes them feel the force of Darwin’s and Turing’s strange inversions of reasoning—namely, the shift from assuming competence requires comprehension to seeing competence operate without it. In Phase 1, have the group collectively act as a blind selective environment on an abstract trait such as “coherence” or “resilience,” using only outcome cards and hidden generative rules that no participant knows; individuals should vote or select based on surface features, so that functional design emerges across rounds without anyone grasping the underlying mechanism. In Phase 2, present anonymized performance logs from “agents” (some human, some algorithmic) and require the group to allocate a limited pool of non-monetary reputation points among them for a shared task, forbidding any appeal to internal architecture, subjective reports, or provenance; after allocation, reveal that high-performing agents included opaque processes the group could not have understood. Specify concrete rules, physical or digital materials, the reputation-point payoff scheme, and the exact sequence of rounds. The experience—not a lecture—should produce the inversion: in Phase 1, students should initially assume someone in the room understands the selection logic and only later realize no one does; in Phase 2, they should feel the pull of treating black-box output as authoritative. Provide a debrief that maps Phase 1 onto Darwin’s inversion of artificial-selection reasoning and Phase 2 onto Turing’s inversion of the comprehension-first view of cognition. Include an honest forecast of what will go wrong the first time—likely demands to expose the hidden rules or attributions of success to undisclosed human insight—and how to handle it without breaking the frame. Finally, state explicitly which feature of the competence-without-comprehension thesis the rules alone cannot reproduce and must instead be supplied by facilitator framing after the fact, such as the total absence of an intentional designer in Phase 1 or the absence of phenomenological access in Phase 2. Do not include any pre-session readings or named theoretical texts during the activity; the idea should arrive through what the rules make the participants do.
