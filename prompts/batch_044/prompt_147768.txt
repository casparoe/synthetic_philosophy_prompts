I'm writing a feature on philosophical risk and longtermism, and I'd like your help adjudicating a seminar exchange I witnessed between two researchers—call them R and S—about whether simulation reasoning should dampen far-future fanaticism. R argued that, if ancestor simulations are prevalent, the reference class of actual physical descendants is dwarfed by simulated minds; therefore, projects aimed at shaping real cosmological history carry negligible expected moral weight relative to near-term actions that might influence simulator behavior or present suffering. S replied that this mistakes ontology for moral patienthood: a functionally identical simulated being suffers equally, so high simulation density amplifies, rather than reduces, the stakes of our choices, since they may be replayed across countless instances. R countered that S's replay claim presumes a modal realism about cross-simulation suffering that longtermists typically reject when they demand causal efficacy; if we cannot affect other instances, then caring about them is precisely the kind of ungrounded fanaticism the argument should check. S then proposed what she called "epistemic truncation": rather than discounting the future by time, we should weight far-future goods by our credence that they belong to our actual causal lineage, which the simulation argument lowers but does not eliminate. R dismissed this as an arbitrary fudge factor that collapses back into standard longtermism with an ad hoc adjustment. Please go through the exchange move by move—rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right—and say who is more right overall, including what both parties miss about whether simulation uncertainty genuinely undermines moral urgency about the deep future.
