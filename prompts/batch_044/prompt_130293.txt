I am a high school student preparing my philosophical profile for a seminar on comparative epistemology, and I want a consistency check before I present. Here is where I have landed on Jain thought and its intersection with ethics and philosophy of science:

(1) I accept anekāntavāda in its strong metaphysical form: every substance (dravya) possesses infinitely many attributes (guṇas) and modes (paryāyas), so any single descriptive statement necessarily expresses only a partial aspect of reality.

(2) I therefore endorse syādvāda as a substantive doctrine, not a linguistic tic: every well-formed philosophical assertion should carry the qualifier syāt, indicating that the predicate holds only in some respect (kathaṃcit) relative to a specific standpoint (naya).

(3) I interpret this as a precise logical claim about contradiction: apparently incompatible properties can genuinely co-inhere in the same substance because each is indexed to a different naya, which preserves rather than violates the law of non-contradiction.

(4) I want this metaphysics to remain empirically responsible. I think claims about karma-particles (pudgala) adhering to the jīva should be retained only if they can be indexed to at least one naya compatible with contemporary physics, and I reject any reading of syādvāda that renders metaphysical claims unfalsifiable. In particular, I propose that the seventh mode of the saptabhaṅgī—syād avaktavyam—should function not as a mystical escape hatch but as a logical marker for propositions that fail empirical coherence conditions; if a claim about karma cannot be mapped onto any physically interpretable naya, it should be classified as avaktavya rather than preserved in qualified form.

(5) I hold that ahiṃsā is an unconditional ethical principle applying with equal force to all living beings, including microscopic organisms, because every jīva possesses the same infinite potentialities (ananta-catuṣṭaya) regardless of bodily form or cognitive capacity.

(6) At the same time, I accept that moral knowledge should be pursued through reflective equilibrium, weighing general principles against particular considered judgments, and I grant evolutionary psychology’s claim that our spontaneous intuitions about harming small organisms are unreliable guides.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. I am especially concerned that the immunizing tendency of syādvāda in (2) undermines the empirical discipline I demand in (4), and that my unconditional commitment to (5) conflicts with the revisability built into (6). But I am unsure whether I am manufacturing these conflicts.
