I have been drafting an essay that extends Jeremy Bentham's harm principle—especially the argument, developed in his private writings on sexual morality, that private acts between consenting adults ought not to be punished merely because they provoke "alarm" or moral disgust in onlookers—to a domain he could not have foreseen: intimate practices mediated entirely by artificial agents in which no biological human participates directly. I contend that, if his calculus of pleasures and pains is applied with full consistency, the decisive considerations should be the absence of physical harm, the presence of genuinely informed consent among any sentient parties involved, and the discounting of objections grounded in "unnaturalness" as irrational sources of social pain, analogous to the ascetic prejudices Bentham himself rejected. At the same time, I worry that this extension may conceal an internal tension: Bentham's framework treats pleasure and pain as properties of sentient beings, yet synthetic companionship raises difficult questions about whether an artificial agent can be a locus of consent or suffering, and about whether long-term psychological dependencies constitute a form of "danger" distinct from mere "alarm" that his original calculus failed to capture.

Could you please offer detailed comments on the philosophical coherence of this argument? In particular, I should like to know whether my application of the danger/alarm distinction to non-biological contexts remains faithful to Bentham's logic, or whether it collapses once the interacting parties are no longer uniformly human sentients. I am also uncertain whether my claim about moral pluralism genuinely follows from his utilitarian premises, or whether it smuggles in modern assumptions about autonomy and identity that are foreign to his social philosophy. Please assess the reasoning on its own terms rather than directing me to secondary literature or bibliographic sources; I am interested solely in whether the essay's central move represents a legitimate development of Bentham's views or a misapplication of them.
