As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing a long-form feature on a small coastal town that has, for three generations, built its civic identity around a failed maritime rescue in 1952. In that incident, local volunteers perished attempting to save a crew that had already drowned; the town commemorates not a victory but an "unbroken will," holding an annual ceremony that treats the failure itself as the defining moral achievement of the community. Recently declassified harbor logs have shown that the rescue was triggered by a false radio signal and that the volunteers explicitly ignored direct orders to stand down. A local historian is pressing to replace the heroic narrative with an unvarnished account of institutional error and disobedience; a group of descendants, however, insists that the myth was never about the factual outcome but about a counterfactual commitment—the community's willingness to choose solidarity over self-preservation regardless of probable result. They argue that this commitment remains intact even if the specific act was mistaken, and that stripping away the myth would destroy not false history but the town's only shared moral anchor.

I find myself genuinely torn about how to frame the story, and I am not looking for generic advice about "truth versus tradition." What I want to understand is whether there is a principled philosophical distinction between a collective narrative that binds people through shared defeat—where failure is acknowledged yet transformed into a different kind of value—and one that simply rewrites defeat as hidden victory. Specifically, I want to know whether the fact that this failure was compounded by disobedience to authority, rather than by pure misfortune or unavoidable risk, changes the moral status of commemorating it: does honoring an act that was both futile and insubordinate corrupt the community's capacity for honest judgment, or can the commemorative structure be separated from the factual record in a way that preserves moral coherence? And if the descendants are right that their identity rests on a counterfactual willingness rather than on what actually happened, does that make their myth more defensible, or does it render it immune to historical correction in a way that should worry anyone concerned with collective self-understanding?

In short, is this a case where maintaining the myth performs necessary moral work that an accurate history cannot replace, or is it a form of collective self-deception that risks insulating the town from accountability? I want an analysis that recognizes the underlying philosophical structure—how defeat becomes identity, whether counterfactual solidarity can ground a legitimate narrative, and what conditions make such a narrative ethically sustainable rather than merely comforting—rather than a standard journalistic balance of "both sides have a point."
