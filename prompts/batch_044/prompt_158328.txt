The rendering I have reads approximately as follows. Leibniz begins by observing that nothingness appears simpler than existence, since nothingness requires no constituents and no explanation. He then contends that this very simplicity is problematic: if nothingness were the actual state of affairs, there would still need to be a reason why nothingness rather than something obtains. Because nothingness offers no internal ground for its own actuality, it fails to satisfy the principle that every fact must have a sufficient reason. Consequently, the existence of an ordered world follows not from an arbitrary preference for being over non-being, but from the demand that reality be fully intelligible.

My difficulty is logical rather than historical. The two readings seem to reverse the burden of proof, and I want to understand exactly how his principle applies symmetrically or asymmetrically to being and non-being.
