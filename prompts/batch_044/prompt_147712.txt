I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a friend about whether introspection is globally unreliable or only fails in specific domains, and I want to turn that tension into the essay question for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar final. Write one 90-minute exam question that students cannot answer by summarizing Schwitzgebel’s pessimism, the confabulation literature, or Shoemaker on first-person authority. Instead, the question should present and apply a domain-specific framework—call it the representational decoupling gap—according to which introspection is generally reliable for the phenomenal structure (intensity, duration, bodily location) of a mental state but systematically unreliable for its intentional object and causal origin, especially for epistemic emotions such as a feeling of knowing or a sense of moral unease that tend to be misattributed to rational causes. Include a concrete scenario: a subject reports calm, reasoned conviction that a decision is fair, yet exhibits physiological and behavioral markers of unresolved anxiety; upon introspection she denies any emotional conflict and attributes her slight discomfort solely to physical fatigue. Ask students whether her introspective denial should be treated as fully authoritative, partly authoritative, or overridden, and require them to defend their answer by addressing (i) the decoupling-gap framework itself, (ii) whether first-person privilege can survive if it is restricted to phenomenal structure alone, and (iii) one serious objection—such as the claim that phenomenal structure and intentional content are inseparable in emotional experience. Provide the exam question, then a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 points that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific argumentative moves each must contain (for example, clearly distinguishing phenomenal from intentional access, applying the distinction precisely to the scenario, engaging the inseparability objection with a substantive reply rather than a dismissal, and reaching a nuanced conclusion rather than a blanket endorsement or rejection of introspection) and that lists the common mistakes that should cost marks. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. For the model answer, lead with its bottom line in the first sentence and then develop the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
