I'm giving the opening remarks tommorrow morning at a two-day workshop — half archaeologists who actually dig, half philosophers of science who worry about underdetermination. The theme is reconstructing beliefs and institutions from artifcats, especially when the artifcats are missing. I want the talk to do real structural work, not just decorate. Specifically: I want to frame the workshop around the idea that absence isn't neutral data. Preservation bias isn't random noise; it's often tied to the very institutions we're trying to study. So I can't welcome people by promising we'll "read beliefs off the remains." Instead, the speech should build that constraint in from the start, using one slightly weird concrete case: imagine a Late Bronze Age settlement where every storage jar carries an administrative seal, but there are zero ritual figurines, even in contexts where they'd normally survive. The usual inference is "no cult practice here," but I think the absence might be institutionally produced — maybe ritual was deliberately kept off durable media. So the welcome should set expectations: we're not solving belief-puzzles, we're mapping the conditions under which beliefs become invisible. It needs to land for the diggers (who know taphonomy and equifinality) without losing the philosophers, and it should be about five minutes. Don't turn it into a lecture, don't fake any citations or page numbers, and please don't let the philosophical point sit on top like a garnish — let it actually shape what I can and can't promise about the workshop's goals.
