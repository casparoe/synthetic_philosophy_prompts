I'm structuring a long-form research agenda on the conceptual architecture of effective altruism's critics, and I'm trying to avoid a standard survey format. My specific concern is the interaction between three lines of critique—the institutional argument that EA functions as a moral-credentialing mechanism for elite sectors in technology and finance; the earning-to-give proposal and its largely unexamined implications for long-term career-path dependency and moral licensing; and the measurability bias, especially the way quantification protocols systematically disfavor structural or political interventions that resist clean metricization. Rather than treating these as separate objections, I want to know which open conceptual questions within them are genuinely upstream: if one is resolved in a particular direction, which others collapse, require revision, or turn out to be independent?

Please give me a defended prioritization, not a list. For each candidate—say, whether the institutional dynamics are constitutive of the measurability problem, or whether earning-to-give survives if we grant a strong version of the institutional critique—explain its dependency relations to the others, its tractability for philosophical analysis without heavy empirical sociology, and which practical implications (for philanthropic governance, for individual career reasoning, or for movement strategy) actually hang on its resolution versus which are largely rhetorical distractions. I am particularly interested in whether resolving the measurability bias first would render the institutional critique moot by removing the incentive structures that generate credentialing, or whether the institutional dynamics are independent enough that fixing metrics wouldn't touch them. Rank these lines of work, defend the ordering with explicit criteria—upstreamness, tractability for conceptual work, and practical payoff—and indicate what kind of argument or conceptual clarification over the next few years would cause you to reorder them.
