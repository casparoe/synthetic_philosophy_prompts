As a journalist covering the philosophy of science and rational choice, I have been tracking a particular tension in expert disagreement that sits adjacent to Aumann's agreement theorem yet seems to slip through its standard net. Consider two specialists—say, quantitative modelers assessing a shared parameter space—who begin with genuinely common priors, observe identical public data, and then announce divergent posteriors. They subsequently learn of each other's conclusions. In the canonical Aumann framework, common knowledge of those posteriors should compel convergence. But I keep noticing a variant where convergence is blocked not by hidden private information, but by divergent internal protocols for interpreting the disagreement itself. One specialist treats peer divergence as diagnostic noise in her own reasoning chain, and therefore systematically downweights her original conclusion. The other treats the very same divergence as signaling unobserved contextual variables—local boundary conditions, unstated specification choices, or measurement-context differences that she has not accessed—and therefore preserves her posterior with only marginal adjustment. Because these interpretive protocols are embedded in each agent's prior cognitive architecture and are applied symmetrically, neither party achieves common knowledge of a shared updating rule. The disagreement remains stable and fully rational: the posteriors are indeed common knowledge, yet there is no common knowledge of how to respond to them. Does this configuration have an established name in the literature on agreement, belief revision, or epistemic game theory? And if it remains unnamed, can it be precisely delineated—particularly with respect to how it differs from ordinary failures of common knowledge, from the standard "agreeing to disagree" results, and from simple overconfidence or confirmation bias?
