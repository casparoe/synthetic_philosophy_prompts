Write a poem in blank verse, exactly sixteen lines, with a clear volta between lines 8 and 9. Take as your subject the problem of comparing infinite populations in ethics: simple additive aggregation collapses when lives are uncountably or even countably infinite; “limiting average” welfare fails too, since—like a conditionally convergent series—reordering the lives can shift the limit arbitrarily, and there is no canonical uniform measure over the natural numbers to fix the ordering. Speak in the first-person plural, as though a seminar of moral philosophers has just discovered that their framework demands an ordering ethics cannot supply. Repeat a phrase such as “we weigh” or “the total” across the poem so that its meaning shifts at the volta: before line 9 it should sound like confident aggregation; after line 9 it should sound hollow or vertiginous, tracking the collapse of comparison. The turn should move from the satisfaction of counting to the disorientation of non-measurable comparison, leaving the tension open—no sermon about intervention, no shrug about cosmic insignificance. Render the argument precisely enough that a reader familiar with infinite ethics (domination failures in infinite cases, the absence of a uniform distribution on ℕ, and the rearrangement problem for welfare limits) recognizes the reasoning, but write it so that a reader without that background still finds a coherent meditation on what happens when the sum outruns every scale. Do not cite authors, request a bibliography, or invoke named texts. Avoid rhyme; let the blank verse rhythm carry the structure.
