I was recently appointed to chair the enrollment-ethics review desk for psychiatric clinical trials at a university hospital, and I need a reusable decision procedure my staff can apply to any proposed study protocol. Our cases involve patients whose decision-making capacity fluctuates with symptom severity, and we frequently face family members—particularly in our large immigrant patient population—who strongly encourage or discourage enrollment in ways that blur the line between support and coercion. Please operationalize the philosophical conditions for valid consent—adequate disclosure without deception, absence of coercion, and competence—into a step-by-step procedure: the specific questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with a rejection, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question involves values rather than facts. The procedure must handle hard cases: therapeutic misconception in consent forms, culturally normative family influence that may be coercive, and competence assessments during partial remission. Constraints: my team has forty-eight hours per protocol, includes clinicians with no philosophy background, and requires both my sign-off and that of an independent patient advocate. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) enrolling acutely psychotic patients in a rapid-acting antipsychotic trial where family members actively advocate for participation; (2) enrolling stabilized outpatients in a placebo-controlled study whose consent form uses dense statistical language; and (3) enrolling adolescents with full parental consent but visibly ambivalent personal assent. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an approval I will likely find ethically troubling, and conclude by stating clearly what this procedure cannot determine.
