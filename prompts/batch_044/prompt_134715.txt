Question: Present the so-called dogmatism paradox and explain whether it genuinely demonstrates that anyone who knows p is rationally required to dismiss all subsequent counterevidence, or whether the appearance of irrational closure results from an ambiguity in "justified belief." In your answer, distinguish rational steadfastness from epistemic closure, evaluate whether fallibilist knowledge avoids the paradox, and consider whether the "epistemic friction" model—according to which justified beliefs resist revision in proportion to their integrative depth within a doxastic system rather than to their bare evidential weight—offers a more precise resolution than standard fallibilist replies.

Student answer:

The dogmatism paradox arises from two premises that seem individually sound. First, if S knows that p, then S possesses justification for p that exceeds any justification she could ever possess for not-p. Second, rational belief revision requires that one reduce one's confidence in p upon encountering counterevidence that has not already been accounted for. From these premises it follows that a knowing subject has no rational basis to lower her credence in p, regardless of what new evidence appears; hence she should ignore it, which is plainly dogmatic.

The paradox dissolves once we recognize that its first premise smuggles in an infallibilist conception of knowledge: it treats knowledge as a form of justification that cannot in principle be outweighed. On a fallibilist account, knowledge is fully compatible with error, so the knowing subject can reduce her credence without contradiction, since she never possessed absolute warrant to begin with. Therefore fallibilism removes the tension between knowing and remaining open-minded.

What the paradox really conflates is rational steadfastness—the refusal to abandon a well-supported belief in the face of misleading or underpowered counterevidence—with epistemic closure, the refusal even to entertain counterevidence. The epistemic friction model clarifies this distinction: beliefs with deep integrative connections resist revision not because they are infallible, but because revising them carries a high systemic cost across the doxastic network. This explains why a knower might rationally maintain p in the face of isolated counterevidence without being dogmatic; she is not ignoring the evidence, but weighing its local strength against the integrative cost of revision. Because fallibilism permits such weighting, there is no remaining paradox, and knowledge is compatible with genuine epistemic humility.
