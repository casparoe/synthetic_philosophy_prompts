You are a philosopher of medicine with a background in conceptual analysis, and I am asking you to help me organize my own thinking. Before you construct anything, please take a few minutes to brainstorm the logical space: list every combination of answers that the three cases below allow, note which ones feel incoherent or merely unexplored, and only then assign the named positions to the cells they actually occupy.

I have been trying to figure out whether health and disease are pure biological facts or whether they always smuggle in value judgments about what is good or bad for a person. Rather than asking me to pick a label, I want a decision tree — built from concrete cases upward — that sorts people by what they actually believe. Each branch should be a diagnostic claim or case judgment that someone can answer without already knowing which theory it leads to.

Please build the taxonomy around these three branch nodes:

Case A — Sickle-cell trait: A person carries the trait, so their hemoglobin function is statistically abnormal; they suffer occasional pain and reduced oxygen capacity, yet in a malaria-endemic environment the trait is strongly protective against infection and death. Is this state a disease, a form of health, or neither?

Case B — Aging: Aging involves widespread cellular dysfunction, complete loss of reproductive capacity, and eventual mortality. It is statistically universal in our species, and in many cultures it is treated as a natural life stage rather than a pathology. Is aging a disease?

Case C — Engineered hyper-vigilance (a hypothetical I am injecting): Imagine an intervention that pushes immune responsiveness far beyond the human statistical norm. The person eliminates nearly all infections, lives longer, and reports no subjective harm, but the trait requires constant caloric intake and does not occur in any natural population. Is this state a disease, an enhancement, or a new form of health?

For each case, formulate the tree node as a specific question (for example: "Does statistical abnormality by itself constitute disease, regardless of evolutionary benefit or harm?" or "Does a condition become non-pathological whenever it is universal in the species?"). Then combine the answers to generate the leaves.

The named positions I want you to locate in this space are:
- Pure naturalism: disease is an internal state that reduces functional ability below a species-typical level, defined without reference to harm or human values.
- Pure normativism: there is no disease without a negative evaluation — by the affected individual or by society; biology describes mechanisms, not pathologies.
- The hybrid harmful-dysfunction view: disease requires both a failure of a naturally selected biological mechanism and an evaluation that the result is harmful.
- One additional leaf for an unexplored or unstable combination — for instance, a view that treats evolutionary adaptiveness as always cancelling dysfunction, yet still insists that statistical deviation alone is never sufficient, applied consistently across all three cases.

For every leaf, provide:
1. The exact combination of answers to Cases A, B, and C that leads there.
2. The name of the position, or "unnamed / unexplored" if appropriate.
3. One sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the strange consequence it must accept about Case A, B, or C.

Please also flag any pair of named positions that this three-case tree cannot actually separate. If two positions yield identical answers across Cases A, B, and C, state that explicitly and indicate what further distinction would be needed to split them.

The tree should not depend on publication dates, page numbers, or verbatim quotations. If you refer to a thinker, paraphrase the view without quotation marks. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation suggestions.

Present the result as nested bullet points or as a clearly described flowchart, and conclude with a brief diagnosis of any empty cells in the logical space: for each unoccupied combination, suggest whether it is likely incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Think carefully about whether the distinctions you have drawn genuinely track the debate, and adjust the branch questions if they collapse positions that should come apart.
