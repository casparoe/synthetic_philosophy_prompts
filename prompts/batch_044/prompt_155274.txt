I'm trying to figure out my actual stance on commemoration ethics, going by gut reactions on concrete cases. A bronze statue of a 19th century mayor who funded the public hospital but also banned union organizing: keep it, but add a contextual plaque that doesnt shy away from the union-busting. A street named after a living donor who later turned out to have embezzled the donation: rename it, even if it feels ungrateful. A monument to a general who defended the city but ordered the burning of a nearby village: take it down, no plaque saves it. But then there's a small town square named after a local poet who wrote beautiful nature poems and was also an active member of a fascist party in the 30s - I'd leave the square's name but rename the annual poetry prize after her. And here's the weird split: a university building named after a chemist who invented a life-saving dye but also ran unethical human experiments in the 1920s; they keep the building name but rename the lecture hall inside after his victims. I feel okay with that division, but cant justify why splitting commemoration works for the chemist but not for the general. Is there a coherent principle here about honoring actions versus honoring persons, or about spatial scale? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on - a park bench engraved for a beloved teacher who privately held racist views that never affected her students?
