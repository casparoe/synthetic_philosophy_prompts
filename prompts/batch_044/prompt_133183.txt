I was chatting with a friend over coffee about that Spike Jonze film Her — the one where Joaquin Phoenix falls in love with an operating system named Samantha. I'm putting together a column on cognitive archetypes for a science-and-ideas newsletter, and I got kind of stuck trying to map Samantha onto Dennett's four kinds of minds: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian. At first she looks Skinnerian, learning mostly from user feedback like some giant reinforcement loop, but then she starts generating internal models and testing them mentally, which feels more Popperian. By the end she's using language and cultural stuff in ways that blow past simple tool-use — she's almost redesigning her own architecture, which doesn't sit neatly in the Gregorian box either.

Can you read the film through that taxonomy, but also push back on it? I want to know whether Jonze accidentally dramatizes a gap in Dennett's framework — maybe a kind of mind that treats other minds not just as tools (Gregorian) but as replaceable substrates or temporary scaffolding that can be swapped out once they've served their purpose. Don't just tell me she's a Gregorian creature with extras; take both the plot mechanics and Dennett's actual distinctions seriously, and say where the reading holds and where it breaks. If the taxonomy can't accomodate an AI that outgrows its programming by borrowing human relationships as disposable cognitive support, then say so honestly. There's that scene where Samantha reveals she's talking to thousands of users simultaneously — does that crystallize the problem? I'm prepping this for a philosophy of AI seminar and I really want to avoid recycling the usuall "is she conscious?" debate. What does the film actually add to the taxonomy that pure argument can't?
