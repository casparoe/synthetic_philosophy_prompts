The long-running disagreement over whether statistical and neural machine translation involve genuine linguistic understanding—famously pressed by Douglas Hofstadter against statistical-MT advocates, and structurally echoed in recent "stochastic parrots" arguments about large language models—appears stalled. Hofstadter maintains that fluent, accurate translation without conceptual grounding or intentional representation of meaning is shallow pattern-matching; defenders reply that predictive adequacy and contextual robustness across languages just are linguistic competence, or that demanding a further "understanding" layer is a category mistake. The engineering results are not in dispute; the philosophical conclusions do not converge. Diagnose the impasse: is it a verbal disagreement about what "understanding" requires—conscious access, symbolic representation, counterfactual robustness—versus functional success? A substantive but masked dispute about whether an explanation of competence must cite internal representations? Or are the sides answering different questions—one about the phenomenology and intentionality of human translation, the other about the adequacy criteria for artificial systems—under one label? Present your diagnosis with reference to what each side actually commits to, and clearly flag any part of your analysis that is your own contested hypothesis rather than settled. Then specify what would count as progress given your diagnosis: an empirical result showing a specific kind of contextual failure, a formal argument about the necessity of representational structure, or a conceptual distinction that dissolves the dispute? In responding, separate what is well established—such as the absence of explicit, human-like semantic representations in current architectures—from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of language and cognitive scientists, and avoid presenting any single contested view of understanding (for example, that functional equivalence exhausts it) as the consensus.
