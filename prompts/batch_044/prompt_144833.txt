I've been thinking on my own about dispositions—qualities like being fragile, soluble, or magnetic—and I recently read a summary of a philosophical debate about so-called finks and masks. The summary explained things this way. Every genuine disposition must be grounded in some actual categorical property of the object, such as its molecular structure. A fink occurs when an object lacks a disposition, yet an interfering mechanism makes it behave as though it has one: for instance, a live electrical wire fitted with a hidden cutoff that kills the current the instant someone touches it, so the wire never actually conducts. A mask is the reverse situation: the object truly possesses the disposition, but an external factor blocks its manifestation—like a fragile crystal vase wrapped in shock-absorbing foam that prevents it from shattering when struck. The summary then claimed that philosophers settled these puzzles with an ideal-conditions analysis, according to which something is disposed to behave a certain way only if it would do so in perfect circumstances with no interference at all. From this, the summary concluded that finks and masks are merely exotic exceptions that pose no real threat to the reality of dispositions, and that most philosophers today agree dispositions are nothing over and above their categorical bases—just convenient descriptions of physical properties. The summary also introduced a combined case: a crystal encased in a gel that both cushions it from impact and chemically dissolves its brittle lattice at the precise moment of contact, arguing that here the mask and fink cancel each other out and leave only bare physical properties behind, which proves dispositions can always be reduced to their underlying grounds. Something about this feels too neat, especially the idea that the debate is settled and that everyone accepts reductionism. Is this summary fair?
