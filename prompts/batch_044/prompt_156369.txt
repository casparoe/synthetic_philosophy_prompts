Write the opening remarks for a workshop on moral responsibility convened to honor a retiring philosopher of action. The setting is formal but intimate: roughly forty guests, evenly split between specialists in free will and moral psychology and the retiree’s family, none of whom are academics. The retiree is known for defending Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument—that ultimate moral responsibility requires being causa sui, which is impossible, so deep desert for actions is never available—and she has explicitly asked that the event avoid framing her career as something she authored ex nihilo. Structure the approximately six-minute speech around one specific, non-obvious extension of that argument: apply the regress not to individual actions but to collaborative intellectual achievements, distinguishing between distributive credit (the proper object of gratitude toward conditions and collaborators) and regressive authorship (the impossibility of any participant claiming to have made themselves into the thinker capable of the work). This philosophical distinction should do real structural work: it must determine exactly what kinds of praise are permissible (acknowledgment of enabling circumstances, not desert for outcomes) and should make standard retirement tropes—“you earned this,” “this legacy is yours alone”—literally unsayable within the speech’s logic. The remarks must be precise enough that the philosophers recognize the move and see that it rules out certain compliments, yet accessible and warm enough that the family understands why the speaker refuses to call the retiree the ultimate author of her own success. Do not include citations, footnotes, page references, or any request for a bibliography or reading list; you may refer to Strawson’s argument by its common name, but the speech should stand entirely on the reasoning presented.
