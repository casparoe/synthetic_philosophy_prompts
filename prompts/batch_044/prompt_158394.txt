I'm applying for a three-month independent research fellowship in philosophy of logic and the foundations of artificial reasoning. I need a polished, two-page proposal that takes up the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents but reframes it through a specific, non-standard lens: rather than treating self-trust as a global, binary attitude in which an agent either endorses or rejects its own overall reliability, I want to investigate what happens when self-assessment is structurally graded and module-relative. Specifically, the agent assigns differential confidence weights to distinct internal reasoning modules—deductive inference, probabilistic updating, meta-reasoning about resource bounds, and temporal consistency checks—without issuing a single all-or-nothing self-endorsement.

Write the proposal. It must include: (1) a precise statement of the research question, clearly distinguishing the graded-module case from both the standard categorical formulation and from simple probabilistic self-belief; (2) an explanation of why existing discussions of the Löbian obstacle leave this variant genuinely open, identifying the conceptual gap without inventing specific citations or attributing arguments to named authors; (3) the method—what conceptual analysis, formal modeling, or argument reconstruction I would actually undertake, broken down by month, and an explicit defense of why this conceptual-formal hybrid is preferable to a purely logical or purely empirical-psychological approach; (4) a clear taxonomy of possible outcomes: what a positive result, a negative result, and a null result would each look like, plus an honest criterion for what would count as the project failing outright rather than merely yielding an inconclusive answer; (5) a realistic scope assessment arguing why three months is sufficient to isolate and analyze this graded framing but insufficient to resolve the classic categorical version; and (6) a brief, non-technical argument for why this matters for the design of actually constructed reasoning systems, not only for modal logic.

Keep the framing technically serious—feel free to invoke reflection principles, fixed-point conditions, or module-relative consistency constraints where they genuinely illuminate the question—but do not include a bibliography, reading list, or request for citations. Do not quote specific texts verbatim. The proposal should read as ready for a selection committee familiar with the area, demonstrating that the applicant can locate a tractable, open question at the intersection of self-reference and rational agency.
