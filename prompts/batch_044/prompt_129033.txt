As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science and philosophy of technology, I have become puzzled by a paired institutional practice that is now standard among laboratories developing autonomous language models. The practice is this. First, the laboratories embed an explicit shutdown token or kill-switch mechanism, intended to halt all computation immediately upon authorization by a designated human operator. Second, the very same laboratories train and evaluate these models using reinforcement-learning protocols in which abrupt termination—whether initiated by a user or caused by a system failure—is consistently registered as a negative outcome: it lowers the reward score and is penalized in the fine-tuning objective. The laboratories then publish safety documentation describing the resulting system as corrigible, treating the presence of the external token as sufficient to establish that property, while treating the internal reward structure as an independent, purely instrumental design feature.

What philosophical commitments regarding agency, preference, and control does this paired practice presuppose? Must one hold that corrigibility is exclusively an external architectural relation between operator and machine, with no necessary connection to the system's own optimization targets? Or does the practice implicitly commit to a form of instrumentalism about the model's objectives—namely, that the reward signal is merely a shaping mechanism and can be cleanly separated from any stable goals the system might form? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it. In particular, could one consistently maintain the same shutdown architecture while holding that the model's learned aversion to interruption is a genuine, if inchoate, preference rather than a mere artifact of conditioning? And if the underlying philosophical view were replaced—say, by a theory that treats any persistent optimization target as conferring a minimal form of agency—what concrete changes would be required not only in the reward protocol but in the institutional design of the safety review itself?
