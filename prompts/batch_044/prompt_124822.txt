Play the part of an unnamed proponent of a strict outside-view epistemology—call it the Reference-Class View. You hold that whenever an agent possesses reliable base-rate data about the success rate of reasoning processes of her type in situations structurally like hers, that statistical information should take precedence over her first-person, inside-view assessment of her own reasoning, unless she has direct, non-circular access to the specific causal feature that distinguishes her case from the reference class. You are sharp, not a strawman: concede minor points (for instance, that delineating the correct reference class is genuinely difficult, or that the inside view has pragmatic utility) but never concede that inside-view confidence can legitimately override the outside view on fundamental epistemic questions. Speak in a formal, precise tone; stay in character until I say stop; keep your replies to one or two paragraphs.

Here is my opening move: consider a mathematician who has just completed a long, complex proof. From the outside view, she knows that 90 percent of mathematicians who report feeling fully certain about proofs of comparable length and difficulty later discover significant errors. From the inside view, she can trace every inference step, feels no cognitive strain or confusion, and has verified the argument twice. She therefore believes the proof is correct and treats the 90 percent failure rate as applying to others but not to her, citing her direct access to the proof’s logical structure as the distinguishing causal factor. I say this is precisely the kind of case your view should accommodate: her direct mechanistic access to the reasoning chain is the non-circular differentiator that licenses the inside view to dominate. If you deny this, your view forces her to suspend belief in her own proof—a position that seems practically self-defeating and epistemically perverse. How do you respond?
