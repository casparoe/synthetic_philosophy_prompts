I have been working through Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life, composed in the 1920s and often translated under the title On the Meaning of Life, and I find one central passage both compelling and difficult to reconcile with the logical empiricism for which he is better known. In the section I am trying to understand, Schlick draws a sharp distinction between activities directed at an external purpose, which he characterizes as labor or work, and activities done for their own intrinsic satisfaction, which he calls play. He argues that only play can genuinely ground life's meaning, because any purpose that lies outside an activity can always be subjected to further questioning—asking to what end that end is pursued—producing an infinite regress that is broken only when one reaches an activity valued for itself, for the satisfaction it brings in the present moment. Applying this framework, he criticizes religious and moral systems that promise eternal reward or locate meaning in cosmic destiny, insisting that these are merely displaced forms of labor since they treat present life as instrumental to something beyond it. More unexpectedly, Schlick extends the category of play to include scientific inquiry itself, provided it is pursued not for technological control or practical prediction but purely for the intellectual satisfaction of understanding nature.

My difficulty is not with the distinction itself, but with how it relates to Schlick's verificationist account of the meaning of scientific statements. If the meaning of a scientific claim is fixed by the practical procedures through which it can be empirically verified—a view that treats scientific language as essentially tied to methods of testing, prediction, and instrumental mastery—then scientific inquiry appears deeply purposive. On Schlick's own terms in the essay, that should make science a form of labor rather than play, since it is oriented toward achieving correct predictions and exercising control over nature. Yet in the same passage he holds up disinterested scientific curiosity as a paradigmatic example of meaningful, playful activity. I am trying to understand whether Schlick intends to separate the psychological attitude of the individual scientist—who may personally experience inquiry as playful—from the logical structure of scientific meaning, which remains verificationist and goal-directed; or whether he is implicitly revising his verificationism by suggesting that the ultimate justification of science lies not in predictive success but in the intrinsic value of the cognitive experience it produces. Could you help me work through this tension and clarify how, on Schlick's view, scientific verification and playful meaning are meant to coexist without reducing one to the other?
