For a feature I'm writing on the afterlife of early modern metaphysics in contemporary philosophy of mind, I'm drafting an essay on Berkeley's master argument in the Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous, and I'd like your critical comments on its reasoning before I finalize it. The central claim is that Berkeley's argument against the existence of matter unperceived succeeds only by equivocating between ontological independence—existing without being perceived—and epistemic independence—being knowable without being perceived. I inject a distinction, not from Berkeley's usual commentators but from phenomenological analyses of absence, between "negative presence" (an object given precisely as absent-to-me, like noticing a missing chair) and "absolute absence" (no mental content whatsoever). I argue that Hylas can consistently conceive of matter existing in the first mode—present-to-no-human-mind yet still structured within divine perception—without committing to the second, allegedly inconceivable mode. This, I contend, defuses the master argument without requiring direct realism, but it also forces Berkeley to concede that the "ideas in the mind of God" are not mere copies of human sensory ideas but structurally different in kind, which in turn risks collapsing immaterialism into a form of panpsychism Berkeley explicitly rejects. Specifically, I want comments on whether I've misread Philonous's slide from conceivability to possibility, whether the phenomenological distinction is genuinely illuminating or anachronistic, and whether the panpsychism charge actually sticks. Please say what textual point or counter-argument would change your assessment, and identify where my reasoning is most likely to fail or collapse.
