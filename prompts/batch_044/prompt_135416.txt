I sit on our city's environmental ethics panel — actually I joined right after that zoning mess last fall, and I'm still learning how these votes actually work. We're facing a decision for next Wednesday: should we approve the "BrightLine" street retrofit that replaces every sodium lamp with high-lumen LEDs? The engineers say it cuts energy use by 18%, but our staff epidemiologists estimate it'll cause subclinical circadian disruption in roughly 42,000 residents — slight sleep fragmentation, not acute injury, spread so thin that no single person can point to a specific harm-event. The alternative is keeping the old system, which concentrates heavy-metal exposure and light-trespass on about 800 households near the industrial corridor. One faction on the panel says 42,000 tiny harms can't aggregate into a moral catastrophe — it's essentially the torture-versus-dust-specks problem, except here there's no identifiable victim to suffer the torture end. The other faction says if we refuse to aggregate subthreshold damage, we're treating diffuse structural wrongs as ethically invisible just because they're imperceptible.

Can you draft the short opinion memo I'll circulate before Wednesday? It should recommend one concrete course — approve with conditions, reject, or approve with a sunset clause and a mitigation fund — with a clear timeline. The reasoning needs to take seriously the view that subthreshold harms don't add up in any morally meaningful way, not just dismiss it as bad arithmetic. I was talking with my friend who teaches decision theory over coffee, and she threw out this idea she calls moral granularity — that maybe the unit of moral concern shouldn't be the individual harm-event but the regulatory choice-structure itself — but I'm not sure wether that actually solves anything or just renames the problem. Anyway, don't include a bibliography or citation list; I just need arguments that stand on their own and can survive a vote where half the room hasn't read the literature. Make sure the memo includes an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side, since I'll need to defend whatever recommendation I make.
