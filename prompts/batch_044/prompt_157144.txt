I facilitate a graduate seminar of eight students for ninety minutes on collective decision-making. Design a classroom activity in which participants physically construct Arrow’s impossibility for themselves, without being told the theorem’s name in advance. Frame the task as aggregating ordinal pairwise rankings of three proposed mechanisms for managing a shared digital commons—open access, licensed access, and tiered access. Before the exercise, secretly assign each student a different normative constraint that sounds reasonable in isolation: (i) if every student prefers mechanism A to B, the group ordering must place A above B; (ii) the group’s ordering of A versus B must not change when mechanism C is added or removed; (iii) no single student should determine the final ordering of any pair. Provide the exact sequence of at least three preference profiles the group should work through, arranged so that applying all three constraints produces either a cycle or an unavoidable dictator, and specify how the students should record their group rankings after each profile so they discover the contradiction through their own attempts. Then outline a ten-to-fifteen-minute debrief that maps each deadlock onto the corresponding Arrow condition, and be explicit about which feature of the theorem the physical setup cannot reproduce—specifically, the universal quantification over all possible preference profiles. Lead with the bottom line: the precise cyclical or dictatorial outcome the group should hit. Keep the response under 350 words; do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
