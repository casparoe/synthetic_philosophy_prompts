ok here's my rough philospohical profile on care ethics for a discussion group, want a consistency check before i present it. here's where i've landed:

1. moral reasoning should start from concrete responsiveness to particular others in dependancy relations, not from applying universal rules.
2. but i also think we have direct obligations to strangers we'll never meet, not just derivative ones through institutions or global structures.
3. i see dependency as a permanent human condition, not a temporary failure state that autonomy should eliminate.
4. still, i think autonomy and self-determination are core goods, especially for people who've had them systematically denied.
5. i draw a line between care-as-attunement (emotional presence, listening, being-with) and care-as-provision (material support, labor, resources). i think only attunement is strictly required; provision is praiseworthy but supererogitory.
6. at the same time, i think care labor should be paid and socially recognized, but i worry that monetizing intimate attunement corrupts the relationship.
7. i think the scope of who we owe attunement to can expand through practice and imagination, but i don't think there's a moral failure in failing to feel attunement for people outside one's immediate relational web.

which ones actually pull against each other? is (1) incompatible with (2) unless i water one down? does (3) clash with (4), or can they fit together if autonomy is understood relationally? is (5) stable, or does calling provision supererogitory undermine my support for economic recognition in (6)? and does (7) just let me dodge (2) by limiting obligation to feeling rather than action?

please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything. for each real conflict say what's the least costly revision. also point out if there's an apparent tension that dissolves once you look closer. and tell me which single change would clean up the most problems.
