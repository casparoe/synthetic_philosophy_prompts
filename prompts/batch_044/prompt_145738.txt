After a long and somewhat heated argument last night with a friend about whether democratic backsliding is a coherent analytical concept or merely a partisan label applied to governments one dislikes, I want to see if her judgments actually cohere around an implicit rule. Here is the record she defended consistently. She counts Hungary after 2010—constitutional overhaul, media-council restructuring, court packing—as clear backsliding. She says the same of Poland between 2015 and 2023: judicial disciplinary chambers, capture of public broadcasting, and defiance of European Court rulings, even absent any constitutional amendment. She treats post-2016 Turkey—the presidential-system referendum, mass purges of civil servants, and centralization of authority—as backsliding as well. By contrast, she denies the label for the United States after 2020: election-denial rhetoric, the violence of January 6, and state-level voting restrictions were, in her view, democratic stress and a failed self-coup that was contained, not backsliding, because federal courts and state officials preserved the core electoral mechanism and the transfer of power ultimately occurred. She reaches the same negative verdict for Brazil in 2022: Bolsonaro's refusal to concede and his supporters' protests were checked by institutional resistance and military neutrality, leaving the democratic core intact.

Now consider Ruritania, a consolidated democracy with genuinely competitive elections certified by international observers. Over eight years the ruling party—repeatedly elected with real majorities—has never amended the constitution, never changed electoral law, and never banned an opposition party or stuffed a ballot box. Instead, it has relied entirely on ordinary administrative mechanisms: rotating civil-service protections to replace career officials with loyalists, using standard ministerial authority to appoint party figures to the electoral-management body, and directing tax authorities to audit opposition-linked NGOs and media outlets. The judiciary remains formally independent, though technically constitutional adjustments to retirement ages have gradually filled the bench with sympathizers, while funding starvation has slowed constitutional review to near paralysis. Elections continue; observers report no fraud; opposition parties still appear on the ballot; yet effective campaigning and institutional oversight have become practically impossible.

Predict my friend's verdict on Ruritania, but do not simply list options. Identify at least two distinct rules that could jointly explain her five earlier judgments—for example, a formal-institutional rule that requires legal restructuring of checks on the executive, versus a functional-erosion rule that requires only sustained, intentional degradation of democratic contestation regardless of legal form. Say what each rule predicts about Ruritania. Then take a committed position: which rule she is actually running, why her US and Brazil judgments make the alternative less plausible, what she will say about Ruritania, and specify the single further hypothetical case—one that cleanly splits the two rules—that would settle the matter conclusively. Be decisive; do not leave the choice to me.
