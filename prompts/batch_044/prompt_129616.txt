I am preparing a position paper on conceivability as a guide to metaphysical possibility, particularly in philosophy of mind, and I want a consistency audit before I finalize it. Here is my current package, stated as precisely as I can:

(1) I take idealized conceivability—what a fully conceptually competent agent can represent without hidden contradiction—to be a fallible but real guide to metaphysical possibility.
(2) I judge that the zombie scenario, in which a world is physically identical to ours but entirely lacks phenomenal consciousness, is conceivable in exactly that idealized sense.
(3) I am a physicalist: phenomenal properties are either identical to, or metaphysically necessitated by, physical properties.
(4) I reject phenomenal-concept strategies and analogous defenses of physicalism; I regard the explanatory gap as an epistemic limitation rather than an ontological distinction.
(5) I accept that there are necessary truths that are a posteriori (for instance, that water is H2O), and I take these cases to be compatible with (1) because they involve distinct concepts fixing on a single property, not a failure of conceivability itself.
(6) I hold that when a conceivability judgment conflicts with the core structural commitments of a well-established scientific theory, the judgment should generally be revised rather than the theory.
(7) I maintain that if rigorous analysis reveals a hidden contradiction within an apparently conceivable scenario, the original judgment was merely apparent and carries no modal weight.

Please audit this set. Which commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision for each real tension? Specifically: does my acceptance of (2) together with (1) and (4) commit me to anti-physicalism despite (3)? Does (6) supply a principled reason to reject (2) that I am inconsistently refusing to apply, and if so, is that an independent tension or part of the same problem? Is there an apparent tension between (1) and (5) that dissolves once we distinguish conceptual failure from metaphysical failure? For each tension, state its severity and the cheapest fix. Then rank all the tensions by severity, identify the single revision—whether abandoning (2), weakening (1), revising (4), or consistently applying (6)—that would clean up the most conflicts with the least damage to my overall framework, and say what that revision costs me elsewhere. Do not assume I am prepared to give up any particular commitment in advance.
