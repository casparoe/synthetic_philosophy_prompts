Read Sophocles' Antigone through Pascal's distinction in the Pensées between justice and force—specifically the idea that justice is inherently disputable, force is unmistakable, and therefore law usually attaches force to custom rather than to reasoned justice, with the result that justice without force is powerless. Take Creon's edict as an exercise of sovereign force claiming to embody justice, and Antigone's burial of Polynices as an appeal to unwritten divine law that possesses no institutional power. Does the play dramatize Pascal's pessimism that pure justice without force is helpless, given that Antigone dies and nothing changes until Creon's family is destroyed? Or does her death function as a kind of moral force that eventually overturns the edict, thereby complicating Pascal's pragmatic claim that one should obey the strongest? I want a reading faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce the tragedy to an illustration of Pascal, and that asks where the play resists the reading—perhaps in the chorus's ambiguity, or in the fact that Creon's collapse comes from prophetic warning rather than rational recognition. What, if anything, does the drama add to the philosophical debate about justice and power that argument alone cannot?
