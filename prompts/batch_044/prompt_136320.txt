I have been working through a summary—apparently from a recent review essay—of Margalit's account of the ethics of memory and Rieff's argument for the legitimacy of forgetting. The author frames both thinkers as addressing a single problem, which she terms mnemonic sovereignty: the question of who has the authority to determine what a community must remember and what it may legitimately forget.

According to the summary, Margalit holds that remembrance is primarily an ethical rather than political matter, governed by the density of one's relations to the dead. For those in thick relations—family, immediate community—there is a direct, non-delegable duty to remember specific victims; for those in thin relations—fellow citizens, distant observers—the obligation becomes shared and symbolic, expressed through collective commemoration. Crucially, the summary claims that Margalit treats these two registers as continuous rather than categorically distinct, so that state-sponsored memorials in a distant nation are, for Margalit, direct expressions of the same thick-relation duty, merely distributed across a larger group. The summary also asserts that Margalit rejects any ethical distinction between remembering and commemorating, insisting that public monuments and private grief answer to identical moral standards.

Rieff is summarized as arguing that modern societies suffer from an imperative to remember that is therapeutically destructive and politically manipulative. The summary concedes that Rieff allows individuals to mourn their own dead, but claims he holds that all collective remembrance—whether state-led or civil-societal—is a form of enforced memory that perpetuates conflict and should therefore be abandoned in post-atrocity contexts.

From these premises, the author derives a principle she calls mnemonic proportionality: the intensity of remembrance owed to a historical evil should scale directly with the density of present social ties to its victims, and inversely with the risk that remembering will reignite present conflict. On this principle, she concludes, Western societies have a weaker obligation to maintain public remembrance of the Holocaust today than they have to suppress remembrance of more recent, locally implicated atrocities, since the latter involve denser present ties and higher conflict risk.

I find the synthesis elegant, but I suspect it misrepresents both thinkers and that the conclusion does not follow. Could you audit the summary? Please separate: (a) what is accurate about Margalit's distinction between thick and thin relations and his view of collective commemoration; (b) whether the summary's claim about continuity between private grief and public monuments, and its erasure of the ethics/politics distinction, are fair to Margalit; (c) whether Rieff's position is correctly rendered or whether he targets a more specific practice of enforced remembrance; and (d) whether the principle of mnemonic proportionality and the final inference about Western obligations actually derive from anything either author holds, or whether they depend on the misrepresentations identified in (b) and (c).
