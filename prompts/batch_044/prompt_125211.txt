I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for an adult education reading group — intelligent non-specialists with backgrounds in law, journalism, and the humanities — on the conceptual boundary between legend and history. I intend to work through three cases in parallel: the Trojan War, the figure of King Arthur, and the Exodus narrative. Rather than deliver an explanation myself, I want you to plan the explanatory arc: the dependency structure of the ideas, the carrying example, the misconception map, the comprehension check, and what must be left unsaid.

Specify the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the group can use them rather than merely remember them: what distinction must be fixed before the cases are introduced — for instance, between evidential independence and narrative coherence, or between legendary accretion and a recoverable historical kernel — and how each subsequent idea depends on the prior one. Choose one of the three cases as the central carrying example that should occupy the middle forty minutes, and explain precisely what that case offers and lacks that makes it suitable, together with how the other two should be positioned in relation to it — as contrast, as reinforcement, or as boundary tests — and why none of them can carry the session alone.

Map the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or form: I anticipate at least 'absence of archaeological evidence is evidence of absence', 'legend is merely old fiction with branding', and 'if professional historians disagree, the question is unanswerable in principle'. For each, state the point in the sequence at which it should be preempted or dissolved, and the specific redirection — not just a correction, but a reframing that turns the misconception into a step toward the method.

Design one concrete comparative exercise for the thirty-minute mark that forces the participants to apply the framework rather than recount the stories, and propose one direct question I can ask at the close to distinguish those who have grasped the method from those who have merely become sceptical or credulous.

Also state explicitly what I should refuse to adjudicate — for example, whether the Exodus occurred as narrated — and how to present that refusal as an integral part of the lesson rather than an evasion.

Finally, inject one niche or under-taught conceptual idea that will lift the discussion out of the familiar 'myth versus fact' groove — perhaps the idea that narrative smoothing is itself an evidential liability in oral transmission, or that source silence operates with different logical weight in textual versus archaeological cultures — and indicate exactly where in the sequence it should appear and what work it should do.

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Write in British English, in a formal, polished register.
