I've been in this philosophy reading group for a few months now and we've been stuck on moral luck since like week three. You know the usual setup - Nagel's four kinds, Williams on Gauguin, everyone nodding at the drunk driver who hits the kid versus the one who gets home safe. But here's what's wierd: in our group, half of us think the anti-luck people are just being Kantian about agency, and the other half thinks the pro-luck people are smuggling in consequentialist intuitions about harm. And we've been going in circles for months, which is frustrating because everyone actually agrees on the cases.

But lately I've started wondering if the stall isn't about morality at all, but about what I guess I'd call 'retroactive calibration' - the way we use outcomes to calibrate how seriously we take an agent's prior mental states. Like, when the driver kills someone, we retroactively upgrade the recklessness from sloppy to monstrous, not because new info about her character came to light, but because the outcome gives us a different scale to measure it against. The anti-luck camp says this is just an epistemic bias we should correct for; the pro-luck camp says the scale shift is part of what moral assessment actually is. So is this actually a hidden disagreement about the metaphysics of character, or is it a verbal fight about whether 'moral assessment' means what you deserve versus how we should respond?

And more generally, has the whole moral luck debate stalled because both sides are describing the same psychological mechanism - this retroactive calibration thing - but one calls it a distortion and the other calls it constitutive? Or am I just inventing a distinction that doesn't help? I want to know what kind of disagreement this really is - substantive, verbal, hidden empirical question, bedrock intuition - and whether there's anything, maybe a conceptual distinction or a change in how we frame the cases, that could actually move it, or if we're just stuck.
