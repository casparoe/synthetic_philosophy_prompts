I have been trying to crystallize my own views on preference aggregation and mechanism design for a working-group discussion on collective choice, and I want an audit of whether the following commitments form a stable package or hide contradictions I have not noticed. My position comprises six claims. First, I accept the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem in its standard form: with three or more alternatives and a universal domain of ordinal preferences, any strategy-proof social choice rule must be either dictatorial or restricted in range. Second, I treat strategy-proofness as a hard institutional constraint—more fundamental than full surjectivity or even Pareto efficiency—because I believe democratic legitimacy requires removing incentives for voters to misrepresent their preferences. Third, I regard approval voting as the best available practical reform for actual elections, mainly on the grounds that it weakens strategic manipulation relative to plurality and standard ranked-choice rules. Fourth, I reject the dictatorial escape clause as democratically illegitimate regardless of whether the dictator is predetermined or rotates; if a rule achieves strategy-proofness only by vesting decisive authority in a single voter, it violates a basic fairness condition and should be discarded. Fifth, I do not consider a restricted range to be a serious democratic flaw, provided the excluded orderings are extreme or pathological ones that would not survive reasonable deliberation. Sixth, I am skeptical that the theorem applies with full force to small-group procedural mechanisms—specifically, I think a four-person committee using a sequential veto-and-rank protocol with partial information disclosure might achieve both strategy-proofness and non-dictatorship without restricting the range, because procedural sequencing can disrupt the direct-revelation structure that the proof assumes.

Please go through these commitments systematically. Identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which tensions are merely apparent and dissolve once the commitments are properly interpreted, and rank the real conflicts by severity. For each genuine tension, specify exactly what pulls against what, assess how damaging the conflict is, and identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency together with what that revision costs me. Finally, tell me which single commitment I should revise or abandon if my goal is to clean up the largest number of conflicts while preserving as much of the rest of my outlook as possible.
