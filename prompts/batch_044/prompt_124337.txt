I am designing a comparative philosophy of death module, and I want a prompt that forces students to hold a literary text and a philosophical position in genuine tension rather than using one as decoration for the other. Read Leo Tolstoy's novella The Death of Ivan Ilyich through the lens of Zhuangzi's philosophy of death—specifically his account of death as transformation rather than annihilation, his refusal to mourn his wife on the grounds that she has merely changed form within the continuous flux of nature, and his butterfly-dream skepticism about fixed boundaries between self and other states. I want a reading that takes both works seriously: does Ivan's progression from denial through terror to a final, silent acceptance represent a failure to achieve the Zhuangzian dissolution of ego-boundaries, or does Tolstoy's framing—particularly the black sack, the wrongness of Ivan's bureaucratic life, and the ambiguous light that appears at the end—resist reduction to a cosmic transformation narrative? Pay close attention to the scene in which Ivan asks what death is and receives no answer: could a Zhuangzian interpreter read that silence not as existential absence but as the natural process speaking through dissolution? At the same time, address where Tolstoy's implicit moral reckoning—death as judgment on a life misspent—blocks that reading. I am also interested in whether Zhuangzi's position adds something to contemporary analytic debates about whether death harms the dead—the deprivation account versus transformation accounts—that arguments by Nagel or Feldman miss, particularly regarding the continuity of the self with natural processes rather than with a future stream of goods. Which scenes should be assigned for close comparative discussion, and where must an honest critic concede that the philosophical framework distorts the novella's religious and moral architecture?
