I've been chewing on that Bernard Williams idea about the husband saving his wife but having one thought too many - the direct commitment should be enough on its own. But there's a variant I keep noticing and I can't tell if it's just the same thing with extra steps. Picture someone acting out of deep personal loyalty, like staying with a failing project or caring for a sick freind, where the direct reason genuinely drives the bus. But they also hold some universal moral principle in reserve - not because they're doubting the personal reason, but like a backup generator running parallel. The principle doesn't motivate anything; it's just shadowing the direct reason. So the act isn't corrupted by replacing love with duty, which is the standard integrity objection, and it's not hypocracy since both motives are real. It's more like the moral reason is redundantly present, changing the texture of the act without changing its cause. Does this specific setup have a name? How exactly does it differ from one-thought-too-many or from just having overdetermined motives? If it's not in the literature, what boundary conditions would separate it from ordinary moral self-assurance?
