I'm trying to isolate the principle behind my judgments about interpersonal welfare comparison across structurally dissimilar minds. Two neurotypical adults with parallel lives: cardinal comparison seems legitimate. A human with chronic pain versus one without: clearly worse off. A satisfied dolphin versus a mildly dissatisfied human: I hesitate to rank them, but comparison does not feel conceptually blocked—only indeterminate within wide bounds. A conscious human versus a non-conscious artificial system that reports identical preference profiles: comparison seems impossible in principle, not merely epistemically hard. Now consider a hypothetical "partitioned" subject: one continuous consciousness split into two parallel, non-interacting streams, each retaining half the original memory and affective range. I judge each stream worse off than the unified original, yet I am unsure whether the pair together should be summed, averaged, or treated as separate moral individuals when weighed against an unpartitioned person. Finally, comparing welfare across generations whose sensory modalities might be radically expanded: standard metrics feel inadequate, yet I still think some forced comparisons are required for longtermist decision-making. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps an architecturally constrained preference-satisfaction view, a capacity-weighted hedonism, or a particularist fallback—that accommodates all of these? If not, which verdict should be abandoned? And what should the best account predict for a case I have not listed: comparing one individual whose subjective experience runs at ten times the normal rate to ten individuals living at standard speed over the same objective duration?
