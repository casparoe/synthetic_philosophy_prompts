I serve on the ethics and programming committee of a national museum of social history. At Thursday’s meeting we must vote on whether to approve institutional funding for an immersive theatrical installation—working title The Unrecorded Hours—about a prominent 19th-century public-health reformer whose speeches and official reports are extensively archived, but whose private correspondence was largely destroyed in a 1920s fire. The proposal uses actors, reconstructed dialogue, and clearly labeled speculative scenes—most controversially, an invented internal monologue in which the reformer debates accepting a factory-owner’s bribe—to dramatize moral conflicts never documented in the surviving record. The reformer’s descendants have objected formally: they do not allege factual error in any documented claim, but argue that fabricating an interior life, especially one involving unverified moral corruption, violates a distinct posthumous interest they term narrative sovereignty—the dead’s claim not to be conscripted into a genre of story (psychological drama about hidden vice) that the historical record neither supports nor rules out. The artists counter that biography is always constructive, that the installation marks every reconstruction as hypothetical, and that granting descendants a veto over speculative interiority would paralyze the museum’s entire dramatic programming.

Draft the committee’s decision memo recommending approval, conditional approval, or rejection, with a concrete course of action and a timeline for implementation or appeal. The memo should be circulated before Thursday’s vote and should be directly actionable by museum staff. In your reasoning, take the descendants’ distinction between epistemic harm (misrepresenting documented actions or statements) and narrative sovereignty seriously: explain whether the museum owes the dead fidelity only to what they did and said, or also to the boundaries of how their consciousness may be dramatized. Give an honest, non-dismissive account of the strongest argument on the artists’ side—specifically, that any bright-line prohibition on invented interior monologue would eliminate not only this installation but large classes of legitimate historical drama—and show how your recommendation accommodates that risk without reducing the committee’s decision to either a pure descendant veto or an unqualified artistic-license standard. Do not request citations or bibliographies; reason from the conceptual distinction and the institutional stakes alone.
