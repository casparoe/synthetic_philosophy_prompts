As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to work out my own position on knowing-how versus knowing-that, largely through the lens of debugging, system design, and what current AI can actually do. I want a consistency audit of the following package before I present it in a discussion group.

(1) I take knowing-how to be genuinely distinct from knowing-that: the ability to diagnose a failing distributed system at 3 a.m. involves contextual judgment, stress-adaptive pattern recognition, and tacit prioritization that I cannot fully reduce to any propositional manual or documentation set.

(2) At the same time, I think a large language model trained only on code repositories and technical documentation can genuinely know how to program in Python—not merely know that certain syntax rules hold—because its outputs in novel coding contexts are adaptively structured rather than mechanically retrieved.

(3) I hold that possessing knowing-how requires the capacity for adaptive performance when problem parameters shift in ways not present in prior training, experience, or explicit specification.

(4) Yet I do not think this adaptive capacity requires biological embodiment, conscious experience, or direct physical interaction with hardware; a purely computational system running in an isolated sandbox could, in principle, satisfy it.

(5) I also maintain that the knowing-how versus knowing-that distinction is ultimately pragmatic rather than metaphysically deep: it tracks differences in how information is organized and accessed for action, not an ontological divide between kinds of mental or computational states.

(6) Finally, I reject the intellectualist view that all knowing-how is just knowing-that accessed through a practical mode of presentation; that framing strikes me as redescribing the phenomenon rather than explaining away the difference I noted in (1).

Do these six commitments form a coherent package? I am particularly unsure whether (1) and (5) pull in opposite directions—if the distinction is only pragmatic, can it still be "genuine" in the strong sense I seem to invoke in (1)? I also wonder whether (2) is compatible with (3) given that current LLMs appear to fail when debugging scenarios involve genuinely unprecedented failure modes. Additionally, I worry that (4) undermines the embodied, situated flavor of the capacities I describe in (1), though I suspect that tension might dissolve once "stress" and "context" are interpreted functionally rather than phenomenologically.

Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs are only apparently incompatible, specify the least costly revision for each genuine conflict, and tell me exactly what I would have to surrender in each case. I have not decided which commitment I am most attached to, so treat none as sacrosanct. The deliverable should be an audit of my own position set, not a general lecture on the topic.
