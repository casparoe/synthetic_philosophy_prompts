I've been going in circles with a grad student about Whig historiograhy and now I can't sleep, so I'm dumping it here. He built this chain and it feels like a trap. (1) To write history at all you have to decide which events matter. (2) But "matter" is always relative to some endpoint or purpose—there's no view-from-nowhere importance, no bare "historically significant" property floating free. (3) The only endpoint a historian actually inhabits is the present, since every source is read now and every question is asked from within our current conceptual scheme. (4) So any selection of relevant events is anchored in present concerns, whether we admit it or not. (5) Anchoring the past in present concerns is exactly what Butterfield called Whig history. (6) Therefore every working historian is neccessarily a Whig historian. (7) But then the standard critique—"don't judge the past by present values"—is self-defeating, since that critique itself can only be voiced from a present standpoint. (8) So either we drop the critique and embrace Whig history, or we stop writing history altogether.

He wants to reject (3), arguing that historians can adopt a "counterfactual endpoint"—say, asking what mattered to the people then, not what matters to us now. I want to keep (1) if I can, because giving up on selection criteria sounds like giving up on explanation altogether. But I feel like there's a hidden slide between (3) and (4), maybe an equivocation between "inhabiting the present" and "using present values as the relevance-filter." Or maybe the argument is valid and we just have to bite the bullet? Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs—especially if it forces us to accept some kind of modal neutrality about historical relevance that sounds implausible.
