I'm preparing a graduate seminar on the sociology of scientific discovery, and I've noticed a recurring historiographical pattern in how simultaneous inventions are narrated—one that doesn't seem to have a precise label of its own. Consider the calculus: Newton develops fluxions within a geometric and physical framework tied to motion and limits, while Leibniz builds a symbolic algorithm for differentials rooted in combinatorics and the law of continuity. Their notations, ontological commitments, and problem-sets differ substantially. Yet standard histories often present them as having "discovered calculus," full stop, and then frame the dispute as a race to the same finish line. The same compression happens with Darwin and Wallace: Darwin's theory embeds natural selection in a deep-time, uniformitarian geology with extensive artificial-selection analogies, while Wallace's 1858 essay is more narrowly biogeographic, lacking the Malthusian mechanism's economic framing and the extensive breeding evidence. Still, textbooks speak of "the theory of evolution by natural selection" as a single idea discovered twice.

What I'm after is the specific move—not the fact of simultaneous discovery itself, but the retrospective flattening of two conceptually heterogeneous achievements into one named entity, which then makes the priority question seem like a dispute over ownership of an identical object rather than over credit for parallel but structurally different constructions. Is there an established term for this kind of retrospective conceptual unification? If not, can you propose a precise definition that distinguishes it from mere convergence of ideas, from independent invention of genuinely identical formalisms, and from Whig history? I'd like the carving to be sharp enough that we could apply it to other pairs—say, Lavoisier and Priestley on oxygen, or Boltzmann and Gibbs on statistical mechanics—and determine whether the flattening is justified in each case, or whether it obscures a real divergence in what was actually achieved.
