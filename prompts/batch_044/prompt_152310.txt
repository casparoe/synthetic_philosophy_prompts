I am a doctoral candidate in moral and political philosophy preparing for an interdisciplinary workshop on the mechanics of normative change, and I need a rigorous prep sheet for the Q&A. The thesis I will defend is that the disappearance of chattel slavery and aristocratic dueling is best explained neither by the rationalist model—according to which universalizable dignity arguments gradually convinced societies—nor by the materialist model—according to which shifting economic interests and state monopolies on violence made the practices obsolete—but by a specific kind of conceptual exhaustion within the honor-based frameworks that made them intelligible. More precisely, both norms depended on what I call status-constitutive asymmetry: the background assumption that certain social positions carry a natural, rather than merely conventional, entitlement to deference or command. Once that asymmetry was reconceived as contingent, the honor codes built upon it did not become immoral in any simple sense; rather, they grew performatively incoherent, in that they could still be enacted but could no longer be sincerely performed as expressions of legitimate status. Arguments and interests certainly played roles, but I claim they operated on conceptual terrain that had already lost its internal stability, not on stable ground that they then overturned.

The audience is precisely calibrated to damage this claim from four directions. There is a social historian of early modern Europe who will treat any philosophical framing as an evasion of institutional facts—state consolidation, insurance markets, and the replacement of personal honor by bureaucratic reputation. There is a moral philosopher committed to a rationalist account of moral progress, for whom slavery ended because the universalizability of human dignity eventually became inescapable, and who will hear my talk as an unwarranted reduction of morality to sociology. There is a scholar deeply versed in Bernard Williams’s work on thick concepts and the limits of ethical theory, who will be suspicious of my treating honor as a framework that can “collapse” without leaving behind genuine moral residue. Finally, there is a political economist who holds that all normative change tracks material incentives, and who will regard my language of conceptual incoherence as decorative superstructure. They share one disciplinary anxiety: that I am smuggling normative conclusions into a descriptive account, or conversely, dissolving normative force into functional explanation.

Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in each interlocutor’s vocabulary and with their characteristic commitments intact. For each objection, indicate whether it carries real force or is mainly rhetorical; tell me what I should concede up front rather than defend; provide the concise reply that must land if the thesis is to survive; and flag the weakest point in my own position—the tension between my claim that honor frameworks collapsed from internal incoherence and my implicit reliance on a normative standard by which that incoherence counts as a reason for abandonment—so that I can defend it deliberately rather than hope they miss it. In particular, I want to know the one objection I am probably hoping nobody raises: the question that exposes whether my account can distinguish between a norm that collapses because it becomes conceptually unstable and a norm that is simply abandoned because people stop caring about it, without secretly appealing to the very moral progress narrative I claim to reject. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need a speaker’s prep sheet, ordered by damage, with concessions and replies marked clearly.
