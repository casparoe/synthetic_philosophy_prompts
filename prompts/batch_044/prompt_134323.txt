I am preparing a single 90-minute session for an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar—twelve graduate students—following a conversation with colleagues about how analogical reasoning in restricted domains seems to depend on concepts that are not rigidly bounded but instead shift or "slip" when pushed to their margins. Rather than presenting this as a thesis, I want the students to inhabit the phenomenon. Design a structured in-class activity built around a fictional microdomain I will call "signal-pattern taxonomy": participants receive cards describing synthetic entities defined solely by three operational properties—latency profile, feedback-loop depth, and boundary permeability—and must classify each card as a core instance, an analogical extension, or an unclassifiable outlier. The rules should initially present the three properties as jointly sufficient for category membership, but the staged sequence of cards should violate them one by one in isolation, then in combination, forcing the group to decide whether the concept has genuinely slipped or whether they are arbitrarily stretching it.

Specify participant roles—such as a boundary-keeper who must defend sharp necessary-and-sufficient conditions, an analogist who argues for family-resemblance extension, and a process-observer who tracks which properties are being weighted differently at each decision point—along with a timeline that allocates the full 90 minutes, materials limited to printed cards and a whiteboard, and a non-monetary payoff structure (for instance, collective agreement is required to advance to the next round, with hidden scoring that rewards consistent analogical reasoning but penalizes ad hoc adjustments). The debrief should map specific decision-points in the game onto the dynamics of conceptual slippage—particularly how a concept's prototypical center exerts gravitational pull on peripheral cases even when defining conditions fail—without requiring students to name any particular theoretical framework until after they have articulated the pattern themselves. Be frank about which feature of fluid-concept or copycat-style reasoning this physical setup cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator in the closing discussion. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—whether role confusion, premature consensus, or resistance to the fictional ontology—and suggest concrete adjustments.
