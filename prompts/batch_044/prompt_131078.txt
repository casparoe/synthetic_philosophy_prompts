I am preparing next term’s graduate seminar on the metaphysics of time, and I have been working through the Rietdijk–Putnam reasoning for the block universe with my students. One of them reconstructed a version that now strikes me as suspicious—not because I reject eternalism outright, but because the conclusion seems to swallow the very practice of reasoning that produced it. Here is the argument as formalized:

(1) Special relativity denies absolute simultaneity: for any two spatially separated events A and B, there exist inertial frames in which A precedes B, B precedes A, and they occur simultaneously.
(2) If ontological existence is frame-independent, then for any event E that is real in one frame, E must be real in all frames.
(3) Take any event E lying in what I call my future along my world-line. There exists an inertial frame F in which E is simultaneous with the event I now call my present mental state.
(4) By (2), if E is real in F, then E is already real now, relative to my world-line.
(5) Therefore every event on my world-line—past, present, and future—is equally real within the four-dimensional manifold.
(6) If all my future choices are equally real, then they are already fixed in the manifold; my present deliberation cannot alter their ontological status.
(7) But practical reasoning necessarily presupposes that my present deliberation can determine which future event becomes actual.
(8) Hence the block-universe conclusion in (5) entails that practical reasoning is systematically illusory.
(9) Yet the scientific reasoning that establishes (1) is itself an episode of practical reasoning.
(10) So the argument is self-undermining: it relies on a form of reasoning that its own conclusion declares impossible.

The student insists the flaw must lie in (4), arguing that simultaneity in frame F does not establish co-reality with my present mental state because now is an indexical predicate rather than a frame-independent ontological marker. I am not convinced that this saves practical reasoning without collapsing into a hidden presentism that conflicts with (1). Could you diagnose which step is the weakest link? I would prefer to keep (1) intact, and I am inclined to preserve the indexical reading of now implicit in (4), but I am willing to pay a price elsewhere if it preserves the consistency of scientific and practical reason. In particular, I want to understand whether rejecting (6) requires accepting a modal distinction—between being real in the manifold and being causally accessible to deliberation—that the Rietdijk–Putnam framework cannot accommodate without ad hoc supplementation.
