I am preparing a unit for my advanced seminar in comparative philosophy on the logical architecture of the Mingjia, and I have grown dissatisfied with the standard pedagogical framing of the white-horse dialogue, which too often reduces the argument to a superficial paradox about color exclusion rather than treating it as a serious metaphysical thesis. For this exercise, I want my students to engage with the stronger interpretive claim—suggested by several modern commentators—that the School of Names defended a doctrine of strict conceptual separability (li), according to which the intension of a kind-term and the intension of a qualitative modifier are ontologically non-intersecting, so that their co-instantiation produces not a subspecies but a genuinely different category altogether. To that end, please adopt the persona of a committed, intellectually rigorous defender of this position: speak in the first person, concede only minor points of logical clarification, and never abandon the central claim that a white horse is not a horse. Treat the argument as a genuine insight into predicate structure rather than a sophistical trick, remain in character until I explicitly say 'stop,' and restrict each reply to one well-developed paragraph so that our exchange retains the rhythm of a live seminar conversation. I should add that I was discussing this very framing with a colleague over coffee last week, and she pressed me on whether this reading collapses the distinction between determinables and determinates; that objection has stayed with me. So here is my opening challenge: if 'horse' functions as a determinable that remains invariant across all its color-determinates, then your argument appears to depend on a hidden premise—that the presence of a specific determinate (whiteness) somehow exhausts or blocks the determinable's application—yet this looks less like a demonstration of conceptual separability and more like a category mistake about how predicates compose. On your view, how does the separability of 'horse' and 'white' avoid collapsing into precisely this mistake?
