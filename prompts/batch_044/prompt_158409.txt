Since I hung up my lab coat I've been digging into Darwin's notebooks and Descent of Man, and I think I cooked up an argument that eats itself. Maybe you can spot the leak?

(1) Darwin treats our moral sense as an outgrowth of social instincts — parental affection widening by reason to strangers and group cohesion — shaped purely by natural selection.
(2) He also treats religious belief as likely an evolutionary byproduct of agent-detection, not a response to any real divinity.
(3) So on his account, both morality and religion are adaptive products, not trackers of objective truth.
(4) That means Darwin's own horror at slavery is just his evolved instincts recoiling, no different in kind from preferring sweet fruit.
(5) His agnosticism about God is also just his cognitive setup speaking, not a reasoned verdict.
(6) But belief in evolutionary theory is itself an evolved cognitive habit, shaped for survival and prediction, not necessarily for truth.
(7) So if (3) is right, Darwin undermines not just morality and religion but the rational authority of his own science — he becomes a self-defeating sceptic about everything.
(8) That proves way too much, so one of the steps must be wrong.

My gut says save (1) and reject (2) by insisting that evolutionary origin doesn't determine justification, but then I worry I'm sneaking in a normative layer Darwin's naturalism can't allow. Or maybe the slip is in (6), treating scientific belief like moral feeling? I don't want to loose Darwin's biological story, but I also don't want science to collapse into just another instinct. Which step should I actually reject, and what's the price of rejecting it?
