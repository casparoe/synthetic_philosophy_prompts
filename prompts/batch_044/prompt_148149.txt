I'm writing a feature on whether history gets stuck in ruts or whether we just tell tidy stories afterwards. I've got these gut reactions to four specific cases, and I want to know if there's an actual principle behind them — not just a post-hoc rationalisation.

My verdicts:

QWERTY is pure path dependence. Sholes arranged the keys in the 1870s to solve a mechanical problem, and now we're stuck with it despite far better layouts existing; there's no hidden functional reason it had to win.

The 4ft 8.5in railway gauge is also an accident — it traces back to mine-cart wheel spacing in the north-east — but I don't feel the same lock-in. My instinct is that standardisation pressure across networks would have pushed railways toward roughly this gauge, or something very close, within a generation or two. So its origin is contingent, yet its survival feels overdetermined.

The AC versus DC current war in the 1890s strikes me as genuinely open. Westinghouse and Tesla's AC system won partly through the Niagara Falls contract and partly through Edison's disastrous PR campaign against it. Had those specific events broken differently, DC might dominate now, and the grid would look radically other. That's contingency without any hidden attractor.

Then there's the global adoption of the Gregorian calendar. Yes, Catholic Europe pushed it first, but its spread elsewhere feels driven by astronomical accuracy rather than political inertia. I wouldn't call it path-dependent at all; it was simply the best clock available and would have displaced local calendars eventually.

So my pattern is inconsistent: QWERTY and AC/DC are locked-in accidents, railway gauge hides an inevitable convergence, and the Gregorian reform was never an accident. Its just describing the cases rather than explaining them, unless there's something deeper. Is there a coherent principle — maybe involving network externalities, functional redundancy, or institutional hysteresis — that saves all four? Or do I have to drop one?

I want a committed answer, not a menu: state the principle precisely, say which clause each case motivates, predict what it says about cases I haven't mentioned (for instance, whether the Unix file heirarchy is locked in, or why the metric system conquered science but not UK road signs), and be honest about which of my four gut reactions it forces me to abandon. I'm also playing with this idea of 'counterfactual redundancy' borrowed from evolutionary biology — that some outcomes have multiple independent paths leading to them, so losing one path doesn't change the result — but I'm unsure whether that maps onto institutions or is just a fancy metaphor. Use it if it helps; reject it plainly if it doesn't.

Please don't just list possibilities. Pick the best account you can, defend it, and tell me exactly where my intuition has to bend.
