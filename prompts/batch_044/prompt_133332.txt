After a long argument tonight with a friend about whether Aristotle's attack on chrematistikē is moralistic or structural, I wrote out a sketch for an essay advancing what I call a limit-based teleological reading. The core claim is that in Politics I.8-10 and Nicomachean Ethics V.5, Aristotle distinguishes oikonomia from chrematistikē not primarily by the presence of greed but by the presence or absence of an internal terminus. Household management aims at satisfying needs, and needs are by nature limited; once acquisition becomes mediated by money and oriented toward exchange rather than consumption, it loses this internal boundary because money is not consumed in use and therefore provides no natural stopping point. I develop this by arguing that Aristotle's comparison of interest (tokos) to breeding is not a loose metaphor but points to an ontological mistake: interest treats money as if it were a living substance capable of reproducing its own form, yet without the formal constraint—the specific form that determines the natural endpoint of growth—that makes biological reproduction genuinely reproductive. On this reading, chrematistikē is unnatural in the precise sense of being a process that lacks an immanent telos, not merely in the vulgar sense of being immoral. My friend insists that I am over-systematizing: tokos is conventional financial vocabulary in Greek, Aristotle's biological analogies are rhetorical rather than ontological, and I am importing a distinction between lacking a telos and being excessive that belongs to later scholastic metaphysics rather than to Aristotle's own framework. She also notes that households clearly accumulate surplus goods for exchange, which seems to undermine my claim that oikonomia is strictly bounded by immediate need. I want direct, point-by-point comments on whether this reading is textually defensible, whether the surplus-objection defeats the internal-limit claim, and whether the argument about tokos collapses once we recognize that Aristotle is using standard economic language rather than advancing an ontology of reproduction. I am not looking for a reading list or a survey of interpretations; I want an assessment of where the reasoning holds and where it slips into anachronism.
