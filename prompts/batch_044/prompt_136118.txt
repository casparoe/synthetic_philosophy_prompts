As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has recently begun working through philosophy of rational choice, I am designing a single 75-minute session for about fifteen philosophy graduate students on the problem of choice without preference. Rather than using the familiar hay-bale fable, I want them to encounter the difficulty inside a situation governed by continuous symmetry—something analogous to selecting an orientation in a perfectly isotropic field or a point on a homogeneous interval—so that any reason breaking the tie must be imported from outside the choice space itself. Please design an in-class activity, with explicit rules, roles, materials, and a non-monetary payoff scheme, that forces participants to act under these conditions before anyone names Buridan’s ass. The structure should make them feel the paralysis, or the arbitrariness of an externally imposed tie-breaker, through what actually happens to them, not through prior explanation. Include: the physical or spatial layout and any props needed; the precise rules that preserve the symmetry condition and the exact moment a choice is demanded; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the logical parts of the problem—the absence of internal differentiating reasons, the risk of an infinite regress of meta-preferences, and the necessity of an exogenous perturbation; an explicit warning about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how to recover; and an honest assessment of which theoretical feature—perhaps the normative status of arbitrary choice, or the metaphysical question of where a breaking point is located—the activity’s rules and stakes cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator afterward. Do not assign advance readings, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to particular authors beyond the common name of the puzzle.
