I sit on the Resource Allocation and Risk Review Board of the Meridian Institute for Public Health, and until three weeks ago I had never seriously questioned our institutional mandate to maximize expected lives saved per dollar spent. That changed when we received the proposal for what its authors call the Atlas Protocol: a single, highly speculative epidemiological intervention that, if successful, would eliminate a particular vector-borne pathogen across an entire region, but carries an 8% risk of accelerating mutation in a way that could cause widespread harm, alongside a 92% probability of yielding no measurable benefit at enormous cost. Under our charter we are required to demonstrate reasonable assurance of net benefit before approving any allocation, which points clearly toward rejection; yet the proposal is accompanied by a formal brief, co-authored by two epidemiological modelers and a decision theorist, arguing that strict institutional risk-aversion is precisely the kind of systematic cognitive bias that produces massive preventable mortality over time. They contend that if one aggregates welfare across all affected populations without applying arbitrary ex ante risk thresholds, the expected value of funding the Atlas Protocol dominates every conventional portfolio we could construct with the same budget, including distributing those resources across hundreds of standard vaccination campaigns. They invoke a principle they label expected-value consistency and explicitly reject the moral relevance of ex post accountability structures, insisting that our institutional discomfort with low-probability catastrophic downside reflects an evolutionary heuristic rather than an ethical constraint worthy of overriding aggregation. I have been asked to draft the Board's written opinion for circulation before our session on the 14th. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—approve with binding conditions, reject with explicit reasoning that engages the authors' framework, or approve an alternative allocation—and it must treat the aggregation argument with philosophical seriousness rather than dismissing it as mere institutional recklessness. The document should also examine whether our charter's requirement of reasonable assurance can be interpreted in a manner consistent with the welfare-maximizing purpose of the institution, or whether that phrase encodes a legitimate deontological or distributive constraint that expected-value reasoning is not entitled to override. Please write in a tone appropriate for formal institutional deliberation, and include an explicit statement of how confident you are in the recommendation you advance, singling out the precise point—particularly regarding whether institutional risk thresholds can be justified without falling into the arbitrariness that the aggregation argument alleges—where your confidence is weakest.
