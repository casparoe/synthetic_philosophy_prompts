You are advising a hospital network’s clinical-ethics audit office, which must review, within thirty minutes and without consulting outside philosophers, the justification logs produced by an intensive-care decision-support system. The system lists not only a recommendation—such as initiating dialysis, withholding vasopressors, or allocating a scarce bed—but also the considerations it treated as counting in its favor. Your task is to operationalize the practical-reason distinction between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons for action into a reusable, step-ordered checklist that a non-philosopher auditor can apply repeatedly, sign, and file.

The checklist must preserve the core conceptual structure: a reason is of the right kind for a clinical decision only if it bears directly on the patient’s medical welfare or on values the patient (or legitimate proxy) has actually endorsed as governing this decision; it is of the wrong kind if it treats properties of the algorithm itself (calibration scores, confidence-interval width, training-set performance) as direct clinical justifications, if it appeals to outcomes the recommendation itself would create in a circular fashion, or if it imports institutional priorities (resource conservation, liability reduction, staff morale) without an explicit, audited bridge linking them to the patient-specific decision frame. The procedure should have explicit ordered tests—first identifying the decision frame (curative, palliative, or triage), then classifying each cited reason by its logical relation to that frame, then flagging meta-level and circular justifications—together with stop-points marked JUDGMENT CALL where interpretation of the patient’s values or the institutional mandate is unavoidable and must be documented rather than hidden. Specify the criteria for marking a reason “disqualified,” “conditionally acceptable with bridge required,” or “fully valid,” and state exactly where the auditor must suspend the checklist rather than force a determination.

Apply the protocol to three worked cases. First, a withdrawal-of-care recommendation justified partly by family emotional readiness and partly by a predicted survival rate below ten percent: show how the checklist treats the survival statistic and the family-state consideration differently depending on whether the decision frame is palliative comfort or life-prolongation. Second, a recommendation to administer an experimental drug justified primarily by the fact that the model’s confidence interval is narrower for this option than for standard care: show the exact step at which this reason is disqualified as wrong-kind, and note what residual information, if any, survives reclassification. Third, apply the checklist to a case where it yields a determination you expect the auditor will find morally uncomfortable: a denial of a scarce ICU bed justified by the patient’s projected long-term tax contribution, where the protocol classifies this as an acceptable institutional reason within an explicit triage frame rather than a wrong-kind clinical reason, and you must state precisely where the line is drawn, why the bridge to the institutional frame succeeds on your criteria, and what feature of the case makes the approval feel wrong despite passing the test.

Finally, list three categories of log entry the procedure is designed to leave unadjudicated—such as reasons that depend on contested metaphysical commitments about personal identity over time, or reasons drawn from religious doctrines that the audit office has no mandate to evaluate—and identify one structural limitation: that if the clinical decision frame itself is disputed (for example, whether a particular intervention should be evaluated under curative or palliative standards), the checklist must suspend judgment rather than resolve the framing dispute, and the auditor’s signed memo must record that suspension explicitly. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke specific named texts with page numbers; and do not ask for references to literature the auditor could not access.
