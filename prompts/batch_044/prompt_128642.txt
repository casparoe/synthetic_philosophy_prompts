Two members of a philosophy-of-science reading group, A and B, argued about whether al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism can be reconciled with the autonomy of scientific explanation. Their exchange went as follows.

A opened by saying that al-Ghazālī’s denial of necessary causation is routinely misread. Al-Ghazālī does not deny that fire is regularly followed by burning; he denies only that the fire possesses any intrinsic power to produce the burning. Science, A claimed, studies precisely these stable conjunctions—the customary course—without needing to assert that natural objects contain metaphysical necessitation. So occasionalism is compatible with science: science describes the patterns, and theology explains why the patterns are sustained.

B replied that this misses the critical point. If every particular instance of burning requires a direct act of divine creation, then the explanation “the fire caused the burning” is false in each case. Science aims to provide true explanations of particular events, not merely to catalog correlations. On occasionalism, the true explanation of every burning is always divine volition, perhaps mediated by the presence of fire but never produced by it. That makes scientific explanation systematically false rather than merely metaphysically incomplete.

A countered that al-Ghazālī allows for a qualified form of secondary causation: God creates effects through intermediaries according to a consistent custom. The fire is not a dead instrument; it is the occasion for divine action, and science can legitimately treat it as the explanatory factor within the created order, provided one remembers that its efficacy is borrowed. A added that this is not fundamentally different from Humean regularism, which also denies intrinsic necessary power in nature but accepts constant conjunction as sufficient for scientific reasoning.

B objected that the comparison to Humean regularism collapses upon inspection. Hume denies necessary connections everywhere, including in any divine will, treating regularity as brute. Al-Ghazālī, by contrast, locates all necessary power in God alone and sustains regularity through an external will that could interrupt it at any moment. This means that counterfactual reasoning central to science—“if the match had not been struck, the cotton would not have burned”—lacks the modal stability that scientific laws require. On occasionalism, God could have created burning without the match, so the counterfactual is not grounded in the nature of things.

A responded that this is precisely the strength of occasionalism: it makes the stability of nature genuinely contingent and therefore allows for miracles without contradiction. Science describes the customary course, but its laws are descriptive, not prescriptive. The counterfactual objection assumes that science needs metaphysical guarantees across possible worlds, but A argued that science only requires predictive reliability within the actual course of events, not modal robustness.

B then pressed that if science needs only predictive reliability, occasionalism adds nothing to scientific practice; it becomes an interpretive overlay with no empirical consequences. Yet B insisted that A is wrong to think it makes no difference: it undermines the autonomy of science by making every scientific explanation ultimately theological. The scientist who explains combustion by chemical properties offers a partial, instrumental account, while the full explanation always invokes God. That hierarchy collapses the distinction between natural and theological explanation in a way that threatens scientific autonomy.

A concluded that autonomy need not entail ontological independence. A scientist can work within the created order, using the language of secondary causes, without denying the primary cause. Al-Ghazālī himself was not hostile to medicine or astronomy. The autonomy of science is methodological, not metaphysical.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the following claims—(1) that occasionalism is compatible with science because science describes conjunctions rather than necessary powers; (2) that occasionalism makes scientific explanations systematically false because the true cause of each event is always divine; (3) that the Humean comparison fails because occasionalism locates all power in God and permits interruption; (4) that occasionalism destroys the modal stability required for scientific counterfactuals; (5) that occasionalism is either epiphenomenal or undermines scientific autonomy—rate whether the claim is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partially correct, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—perhaps something about how al-Ghazālī’s own distinction between customary and miraculous events relates to the scope of scientific law, or about whether the debate conflates explanatory levels that need not compete.
