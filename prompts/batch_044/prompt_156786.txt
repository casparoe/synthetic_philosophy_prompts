I'm part of a small ethics study group — mostly people who've read a lot of Parfit, Singer, and the standard rationalist corpus on normative reasoning — and we've been going in circles for weeks on what our instructor calls the paradox of deontology. The specific sticking point is always the bridge variant of the trolley problem: if you really hold an absolute constraint against killing, then you must stand by and watch five people die rather than push one person to save them, which seems worse by every metric except the one that forbids the push. Whenever someone starts to argue that the five lives saved should outweigh the one life lost, another member kills the move with the line "the ends don't justify the means," used not as a developed premise but as a conversation-stopper that treats the conclusion as already settled. I think that phrase is hiding several distinct precise commitments, and I want them separated cleanly before our next session.

In particular, I have a working taxonomy that may or may not be exhaustive: (1) a logical claim about the structure of practical reasoning, where ends and means belong to categorically incommensurable domains; (2) an axiological claim that rights-violations or dignity-harms cannot be traded off against welfare gains at any exchange rate; (3) a psychological or institutional claim that allowing ends-based reasoning opens the door to rationalization and slippery slopes; and (4) a structural or agent-relative claim about aggregation, where moral constraints apply at the level of individual actions and do not scale across persons in the way consequentialist calculus requires. But I'm unsure which of these actually entail that you must not push the fat man, which are just platitudes dressed up as deep ethics, and whether there's a fifth reading I'm missing that captures the intuition without committing to full-blown absolutism.
