In a graduate seminar on Buddhist moral philosophy, a classmate repeatedly invoked the maxim that cetana—intention—is karma to dismiss discussion of the ship-captain parable in which a bodhisattva kills a robber to save five hundred merchants. She argued that because the captain's dominant mental state was compassionate, the killing was not unwholesome regardless of the fatal consequence, and therefore Buddhist ethics contains no consequentialist dimension whatsoever. Before I reply, I want to take that slogan apart precisely. Could you lay out the distinct theses that "intention alone determines moral quality" might be expressing—ranging from Abhidharmic claims about mental factors as the exclusive bearers of karma, to normative claims about how actions are evaluated, to Mahayana appeals to upaya—indicating for each whether it is definitional, defensible, or an overreach? For each reading, please state whether it actually supports dismissing the parable as non-consequentialist, and whether it can accommodate the possibility that a wrong mental factor (for instance, a trace of aversion or an attachment to the outcome) might coexist with the compassionate aim without altering the karmic verdict. Present this as a menu of readings I can bring to the discussion rather than a single resolution.
