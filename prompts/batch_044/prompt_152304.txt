I've been trying to wrap my head around Mill's late socialism essays — you know, where he starts arguing that socialism might actually be the fulfillment of liberal liberty if it comes through education and voluntary association rather than revolutionary coercion. And I was thinking about this institutional compromise in some European co-determination laws: workers get board seats in large firms, but shareholders keep a veto over mergers and liquidation, and the whole thing only applies once a company hits a certain size. The design treats workplace democracy like an optional feature bolted onto capitalist property rights, not a replacement for them.

So here's where I'm stuck: what philosophical commitments about economic freedom does that practice actually presuppose? Does it depend on Mill's old production-versus-distribution distinction — the idea that you can democratize labor conditions without touching ownership? Or does it silently commit to a negative-liberty view where any limit on shareholder control counts as coercion, even if democracatic? And there's Mill's late claim that real freedom requires transforming the conditions of labor, not just adding worker represention. If that's right, isn't this model philosophically unstable? It wants to honor autonomy while leaving the fundamental power structure intact. I'm also wondering about the size threshold — as if democracy is only appropriate for big bureaucracies but not small ones. What does that presuppose about consent and authority? Can this practice be made consistent with Mill's late committments, or does it only make sense under a rival theory like Nozick's entitlement view?
