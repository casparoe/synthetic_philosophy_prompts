You're the senior reasoning auditor at a small arbitration centre that settles disputes between expert advisory panels — climate scientists, epidemiologists, that sort of crowd. They keep turning up saying "we've seen the same reports but we still disagree on the recommendation," and your director wants you to turn Aumann's agreement theorem into a reusable decision procedre your team can run in under an hour, with no heavy maths and with the director signing off at the end. Before you draft the checklist, do some quick brainstorming: which bits of the theorem actually survive this translation (common prior, common knowledge of posteriors) and which have to be dropped or softened because real people aren't perfect Bayesians? Then lay out the procedre in order — the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with "they're rational to disagree," where it stops with "there's a hidden framing disagreement," and where it hands the call to a human because the issue is values not probabilities. Apply it to three cases: (i) two flood-risk modellers who share rainfall data but disagree on the safety threshold; (ii) two clinical ethicists arguing over ICU triage weights during a shortage — this should be the one where the procedre gives an answer your askers won't like; and (iii) a messy case where they can't even agree what counts as "the evidence" in the first place. Be honest about where it misfires, especially with small panels and bounded rationality, and say clearly what the procedre just can't do. Write like you're explaining it to a tired colleague, not sitting an exam. Use British spelling (centre, behaviour, etc.). Don't ask for citations or a reading list — nobody here's got time for that, and you can't look things up anyway. Think step by step, and make sure the hard cases are actually handled rather than brushed aside.
