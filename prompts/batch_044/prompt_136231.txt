Write an essay on the conceptual coherence of holding an entire nation morally responsible for the First World War, using the Fischer controversy over German war aims as your central case study. The Fischer thesis holds that a small circle of German elites deliberately pursued hegemonic war aims, while its critics argue that Germany was dragged into conflict by alliance dynamics and defensive anxieties; do not treat this merely as a historical dispute, but as a test for two competing philosophical models of collective responsibility. On one side stands a collective-intentionality model, which requires that a nation, to be blameworthy, must possess something like a shared mental state or aggregated will directed at the harm; on the other stands an institutional-transmission model, which holds that responsibility flows through bureaucratic and constitutional pathways—military planning, diplomatic authorization, budgetary commitment—regardless of whether the general population endorsed or even knew of the aims pursued in its name. Weigh the arguments for each model: consider whether the institutional-transmission view collapses into blaming only specific office-holders, whether the collective-intentionality view demands an implausibly unified national psyche, and whether the truth or falsity of the Fischer thesis should alter our verdict about “Germany’s” culpability at all. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: is it conceptually meaningful to blame a nation as such for 1914, and if so, under which conditions and with what limitations? Your conclusion should address whether a nation can be responsible without being guilty in any psychological sense, and whether exonerating the elite automatically exonerates the state.
