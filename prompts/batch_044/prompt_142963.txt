You chair the Classification Review Board of a national statistical agency. Every quarter your team reviews proposals to add, remove, or redesign categories in surveys, medical intake forms, and economic reports. You need a reusable decision procedure — runnable in thirty minutes, without philosophy consultants present, defensible to both statisticians and civil-rights auditors — that determines whether a proposed kind should be treated as socially constructed. Under your agency rules, that designation triggers mandatory opt-in design, periodic sunset review, and anti-reification safeguards; absence permits routine hard-coding.

Ground the procedure in the philosophy of social kinds, but translate it into ordered practical steps. Keep the distinctions that matter: between kinds whose existence depends on collective acceptance or institutional rules and those that do not; between interactive kinds, where classification feeds back onto the properties or behavior of members, and stabilized kinds, where collective belief sustains reality without that individual-level loop; and between administrative disappearance and normative residue — whether people would still have reason to coordinate around the kind if the institution vanished. Inject one niche test: the Counterfactual Dissolution Check, which asks not merely whether the kind would persist, but whether its persistence would take the form of a coordination problem people solve for practical reasons versus a habit they abandon once the rulebook is gone.

Structure the deliverable as a checklist with the questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exact point where the procedure stops with a clear "no — treat as tracking category," the point where it stops with "yes — apply constructed-kind safeguards," and the specific judgment calls that cannot be eliminated and must be flagged for human sign-off. Then apply it to two proposals: (1) adding "neurotype" (autistic, allistic, dyspraxic, etc.) as a mandatory demographic field in federal employment surveys — the case where the procedure should deliver an answer your agency will not like, because it forces opt-in and sunset review on data collectors who want standardized comparability; and (2) adding "central-bank digital-currency unit" as an economic kind for regional GDP allocation — a monetary kind that depends on institutional acceptance but does not loop back onto individuals. Finally, state honestly what the procedure cannot do: which borderline cases it will misfire on, and which contested philosophical issues — for example, whether all interactive kinds are necessarily institutional, and whether economic kinds like money carry looping effects at the macro level — remain unsettled among specialists.

In your response, separate what is settled in social ontology from what is genuinely contested among people who know the field; do not present a contested view as consensus. Keep the entire response under 800 words. Use no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote any text verbatim; paraphrase without quotation marks if you reference a specific idea.
