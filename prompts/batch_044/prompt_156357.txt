I run a grad seminar on rationality, about 10 people, 90 minutes. I want them to feel the pull of superrational reasoning in symetric games and then feel it collapse in the lottery analog—not just hear about it. Design a session that runs a Platonia-style dilemma (both players pick A or B; both B gets 100 points each, both A gets 1 each, mismatch gives 101 to A and 0 to B) followed immediately by a Luring Lottery analog where each student draws one sealed envelope from a bag of 20, but three envelopes are secretly empty and a win slip pays 10 token points, with the twist that anyone reasoning symetrically should realize they'd buy every envelope. Inject something new: in the Platonia round, after locking choices they reveal a "reasoning mirror"—each wrote down their prediction of the other's choice, and we compare whether those predictions match the actual symmetry-breaking. In the lottery round, keep the hidden empties unknown until after all draws. Use only paper, envelopes, and token points—rather then real money, but the session winner gets out of buying coffee next week. Arrange rules so they construct the reasoning steps themselves; don't call it Platonia or superrationality until the debrief. Map the mirror failure onto the common-knowledge gap in symmetric reasoning, and the hidden empties onto why the lottery destroys the symmetry premise. Tell me what will predictably break—the first time someone will try to feel the envelope thickness, or people will cluster their mirror predictions to avoid looking irrational. Be honest about which feature of the original argument this can't reproduce: I think it's the pure solitary reasoning in the lottery case, since the classroom still has social presence, but maybe I'm wrong. Keep it runnable with no lectures up front.
