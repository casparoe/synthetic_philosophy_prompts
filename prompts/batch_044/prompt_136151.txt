The debate sparked by Clark and Chalmers over the extended mind thesis has now run for more than two decades. On one side, integrationists hold that functional parity and reliable causal coupling are sufficient for a resource to count as part of a cognitive process; they point to Otto and his notebook, to smartphone calendars, and to distributed problem-solving in teams. On the other side, critics such as Adams and Aizawa, Rupert, and embedded-cognition advocates insist that cognition carries a specific mark—biological realization, representational format, or phenomenological ownership—that external tools systematically lack. In the seminars and reading groups where I encounter this debate, the arguments are well worn and nobody moves: the integrationists reiterate parity, the critics reiterate the boundary conditions, and both claim they are answering the same question—what belongs to the mind?

Yet I keep noticing a layer that is rarely made explicit. Some recent defenders have begun to speak of normative extension: if a notebook genuinely extends Otto's memory, then epistemic responsibility and perhaps moral agency extend outward with it, altering how we should evaluate belief-formation and blame. Critics, in turn, often respond not by denying that tools can be coupled, but by accepting coupling for practical cognition and denying that it carries any normative weight. The result is that one camp asks whether the notebook is part of the cognitive architecture, while the other asks whether extending architecture changes who is answerable for what. They use the same examples—Otto, smartphones, collaborative cognition—but seem to be running parallel inquiries that happen to share a slogan.
