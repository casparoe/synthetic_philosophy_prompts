I was reading about those fMRI studies where they ask unresponsive ICU patients to imagine playing tennis and you can actually see the motor cortex light up like they understood the command, even though they can't move a finger. Meanwhile the hospital protcols for declaring someone vegetative or deciding on life-support withdrawal still basically run on behavoral checklists—eye tracking, grimacing, that kind of thing. The hidden-brain-response stuff gets treated like a cool lab trick rather than legal-medical proof that "someone's in there."

What view of consciousness does that institutional split presuppose? If consciousness is basically a user illusion—just a simplified interface the brain builds for itself, not some inner spotlight—then why do we treat the behavoral output as the real deal and the internal neural self-modeling as suspicious? Does the practice quietly assume that consciousness only matters when it manifests in the user-facing interface, which sounds weirdly like the illusion is more real than the machinery? Or is there a coherence argument that behavioral tests are more reliable even on an illusion-model?

And if we actually replaced bedside exams with these mental-imagery fMRI protocols, what philosophical commitments about personal identity or moral status would we be signing up for? I'm trying to figure out whether the current system is consistent with the idea that there's no "cartesian theater," or if it smuggles in exactly that kind of theater by only counting consciousness when it performs for an audience.
