I want to teach Kant's absolute prohibition on lying, using the murderer-at-the-door scenario not as a simple intuition pump but as a stress-test for the Formula of Humanity. Do not explain the doctrine to me; instead, design the pedagogical sequence.

Open with the carrying example you would use—either a refined version of the murderer case or a substitute—and in the same opening sentence state the first conceptual dependency that must be in place before it is introduced, explaining why that dependency prevents students from reducing Kant's position to mere stubbornness. Next, lay out the remaining dependency order concisely: which idea about rational agency or perfect duty must follow the example so that the prohibition on lying is seen as structural rather than dogmatic? Then map the misconceptions students will form in sequence—anticipating at least that mental reservation is just a hidden permission to lie, that Kant values rules over human lives, and that Constant's critique makes the view obsolete—and specify the exact point in the lesson where each should be intercepted. Provide one diagnostic question that distinguishes understanding lying as a corruption of rational nature from viewing it merely as an imprudent strategy. Explicitly identify one related topic, such as the Doctrine of Right's treatment of false promises or the casuistry on equivocation, that you will deliberately exclude and justify that omission for this audience. Keep the entire response under 300 words, present the dependency order and misconception map as brief bullet points rather than prose, and do not include any reading list or citations.
