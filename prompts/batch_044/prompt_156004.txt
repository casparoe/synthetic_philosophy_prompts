I retired from physics a few years back and now I'm trying to read philosophy the way I used to read journals - slowly and with suspicion. I keep running into this argument about Hitler and structural history that my study group threw at me. I think it sneaks in a slide but I can't quite pin it.

(1) If an outcome O is overdetermined by background conditions B, then any particular contributing cause C is not necessary for O.
(2) The structural conditions of Weimar collapse, treaty resentment, and economic crisis (B) made some authoritarian nationalist takeover nearly inevitable.
(3) Hitler was one particular contributing cause (C) among many possible leaders who could have chaneled those forces.
(4) So Hitler was not necessary for 'Nazi Germany' in the sense of an authoritarian nationalist regime in 1930s Germany.
(5) But if C is not necessary for O, then C makes no difference to the specific form O takes.
(6) The Holocaust and the specific racial-bureaucratic machinery were part of the specific form of Nazi Germany.
(7) So if Hitler wasn't necessary, he made no difference to whether the Holocaust occured.
(8) But that contradicts the historical record that Hitler's personal ideology drove the Final Solution in ways other potential leaders wouldn't have.
(9) Therefore structural overdetermination is false - or at least (5) is wrong.

I feel like the slip is between 'not necessary for the regime type' and 'makes no difference to the token event', but I can't articulate the cost of fixing it. Which premise do I drop? And if I drop (5), doesn't that mean Great Man theorists have to accept Hitler was necessary for every feature, even ones he didn't cause? Help me out - I'm used to thinking about initial conditions in differential equations, not history.
