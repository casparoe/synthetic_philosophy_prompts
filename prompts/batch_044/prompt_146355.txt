Here are my gut verdicts on Kant-style cases with animals, and I can't tell if there's one principel behind them or I'm just being inconsistent.

Kicking a dog because you're mad: wrong, no question.

Factory farming where they suffer constantly just for cheap meat: wrong too, but it feels like a different kind of wrong — more like a system failure than one person being cruel.

Rescuing a stray and caring for it well: good, but I think it's good because it keeps you kind to people, not because the dog itself has a claim on you.

Now the ones that don't line up. A surgeon spends huge money saving a chimp when it could have funded ten human surgeries. I think that's not immoral, just maybe wasteful. But if she does it because she thinks the chimp has equal moral worth, then I think she's wrong — but not in the same way as the dog-kicker.

Then there's the bioengineered pig that can understand promises and literally ask not to be killed. I keep going back and forth: part of me says now it has direct standing, part of me says it's still not rational in the Kantian sense, just very clever.

And torturing a robot that perfectly mimicks animal pain responses — I feel like that's not actually wrong, just creepy. No real suffering, so no harm to my charcter?

So is there a single Kantian principle that gets all of these right, or do I have to drop one? I want the principle stated precisely, with which case motivates each part, what it says about the engineered pig and the robot, and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't save. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reasoning, no preamble, and don't restate the question.
