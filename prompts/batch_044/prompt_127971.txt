Consider the following summary of a central argument in Augustine’s *De doctrina christiana* (late fourth to early fifth century), particularly in Book III, where he treats the interpretation of obscure scriptural passages. Augustine holds that signs (*signa*) point to things (*res*), and that the reader’s primary task is to recover the author’s intended meaning—what he treats as the authoritative aim of the text. At the same time, he insists that when a passage is obscure it must be read in light of clearer passages, especially those that teach charity, even if the grammatical or literal sense seems to point elsewhere. He further warns that misunderstanding is the default condition of reading, since the same signs can carry different meanings across contexts and minds.

What puzzles me is how these two commitments cohere. On one hand, authorial intention functions as the normative anchor: if we fail to grasp what the writer meant, we have failed as interpreters. On the other hand, the rule that obscurity should be resolved by reference to a broader canonical context—one that expresses charity—appears to license, and even require, readings that may depart from what the particular author of the obscure passage actually intended. Does Augustine provide a principled criterion for when grammatical interpretation must yield to this context-driven correction? Or does the appeal to authorial intention become largely rhetorical, masking an interpretive practice actually governed by communal norms of coherence and charity?

I also want to press the problem on a boundary case Augustine could not have anticipated. Imagine a text that lacks a unitary conscious author—say, a legal statute assembled through successive committee amendments, or a particular sentence produced by an algorithmic system trained on a broad corpus but bearing no specific communicative intent behind that formulation. Here grammatical interpretation may proceed without difficulty, but psychological interpretation—recovering an author’s mind—seems either impossible or distributed across too many agents to be recoverable. Within Augustine’s framework, does such a text admit of legitimate interpretation, or does the absence of recoverable intent reduce the exercise to a mere technical analysis of signs? More specifically, if we apply his rule about resolving obscurity through clearer contexts, but there is no single author’s broader corpus to supply those contexts, have we abandoned the heart of his hermeneutic, or have we merely generalized it?
