I sit on a small city heritage board and every month we recieve proposals asking whether some building counts as 'historically significant' so it can get a tax break or a demolition delay. The term is obviously vague but we still have to deliver a yes, no, or 'can't call it' by the end of the meeting, and right now we just argue until someone gets tired. I was reading about epistemicism versus supervalutionism about vagueness — the idea that either there's a hidden sharp cutoff we don't know, or there's no single cutoff but only admissible precisifications with super-truth across them — and I want you to turn that actual distinction into a practical decision proceedure my board can run in about thirty minutes without anyone needing a philosophy degree.

Please write the checklist: the ordered questions we ask, what counts as sufficient answer at each step, where the flow stops with a clear no, where it stops with indeterminate and forces a value-based vote, and where it hands off because the question is about values not facts. You have to keep the real philosophical difference intact — don't collapse it into 'we're just unsure' — and you should flag exactly where the proceedure will misfire or give an answer people will hate. Include a small set of test conditions or sentences the board can apply to any proposal.

Then run it on three cases: a 1923 courthouse that everyone agrees is old, a 1978 brutalist parking garage that splits the board, and a 1999 community center designed by an architect who only became influential after 2010. Make sure one of these produces a result the board won't like, and say what the proceedure genuinely can't do — for instance, whether it can ever justify demolishing a borderline building, or whether it just passes the buck.
