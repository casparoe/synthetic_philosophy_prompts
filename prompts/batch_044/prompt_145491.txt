Aristotle's problem begins from the intuition that if the statement "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is either true or false today, then the outcome is already fixed: if it is true now, then its being false tomorrow is impossible, which seems to render present deliberation causally idle. This is essentially the same argument that Arthur Prior later formalized with tense-logical operators P and F, except that Prior frames the dependency modally rather than through direct temporal reference. To escape fatalism, Aristotle denies bivalence for future contingents and assigns them an intermediate status, very much like the three-valued logic that Łukasiewicz later constructed specifically for this difficulty; on that reading, the statement is indeterminate rather than false. The truth-maker asymmetry resolution improves on this by observing that present truths require present actualities to serve as truth-makers, whereas tomorrow's battle is not yet actual, so nothing in the present world grounds either the statement or its negation.
