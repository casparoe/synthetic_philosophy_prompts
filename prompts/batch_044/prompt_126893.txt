I'm taking a course on philosophy of economics and I keep hitting the same wall with my study group. We keep circling back to revealed preference theory — the idea that utility is just a bookkeeping number you read straight off choice behavior — versus the mentalist view that utility is a real psychological state that explains why people pick what they pick. Both sides have been trading the same moves for decades: the revealed-preference camp says mentalism is unscientific because you can't observe inner states, and the mentalists reply that without some prior idea of preference you can't distinguish a genuine choice from a framing mistake or an adaptive preference formed under pressure. Its frustrating because everyone agrees on the formal results and the classic examples, yet nobody moves an inch. I can't tell if this is actually a disagreement about how explanation works, or if they're just using "utility" for two different jobs — one predictive, one causal — and talking past each other.

Here's the kind of case that makes me think there's a hidden structural issue: imagine an agent who, when options A, B, and C are all present, chooses B over A, but when C is removed she chooses A over B, and she insists that her "real" preference between A and B hasn't changed at all — she just noticed different features in each context. The revealed-preference theorist can absorb this by saying her revealed preference simply switched with the menu; the mentalist can absorb it by saying her underlying preference stayed stable and the choice was distorted by salience. Both sides explain the case without changing their core view. So I'm wondering: is the stall happening because they disagree about what counts as data — whether reported introspection or only observed choice should ground the theory — or because there's a deeper split about explanatory direction, with one side thinking choice explains preference and the other thinking preference explains choice? Or is it just verbal? And whatever the diagnosis is, what would actually count as progress — a formal result showing revealed rankings can't be uniquely recovered under menu-dependence, a neural correlation study, or maybe a conceptual distinction that splits prediction from explanation?

Please don't just survey the two views; diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, offer your own hypothesis about whether it's substantive, verbal, or a hidden empirical question in disguise, and tell me what result or distinction — if any — could break the impasse.
