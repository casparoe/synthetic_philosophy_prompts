I was reading Kafka's The Metamorphosis for a book cllub and kept thinking about Buddhist anatta, but not the pop-version where "there's no self." I mean the Abhidharmic breakdown: there's no owner behind the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness — just these processes arising and passing. Gregor wakes up as a giant insect, but he still has memories, shame, affection for his sister Grete, and he understands language. His body (rupa) has totally changed, but the mental aggregates seem continuous. So is Gregor still Gregor? Or was "Gregor" always just a conventional label (prajnapti) for a cluster of aggregates and social roles?

I'm trying to write a short esssay and I'm stuckk on whether the story actually supports the Buddhist view or secretly undermines it. On one hand, Gregor's family eventually treats him as dead or replaced, which sounds like they're recognizing that without the right bodily form and social function, the person-label no longer applies — that's kind of like saying the self is just a conventional designation. On the other hand, Gregor's inner life persists with all its guilt and love, which makes it feel like there's something "in there" that survives the bodily transformation. Grete's shift is especially interesting: she starts caring for him, then decides he's not Gregor anymore and they need to get rid of him. Is that a dramatic illustration of anatta — that identity is just the clinging of others to a familiar pattern — or is it the opposite, that there's a real subject of experience that they're now murdering?
