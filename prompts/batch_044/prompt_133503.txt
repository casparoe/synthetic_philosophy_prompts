My colleague applies what she takes to be Montaigne's framework for comparing customs across societies. Her track record of verdicts: she judges European torture of living prisoners more barbaric than Tupinambá ritual consumption of dead enemies, since the former multiplies suffering while the latter is a posthumous honor-practice; she judges cannibalism directed at one's own kin worse than cannibalism of enemies, because the latter respects an internal logic of warfare and solidarity while the former violates that same logic; she judges European religious persecution of heretics more blameworthy than foreign practices of body modification, since persecution inflicts suffering without serving any coherent communal purpose whereas scarification expresses social identity; and she praises Tupinambá simplicity of dress and dwelling as closer to an unmediated natural human state, while denying that this gives them rational superiority over Europeans—only that European artificial conventions are equally groundless. She now considers a society that institutes, with universal democratic consent and without physical pain, a permanent cognitive modification of all adolescents that eliminates aggression, envy, and the capacity for moral outrage, producing complete social harmony but also removing any emotional basis for recognizing injustice. Predict whether she will treat this practice as more admirable than cannibalism, as equally arbitrary, or as more barbaric; identify the competing rules her earlier judgments jointly support—such as suffering-minimization, internal cultural coherence, and fidelity to an unmodified human nature—state what each predicts here, argue which rule she is actually running and why, and propose one further hypothetical practice that would cleanly separate the two rules you think are closest.
