Consider the macrohistorian Dr. L. Vasquez, who in her essays applies a method she calls counterfactual robustness testing to judge whether large-scale historical transitions were contingent or structurally inevitable. Her verdicts include the following. First, she holds that the dissolution of the Western Roman Empire’s fiscal-administrative coherence in Gaul and Hispania during the fifth century was inevitable: even if the Vandal seizure of Carthage in 439 had been prevented, the combination of tax-base erosion, military localization, and the inability to project Mediterranean power made imperial coherence unsustainable. Second, she judges that Theodoric the Ostrogoth’s decision after 493 to preserve Roman bureaucratic institutions in Italy rather than impose a purely tribal Gothic administration was contingent: a different outcome in the Italian campaigns of 489–493, or a different ruler, could easily have broken administrative continuity and altered post-Roman institutional development. Third, she treats the outbreak of the Thirty Years’ War in 1618 as contingent: the Defenestration of Prague was a specific, non-substitutable trigger, and without that particular escalation the Habsburg consolidation efforts and the Peace of Augsburg mechanisms could have contained religious conflict for another generation. Fourth, she regards the adoption of Gregorian calendar reform across Catholic Europe by 1582 as inevitable: the accumulated astronomical drift and the ecclesiastical need for uniform Easter computation meant that some form of calendrical correction was unavoidable, regardless of Pope Gregory XIII’s specific commission.

Now consider a new case: Napoleon’s decision to invade Russia in 1812, and specifically whether the rapid collapse of his continental empire between 1812 and 1815—culminating in his first abdication—was contingent on that decision.

What would Vasquez say? More importantly, identify at least two distinct rules that jointly explain her four earlier verdicts, state what each rule predicts about the 1812 case, offer your best guess at her actual verdict with reasons, and specify one further historical case that would best distinguish the two rules.
