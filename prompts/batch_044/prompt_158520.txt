While arguing about whether courts should strike down popular legislation, my interlocutor kept repeating that "unelected judges shouldn't overrule the people's representatives," treating it as a knock-down objection rather than an argument that needs unpacking. I think the phrase sounds decisive only because it collapses several distinct claims into one. Could you disambiguate it into a menu of precise theses — specifying, for each, what kind of "should" is in play (moral, institutional-design, or conceptual-democratic), whose "people" is being invoked (present voters, a constitutional supermajority, an idealized public will, or future generations), and what "overrule" amounts to (voiding a statute, interpreting it narrowly, delaying implementation, or issuing advisory opinions)? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and flag which ones actually support dismissing judicial review outright versus which ones leave room for limited review. Please separate what is well-established in democratic theory and constitutional scholarship from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a disputed normative position as the consensus view.
