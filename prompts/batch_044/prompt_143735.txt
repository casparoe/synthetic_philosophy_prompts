As a novelist who has spent the past year revising a single manuscript through so many incremental drafts that the original file and the present version share almost no sentences, I recently found myself in a prolonged discussion with two fellow writers that has started to bleed into my own thinking about vagueness and the sorites paradox. My friend Julian argued that if altering one sentence does not destroy the identity of the novel, then altering every sentence one by one should not either, which means my current manuscript and the first draft are literally the same work despite bearing no textual resemblance; he treats this as a reductio of the idea that artistic identity can survive continuous change without a hidden sharp boundary somewhere in the sequence. Our other friend, Clara, responded that Julian is misapplying the sorites paradox by treating artistic identity as a quantitative property like height or hair count, when in fact it is sustained by an unbroken intentional-causal trajectory: the same author, guided by the same overarching design, working within the same creative project, maintains the work's identity regardless of how many individual sentences are replaced, and the paradox only arises because we mistakenly assume that identity must be grounded in measurable similarity rather than in historical continuity. Julian countered that Clara's appeal to intentional continuity merely pushes the vagueness one level back, since an author's intentions can drift gradually, be interrupted by collaboration or mental decline, or even be simulated by an editor or algorithm without anyone being able to pinpoint the exact moment the original intention was lost; he added that if Clara claims there is always a precise metaphysical fact about whether the chain is intact, she is adopting an epistemicist stance about vagueness that saves the boundary only by rendering it permanently inaccessible, which he considers no better than admitting the boundary does not exist. Clara then said that Julian's demand for an observable boundary confuses metaphysics with epistemology, and that our inability to locate the breaking point does not entail there is none, just as our inability to locate the precise millisecond a seed becomes a tree does not mean there is no fact of the matter. They turned to me and asked who is right at each turn, whether either is missing a third option, and which view is closer to the truth about how vagueness operates in cases of creative identity.
