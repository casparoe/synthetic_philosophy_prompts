Your colleague finds an injured stray animal outside her office and wants to take it to a vet at her own expense. Her partner argues that she has no genuine obligation: evolutionary psychology shows compassion is an adaptive social instinct calibrated for kin and reciprocal allies, and Darwin's naturalism undermines any religious claim that humans possess special moral standing over other species. She feels a real pull to help nonetheless, recalling that Darwin treated the extension of sympathy beyond one's own group as the highest form of moral progress. The partner replies that explaining an emotion's evolutionary origin dissolves its normative authority. How should she think about this? Does Darwin's evolutionary account of morality actually debunk the obligation she feels, or does his framework allow that an evolved capacity for sympathy can be genuinely directed at non-kin animals without being a mere misfiring? And given Darwin's skeptical, agnostic stance toward religious assertions of human uniqueness, is there any non-arbitrary ground within his view for distinguishing a moral reason to care from an indulgence of instinct?
