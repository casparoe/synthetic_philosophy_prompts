I'm serving as a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar in metaphysics and philosophy of logic, and I'm currently annotating a midterm exam on classical problems of relational unity. The prompt I gave the students was the following: "Explain F.H. Bradley's argument in Appearance and Reality that the analysis of a proposition into terms and relations generates an infinite regress and thereby undermines the possibility of genuine propositional unity. Then evaluate Russell's doctrine of external relations as a response to this regress, and consider whether a contemporary grounding-theoretic framework—in which relational facts are metaphysically grounded in the intrinsic, non-relational properties of their relata—can dissolve Bradley's challenge without committing to Bradley's own conclusion that reality is an undifferentiated Absolute. Be precise about whether Russell treats relations as primitive entities or as reducible features of terms."

Below is one student's response (roughly 400 words). I think the student understands the basic dialectic, but I'm worried there are one or two subtle confusions—perhaps a misattributed view about Russell, or an unrecognized tension in the grounding proposal—that a hasty reader might overlook. I want to be fair but granular.

Student answer:
Bradley's regress begins with any relational judgment, such as that a is taller than b. If we analyze this into the terms a and b and the relation taller-than, we must then ask how that relation is connected to each term. This seems to require two further relations: one linking taller-than to a, and one linking taller-than to b. Those linking relations will themselves need to be connected to their relata, producing an infinite hierarchy. Bradley takes this to show that relational analysis is contradictory, and he concludes that reality is an undifferentiated Absolute in which all distinctions are merely apparent rather than genuinely real.

Russell's response is to deny that relations need any further connecting link. By treating relations as internal to the terms they relate, Russell holds that the nature of a term already includes its relations to other terms, so no separate relating-relation is required. This allows him to preserve relational facts without generating an infinite regress, though he pays the price of making every term essentially connected to every other term in the universe.

I think Russell's move is ultimately unsatisfactory because it collapses into a form of metaphysical holism that Bradley himself would have endorsed. A better modern approach is to adopt a grounding framework: the fact that a is taller than b is grounded in a's having height h1 and b's having height h2. Because grounding is a dependency relation rather than an ordinary relational connection, it is not subject to Bradley's regress—the dependency holds immediately, without requiring a further dependency-dependency. This lets us keep relational discourse as derivative but real, and it avoids Bradley's Absolute because the grounding base consists in genuinely distinct, non-relational properties.

That said, Bradley's own view is frequently misinterpreted as an epistemic coherentist position, according to which a belief is true just in case it belongs to a maximally consistent system. In reality, Bradley's Absolute is not a system of beliefs at all but a non-propositional, concrete whole that transcends relational thought. So the coherentist reading is a category mistake, even if it captures a superficial similarity.
