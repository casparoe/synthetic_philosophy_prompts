I want to figure out what I actually think about when psychiatric categories track a real dysfunction versus just medicalizing a problem of living, so I'm going by my gut on a bunch of cases and hoping there's a pattern.

Someone hears commanding voices, has zero insight they're abnormal, and acts on them — that's real mental illness, no question. A high-powered lawyer who works eighty-hour weeks and destroys her relationships because she feels driven to win — if we call that an addiction or a personality disorder, that feels wrong, like we're pathologizing values she genuinely holds. A teenager who refuses food because she genuinely believes she's overweight despite being emaciated, and this belief won't budge with any evidence — that feels like a real disorder, not just vanity. But then there's my uncle who lost his job and hasn't gotten out of bed in four months; I keep flip-flopping, because it seems like grief that got stuck but also like something in his reasoning about what's still possible is genuinely broken. A kid who physically cannot sit through a classroom built for passive listening gets an ADHD label — that feels like the myth Szasz warned about, turning a mismatch between person and environment into a brain defect. Someone with Capgras delusion insisting their spouse is an imposter despite all proof — clearly illness, a breakdown in theoretical rationality. But then there's a person in an isolated religious community who refuses to eat food they believe is spiritually contaminated; if everyone around them shares the belief, is that still a failure of reasoning or just cultural practice? I'm wondering if the principle is that mental illness requires both suffering and a specific failure of theoretical rationality — false beliefs or perceptual errors that resist correction — while practical rationality failures, like overworking or prolonged sadness about real losses, aren't illness even if they're destructive.
