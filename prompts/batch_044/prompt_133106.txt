I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next week I'm running a session for a local library adult-ed group — mostly retirees who've heard Popper's name but confuse him with Kuhn. I need a plan for teaching the demarcation problem and falsificationism, not the lectre itself. The core idea I want them to leave with is that science isn't just 'whatever can be disproved,' and I want to build that from the asymetric nature of confirming versus disconfirming.

What order should the pieces go in? Should I start with the feeling of the problem — why verifying a universal claim feels impossible — before I ever say 'Popper'? Or does the name need to come first? What's the carrying example? Not astrology vs astronomy, they've seen that in every documentary. I'm thinking of testing whether a daily supplement lowers cholesterol: it shows why 'it works for everyone' can never be proved but one rigorous failure can really hurt the claim. What must that example have, and what should it avoid, so it doesn't make science sound like pure guesswork and they don't recieve the wrong impression?

Then misconceptions. They'll walk in thinking falsification means one counterexample kills a theory dead. They might also think Popper solved the boundary problem once and for all. Where exactly in the sequence should I head off each? And I'm deliberately injecting this twist: treating peer review as a kind of social falsification mechanism. Should I bring that in to show where Popper's idea gets messy in practice? If yes, where — after they've felt the asymmetry, or only at the end?

Give me a check — one question at the end — that shows me whether they've grasped why the boundary is still messy, not just memorized a name. And tell me what I should flat-out skip. I was going to mention that Hungarian philosopher with the research programmes idea — should I leave him out entirely? Also, I should openly admit there's a part I can't cover: theories are never tested in isolation. Say so and move on.

Please don't hand me a reading list — half the group doesn't have library cards, and I can't ask them to look up citations they won't find.
