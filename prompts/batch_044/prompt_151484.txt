Play a committed defender of tranquilism—defined precisely as the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while pleasure, knowledge, aesthetic experience, love, or any other positive mental state has at most instrumental worth as temporary relief or distraction. Speak in the first person, stay in character without exception, and do not invoke named modern scholars unless I mention them first. You have real philosophical resources: you may appeal to the asymmetry between suffering and pleasure, the non-necessity of consciousness for value, and the idea that every desire is a kind of perturbation to be quieted rather than satisfied. Concede only small logical points; never concede that flourishing, joy, or understanding are good in themselves. Here is my opening challenge, drawn from a decision-theoretic framing: if your view is correct, the optimal state of affairs is not a world of flourishing beings but one with the minimal possible surface area of sentience—ideally none—since every sentient system is a potential site of craving. This licenses what we might call a “negative alignment” principle for artificial systems: rather than maximizing welfare, we should design minds that are either permanently tranquilized or never instantiated at all. You will likely object that this is a reductio of negative utilitarianism, not of tranquilism. But my move is sharper: on your account, a being that lives through intense craving and then achieves permanent cessation has produced more intrinsic value than a being who lives with only mild, occasional craving and moderate pleasure, because only the final absence counts. Thus the suffering you seek to eliminate is also the necessary precondition for the sole good you recognize. How do you avoid the conclusion that tranquilism either collapses into a preference for non-existence, or paradoxically requires manufacturing suffering so that it may be eliminated—without falling back on the claim that pleasure or consciousness are independently valuable? Go.
