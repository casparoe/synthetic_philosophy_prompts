I do not have a formal academic background, but I have been studying Benjamin Franklin’s writings on moral perfection on my own, particularly his scheme of virtues arranged in a specific sequence and his practice of tracking failures in a chart. I want to turn a half-formed idea into a short research proposal for an independent, part-time project lasting about three months. The idea is this: Franklin is usually read as offering a linear curriculum—master one virtue, then move cleanly to the next—yet I suspect the ordering actually encodes a hidden structural theory of moral compromise. Specifically, I wonder whether advancing in one virtue requires a temporary, strategic deficit in another, so that moral improvement operates less like a checklist and more like an internal economy of trade-offs, with gains in one area bought by tolerated losses elsewhere. The proposal should state this question precisely: does Franklin’s sequence map necessary inter-virtue dependencies and compromises, or is it merely pedagogical and arbitrary? It should explain why standard interpretations—treating the virtues as separable skills or as an independent ladder—leave this structural possibility untouched. It should describe the method: a close conceptual analysis of the ordering logic and the bookkeeping system, treating the scheme as a live model for thinking about improvement through negotiated compromise rather than as biography or historical curiosity. It should specify what a positive result would look like (for example, identifying specific virtue pairs where progress in one requires tolerated regression in another) and what would count as failure (demonstrating that the sequence has no internal dependency structure). It should also include an honest scope assessment for a three-month, unsupported project, and it should not ask for a bibliography, reading list, or citation review—I will supply references later if needed. The deliverable is a two-page plan arguing that this framing is both conceptually fruitful and executable within these limits.
