In my philosophy reading group—mostly software engineers like myself—we have been stuck for months on whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible, meaning situations where an agent faces two or more irreducible moral demands that cannot all be satisfied, so that any action violates some obligation and leaves what some call a moral remainder. One side, which I think of as the consistency camp, insists that moral obligations are like logical axioms: if two commands truly conflict, at least one was never a genuine obligation, so the agent does the best available act without real wrongdoing, though regret may remain. The other side, the conflict camp, points to tragic forced choices—such as having to decide which child to save—where the agent seems genuinely guilty regardless of outcome, suggesting that morality itself can be overdetermined and that loss is not merely emotional residue but evidence of an unfulfilled duty.

What frustrates me is that both sides accept the same descriptive facts about these cases and both agree that suffering and moral loss are bad. The consistency camp explains away the feeling of guilt as non-moral regret, while the conflict camp treats that same feeling as proof of an independent obligation that was violated. My working hypothesis is that we are not arguing about ethics in a straightforward sense, but about the formal architecture of normative reasoning: the consistency camp treats moral reasoning like a deterministic inference engine that must yield a unique correct output, whereas the conflict camp treats it like a multi-objective constraint-satisfaction problem in which hard constraints can be jointly unsatisfiable. If that is right, the disagreement may be partly verbal—hinging on whether “obligation” means an all-things-considered duty or a pro tanto reason with independent weight—and partly about an unexamined intuition that moral systems must be consistent in the way logical systems are.
