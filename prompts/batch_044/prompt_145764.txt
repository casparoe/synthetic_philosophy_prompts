Write the opening remarks for a workshop titled "The Demos We Draw," convened in the wake of a municipal decision to extend local voting rights to non-citizen residents of five years' standing while excluding seasonal agricultural workers on rolling visas who have lived continuously in the jurisdiction for three years. The audience is mixed: democratic theorists, local officials, members of the newly enfranchised community, and representatives of the excluded workers. The remarks must take the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos?—as structurally unresolved rather than decorative: every criterion proposed (residence, citizenship, affected interests, subjection to law) must be shown to draw a line that includes some and excludes others without natural justification. To avoid a standard recitation of familiar arguments, inject and rely on a specific conceptual distinction: separate "membership as authorization" (the standing to authorize collective decisions) from "membership as exposure" (being structurally subject to decisions made by others without having authorized them). The distinction should do real work in the speech, constraining what the speaker can claim—honoring the council's expansion without treating it as a solution, and acknowledging the excluded workers without collapsing their claim into that of citizens. Deliver the remarks as exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding framing outside those five. Do not name specific philosophers, cite texts, or include bibliographic references; write for an audience that includes both specialists and non-specialists.
