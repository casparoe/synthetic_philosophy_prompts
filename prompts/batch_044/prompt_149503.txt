For an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar on collective action (sixteen students, seventy-five minutes), design a structured simulation that makes participants feel why the tragedy of the commons is not primarily a failure of greed or ignorance, but a structural misalignment between privatized extraction benefits and socialized, temporally delayed maintenance costs—specifically in a version where the resource’s regeneration rate is partially hidden and contributions to upkeep are non-observable. The group should come away seeing that rational individual choice can systematically erode a common pool even when every participant knows the risk and sincerely prefers collective preservation, because the decision architecture severs the visible link between contribution and outcome.

Specify the physical materials and token economy; the rules for extraction and optional, costly maintenance across four rounds; any assigned roles (for example, extractors, monitors with imperfect information, and enforcers with limited sanction power); a precise timeline; and the payoff structure that makes defection individually dominant without making collective collapse obviously inevitable in early rounds.

Then provide the debrief: map specific moments—such as the first hidden depletion, the collapse of voluntary contribution, and the point at which monitoring reveals irreversible loss—onto conceptual features of the problem (asymmetric information about regeneration, non-excludability, intertemporal discounting). Note what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run, especially the tendency to blame particular actors rather than structure, and how the facilitator should redirect that interpretation. Finally, be explicit about which feature of real-world common-pool degradation this classroom version cannot reproduce—most likely the intergenerational timescale or the true irreversibility of ecological collapse—and state exactly how the facilitator must supply that missing element verbally rather than through the rules.
