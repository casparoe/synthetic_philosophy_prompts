I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am designing a single ninety-minute workshop for an adult-education reading group—lawyers, teachers, and local activists, none of them comparative-politics specialists—on whether “democratic backsliding” denotes a real institutional process or is largely a partisan label we apply to governments we happen to oppose. I want the session anchored in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and recent state-level dynamics in the United States, but not as a country-by-country survey; instead, I want one synthetic carrying example that makes the conceptual tension tangible. In discussing the outline with a colleague who edits our foreign desk, I found we kept circling back to the same disagreement: if elections still occur and power still formally transfers, as in parts of the U.S., can the concept apply at all, or does it collapse into mere complaint about policy? That exchange is precisely the misconception I expect the room to bring in.

Plan the explanatory arc for me, not the explanation itself: the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must enter so that the audience can understand backsliding as a gradient of institutional decoupling—formal rules remaining intact while their practical enforcement erodes—rather than as a single dramatic rupture; specify the one concrete scenario (drawn from or synthesized across the four contexts) that should carry the entire session, and state exactly what that scenario must display and what it must omit so that it illustrates both democratic continuity and substantive erosion without becoming a partisan Rorschach test; map the misconceptions the audience will form in sequence, beginning with “this is just sour grapes about losing an election,” moving through “competitive elections rule it out by definition,” and arriving at the U.S.-exceptionalist objection my colleague raised, identifying the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; propose one check I can use at the close to determine whether participants have grasped the analytical distinction rather than merely adopted a political stance; and state openly what I should refuse to cover at this level—whether quantitative democracy indices, comparative constitutional mechanics, or the full arc of Turkish secularism—so that I do not pretend to an expertise I do not possess. Lead with the bottom-line dependency structure of the teaching plan in your first sentence, then unfold the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
