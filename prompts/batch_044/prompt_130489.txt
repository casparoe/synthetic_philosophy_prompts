Consider the following argument, which I take to show that conciliationism is structurally incapable of guiding belief revision in deep disagreement, though I suspect a hidden slide between its premises.

(1) Conciliationism holds that when two epistemic peers disagree about a proposition P, each should adjust their credence in P toward the other’s.
(2) An epistemic peer, on the standard account, is someone with equal cognitive ability, equal access to the relevant evidence, and equal intellectual virtue within the domain of P.
(3) In deep disagreement—say, over whether a particular normative framework is correct—the disputants typically disagree not only about P, but about which considerations count as evidence for P, about the reliability of the reasoning faculties each employs in this domain, and about whether they themselves satisfy the peer conditions with respect to one another.
(4) To apply conciliationism to P, one must therefore first determine whether one’s interlocutor is an epistemic peer for P.
(5) But determining peer status requires resolving disagreements about evidence-relevance and about the reliability of one’s own and the other’s cognitive processes—disagreements that are themselves candidates for conciliation.
(6) This generates a regress: peer status for P depends on resolving meta-level disagreements, the resolution of which depends on peer status for those meta-issues, which in turn depends on further peer assessments, and so on.
(7) To halt the regress, one must either arbitrarily fix peer status at some level—thereby violating the impartiality conciliationism demands—or suspend judgment about peer status altogether.
(8) If peer status is suspended, conciliationism provides no directive about how to revise belief in P.
(9) Therefore, conciliationism either applies only to shallow disagreements where peer status is uncontroversial, or it collapses into a global suspension of judgment precisely in the hard cases where it was meant to operate.

The conclusion seems unacceptable if one wishes to preserve conciliationism as a live option for deep disagreement, and I am inclined to retain both (1) and (3). Yet I feel that something slipped between (5) and (6)—perhaps an unmotivated scope shift that forces conciliation onto the conditions of its own application, or an equivocation between assessing peerhood from a first-personal standpoint versus from within the disagreement itself. Which step should be rejected? And what does rejecting it cost—does it require accepting that peerhood can be assessed from a neutral, non-conciliated vantage point, and if so, does that reintroduce the very steadfastness that conciliationism was designed to eliminate?
