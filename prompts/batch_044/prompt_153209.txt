I am taking a seminar on virtual worlds and ontology, and I am trying to clarify my own views on whether virtual objects and virtual harms can be genuinely real. Below are my verdicts on five concrete cases. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle that captures all of them, or whether some verdict has to be abandoned.

Case A: A player spends roughly four hundred hours constructing an elaborate virtual cathedral in a persistent multiplayer world. Another player destroys it. The builder reports sustained distress comparable to the vandalism of a physical building. My verdict: a real harm has occurred, though the harm derives from destroyed labor and social meaning rather than from the loss of physical matter.

Case B: In an immersive VR therapy session, a patient with full haptic feedback is subjected to a simulated assault. The patient knows the scenario is simulated, yet develops genuine post-traumatic symptoms afterward. My verdict: this is a real psychological harm, not a pretend one, despite the absence of any physical contact.

Case C: A unique digital artwork exists only as a file on a single server. The file is permanently deleted with no backups, but the artist retains complete memory of how to recreate it exactly. My verdict: the specific token is destroyed, but the harm is less severe than destroying an irreplaceable physical painting, because the informational pattern survives in the artist's mind and could be reinstantiated.

Case D: Two people maintain a close friendship for five years entirely through avatars in a virtual world. One avatar is permanently banned and deleted. The surviving person grieves intensely, yet the underlying human being still exists and could reconnect through other platforms. My verdict: the grief is real, but describing the event as the death of a friend seems metaphorical rather than literal.

Case E: A player loses a rare virtual sword in an online game due to a server glitch. Thousands of identical copies exist, and the sword has no unique history or social attachment. The player feels only mild annoyance. My verdict: no significant harm has occurred, even though an object was technically lost.

The tension is this: Cases A and C both involve the destruction of a virtual object into which labor has been invested, yet I treat them differently—A counts as serious harm, C as moderate loss—apparently because in C the pattern survives in a mind. But Case B involves no object destruction at all, yet I treat it as serious harm, while Case D involves the disappearance of an avatar with deep social attachment, yet I deny that it is literally death, even though the connection is disrupted. Meanwhile, Case E involves object loss with minimal investment, and I deny harm entirely.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps centered on functional irreplaceability combined with phenomenal presence—that can accommodate all five verdicts? Please state the principle exactly, identify which clause handles each case, indicate what it implies for an unconsidered case such as a virtual contract that is breached but instantly restored from backup, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to drop. Please answer in American English.
