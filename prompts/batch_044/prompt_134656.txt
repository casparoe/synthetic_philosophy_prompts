I am constructing a character who is a celebrated novelist facing extreme moral trade-offs, and in working out her ethics I have reached verdicts that resist any simple consequentialist calculus. First, she chooses a literary career over surgery, knowing she could have saved hundreds of lives over decades; I judge this not blameworthy. Second, she ignores a drowning child near her desk to finish a sentence; I judge this monstrous. Third, she donates ten percent of her income but refuses to donate fifty percent, even though the latter would save far more lives; I do not count the refusal a moral failure. Fourth, she declines to perform an emergency surgery she alone can do—saving one life—because it conflicts with her long-planned book launch; here I think she is blameworthy, despite the personal and professional cost being far smaller than the career sacrifice in my first case. Fifth, she deliberately inserts falsified clinical data into her fiction to manipulate readers into funding a charity, and I judge this wrong in a structurally different register from mere selfishness. Is there a principle—perhaps one weighing demands not by lives saved but by whether they fracture what I call the compositional continuity of a life-project—that captures all five, or must I overturn one? Please respond as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: state the principle or candidates, show which verdict motivates each clause, predict its judgment on an artist who abandons a gallery opening to administer an antidote to a stranger, and say honestly which of my five judgments it cannot accommodate.
