Since my father died last autumn, I have been cataloguing his study — a theoretical physicist’s archive of annotated monographs and handwritten notebooks — and I have become unreasonably preoccupied with whether a preserved record of thought can ever amount to a thinker. I recently returned to the dialogue in The Mind's I in which Einstein’s preserved brain debates whether it remains conscious without a body, and I have devised a contrived case for the MA seminar I convene on consciousness and artificial cognition: the Autographic Volume. This hypothetical object comprises electronic paper, thermochromic ink, and embedded conductive polymers, arranged to instantiate a complete functional isomorphism of Einstein’s cognitive architecture. It contains no biological tissue; its processing occurs through chemical reactions and circuit responses triggered by environmental stimuli, and it continuously rewrites its own annotations, producing a closed feedback loop that resembles narrative self-reference.

Please draft a single essay question for a 90-minute final examination in an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar. The question should ask students whether the Autographic Volume could be conscious, using the issues raised by the Einstein’s brain dialogue as a framework but forbidding them from stopping at standard functionalist slogans. Specifically, the prompt should require candidates to address: (i) whether substrate independence genuinely follows from the dialogue’s premises when the substrate is non-biological and distributed across paper and polymer; (ii) whether the absence of biological homeostasis undermines any claim to consciousness; (iii) whether the volume’s capacity for self-modification introduces a form of autopoiesis that static texts lack; and (iv) whether the dialogue’s implicit reliance on narrative continuity applies differently to an object that literally rewrites its own history.

Then provide a model answer of approximately 700 words that adjudicates these points with philosophical precision rather than mere summary. Finally, provide a marking scheme built around six must-hit analytical moves, with marks assigned out of 20 total. For each move, state the specific misunderstanding that typically costs candidates marks, and indicate briefly how an answer that earns an A differs from one that earns a B. The scheme should reward students who recognise where the niche thought experiment breaks the standard functionalist analogy rather than those who simply apply it. Write in British English, keep the tone formal and polished, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
