My colleague applies a framework that combines Buddhist ethical analysis with consequentialist evaluation to judge whether proposed interventions are skillful (kusala), and she has given me her verdicts on five prior cases.

Her record so far:
- A progressive tax funds universal basic nutrition; taxpayers feel resentment, recipients avoid starvation. Verdict: skillful.
- A psychiatrist discloses a severe, stigmatizing diagnosis directly and without sugarcoating; the patient suffers acute anxiety for months but eventually integrates the knowledge and seeks effective treatment. Verdict: skillful.
- A palliative care physician deliberately frames a terminal prognosis as uncertain and possibly reversible to prevent immediate despair; the patient dies peacefully without ever learning the truth, and the family praises the physician. Verdict: unskillful.
- A school offers an entirely optional, transparent mindfulness curriculum; participation is voluntary, and long-term well-being metrics improve among participants without harming non-participants. Verdict: skillful.
- A municipal government redesigns public parks using evidence-based environmental psychology—lighting, path curvature, soundscaping—to subtly reduce aggression and anxiety, without informing citizens that the design is therapeutic; post-occupancy studies show reduced violence and stress. Verdict: unskillful.

The new case: A trauma surgeon receives a conscious but shock-impaired patient with a life-threatening yet treatable injury. Knowing that full disclosure of severity will likely trigger panic-induced cardiac arrest before treatment can begin, the surgeon says only “we need to fix something quickly,” proceeds with the intervention, and saves the patient’s life. The patient later expresses gratitude but also lingering betrayal at having been kept in partial ignorance during a moment of extreme vulnerability. The surgeon’s sole motivation was life preservation; no false statement was uttered, only an omission of severity, and the patient’s rational autonomy was severely compromised by shock at the time.

Predict her verdict. Identify at least two plausible rules she might be running—such as an outcome-weighted evaluation hard-constrained by transparency and autonomy, versus an agent-centered evaluation focused on mental-state purity and direct-action integrity—and say what each predicts for this surgeon. Give your best-guess verdict with the reasoning for preferring that rule, then specify the further case that would best distinguish the two rules. Finally, state plainly if the task rests on a confused premise, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict more severely than presented, or if the case descriptions fail to isolate the rules you identify.
