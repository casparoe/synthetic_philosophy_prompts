A fellow student in my philosophy of mind seminar summarized David Chalmers's conceivability argument against physicalism as follows. Chalmers asks us to imagine a zombie world physically identical to ours in every particle and law but in which no one has any conscious experience whatsoever. Because this scenario is clearly and distinctly imaginable, it is metaphysically possible. From that modal claim Chalmers infers that phenomenal properties are not entailed by physical properties, so physicalism is false. The student added that Chalmers reinforces the point by appealing to phenomenal concepts—the special first-person modes of grasp by which we access our own experiences—and argued that since zombies entirely lack these concepts, they demonstrate that phenomenal facts float free of physical facts even in principle. The student concluded that the argument is decisive and that neuroscience must therefore be permanently supplemented by a non-physical ontology of consciousness.

Is this summary fair? Go through it point by point: which parts accurately reflect Chalmers's reasoning, which are acceptable popular simplifications, and which are misrepresentations or ideas imported from adjacent debates he does not actually invoke. In particular, clarify whether phenomenal concepts play the role the summary assigns them in the zombie argument itself; whether the inference from conceivability to metaphysical possibility is as direct and Cartesian as presented; and whether the claim that neuroscience requires a non-physical supplement follows from any premise that survives correction. If the argument is weaker, differently structured, or relies on distinctions the summary collapses, say exactly where the overreach occurs and what a more accurate reconstruction would look like.
