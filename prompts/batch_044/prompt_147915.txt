I serve on the planning commission for a small river town that is deciding whether to approve a hydroelectric diversion. The project would provide cheap energy for the next thirty years, but it would permanently alter the downstream ecology. A fellow commissioner argues that rejecting the plan would change the town's entire economic and social trajectory, so the specific people living here two centuries from now—who they marry, where they settle—would be numerically different individuals. From this he concludes that we cannot be harming anyone by approving the dam, since those particular future residents exist only because of our choice. He further claims that, because the project sustains a larger population over time and those lives are worth living, we actually have reason to build it rather than forgo bringing those good existences into being. I still feel we are wronging whoever ends up in this place, regardless of whether they owe their existence to us. Is my colleague correct that identity-dependence dissolves our obligation, and does his appeal to the value of creating those lives strengthen his case?
