Design a 75-minute, 16-student undergraduate workshop activity on the ethics of commemorative public space—statues, street names, and plazas—that makes participants feel, before anyone names it, a tension between corrective removal or renaming and what I will call mnemonic displacement: the invisible redistribution of symbolic weight onto adjacent spaces and remaining markers when one is taken down. Invent a fictional six-site town map (one square, one bridge, four streets) all named after a single layered figure—part civic founder, part architect of a now-condemned exclusion policy—and assign roles: an archivist holding hidden documents linking the adjacent sites to the same legacy; residents from four districts with different material stakes in the figure’s memory; and a planner constrained by a rule allowing only two commemorative changes total. The group must physically rearrange a printed map and vote on a final layout within 20 minutes; unanimous agreement is required for a shared “city stability” score, but each district independently rates its own comfort, generating cross-pressure. Structure the rules so that removing the central square’s name does not dissolve association but visibly migrates it to the bridge and two untouched streets, forcing the group to discover the migration themselves. Include the materials needed, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief that maps each decision point onto corrective justice versus mnemonic displacement, an honest admission of which feature of real-world commemoration—such as intergenerational trauma, the aesthetic presence of monumental scale, or state legitimacy—the paper-map exercise cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally, and a note on the predictable failure mode (rapid consensus to rename the obvious central site followed by paralysis about the hidden redistribution) and how to redirect it. Do not request citations, named theorists, or reading lists; the conceptual machinery should be embedded entirely in the roles, rules, and stakes.
