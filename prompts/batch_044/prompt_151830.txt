I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language working on moral expressivism, and I'm trying to systematize my intuitions about embedded moral language in order to assess whether the Frege-Geach problem is genuinely damaging or merely a compositional puzzle that can be solved by the right taxonomy of embedding operators. I have the following verdicts on concrete cases, which I want tested against a precise principle.

Case 1 — Unembedded assertion. "Torturing prisoners for information is wrong." My verdict: the speaker expresses a categorical non-cognitive attitude of disapproval; there is no representational belief being asserted.

Case 2 — Simple material conditional. "If torturing prisoners is wrong, then using coercive interrogation is wrong." My verdict: in the antecedent, the moral clause does not express the speaker's disapproval; the speaker merely entertains the content without endorsing the attitude. The conditional as a whole expresses a relation between two contents, not a complex attitude directed at both.

Case 3 — Belief attribution. "Maria believes that torturing prisoners is wrong." My verdict: the embedded clause expresses Maria's disapproving attitude, not mine. The embedding verb licenses the preservation of attitudinal force, but redirects it to the subject.

Case 4 — Epistemic modal embedding. "There is a serious possibility that torturing prisoners is wrong." My verdict: unlike the conditional, the speaker here expresses a graded, second-order attitude—something like suspended or probabilistic disapproval—rather than merely entertaining content. The epistemic modal does not suppress the attitude; it qualifies its strength.

Case 5 — Disjunction. "Either torturing prisoners is wrong or coercive interrogation is wrong." My verdict: this feels different from the conditional. The speaker seems to be expressing a kind of disjunctive commitment: they are not neutral between the disjuncts, but they are not fully committed to either. I am inclined to say that each disjunct retains potential attitudinal force, yet the whole expresses an unsettled stance that is not reducible to a single categorical attitude.

Case 6 — Explicit negation with attitude report. "Torturing prisoners is not wrong, and in fact I approve of it in emergencies." My verdict: the negation cancels the disapproving attitude expressed by the unembedded clause, but "not wrong" can itself function to express a positive attitude (approval or tolerance). Thus negation does not merely leave an attitudinal vacuum; it can invert the valence.

These verdicts seem partly coherent, but there is a tension. Cases 2 and 5 both involve logical operators, yet I treat the conditional as suppressing attitude entirely while treating disjunction as retaining partial attitudinal force. Meanwhile, Case 4 involves an operator that is not psychological, yet I allow attitude to persist in qualified form, which seems closer to Case 3 than to Case 2.

My tentative principle is that embedding operators fall into three categories: (i) psychological operators (belief, desire) that preserve and redirect attitudinal force; (ii) epistemic and probabilistic operators (possible, likely, uncertain) that preserve but gradate it; and (iii) purely logical operators (conditional, disjunction, negation) that suspend attitudinal force and operate only on content, with negation additionally allowing inverse attitude only when explicitly paired with a positive report.

This principle captures Cases 1–3 neatly, but it struggles with Case 4 (why does an epistemic modal gradate rather than suspend?) and with Case 5 (why does disjunction retain partial force if it is logical?). It also has trouble with Case 6, since negation seems to do more than suspend.

So my questions: Is there a more precise principle—perhaps distinguishing between "attitude-licensing" and "content-manipulating" embeddings, or invoking a distinction between propositional attitude objects and speech-act potentials—that can accommodate all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which of my verdicts (probably Case 4 or Case 5) must be abandoned for the sake of compositional uniformity? Please state the principle precisely, specify which clause governs each case, extend it to at least two unconsidered cases (for instance, nested embedding inside a counterfactual: "If Maria believed that torturing prisoners was wrong, she would have resigned"; and a probabilistic disjunction: "It is more likely that either tax fraud or bribery is wrong than that neither is"), and be explicit about which of my original verdicts the principle cannot save.
