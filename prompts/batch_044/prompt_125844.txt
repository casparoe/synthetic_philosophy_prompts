I chair the ethics review board for a mid-sized municipality, and every month we must approve, reject, or send back development proposals that pit genuinely different kinds of goods against one another—ecological resilience, cultural continuity, distributive justice, intergenerational fairness—where no single metric (cost-benefit, utility, rights-based veto) can settle the matter. I need a reusable decision procedure that my board can run in a ninety-minute session, with no philosophers present and no quantitative aggregation of values, that operationalizes the idea that values can be incommensurable (not reducible to a common scale) without being incomparable, and that distinguishes hard choices—where reason constrains the permissible options but does not uniquely determine one—from cases where a master principle or dominance relation actually applies.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions, tests, and stopping conditions, with explicit flags for every point where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be documented in writing by the board. The procedure should be usable by a trained planner or attorney without philosophical training, but it must preserve the conceptual distinctions: do not collapse incommensurability into “weighing pros and cons,” and do not allow hidden domain collapse (for example, treating cultural identity as merely an economic preference).

The procedure must include the following stages:

1. Domain identification: list the value domains genuinely at stake in the proposal, with a check for hidden collapse or omission.
2. Translation audit: test whether rendering any domain in decision-relevant terms has caused “value leakage” (a loss of normative force specific to that domain), with concrete indicators of distortion.
3. Dominance and critical-threshold screen: reject if one option is worse in every domain; flag for exceptional justification if any domain reaches severe, irreversible harm.
4. Comparative judgment stage: for remaining cases, since scores cannot be summed, articulate a context-specific reason why one option is more appropriate, tied to the board’s mandate. Include a “structural parity” protocol for when reasons across domains are genuinely on a par: rather than arbitrary selection, the board must record a “contextual priority commitment” with a justification specific to the proposal’s scope.
5. Hard-choice protocol: produce an answer the board will not like—specifically, approve a proposal that causes irreplaceable harm in one domain because no permissible alternative exists, requiring a signed “hard choice” record.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete proposals:

A. Rerouting a flood-defense canal through a century-old immigrant-market district (cultural continuity, local economic identity) versus leaving an adjacent low-income neighborhood exposed to projected flooding (distributive justice, ecological adaptation).

B. Funding immediate elder-care expansion versus long-term climate-hardening infrastructure, both serving vulnerable populations but across different temporal frames.

C. One additional case of your own design that tests the structural-parity protocol, where two options each cause severe harm in different domains and neither dominates.

For each application, show exactly where the procedure stops with a clear answer, where it demands a judgment call, and what the written sign-off must contain. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do—for example, resolve disputes about the board’s mandate itself, eliminate moral regret, or substitute for democratic input on value priorities. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not quote specific texts.
