I serve on my university’s Committee on Research Integrity and Funding Review. We must decide by Thursday whether to renew a three-year grant for a behavioral-science group that studies urban residential mobility with an agent-based model. The model treats every household as a utility-maximizing calculator with perfect market information, zero place attachment, no social-network effects, and deterministic gradient-descent rules—assumptions everyone agrees are descriptively false about actual families. Yet it predicts census-tract turnover rates more accurately than richer, more realistic specifications. The principal investigator defends this by invoking the Friedman-style F-twist: unrealistic premises are methodologically virtuous precisely when they strip away causally inert complexity, and predictive success vindicates the omissions. Two committee members want unconditional renewal, arguing that demanding realistic microfoundations is an anti-scientific aesthetic preference. Two others want rejection, contending that in social science a false-mechanism model with good fit risks misleading policymakers into treating predictive correlation as intervention-ready causation, especially if the grant’s next phase proposes direct housing-policy recommendations. I need a short decision memo—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—for circulation before the meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action with a specific reporting requirement or time frame, and engage the F-twist defense seriously rather than dismissing it as mere instrumentalism. Give an honest reply to the strongest consideration on the losing side.
