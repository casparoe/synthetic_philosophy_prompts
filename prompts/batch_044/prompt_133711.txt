I have been studying the Gettier problem, particularly cases in which a false premise seems to cancel out a separate error—what one might call an epistemic cancellation structure, like reasoning from a mistaken belief that happens to be corrected by an unknown second mistake, yielding a true conclusion. For decades philosophers have disputed whether such structures defeat the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, with one camp treating justification as an internal feature of reasoning quality and another treating it as an external modal feature such as safety or sensitivity. My working theory is that the disagreement has stalled not because of missing data, but because the two sides use "justification" for different things—one for rational procedure, one for reliable tracking—while both claim to be analyzing the same concept of knowledge. Is this impasse genuinely substantive, partly verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about what makes belief reasonable, one about what makes belief non-accidental—under a single heading? And if you diagnose the structure of the disagreement, what specific conceptual distinction—perhaps separating reasoning-path quality from environmental modal robustness—would actually count as progress, or should we expect that no single example can resolve it?
