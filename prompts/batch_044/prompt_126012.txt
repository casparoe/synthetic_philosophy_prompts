I'm trying to get a precise conceptual handle on the Pasadena paradox, specifically on the form in which the expected payoff is not merely infinite but genuinely undefined. I already understand how the St. Petersburg paradox yields an infinite expectation that can be tamed by bounded utility or risk aversion, but the Pasadena variant seems to arrange the payoff schedule so that positive and negative contributions oscillate without converging even in an extended real-number sense—leaving the expectation without any value, not even ±∞.

Could you explain, in technically careful but intuitive terms, exactly why the expectation fails to exist in this stronger sense? In particular, I'd like to understand the difference between a divergent series that tends to infinity and one that is undefined because its partial sums do not settle in any direction. Then consider a slight twist: imagine we embed the discrete round number into a continuous parameter space and multiply the payoff by an indicator of a non-measurable subset of that space. The discrete probabilities of reaching each round remain unchanged, but the payoff now includes a component that is not integrable for purely measure-theoretic reasons. Does this modification show that any decision rule relying on linear expectation collapses as soon as absolute integrability fails, or is there a principled way to restrict attention to the measurable part of the payoff?

I'd prefer you not simply rehearse the standard St. Petersburg resolutions; I'm mainly interested in how an undefined expectation undermines the normative claim that a rational agent should maximize expected utility, especially when there is literally no expectation to maximize.
