For a philosophy of mind seminar I'm assisting with, I'm developing material on distributed personal identity and the limits of substrate-independent theories of the self. I've been working through Douglas Hofstadter's argument that an I is not a fixed substance but a recursive pattern—what he terms a strange loop—that could in principle persist across multiple brains if the structural resonance is high enough. Most fiction on this topic collapses into brain-upload clichés or symmetrical duplication scenarios, so I'd like a prompt that forces a model to think through the phenomenological weirdness rather than the technological logistics.

Please write a short story—quiet, clinical, not a thriller—centered on Dr. Elena Vasquez, a neuroscientist with a progressive neurodegenerative condition, who arranges an experimental neural graft with her identical twin sister Mara. The graft is deliberately partial: it does not copy Elena's full episodic memory or personality, but attempts to instantiate only the core recursive self-model—the specific pattern that generates first-person authority—into Mara's prefrontal architecture. The procedure succeeds, but it produces an asymmetrical distributed self. Elena experiences a gradual dissolution of first-person ownership over certain thoughts and decisions, as though parts of her loop are now being executed in Mara's brain. Mara, however, does not experience herself as Elena; she experiences herself as a single self that has suddenly acquired alien patterns claiming proprietary rights over some of her mental states.

The story should focus on a specific, mundane scene: both women are present at a joint clinical evaluation, and a single decision must be made—whether to maintain, reduce, or terminate the graft. The narrative perspective should shift ambiguously between Elena and Mara without resolving whose first-person authority governs the choice. I want the story to dramatize the philosophical problem: if personal identity is just a pattern, why does its distribution feel like theft or erosion to the hosts rather than continuity? Avoid any definitive answer about survival; instead, let the clinical details—the way each woman describes the location of a particular intention—carry the philosophical weight. The setting should be contemporary, the tone restrained, and the technology described only in functional, not speculative, terms.
