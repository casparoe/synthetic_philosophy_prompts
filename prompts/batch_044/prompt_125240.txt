I have been playing a cooperative simulation with a friend in which the virtual inhabitants are not confined to our local devices. They live in a persistent server environment, carry out autonomous routines when we are absent, retain memories of specific conversations and gifts across sessions, and suffer permanent in-world consequences—such as the loss of a companion—that remain visible to anyone who logs back in. This week my friend proposed that we delete the master save file and start over, arguing that this is ethically no different from turning off our monitors: in either case, the inhabitants stop being present to us, and none of them possess consciousness that could be harmed. I resisted, feeling that shutting down merely suspends their existence within our reach, whereas erasing the save annihilates the particular histories and relationships that had accumulated, almost like destroying a record that happens to contain lives rather than just data. I do not want generic reassurance or a lecture about fiction; I want to understand whether there is a coherent philosophical framework that distinguishes suspending interaction from erasing the conditions of a being's particular identity, and whether applying that framework here is appropriate or is just a category error driven by my own attachment. How should I think about the difference?
