I have come to read Cicero's dialogue on old age as advancing a capacity-sensitive, Aristotelian-style account of virtue in later life rather than the Stoic apatheia it is usually taken to defend. On my reading, Cicero does not claim that old age eliminates the passions—only that it shifts the domain of virtuous activity from bodily action to counsel, memory, and deliberative judgment, and that this shift is normatively appropriate rather than merely compensatory. I take this to mean that the virtues of old age are not diminished forms of youthful virtues but specifically structured excellences calibrated to a different practical context. What would count against this reading? I want more than the standard observation that Cicero invokes Cato and Stoic commonplaces; I want to know which specific conceptual commitments of my interpretation are exposed to defeat—whether by internal inconsistencies in the dialogue's treatment of pleasure and desire, by tensions between this Aristotelian framing and Cicero's explicit endorsements of Stoic doctrine elsewhere, or by the risk that my capacity-sensitive qualifier renders the view unfalsifiable. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate which ones a defender of this reading could reasonably concede as damaging without abandoning the core claim, versus which ones they should treat as misreadings. Be honest about whether the interpretation is well insulated.
