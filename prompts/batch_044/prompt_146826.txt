I'm giving opening remarks at a workshop next tuesday — it's called "History for the Future: Archives, Climate Adaptation, and the Weight of Precedent." The room is split: half are historians who live and breathe Nietzche's three modes of history — monumental, antiquarian, critical — and the other half are urban planners and climate officers who have never heard of them and frankly don't care about 19th century german philosophy. I need about five minutes.

The idea is that the three modes should actually structure what I can and can't say. Monumental history is dangerous here because it flatters us with heroic stories of past resilience that don't actually apply to our infrastructure problems; antiquarian is seductive but paralyzing, since every local archive starts feeling sacred and untouchable; critical is the only mode that lets us actually abandon failed precedents, but I can't sound like I'm dismissing the historians' craft, because half the audience is them.

So I need a speech that warns the historians their usual tools are partly the problem, comforts the planners that they're not being anti-history, and doesn't let anyone walk out angry. There should be one slightly awkward joke about a monument being built to a flood wall that never actually stopped anything — it needs to land for both sides. Please don't turn this into a lecture, and don't ask me to cite page numbers, I don't have them memorized.
