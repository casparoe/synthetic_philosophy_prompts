I've been thinking lately about the idea that suffering isn't just a medical problem to be treated but something we might eventually engineer out of the human condition entirely—not with therapy or drugs, but with a kind of biotechnology that rewrites the affective baseline itself, so that trauma, grief, or existential dread don't just diminish but become neurologically impossible, replaced by a calibrated well-being that never dips below a set point. Some people call this moral vision the hedonistic imperative, though I'm less interested in whether it's obligatory and more struck by a tension I can't shake: if you could turn a dial that raises your emotional floor high enough that betrayal, loss, or injustice can't actually hurt you, you might also erase the specific weight that makes moral outrage or deep mourning feel real and necessary. I was talking this over with friends, and we kept circling the same problem—everyone starts by saying of course we should end suffering, but when we imagine what capacities we'd have to trade away, the group splits in ways nobody predicted.

I'm running an informal philosophy discussion group—about fourteen adults with no required academic background, meeting in a community space for seventy-five minutes—and I want an activity that makes that split happen physically and socially, not just intellectually. Design a structured deliberation or game in which participants act as members of a hypothetical bioethics council deciding on a graduated affective calibration policy for a small community: they must vote on how high to set the irreversible emotional floor, knowing that each increment eliminates specific capacities (the particular sorrow that drives reconciliation, the sharp pain that makes betrayal morally legible, the low-grade dread that fuels political urgency). The rules and roles should be arranged so that participants feel the pull of both abolition and preservation before anyone names the concept, using symbolic payoffs or small real trade-offs rather than cash. Include materials, a timeline for the session, a debrief that maps each decision point onto the philosophical tension, an honest forecast of how participants will game or avoid the irreversible choice the first time it runs, and a frank note on which feature of actual biotechnological irreversibility this exercise necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally.

The idea should emerge from the choices they make under the rules, with no lecture up front and nothing to read beforehand. I want to see whether a group that enters unanimous about ending suffering fractures once they discover, through their own negotiation, which emotional capacities they are actually unwilling to delete—and I want to come away understanding exactly where the conceptual fault line lies.
