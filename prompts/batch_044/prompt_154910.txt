As a retired natural scientist—previously working in field ecology—who has only recently begun to study philosophy systematically, I find myself repeatedly asked to serve as an independent methodological referee for a small regional body that reviews proposals for intervention in wild ecosystems. The proposals invariably assert that some environmental factor causally produces an observed change in a population, and they request funding or permission to act on that basis. In my philosophical reading I have become aware of the distinction between counterfactual theories of causation, which analyse causal relations through modal dependencies on what would occur under alternative conditions, and interventionist theories, which analyse them through invariant structural relationships that hold under idealised manipulations. I should like these two frameworks translated into a practical, reusable decision procedure that I can apply without returning to first principles each time.

Please construct for me a formal decision procedure—presented as an ordered sequence of explicit tests, with stopping rules and points of mandatory handover—through which I can evaluate any such proposal. The procedure should distinguish clearly between steps that derive from counterfactual reasoning and those that derive from interventionist reasoning, specifying at each stage what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as failure. It must identify the precise juncture at which the procedure yields a definitive recommendation against proceeding, and the junctures at which further assessment is no longer a matter of fact but a matter of values, requiring explicit human judgment rather than methodological resolution. It should also state explicitly where the two theoretical frameworks conflict, and what the procedure can and cannot do in those cases.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals: (i) a plan to restrict agricultural runoff in a watershed on the grounds that nitrate levels causally drive amphibian deformities; (ii) a plan to install artificial nesting platforms for a raptor species, claiming that nest-site availability causally limits population recovery; and (iii) a plan to suppress, through vaccination, a chronic pathogen in a wild mammal population, where the proponents emphasise welfare benefits but where long-term demographic modelling suggests that pathogen suppression might trigger overpopulation and subsequent starvation. In case (iii), the procedure should reach a conclusion that a welfare-oriented board would find unwelcome, and you should explain precisely which step produces that result. Throughout, flag the judgments that remain irreducibly subjective despite the procedural form, and be honest about the misfires and limitations of the framework.

Please write your answer in British English, and avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of specific texts.
