I am designing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar on mental causation for students who have already worked through Davidson's anomalous monism and are comfortable with the basic machinery of supervenience, but who have not yet confronted Jaegwon Kim's exclusion argument in its strongest form. Rather than using the familiar arm-raising scenario, I intend to carry the entire argument through one institutional case: a board of directors collectively deciding to dissolve a corporation, where the mental content of the vote appears to cause the legal dissolution only through the physical movements of hands and signatures, yet we want to preserve an irreducible explanatory role for the intentional description of the decision. Plan the pedagogical sequence: the exact order in which the closure principle, the exclusion principle, and the supervenience thesis must be introduced so that the conflict emerges as structurally inevitable rather than contrived, and what logical dependency forces that ordering; what features the board-vote case must possess and what it must lack in order to make the exclusion visible, together with why personal action examples actually obscure the problem at this level; the specific misconceptions students will import from Davidson—particularly the belief that anomalous monism already blocks overdetermination—and the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and neutralized; the diagnostic question I should pose at the end that would distinguish genuine grasp of the structural problem from mere memorization of Kim's premises; and which responses—specifically emergentist and compatibilist strategies—I should explicitly bracket and refuse to discuss in this session, with a brief pedagogical justification for each omission that I can present to the class without sounding evasive.
