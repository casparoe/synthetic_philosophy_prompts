Having spent three decades in population ecology before turning, somewhat belatedly, to moral and political philosophy, I have grown dissatisfied with the standard literature on reparations—discussions of slavery, colonial extraction, and forced assimilation—because it trades in broad moral intuition without first specifying what an adequate theory must actually deliver. In a recent discussion with colleagues from an interdisciplinary reading group, I was pressed to say precisely what I would require of any account of reparations for historical injustice before I could accept it, and I realized I could not answer without constructing the specification myself. I am therefore asking you to write the desiderata: what must a theory of reparations for historical injustice be able to do to count as adequate?

Please provide the full list of requirements—the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must handle correctly, the distinctions it must preserve, and the adjacent results it must remain consistent with—together with the motivating consideration behind each. Specifically, I want the specification to include the following, though you should organize and supplement them as needed: first, a principled distinction between direct corrective obligations owed to identifiable victims (or their lineal descendants) and structural-repair obligations owed to present communities suffering from persistent institutional distortion, with the reason this distinction cannot be collapsed; second, an explicit account of temporal attenuation—whether moral obligations weaken across generations, and if so by what functional form (linear decay, threshold extinction, or non-decreasing structural compounding)—since leaving this to intuition produces contradictory verdicts in multi-generational cases; third, consistency with the non-identity problem, explaining how present persons can be owed reparations for past harms without presupposing that those same persons would have existed had the harm not occurred; fourth, a defensible method for selecting the counterfactual baseline against which loss is measured—what economic, political, and ecological conditions should serve as the comparison—without requiring an impossible reconstruction of counterfactual history; fifth, compatibility with liberal constraints on collective punishment, so that present beneficiaries are not treated as mere liability vessels for ancestral actions they did not choose; and sixth, an answer to whether ecological or interspecies harms—such as the extraction of resources that degraded ecosystems sustaining indigenous communities—belong within reparative justice or fall under a separate distributive or conservation framework.

For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable for any adequate theory or merely a preference of one camp (for instance, those prioritizing backward-looking rectification versus those prioritizing forward-looking equality). Then examine whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable: in particular, whether strict temporal attenuation, a resolution of the non-identity problem that severs lineal claims, and liberal anti-punishment constraints together generate a trilemma in which repairing structural harm requires attributing collective liability that individualist liberalism forbids. Finally, score briefly how the standard contenders—pure corrective-justice models, structural-injustice frameworks, and hybrid institutional-repair approaches—fare against your list, but keep the conceptual weight on the criteria themselves, since that is where the philosophical work resides.
