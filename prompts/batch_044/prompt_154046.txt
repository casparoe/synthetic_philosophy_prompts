For my philosophy of collective action course, I have been developing a specific defense of individual obligations in collective harm cases—climate change, meat consumption, and voting—against the inefficacy objection. The position is not merely that expected causal utility is nonzero, but that individual acts operate as "trigger nodes" within social-norm cascades: their moral significance derives not from direct contribution to harm but from their structural role in stabilizing or destabilizing the collective equilibrium that sustains the practice. Thus, an act can be wrong in virtue of reinforcing a harmful practice that depends on widespread participation, even when one's particular omission would be causally redundant.

What would have to be true for this view to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings about norm transmission or collective behavior, which formal results from game theory or decision theory, and which intuitions about responsibility in large groups would be decisive rather than merely awkward. In particular, if individual actions turned out to be largely unobservable nodes for others, or if the idea of "stabilizing an equilibrium" collapsed back into standard aggregate utilitarianism, would that be fatal? Which of these defeaters would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging—perhaps the risk that every act can be redescribed as structurally significant, rendering the view unfalsifiable—versus which they would dismiss? Please rank the vulnerabilities from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether the view's insulation from direct causal testing is a theoretical virtue or a vice for a normative account of this kind.
