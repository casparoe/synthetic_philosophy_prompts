I'm a doctoral student in early modern philosophy and political theology, currently drafting an essay on a thread in Blaise Pascal's Pensées that I think is usually flattened by standard commentary. Most readers treat Pascal's pairing of justice and force as a pragmatic compromise for fallen human beings: since true justice belongs to God alone, we settle for conventional justice backed by coercive power, and the best we can do is keep them together to avoid either tyranny or anarchy. I want to argue something stronger—that for Pascal, justice without force is not merely impractical but conceptually unstable, because in the postlapsarian condition the recognition of a normative claim seems to depend on some institutional pathway by which it can be enforced or at least acknowledged as authoritative. Force is therefore not an external instrument but part of justice's phenomenological structure in a world where human reason is too weak to secure agreement on first principles. To sharpen this, I draw an analogy with a problem in contemporary social epistemology: if a normative standard lacks any mechanism for institutional uptake or enforcement, does it retain genuine normative weight, or does it collapse into private preference? I then ask whether Pascal's position anticipates a form of institutional realism usually credited to later thinkers, and whether this makes him closer to Hobbes than is acknowledged, despite their obvious theological divergence. I also include a speculative section mapping Pascal's distinction between what I call the justice of force and the force of justice onto the modern distinction between de facto authority and de jure legitimacy, though I worry this imports conceptual machinery he would reject.

I'd welcome comments on whether this framing is textually defensible, whether the social-epistemological analogy clarifies or obscures Pascal's theological commitments—particularly his doctrine of the three orders and the idea that charity belongs to a different order than justice—and whether there are specific discussions in the Pensées regarding custom, law, and the order of charity that either support or undermine my claim that force is conceptually internal to justice rather than merely instrumentally necessary. I'm especially interested in whether the essay risks anachronism by treating Pascal's theological pessimism about human reason as continuous with secular institutional theories, and in suggestions for how to maintain that tension without resolving it too neatly.
