I am a novelist working on a historical novel set in twelfth-century Baghdad, and I am trying to render an intellectual debate scene with enough philosophical precision that it feels authentic rather than costume-drama. In my research I have encountered Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism, but a philosopher friend recently summarized the view for me in terms that I now want to verify before I put them into a character’s mouth. My friend said: Al-Ghazālī argued that nature has no real causal power whatsoever. Fire does not actually burn cotton; rather, God directly creates the burning in each particular instance, and if He so willed the cotton could remain cold despite contact with flame. The philosophers, he claimed, wrongly assumed that causes necessarily produce their effects, but in reality all causation is merely God repeating the same direct intervention over and over according to habit. Consequently, for Al-Ghazālī there are no genuine natural laws—only arbitrary divine customs that might change without warning—so empirical science is ultimately impossible. The friend added that this is essentially the same position as Hume’s skepticism about necessary connection, except that Al-Ghazālī deployed it specifically to keep the possibility of miracles permanently open.

Please take the role of an expert in medieval Islamic philosophy and metaphysics, think carefully, and begin with a brief brainstorming session that distinguishes the key concepts at play—metaphysical occasionalism, the denial of necessary causal connection, the doctrine of ʿāda (custom or habitual course), the status of empirical reasoning, and the difference between epistemological skepticism and theological direct agency. Then go through my friend’s summary claim by claim. For each part, indicate whether it is accurate, whether it is a common but misleading simplification, or whether it subtly misrepresents Al-Ghazālī’s actual argument in Tahāfut al-Falāsifa. I am especially interested in whether the conclusion that science is impossible actually follows from his premises, and whether the comparison to Hume collapses two distinct kinds of doubt into one. Please separate what a careful defender of Al-Ghazālī’s position would accept from what they would object to, and conclude by telling me whether the corrected view still supports the dramatic tension I intend: a thinker who denies that fire possesses any intrinsic power to burn, yet who nevertheless believes human beings can reason about the world with confidence and regularity.
