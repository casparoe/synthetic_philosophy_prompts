I'm consolidating my position on divine foreknowledge and human freedom for a workshop paper and want a consistency audit before I commit to the framing. Here is where I've landed: (1) I accept Molinism—God possesses middle knowledge of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom prior to any creative decree. (2) I hold a libertarian view of free will: for any free act, the agent genuinely could have done otherwise in precisely the same causal and psychological circumstances, including identical prior mental states. (3) I think the grounding objection to Molinism fails because counterfactuals of freedom are grounded not in God's will but in the modal profile—or dispositional essence—of each possible creature, which fixes what that creature would freely do in various situations. (4) I also think the correct semantics for counterfactuals is broadly similarity-based in the tradition of Lewis and Stalnaker, where the truth of a counterfactual depends on comparative similarity relations among possible worlds. (5) I maintain that foreknowledge based on middle knowledge poses no threat to freedom, since knowing an outcome is not causally producing it. (6) Finally, I take middle knowledge to help with the logical problem of evil: God weakly actualizes worlds by selecting circumstances, knowing in advance which free responses will occur, thereby being able to actualize worlds with optimal combinations of free good and permitted evil.

Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, and which tensions are only apparent? In particular, I'm worried that my essentialist grounding in (3) may conflict with my libertarianism in (2)—if a creature's modal profile determines its free choice in a situation, then in identical circumstances it seems the choice is fixed after all. I'm also unsure whether (1) and (4) can coexist: on a similarity-based semantics, libertarian freedom seems to make nearby worlds diverge with respect to free choices, which threatens the determinacy of the very counterfactuals Molinism requires God to know. Meanwhile, (5) looks like it might conflict with (2), but I suspect that is only apparent. Go through the pairs systematically, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Then tell me whether there is a single revision—say, abandoning the similarity semantics, weakening libertarianism, or giving up the essentialist grounding—that would resolve the most tension at once, or whether I need to make separate local adjustments.
