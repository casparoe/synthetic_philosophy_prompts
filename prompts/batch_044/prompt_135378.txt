For the closing exercise in our graduate seminar on persistence and time, I would like you to compose a poem of exactly eighteen lines, arranged in three stanzas of six lines each, in unrhymed blank verse with occasional slant rhyme. Impose this formal constraint: Stanza One must open with the word Whole and close with the word part; Stanza Two must open with Part and close with whole; Stanza Three must open with Whole and close with part. Let that reversal of the opening and closing terms do structural work: it should enact the fact that endurantism and perdurantism share the same vocabulary—wholeness, partiality, presence across time—while assigning those terms opposite metaphysical roles.

The poem must take as its serious subject the problem of temporary intrinsics: the difficulty of explaining how a single persisting thing can be straight at one time and bent at another without contradiction. Stanza One should articulate the endurantist commitment to being wholly present at each moment, apparently bearing incompatible properties in succession. Stanza Two should articulate the perdurantist reply that strict presence belongs only to temporal parts, and that the enduring entity is a four-dimensional fusion of such parts rather than a three-dimensional object that travels through time. Stanza Three should attempt, and visibly fail, to synthesize the two pictures; let the formal repetition of Whole ... part in the final stanza register an unresolved tension rather than a reconciliation.

Do not invoke named philosophers, texts, or technical neologisms that depend on bibliographic knowledge—no references to specific authors or canonical formulations. The poem should be conceptually precise enough that a specialist in the metaphysics of time recognizes the temporary-intrinsics problem and the structural contrast between three-dimensional and four-dimensional accounts, yet it must stand as an independent poetic object, not as an annotated diagram. Because this is meant to close our seminar, leave the dispute open: the verse should hold the contradiction in suspension, not dissolve it into a verdict. Write with the formal discipline appropriate to a philosophical exercise rendered in verse.
