The gender-equality paradox — that societies with stronger gender-egalitarian institutions and norms often show larger gender differences in occupational and educational choices — has produced a long-running dispute about what, if anything, it reveals about nature, culture, and distributive justice. One camp treats the pattern as a release effect: once material and legal barriers diminish, sex-differentiated preferences emerge more clearly, suggesting that some segregation reflects authentic choice rather than oppression. Another camp contends that egalitarian contexts do not weaken gendered socialisation but amplify it through more subtle cultural signalling, so the paradox demonstrates that culture operates more powerfully when basic needs are secured. A third strand asks whether the observation carries any normative weight at all, arguing that even genuinely free choices yielding gender-imbalanced outcomes do not threaten justice unless one smuggles in a controversial prior that statistical representation across groups is itself a measure of fairness. Despite decades of empirical work — cross-national comparisons, within-country panels, and mediation analyses through personality and interest measures — these positions have scarcely shifted; each camp absorbs new data into its existing framework without altering its core claim. Is this stalemate a substantive disagreement about hidden causal mechanisms, a verbal dispute about what counts as choice or equality of opportunity, or are the three groups in fact addressing different questions — one descriptive about preference formation, one explanatory about institutional feedback, one normative about the legitimacy of group-differentiated outcomes — under a single heading?
