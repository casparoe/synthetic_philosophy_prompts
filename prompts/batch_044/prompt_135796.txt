I am currently working through a seminar on the philosophy of legal reasoning, and we have been debating whether common-law adjudication is best understood as a disguised form of rule-application or as something more cognitively fundamental. Recently I encountered Hofstadter and Sanders's Surfaces and Essences, in which they argue that analogy is not merely a heuristic but the very core of cognition, and that human concepts are structured as fluid analogical networks marked by slippage rather than as categories with sharp boundaries. This framing made me return to an institutional practice I had long taken for granted: the way appellate courts determine whether a prior case is analogous enough to guide or bind a present dispute.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of cognition and concept-formation does this practice actually presuppose? Specifically, must common-law analogical reasoning presuppose that legal categories—such as contract, tort, or reasonable care—have a family-resemblance structure lacking necessary and sufficient conditions, precisely because judicial reasoning proceeds by mapping deep and surface similarities rather than by subsuming facts under explicit rules? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined, equally compatible with a formalist view on which judges apply precise legal rules and invoke analogy merely as rhetorical cover? Moreover, when courts distinguish a precedent primarily because the analogical mapping breaks down at the level of surface features—rather than because the underlying legal principle genuinely differs—does that reveal an implicit commitment to a Hofstadter-style view that conceptual slippage is inevitable and that judgment operates at a subcognitive analogical level? And if we replaced analogical precedent with a purely probabilistic or utility-based decision procedure—one that calculated the expected social value of each ruling without reference to past cases—what essential aspect of legal coherence or justice would have to be abandoned?

Please answer in American English. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology and theory of concepts embedded in this practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate whether the practice could survive a shift to a fully non-analogical framework without losing something central to the law.
