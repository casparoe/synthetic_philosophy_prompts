I am preparing a defence for a paper I am presenting next Thursday in a graduate seminar on value theory, having spent the winter drafting a novel about a conservator who restores ruined frescoes and finds herself unable to justify her labour in economic terms. The thesis I intend to defend is that G. E. Moore's treatment of beauty and friendship as intrinsic goods—bound together by what he terms organic unity—is not the naive intuitionism his detractors suppose, but a structural claim about non-aggregative valuation that can survive both hedonist reduction and the institutional constructivism now prevalent in aesthetics. Specifically, I wish to argue that for Moore the value of a friendship or a beautiful object does not reside in either party alone, nor in any summed pleasure, but in an irreducible relational fundament that I have been calling "affective fundamentality": a normative analogue to metaphysical grounding in which the relation itself bears intrinsic worth, rather than being derived from the properties of the relata.

My audience will be a seminar room of analytic aestheticians and critical theorists who treat "intrinsic value" as a discredited remnant of bourgeois aesthetics, read Moore through the lens of later emotivism, and are primed to hear any defence of "useless" art as covert elitism. They will object in their own vocabulary—concerning power, institutional framing, and whether I am smuggling a metaphysics of presence through the back door. Prepare me for the objections they will actually raise, ordered by likelihood and damage. For each, show me how they will phrase it, which parts carry real force and ought to be conceded up front, what reply I can deliver within the time available, and precisely where that reply is weakest. Identify the single reply that must land if the proposal is to survive, and tell me the one objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks. Give me the bottom line first, then the reasoning, write in British English, and keep the entire response within 450 words.
