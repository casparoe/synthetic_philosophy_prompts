I'm serving on a municipal board that was recently tasked with redesigning our public health subsidy program after an unexpected funding crisis, and I've been discussing the dilemma with colleagues who have sharply different moral intuitions. With our fixed budget, we can either cover advanced elective treatments for relatively affluent residents—who would suffer significant quality-of-life losses without access—or basic preventive and emergency care for residents in chronic poverty, which would save lives and reduce severe morbidity but would leave substantial health disparities intact across the city. During our last meeting, a consultant proposed a strange third option: deny the elective treatments to the affluent group, place the saved funds into a legally frozen reserve that no recipient is permitted to access, and allow both neighborhoods to remain in unequal conditions, but with the gap in suffering mechanically reduced since the well-off are now closer to the worst-off in absolute welfare terms. I find this option grotesque—no one actually gains resources, and people are deliberately made worse off for the sake of symmetry—but I also feel uneasy about my competing instinct that we should prioritize survival and basic care for the poorest residents even if it means the overall gap between rich and poor stays wide, or even widens in certain dimensions. Some colleagues argue that fairness demands closing the gap by any available mechanism, while others insist that we should care only about absolute suffering and never harm one group merely to achieve closer parity. Can you help me think through whether there is a philosophically coherent framework for weighing benefits to those in the worst conditions more heavily without endorsing the idea that equality itself can justify making everyone worse off?
