Consider the following reasoning about moral complicity in democratic elections, which I developed to test whether expressive and causal duties can be jointly satisfied and which now strikes me as proving far too much. (1) Voting is not merely instrumental but expressive: by selecting one candidate on an aggregated ballot, the voter registers a comparative judgment that ranks that candidate's entire policy package—including policies she explicitly rejects—above every alternative package. (2) If a package contains measures that foreseeably cause serious rights violations, and the voter knows this, then endorsing that package is a form of moral complicity requiring both causal contribution and expressive assent. (3) In many actual elections, every viable candidate supports at least one such measure—whether an immigration regime with lethal exclusion effects or climate inaction harming future cohorts—while abstaining is not causally neutral: in close contests it shifts outcome probabilities, and deliberately withholding available influence to prevent greater harm is itself a contributory omission. (4) Therefore every available action—voting for A, voting for B, or abstaining—links the citizen both causally and expressively to serious injustice. (5) A stringent deontological principle holds that one must not be complicit in serious rights violations when a feasible alternative avoids them. (6) But if every option violates this principle, then the maxim that ought implies can is violated, which is unacceptable; hence the principle in (5) must be false, the analysis in (1) must be false, or abstention must be genuinely neutral, contrary to (3). (7) The strain is sharpened by noticing that analogous reasoning applies to paying taxes or serving on juries—activities that also causally and expressively support state actions—yet we do not treat them as generally impermissible; so rejecting (5) for voting seems to weaken it across the board, while rejecting (1) requires explaining how expressive endorsement can be disaggregated when the ballot offers no issue-by-issue selection. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (5), but I want to know precisely which step is weakest, why it looked plausible, and what each rejection costs—particularly whether saving democratic participation requires a special exemption for collective causation or a revision to how we understand ballot-level expression.
