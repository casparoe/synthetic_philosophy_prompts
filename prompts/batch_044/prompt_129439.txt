As a journalist covering the intersection of science and moral philosophy, I have been discussing with editors and researchers how institutions can distinguish a legitimate value preference from a hidden cognitive bias, and my own intuitive verdicts on concrete cases have settled into a pattern I cannot formalize without help. Here are my judgments. A university admissions officer who favors rural applicants because she herself grew up in a small town and feels an unexamined affinity for them: that is bias, even if the outcome improves geographic diversity. A climate researcher who declines to fly to international conferences not on carbon-accounting grounds but because she finds air travel aesthetically wasteful and spiritually flattening: that is a defensible preference about the life she wants to model, not a bias, even though it limits her professional reach. A criminal-court judge who consistently chooses restorative sentences over carceral ones because she holds that human dignity is inviolable and punishment is a moral error: that is a principled commitment, not a bias, despite its correlation with her religious formation. By contrast, a public-health official who rejects vaccine mandates because she finds the rhetoric of bodily autonomy poetically compelling and metaphysically resonant, irrespective of epidemiological modeling: that is a bias disguised as taste. An art curator who removes violent imagery from a public exhibition because she personally finds it viscerally distasteful: bias. But a curator who removes the exact same imagery because she judges that the institutional purpose of this particular gallery is to serve as a sanctuary for collective emotional repair, and violent imagery undermines that purpose: a preference legitimately anchored in role-defined values. The case I keep reversing on is an AI-safety reviewer who rejects predictive-policing algorithms not on the basis of documented disparate-impact studies but because she finds the very concept of algorithmic prediction metaphysically ugly and aesthetically alien to the kind of social world she wishes to inhabit. I want a principle precise enough to deliver all six settled verdicts, to explain whether this reviewer case is closer to the climate researcher or to the public-health official, and to identify honestly which of my judgments must be overturned if the principle is to remain coherent. Please take a committed position: state the principle definitively in your opening sentence, then defend it with reasoning that shows exactly which cases it saves and which it sacrifices, with no preamble and no restatement of the problem.
