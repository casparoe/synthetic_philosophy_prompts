I am preparing a seminar on intertemporal choice and institutional design, and I have been thinking about the structural parallels between individual-level self-binding and collective constitutional arrangements. On the one hand, there is the familiar logic of a Ulysses contract: an agent anticipates that future preferences will be distorted by temporary temptations, and therefore engineers present constraints—such as locking away resources or delegating authority—to prevent that future self from acting against its own enduring interests. On the other hand, there is constitutional precommitment, whereby a democratic polity entrenches fundamental rules precisely to insulate them from the fluctuating passions of future majorities, treating the collective will across time as something that must be protected from its own momentary deviations.

Recently, I encountered a hypothetical case in AI governance that seems to blur the boundary between these two levels. Imagine a research consortium that deploys a highly capable system governed by a hard-coded 'constitutional' layer: a set of non-negotiable ethical and operational constraints that neither the system's future iterations nor human overseers can revise, even if subsequent cost-benefit analyses strongly recommend modification. The designers justify this not merely as a technical safeguard, but as a deliberate act of precommitment on behalf of a future collective that includes both human stakeholders and the system itself. In your view, does this technological instantiation illuminate genuine similarities between Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment—particularly regarding who counts as the binding agent, whether the commitment is genuinely irreversible, and how we should evaluate the legitimacy of constraining preferences that do not yet exist? Or does it instead reveal a category mistake, since an artificial system lacks the continuous personal identity that makes individual self-binding coherent, thereby reducing the scenario to a problem of diachronic rationality applied to an entity that is not properly a 'self' at all?

Please compare the two frameworks with these considerations in mind. I am not looking for a historical survey, but for a conceptual analysis that takes seriously the possibility that collective and artificial agents might require modified versions of the standard arguments.
