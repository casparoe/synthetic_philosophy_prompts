I'm taking a seminar on collective responsibility, and I keep returning to a puzzling institutional pattern in how modern democratic states handle historical injustice. Consider the practice in which a present-day legislature or head of state delivers a formal, ceremonial apology for atrocities—forced assimilation programs, colonial extraction, or chattel slavery—committed generations earlier by regimes with different constitutional structures and legal personalities. These apologies are typically framed in the first-person plural, as if the same collective subject that perpetrated the harm is now acknowledging it, and they are defended as moral acts that only that enduring community can properly perform. Yet the very same resolutions often include explicit disclaimers that the statement creates no legal liability, establishes no precedent for reparations, and should not be read as admitting that the current state is the legal successor to the perpetrator regime in any material sense. The ritual thus seems to depend on collective identity for moral authority while denying institutional continuity for legal obligation.

What theory of collective agency, diachronic identity, and political obligation does this practice presuppose? Must it assume that groups possess a kind of metaphysical or narrative persistence that outlasts their legal and constitutional forms, or can a purely functional, constructivist account of "we" make the practice coherent without smuggling in a substantial group mind? Is the practice philosophically consistent, or does it quietly rely on one conception of the state when expressing regret and an incompatible conception when refusing liability? Furthermore, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it: could a government consistently apologize in this first-person mode while genuinely holding that only continuous legal persons bear obligations, and if not, what exactly in the rhetoric, ceremony, or institutional design of state apology would have to change?
