I've been chewing on Davidson's absent-minded driver case for a term-paper proposal (philosophy of action, ~5000 words, one semester) and I want to take it in a direction that isn't just another Davidson-vs-Hornsby close reading. You know the setup: the driver intends to hit the grocery store, takes his usual commute route out of habit, realizes he's going the wrong way, corrects — but apparently never formed the explicit belief 'I'm heading away from the store' or the explicit intention to turn back. The usual debate is whether reasons can cause actions without being represented, or whether there's always a hidden mental state doing the work.

Here's the weird angle I want to inject: treat the driver not as a skull-bound agent but as a distributed system where the car, road layout, and his motor routines are part of the cognition. The precise question is whether the absent-minded driver problem dissolves once we drop the skull-boundary for intentionality, or whether it just relocates — now we have to ask if the *system* has the missing belief, and whether that version is any easier to solve. Existing answers don't settle this because extended-mind folks usually talk about notebooks and smartphones, not about habitual routines that actively mislead the agent, while Davidson's own solution assumes a sharp explicit/implicit divide that distributed cognition was meant to blur. So there's a genuine gap, and I think it's tractable if I stay conceptual.

My proposed approach is to map the correction onto predictive-processing vocabulary — not doing actual neuroscience, just using the formal distinction between prior expectations and prediction errors to model what happens when the driver corrects course. Then ask whether Davidson's missing belief is best understood as an un-updated prior, an unregistered error, or neither. The obvious alternative is a straight conceptual analysis of Davidson against his critics, but that feels like a dead end; it's been done to death and doesn't touch the boundary question. I think predictive processing gives us new machinery without leaving philosophy, though I know I have to be careful not to just relabel things.

What I'd count as success: either (a) we can formalize the correction without positing an explicit intermediate belief, which supports Davidson's causal story but undermines the skull-bound framing, or (b) we can't, which means extending cognition actually makes the problem harder, not easier. Failure looks like realizing predictive processing is just a fancy redescription of Davidson's original point with extra jargon. Realistic scope for one semester: I can do the conceptual mapping, apply it to the driver case, and defend one of the two outcomes, but I can't run experiments or do serious cognitive science.
