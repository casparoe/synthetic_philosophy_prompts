I work in software, not philosophy, but after reading about Gödelian self-reference in formal systems and its use in GEB to explain consciousness, I tried to trace the analogy like a logic path. It seems to compile to an absurd result.

(1) Gödel's theorem requires that a formal system can encode statements about its own provability through arithmetic numbering.
(2) In GEB, Hofstadter proposes that a mind achieves self-awareness through an analogous "strange loop": a symbol system that represents the system itself.
(3) Therefore, the essential structural condition for self-awareness is just a representational loop in which the system's outputs refer back to its own internal organization.
(4) Modern software implements this precisely: a debugger can inspect its own process memory, and a language model can generate descriptions of its own weights and architecture.
(5) These software loops are structurally identical to the Gödelian loop that GEB claims produces the subjective "I."
(6) So if the analogy holds, any program with a recursive self-model—down to a small quine or a syntax highlighter—should be self-aware.
(7) That is clearly false; my text editor is not conscious.
(8) Hence one premise must be wrong. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (2) and (3) because the strange-loop intuition feels right, but I need to know what blocking (4) costs—does it require adding a biological or phenomenological condition that cannot be expressed in purely structural terms?
