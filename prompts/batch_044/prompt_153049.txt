I am facing a diagnosis that may eventually require an experimental gradual-replacement therapy, in which my biological neurons are swapped over several years for functionally identical synthetic analogues that preserve neural architecture but alter physical substrate. I recently encountered a speculative but rigorously formulated argument—largely absent from mainstream literature on mind uploading—that phenomenal continuity depends not on functional equivalence alone but on maintaining a critical mass of original substrate. Proponents of this view propose a precise “retention threshold” near 61.8 percent biological substrate, below which consciousness is said not to persist as a single continuous stream but to fracture into discrete successor episodes, each inheriting memories without being the original subject. I need to inhabit this ambiguity rather than merely analyze it. Write a short story about a patient who has reached 63 percent synthetic replacement and must decide whether to complete the transition; focus on the lived experiential tension of whether the self that began the procedure is still present, or has already yielded to a chain of successors, using the retention-threshold concept as the central philosophical problem rather than as decorative science-fiction premise.
