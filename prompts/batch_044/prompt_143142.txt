I have recently taken over stewardship of a family coastal property that climate projections suggest will be uninhabitable within eighty years, and I now serve on a small regional board weighing protective infrastructure against managed retreat. The board's decision will shape not merely property values but long-term migration patterns, which in turn affect which families form and which individuals come to exist in this region a century from now. Those practical stakes have made the nonidentity problem urgent for me: if our retreat policy results in lower welfare for the people living here in 2100, but those particular people would never have existed had we built seawalls instead, in what sense—if any—have we wronged them?

I am seeking funding for a six-month philosophical research project that asks a more specific, and I think unsettled, question. Existing discussions of the nonidentity problem tend to treat the paradigm case as an individual reproductive choice—whether a couple conceives now or waits—where the counterfactual dependency is direct and personal. But collective institutional choices, such as a resilience board's selection between infrastructure and retreat, operate through diffuse structural causation over generations. I want to examine whether this structural pathway weakens or transforms the counterfactual dependency that generates the problem: does the fact that hundreds of independent migration and partnership decisions intervene between the board's vote and any particular future person's existence mean that the standard reply—that no one is made worse off relative to their own non-existence—fails, or succeeds differently, in the collective case?

The project would be conceptual, not empirical. I do not need demographic modeling; I need a rigorous framework for distinguishing harm-based obligations, which seem to require identity preservation, from welfare-based obligations, which do not, in contexts where agency is distributed and causation is mediated by social structure. I want to know whether the standard conclusion that no harm occurs still applies when the choice is institutional rather than reproductive, and whether this distinction can yield a practical decision criterion for boards like mine—one that tells us whether we should evaluate policies by comparing welfare levels across possible worlds, or whether we must preserve specific lives.

A useful deliverable would be a concise analytical framework—perhaps a taxonomy of institutional choice-types mapped onto their counterfactual dependency structures—together with an argument for which evaluation metric is appropriate for each. Failure would be a result that collapses the institutional and reproductive distinctions without argument, or that leaves boards with no actionable difference between harming and failing to benefit.

The scope is deliberately narrow: one conceptual distinction, tested against two or three stylized policy scenarios, with no literature review by citation required since I will supply references myself. If someone asks why a foundation should fund philosophy on this, my answer is that the board's legal and moral justification currently rests on an unexamined assumption about whether adaptation policies harm future residents. Clarifying that assumption does not require new data; it requires conceptual precision that empirical research cannot provide, and that could reshape how resilience funding is justified to stakeholders and courts.
