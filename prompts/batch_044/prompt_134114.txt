I've been thinking about this since I started volunteering at a shelter and realized I'm always second-guessing whether I'm doing the right thing in the moment versus just building better routines. Anyway, here's an argument my roommate threw at me and I can't find the exit.

(1) If indirect normativity is right, then when following a rule directly is hard or unreliable, we should instead change our habits or environment so we follow it automatically.
(2) For any moral rule R, a fully rational agent can design an indirect stratagy that makes R-compliance happen without case-by-case deliberation.
(3) Once that stratagy is running, deliberately thinking about R in particular cases actually disrupts the automaticity and makes failure more likely.
(4) So indirect normativity gives us reason to stop directly deliberating about R altogether, and only to maintain the system.
(5) Now add idealized deliberation: what I ought to do is what I would choose after fully informed, unbiased reflection.
(6) My idealized self already has the perfect indirect systems in place, so in any particular situation she wouldn't deliberate about R — she'd just act via the system, or if it failed, fix the system later.
(7) Therefore, combining both views, there are never direct obligations to do particular acts; there are only obligations to maintain our indirect systems.
(8) But that means if I see a kid drowning, my only duty is to check whether my "rescue-children" habit needs tuning, not to jump in.
(9) That's clearly wrong, so one of (1)-(6) has to be false.

Which step slips? I definatly want to keep (1) and (5) if I can, since they both feel right in different ways. What's the cost of giving up whichever one I actually have to?
