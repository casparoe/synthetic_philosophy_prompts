I keep encountering a particular argumentative move when people try to explain how distinct items make up a single, unified thing. They begin by saying that item A and item B do not form a whole on their own, so they posit a third element, C, that binds or connects them. Once C is introduced, it is natural to ask how C itself is connected to A and to B; at that point, defenders usually change their description — C is no longer an external connector but is said to be internal to or constitutive of A and B, and therefore does not require its own binding mechanism. The same pattern appears whether the discussion concerns the parts of a sentence, the members of a collective, or the components of a system. Is there an established philosophical term for this specific sequence — the initial demand for an explicit binder followed by its quiet reconception as non-binding? If the pattern belongs to a recognised difficulty about how parts form wholes, which aspect does it correspond to, and how should it be distinguished from ordinary equivocation or from a simple change of topic? If there is no precise name for the move itself, could you propose one with clear boundary conditions, and say what would count as a genuine counter-example rather than another instance of the same shift?
