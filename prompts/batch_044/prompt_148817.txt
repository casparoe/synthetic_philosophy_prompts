I am preparing a diagnostic instrument for my advanced moral-philosophy seminar, where we are working through aggregation problems in distributive ethics. Rather than asking for a standard comparison of named theories, I need a decision tree—no more than six branching nodes—that sorts respondents into normative positions on the utility monster problem according to the concrete commitments they actually hold, not according to the labels they happen to use.

Each node should be phrased as a question or case judgment that an advanced undergraduate could answer without knowing in advance which leaf it produces. The tree should begin with the canonical hedonic version—where the monster derives enormous welfare from resources allocated to it—but it must also apply to a structural variant we discussed in class: the monster achieves a non-hedonic, systemic flourishing (for instance, an irreducibly complex aesthetic or epistemic order) that does not reduce to preference satisfaction or subjective well-being. For each leaf, please indicate whether accepting this structural variant forces a revision of that position or leaves its diagnosis intact.

The leaves must include at least: (1) unrestricted classical utilitarian aggregation; (2) a prioritarian or weighted-utilitarian view with diminishing marginal weight for the monster’s gains; (3) a rights-based or side-constraint position in the Nozickian family; (4) a contractualist or ex-ante-consent rejection of aggregation; (5) a strict egalitarian or lexical-priority view; and (6) one hybrid or institutional-separation position—such as a two-level view that permits aggregation within a bounded domain but protects individuals via a distributive floor. In addition, map one logically available combination of answers that appears unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether its emptiness is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Please flag any pair of leaves that share all diagnostic answers and therefore cannot be separated by this tree—especially if they differ only in metaphysical backing or theoretical motivation rather than in normative output. The instrument should not depend on bibliographic knowledge, specific textual citations, or familiarity with the secondary literature; it should function as a “which position do you actually hold?” quiz for someone who understands basic normative ethics but has not memorized the canonical debates. Finally, specify the audience and keep every node concrete enough that a respondent can answer it without already knowing the taxonomy’s structure.
