I have been working through an argument about self-knowledge for a philosophy seminar, and the conclusion strikes me as too destructive to be right, though I cannot locate the precise error. The argument combines two principles that both seem plausible: luminosity, according to which if a subject is actually in some mental condition, then she is in a position to know she is in that condition; and the KK principle, according to which if a subject knows p, then she knows that she knows p. Here is the chain as I have reconstructed it:

(1) If luminosity holds for being in pain, then anyone who is in pain is in a position to know she is in pain.
(2) By the KK principle, anyone who knows she is in pain knows that she knows she is in pain.
(3) So, if both principles hold, anyone in pain knows that she knows she is in pain, and by reiteration knows that she knows that she knows, generating an infinite ascent of nested knowledge states.
(4) Actual knowledge requires a finite mental architecture; sustaining an actual infinite hierarchy of known propositions exceeds any finite cognitive capacity.
(5) Therefore at least one of (1) or (2) must fail for pain, and by parallel reasoning for every mental state.
(6) If luminosity fails generally, then subjects can occupy mental states they are not in a position to know, which does not merely restrict first-person authority but seems to eliminate it altogether.

What makes this suspicious is that (6) functions like a reductio of self-knowledge itself rather than a measured boundary on it. Is there a hidden equivocation in the move from (1) to (3) between being in a position to know and actually knowing? Or does the KK principle apply differently—perhaps not at all—to first-person mental states? I want a committed answer: take a definite stand on which premise collapses, explain why that premise initially looked acceptable, and specify exactly what we must pay in terms of first-person authority if that rejection is correct. Do not merely survey the options; defend one diagnosis and price its cost precisely.
