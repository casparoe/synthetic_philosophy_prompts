I'm running a seminar on political theory for about 14 particpants, 80 min session, and we've been talking about Arendt's idea that lying in politics isn't just hiding facts but can destroy the shared reality that makes judgment possible at all. The students keep reducing it to "politicians lie" so I need an activity where they actually feel the difference between a regular lie about one fact and that bigger thing where the categories themselves get replaced and people can't talk to each other anymore.

Can you design a classroom game for this? I want it split into two rounds with different rules. First round: everyone gets the same data cards about a fictional crisis (say a water contamination in some made-up town) and has to decide whether to evacuate, treat, or wait. Then the facilitator sneaks one false evidence card into half the groups — classic deception within a shared world. They vote, we see who caught it.

Second round is where I want your niche twist. Don't just make it "two interpretations." I want Frame Cards that literally redefine the key terms — like one frame says "harm" only means immediate death, another says "the public" only means property owners — and these aren't up for debate, they're enforced by the rules. The groups have to try reaching a joint decision across frames, but they can't because the terms don't map. And I want hidden roles: two "Memory Keepers" in each group who still have the round-one data, but if they use the old vocabulary they lose credibilty points and the group can vote to silence them. The rest are "Image-Makers" who have to defend the new frame coherantly, even to themselves.

So I need: full rules and materails, timing for each round, how the credibility-point system works (no real money), the debrief that maps round 1 onto traditional lying and round 2 onto Arendt's organized image-making where the liar eventually lives inside the fiction, plus an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce — probably the full historical machinery of propaganda — and what will break the first time (like students trying to "translate" frames to be cooperative, or Memory Keepers going quiet). Make sure they hit the wall before anyone says Arendt's name, and don't include any request for citations or a reading list — just the runnable thing.
