You are an expert philosopher of consent and institutional design with experience running participatory seminars. Before outlining anything, briefly brainstorm three ways a classroom simulation could make students feel—rather than merely understand—that consent can fail structurally even when no individual lies or threatens. Then proceed.

Design a 90-minute, in-class simulation for an upper-level seminar of about 14 students. The topic is what makes consent valid, but do not frame it as standard deception or coercion. Instead, build the exercise around a niche structural failure you will call "recursive authorization decay": a participant authorizes step A with full information and competence; the rules then require them to authorize step B, where the risk profile of B depends on A's outcome in a way that was technically disclosed in the initial packet but never integrated into a usable mental model. Because the first authorization alters the cognitive resources available for the second—without any new falsehood or pressure—the sequence feels voluntary and informed at each point yet fails as a unified consent.

Requirements:
- Specify roles (e.g., authorizer, institutional recorder, observer), materials (cards or tokens representing authorization stages, a simple non-monetary payoff or reputation-point scheme), and rules that let students run the sequence themselves.
- Structure it so they commit to both steps before recognizing that the competence required for step B was undermined by step A's outcome, not by any hidden clause.
- Include a debrief that maps each simulation moment onto the standard components of valid consent (disclosure, voluntariness, competence) and shows why binary "yes/no" analyses miss the problem.
- State what will predictably go wrong on the first run—likely that students blame an individual "bad actor" rather than the architecture—and how to correct it.
- Be frank about which feature of recursive authorization decay the 90-minute format cannot reproduce (e.g., long-term erosion across months) and what you, as facilitator, must supply verbally.

Keep the activity self-contained: no bibliographies, named theories, or citation requests. The force of the idea must come from the rules and incentives, not from your post-hoc explanation.
