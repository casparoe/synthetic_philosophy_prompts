I am a high school student taking an independent seminar on philosophy of language, and during our unit on Donald Davidson’s theory of radical interpretation, two of my classmates—Maya and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement that neither our instructor nor I could fully resolve. I am writing to ask for a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange, since I want to understand not merely who “wins” overall but where each argument succeeds or fails, and whether both are missing something important.

Maya began by defending a strong reading of Davidson’s principle of charity: to interpret anyone at all, she argued, we must attribute to them a largely true and rational set of beliefs, because meaning and belief are holistically interdependent. She offered the case of an interlocutor who repeatedly asserts that snow is black. Rather than attributing a bizarre false belief, charity should lead us to reinterpret the speaker’s term “snow” or “black” so that the utterance comes out true. Leo objected that this makes genuine disagreement impossible—if charity always rescues the speaker’s beliefs by redefining terms, then we can never understand someone who actually holds false views. He insisted that charity concerns rational coherence in action and inference, not truth per se; we can therefore understand someone who is plainly wrong, provided their errors are localized and their behavior remains broadly rational. Maya then pressed the point with a more technical example: suppose a speaker consistently utters “two plus two equals five” yet behaves flawlessly when counting objects and distributing goods. Here, she said, charity should resolve the tension between speech and behavior by reinterpreting the speaker’s numerical terms or the operation signified, rather than attributing a false arithmetical belief. Leo countered that this mistakes the direction of interpretation: if the speaker’s practical behavior demonstrates mastery of standard arithmetic, then charity should align belief attribution with that behavior, allowing us to say they genuinely hold the false belief expressed in their words—perhaps as a slip, a joke, or an isolated error—without twisting the meaning of “plus.” Maya then asked whether Leo’s view can accommodate a deeper divergence: what if the speaker systematically rejects the law of excluded middle in every domain, not merely in isolated utterances? Leo replied that Davidson treats basic logic as constitutive of interpretation itself; therefore, if the divergence reaches the logical framework, we cannot interpret the speaker without altering the meaning of logical connectives like negation. Maya concluded that this actually concedes her central point—that charity forces reinterpretation at the foundational level—while Leo maintained that it only shows logical agreement is a precondition for interpretation, leaving empirical beliefs free to be false.

Please go through each move in their exchange—Maya’s initial strong charity claim, Leo’s disagreement objection, Maya’s arithmetic case, Leo’s behavior-based response, Maya’s law-of-excluded-middle challenge, and Leo’s constitutive-logic reply—and rate each as clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing, particularly regarding whether Davidson’s view allows for partial interpretation or graded failure of interpretation when divergence becomes too extreme. Finally, please state how confident you are in your adjudication and explicitly single out the point about which you are least certain.
