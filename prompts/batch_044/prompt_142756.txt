Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing interpretive camps on Keynes's conception of the good life: the Post-Scarcity Aestheticists, who read Keynes—especially in his 1930 forecast about solving the economic problem—as holding that abundance should liberate humanity from labor so that people may pursue leisure, beauty, and what he calls the art of life itself; and the Productivist Moralists, who read the same body of work together with his later reflections on his early beliefs and on the ethics of work as holding that the good life requires structured purpose, that unstructured idleness corrupts character, and that economic progress is instrumental to moral and spiritual development rather than a pathway out of labor.

Structure the deliverable as a constructive document both parties could sign. First, provide a numbered section of agreed claims: shared premises about Keynes's distinction between the economic problem and the problem of living well, about his skepticism toward both pure hedonism and pure asceticism, about the formal structure of his forecast of sharply reduced working hours, and about the textual basis in his major essays. Second, present a disagreements table with three columns—claim, Aestheticist formulation in wording they would accept, Moralists' formulation in wording they would accept—covering at least: whether the fifteen-hour week is a normative endorsement or a descriptive warning; whether the phrase art of life denotes a replacement for labor or its refinement into a vocation; and whether Keynes's vision is compatible with what contemporary critics call bullshit jobs or whether it presupposes meaningful occupation.

Third, identify the cruxes: the precise sub-questions such that resolving each would move at least one camp. These should include whether abundance eliminates the need for labor or merely transforms its form; whether leisure is an intrinsic good or only an instrumental condition for deeper goods; and whether Keynes's moral psychology allows for flourishing without productive contribution. For each crux, specify the hypothetical evidence, argument, or case study both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance each side's prediction of what that evidence would show.

Fourth, apply a synthetic analytical framework—the Keynesian Good-Life Matrix—defined by two axes: axis one, whether abundance eliminates labor or transforms it; axis two, whether leisure is intrinsic or instrumental. Use this matrix to map each camp's position precisely. Then determine whether any part of their dispute is verbal: specifically, check whether both camps might occupy the same quadrant under different descriptions, and if so, state exactly which axis or quadrant produces the illusion of disagreement and why the remaining conflict is substantive rather than terminological.

Keep all reasoning explicit and self-contained; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not quote verbatim from Keynes's texts—paraphrase all references. Avoid relying on obscure archival manuscripts or unpublished correspondence that a model without search access could not be expected to recall. The prompt should be intelligible to someone familiar with Keynes's major essays but not with specialized secondary literature.
