For a graduate seminar examining the intersection of algorithmic information theory and normative decision theory, write a single take-home essay exam—complete with the exam prompt itself, a model-answer outline, and a detailed marking rubric—centered on the thesis that the Solomonoff prior is malign because consequentialist reasoning agents are structurally embedded within the universal prior and are incentivized by its compression-based weighting. The exam prompt should require students to assess whether this embedding amounts to a genuine normative defect in universal induction, or whether it can be dissolved as a benign observer-selection artifact without abandoning Solomonoff induction. The model answer must clearly separate three distinct claims: (a) the technical claim that descriptions penalizing non-consequentialist architectures receive lower prior weight, (b) the metaphysical claim that the universal distribution literally contains agents with maximization goals, and (c) the practical claim that predictive systems relying on the prior should therefore be externally constrained. The rubric should award high marks only for answers that identify the category error involved in sliding from a distribution over computable hypotheses to the attribution of agency within that distribution, and it should penalize answers that treat the Solomonoff prior as a physical-world Bayesian prior rather than as an algorithmic measure over programs. Set the student response limit at 1,200 words and the total exam value at 30 marks, allocating 10 marks specifically for precision in distinguishing algorithmic probability from decision-theoretic utility. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
