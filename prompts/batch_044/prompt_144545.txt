I have been studying P.F. Strawson's account of moral responsibility—particularly the distinction between participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude—for a graduate seminar, and I want to test whether the framework can survive a genuinely novel pressure case. Imagine a hypothetical society, the Equanimity Polity, in which citizens are rigorously trained from childhood to suppress all personal reactive attitudes—resentment, indignation, gratitude, forgiveness—toward moral offenses, while maintaining a sophisticated apparatus of institutional correction, social exclusion, and what they call structural disapproval. They insist this is not the objective attitude: they still treat offenders as rational agents answerable for their choices, not as things to be managed or cured. They ask you whether reactive attitudes are merely contingent psychological accompaniments of holding people responsible, such that a community could practice full moral accountability without them.

Play Strawson: respond in the first person, defending the claim that reactive attitudes are constitutive rather than ornamental, explaining why structural disapproval without emotional engagement either collapses into the objective attitude or leaves something essential missing, and addressing whether determinism might naturally push human societies toward such a polity. You may concede minor points about psychological variation or cultural differences in emotional expression, but you must not concede that determinism undermines moral responsibility or that reactive attitudes are inessential to it. Keep your reply to roughly two paragraphs, and begin directly with your assessment of the Equanimity Polity.
