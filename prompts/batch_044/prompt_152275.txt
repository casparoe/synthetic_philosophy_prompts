In the salon where I sometimes read drafts aloud, two regulars—an art conservator named Mira and a philosopher of language named Soren—fell into a dispute about material constitution that I have not been able to settle in my own mind. I report it here as a bystander and do not signal which side I favor; I want a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded, naming who is right about each specific move, what both parties are missing, and whether the disagreement is genuinely ontological or merely a clash of classification schemes.

The argument began with the familiar statue and clay. Soren insisted that only one object is present, described either materially or functionally. He said that crushing the statue flat does not destroy a separate entity but merely alters the form of the single object that was always there, much as melting a snowman does not kill a distinct snowman-essence but only changes the shape of the snow.

Mira replied that this misses an asymmetry in persistence conditions. The clay survives reshaping; the statue does not. If they shared all modal properties, they would be identical, yet they plainly diverge. She allowed that they occupy the same space, but only because the clay constitutes the statue—two entities with different conditions of existence, not one entity with two names.

Soren pressed the colocation worry: if Mira is right, then touching the statue in the gallery is touching two things at once, an ontological extravagance that violates parsimony. Mira countered that constitutional colocation is not extravagant but standard—like a body constituting a person—because the entities are not independent but related by a dependence of form on matter.

Then Soren introduced a case he had invented for a story: a museum installation of an ice figure kept precisely at its melting point, with automated molds collecting the runoff and refreezing it into the identical form every hour. Over a year, every original water molecule has been replaced, yet the shape, title, and curatorial record remain continuous. He asked whether this is still the original sculpture.

Mira said yes—the intentional, institutional maintenance of form preserves the artifactual object, even though the material substrate has turned over entirely. The original ice is long gone, but the constituted statue survives, just as a restored marble figure survives the replacement of its stone.

Soren seized on this. He argued that Mira had now conceded that material continuity is not necessary for the statue's survival, which undermines her original reason for distinguishing statue from clay. In the crushing case she appealed to material survival; in the ice case she appeals to formal continuity maintained by process. But if form-continuity is sufficient, then in the original clay case there is no need to posit a second object—only one material object that either instantiates the statue-form or does not.

Mira refined her position. In the crushing case there is abrupt discontinuity of form without any sustaining process; the material survives in a new configuration, and the artifactual kind is lost. In the ice installation there is continuous causal and intentional linkage—the automated system functions as an artificial analog to biological maintenance—so the constituted object never lapses. The distinction is not about material versus form alone, but about whether the conditions for the artifactual kind's persistence are continuously satisfied.

Soren responded that this smuggles normative and intentional conditions into ontology. The clay's survival is determined by physics alone; the statue's survival, on Mira's account, depends on curators, algorithms, and institutional records. That proves "statue" is an artifact-kind applied to a physical object, not a separate entity with its own physical properties. At any moment in the ice installation, he said, there are not two objects—there is one physical system of water molecules arranged in a shape, and we apply the artifactual concept "sculpture" to it when the sustaining conditions hold.

Mira's final move was modal. Even if the statue is an artifact-kind, it grounds real differences in counterfactual behavior. If the power fails, the ice melts: the physical water survives in liquid form, but the sculpture-object is destroyed or suspended. The divergence in what can happen to them—without any change in the underlying physical particles—reveals that they have different essential properties and therefore are not identical.

Neither would yield. I want the verdict broken down round by round: in each exchange, whose point holds and where it weakens; whether Soren is correct that Mira's ice-case concession collapses her distinction, or whether her refinement about sustaining processes saves it; whether the colocation problem is fatal or dissolved by constitutional dependence; and what, if anything, both are missing—perhaps a middle path that treats artifactual kinds as real but not as spatio-temporally coincident objects with the matter that instantiates them. Be precise about which commitments each side has tacitly adopted, and say whether their disagreement is ultimately about ontology or about how to apply our descriptive schemes.
