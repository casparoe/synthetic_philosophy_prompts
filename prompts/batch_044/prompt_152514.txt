I'm serving on the ethics board for a regional emergency-response cooperative, and we are finalizing a dispatch protocol for simultaneous crises. The proposed rule—call it the Redirection Protocol—would authorize sending a single ambulance away from a nearby, isolated patient toward a distant mass-casualty event, with the explicit understanding that the chosen route crosses a structurally fragile bridge adjacent to the isolated patient's residence. The vehicle's passage will almost certainly compromise that bridge, severing the patient's only alternative route to care and substantially delaying treatment. Defenders of the protocol invoke a distinction between intended effects and foreseen side effects: the bridge damage is not the purpose of the dispatch, they argue, nor is it the causal mechanism by which the distant victims are saved—the ambulance reaches them regardless of the bridge's condition—while critics contend that this is merely a geographic variant of a lever-pulling dilemma, where the harm is instrumentally embedded in the path of rescue. I am also troubled by an apparent tension with our cooperative's own negligence framework, which holds responders strictly accountable for foreseeable physical consequences of their routing decisions regardless of subjective intent; if we license the Redirection Protocol on the grounds that harmful outcomes matter less when unintended, we seem to be adopting a theory of moral responsibility that our liability rules explicitly reject. Can the protocol be defended in a philosophically consistent manner, or does it smuggle in an asymmetry between action and omission that collapses once the physical structure of the choice is made explicit? And does the fact that the harm arises from the very movement that constitutes the rescue—rather than from a separable, secondary act—place this case closer to one moral category than to another?
