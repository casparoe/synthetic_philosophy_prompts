As a novelist presently drafting a narrative in which two scientists fall out over the ethics of modelling, I recently overheard a disagreement that I should like to see adjudicated with philosophical rigour. The exchange took place between two guests at a dinner—an epidemiological modeller and a philosopher of science—neither of whom is known to me personally, and I report their argument here without taking sides.

The modeller began by defending the standard SIR compartmental model, despite its assumption that a population is perfectly well-mixed with uniform susceptibility and contact rates. She noted that real social life is nothing like this—contact is clustered by age, geography, and class—yet during the initial waves of 2020 the model predicted infection peaks with surprising accuracy. Citing what she called Friedman’s F-twist—the idea that the realism of a model’s assumptions is irrelevant provided its predictions hold—she argued that the homogeneous-mixing assumption should be judged solely by its predictive fruit, not by its descriptive fidelity.

The philosopher replied that Friedman wrote about economics, where the aim is typically predictive accuracy for aggregate variables. In epidemiology, however, models are used not merely to forecast curves but to identify causal levers for intervention—whether to close schools, prioritise vaccination by age, or restrict indoor gatherings. If the model assumes uniform mixing, it misrepresents the very channels through which policy operates. Thus an unrealistic assumption is not merely descriptively false; it is structurally misleading. The F-twist, he said, applies only when prediction is the sole purpose, not when explanation or mechanism identification is required.

The modeller countered that even for policy design, one needs only predictive accuracy at the margin. If one wishes to assess school closures, one can calibrate the homogeneous model with a reduced transmission parameter for children. There is no need for a full network architecture. The unrealistic assumption is a computational shortcut, not a conceptual error, and its predictive success validates its use.

The philosopher responded that calibrating a false model to match past data is precisely the epistemic danger: it conflates correlation with mechanism. The reduced parameter may capture the aggregate effect of closures, but it cannot distinguish whether they worked by reducing child-to-adult transmission or by altering general risk perception. For a novel pathogen with a different age-specific profile, the calibrated homogeneous model could fail catastrophically, because it never represented the actual transmission architecture. Relying on predictive accuracy in one context is therefore reckless.

The modeller then observed that all models are false; the question is only which falsehood is tractable. A full agent-based network model is also false—edges are probabilistic, individuals adapt behaviour, and measurement error abounds. If we reject every unrealistic assumption, we reject scientific modelling altogether. The F-twist is merely the recognition that science proceeds through strategic falsehood.

The philosopher conceded that idealisation is unavoidable, but insisted on a distinction between benign and malignant simplification. Benign omissions—ignoring air resistance in a falling-body problem—do not affect the variables one intends to manipulate. Malignant omissions—ignoring friction in a brake-system model—remove precisely the structural features that policymakers need to control. Homogeneous mixing, he argued, belongs to the latter category: it is not like neglecting air resistance; it is like neglecting friction when designing brakes.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves above—(1) the modeller’s initial defence of the SIR model via predictive accuracy and the F-twist; (2) the philosopher’s purpose-relative objection; (3) the modeller’s calibration defence; (4) the philosopher’s epistemic-risk and mechanism-distinction reply; and (5) the modeller’s universal-falsehood claim followed by the philosopher’s benign-versus-malignant distinction—rate the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what important point, if any, both parties are missing.
