As the engineering representative serving on Vertex Systems’ Governance Board, I must circulate a decision memo by Thursday regarding whether we should adopt the proposed Commit-Graph Surplus Ledger (CGSL) for Q3 performance bonuses. I have no formal background in philosophy, but the board’s debate has begun to invoke concerns about exploitation and alienation, and I want to address them rigorously rather than dismissively. The CGSL calculates each developer’s “extractable contribution surplus” by weighting committed lines of code according to temporal proximity to deployment deadlines; maintenance labor—refactoring, documentation, mentoring—is classified as “baseline labor obligation” and excluded from the surplus calculation. Proponents argue that this eliminates subjective managerial bias and rewards high-impact output. Critics, led by our senior staff engineer, contend that the metric replicates exploitation by appropriating value the engineer produces beyond what salary returns, and that it creates not only separation from the finished product but also what they term “procedural alienation”: a separation from any meaningful control over how one’s labor is quantified and valued. They urge outright rejection and a transition to peer-reviewed portfolio assessment within sixty days.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It must recommend a concrete course of action—approve the CGSL, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions—and include an explicit implementation or transition timeline. The memo should treat the critics’ position seriously: explain why the exploitation and procedural-alienation concerns are substantive rather than rhetorical, engage the strongest version of their argument (including the claim that excluding maintenance labor systematically undervalues reproductive work necessary to the codebase), and then show either why your recommendation still stands despite that strength or why the critics’ proposed remedy is insufficient or impractical. Write in a polished, accessible tone suitable for board members who are engineers, managers, and legal counsel, not academic philosophers. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; ground all reasoning in the mechanics of the CGSL and in the concrete working conditions it would create.
