I've been thinking about a specific pattern that shows up whenever people discuss the aggregation of small harms—the familiar territory where one torture is apparently worse than 3^^^3 people each getting a dust speck in the eye, and debates about whether that intuition survives formalization. But I'm after something more granular. I keep noticing a consistent asymmetry in how people treat aggregation depending on whether the distributed harm is phenomenally transparent to its bearer.

Consider two scenarios. In the first, each of ten billion people experiences a barely-noticeable moment of confusion once per year—not enough to register as a complaint, but collectively representing an enormous loss of clear-headedness. In the second, the same number of people each experience a barely-noticeable physical irritation of equivalent subjective intensity, distributed in the same way. In discussions I've seen, people are far more willing to aggregate the physical irritations into a serious moral concern than the epistemic confusions, even when the total welfare impact is stipulated to be identical and even when the epistemic harm is described as persistent and cumulative.

This isn't just scope insensitivity, because the resistance to aggregation runs in opposite directions for the two harm-types, and it isn't the standard torture-versus-specks framing, because both scenarios involve only sub-threshold distributed harms with no severe focal victim. Is there an established name for this domain-sensitive resistance to aggregation—specifically, the tendency to treat phenomenally opaque or epistemic micro-harms as non-aggregable in a way that transparent or somatic micro-harms are not? If not, can it be defined precisely, with clear boundary conditions distinguishing it from ordinary scope neglect, from person-affecting restrictions, and from the non-identity problem? I'd appreciate an answer in American English, and I'm not looking for a bibliography—just the conceptual carving: what the phenomenon is, what it borders on, and where its edges fail.
