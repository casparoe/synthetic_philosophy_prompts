I'm working on a paper for my history and philosophy of science seminar, and I'm stuck on a conceptual tension in Johannes Kepler's astrological writings that standard surveys treat too quickly. The usual story is that Kepler retained astrology for practical and financial reasons but stripped away superstition by insisting that celestial influence works mechanically—through geometric angles (aspects) and resonant harmonic ratios—rather than through occult qualities or hidden sympathies. What I can't see clearly is how he reconciles that mechanistic, aspect-only framework with the traditional apparatus of planetary dignities: domicile, exaltation, detriment, and fall, which assign discrete qualitative strengths to planets based on their specific zodiacal signs. If influence is genuinely continuous and geometric, then a planet's "strength" in, say, Leo versus Aquarius should reduce to the changing angles it makes with other bodies, not to an intrinsic qualitative property of being in domicile or fall. Yet Kepler continues to cast and interpret natal charts whose judgments depend precisely on those discrete zodiacal categories. So my question is: what is the conceptual bridge—if there is one—between his rejection of qualitative, sign-based dignities in favor of pure geometry and his continued reliance on birth-chart interpretation that presupposes them? Does he implicitly reconceive dignity as a statistical regularity of favorable geometric configurations, or does this remain an unreconciled contradiction in his system? Please explain the conceptual issue in detail, without appealing to modern astrological practice or asking me to consult specific editions of his texts.
