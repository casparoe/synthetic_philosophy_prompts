I facilitate a philosophy-of-mind seminar for twelve graduate students, ninety minutes, with no external readings assigned in advance. Design a classroom activity that makes participants feel the force of the problem of introspective unreliability—specifically, the gap between what they take themselves to be reasoning and the actual causal origin of their inferences—before naming concepts such as confabulation, heterophenomenology, or introspective transparency. The setup should place students in pairs working through a structured argument-mapping exercise in which each partner must articulate the explicit premises leading to a shared conclusion; unbeknownst to them, the facilitator has seeded both partners with subtly different analogical frames (presented as neutral background examples) that deterministically steer each partner toward contradictory intermediate steps, which they then experience as spontaneously generated insights. Rules should govern turn-taking, the prohibition of external notes, and a scoring mechanism (non-monetary, e.g., cumulative confidence tokens) that rewards coherent articulation but penalizes appeals to outside authority. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between access to mental content and access to mental origin, identifies what will predictably go wrong on the first run (e.g., participants attributing discrepancy to partner error rather than self-misreporting), and states frankly which feature of introspective failure—namely, unconscious framing by prior semantic priming—cannot be fully reproduced without deceptive facilitation and therefore must be supplied conceptually rather than experienced directly.
