You are an expert philosophy educator with a background in classical epistemology and pedagogical design. Before you lay out the plan, take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual dependencies and failure modes for this specific audience, then proceed step-by-step.

I am running a single 75-minute session for a reading group of intellectually curious non-specialists—lawyers, engineers, and writers with no formal philosophy coursework—on Plato’s Meno problem: why is knowledge better than mere true belief? I want them to leave understanding not just the familiar guidance answer, but also why that answer often feels unsatisfying and what it commits us to about the value of understanding itself.

Plan the session’s explanatory arc, addressing each point below in order.

1. Dependency structure and sequencing. What idea must be firmly in place before the Meno problem can even be posed as a genuine puzzle rather than an obvious “knowledge is more useful” platitude? In what exact sequence should belief, truth, justification, understanding, and action-guidance be introduced so that each layer depends on the previous one without collapsing into it? Specifically, how do you establish the distinction between “getting it right by luck” and “getting it right in a way that explains your being right” before you ask why the latter matters?

2. The carrying example. Choose one concrete, ordinary-life scenario—not the road to Larissa, not the slave-boy geometry demonstration, not a lottery case, and not a fake-barn variant—to carry the entire distinction across the session. Specify exactly what features the scenario must have (e.g., the true belief must seem genuinely reasonable from the inside, there must be no obvious appeal to authority that renders the belief unjustified) and what it must lack. Explain why this particular example works for adults who will instinctively defend “if you’re right, you’re right” and where in the arc it should be introduced.

3. Misconceptions in sequence. Predict the misconceptions or objections they will form, in the approximate order they will arise. I am especially concerned that they will conflate “knowledge is better because it is more reliable” with “knowledge is just true belief plus evidence” (the JTB fallacy), and that they will reduce Plato’s answer to “knowledge guides action” without grasping what kind of guidance requires understanding rather than luck. For each misconception, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and explain how the example from point 2 is used to defuse it.

4. The niche conceptual twist. Near the close, I want to introduce a non-obvious framing: the value of knowledge over true belief may lie partly in retrospective epistemic credit—we hold agents accountable for what they understand, not merely for what they happen to get right—rather than solely in future-oriented action-guidance. At what point in the arc should this twist appear so that it deepens rather than derails the basic distinction? How should it be connected back to the carrying example?

5. Check for understanding. Design one question I can ask at the end that distinguishes a participant who has grasped the dependency structure (why true belief is not enough, why understanding matters for credit) from one who has only memorized that “knowledge is justified true belief.”

6. What to leave out. Explicitly state what standard material should be omitted at this level—such as specific Gettier-style counterexamples, historical debates about Platonic recollection, or contemporary virtue-epistemology frameworks—and confirm openly that these are being set aside. Do not ask for readings, advance preparation, bibliographies, or citations.
