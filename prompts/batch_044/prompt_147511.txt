Plutarch’s Parallel Lives is conventionally read as comparative exemplification: pairing Greek and Roman figures to isolate portable virtues. Medieval and early modern hagiography extends this structure but introduces what I call normative anachronism — the deliberate, non-deceptive projection of present ethical categories onto past lives so that the subject becomes available for contemporary moral formation rather than remaining historically other. This is not distortion in the ordinary sense but a hermeneutic method: the saint or hero is compressed into an atemporal normative scaffold. The danger is that biography collapses into disguised autobiography, and the reader learns only what she already values.
