In a discussion about the moral foundations of meritocracy, two participants—call them A and B—became deadlocked over whether desert is necessarily backward-looking. A argued that to deserve a reward, one's past actions or cultivated capacities must have causally produced the valued outcome, so that rewarding raw innate talent is unjust unless that talent was developed through responsible effort. B replied that this makes desert parasitic on a prior, contested theory of moral responsibility, and proposed instead a "structural desert" account: people deserve rewards in proportion to the social value their actual capacities generate, regardless of origin, because desert's proper function is not retrospective moral accounting but the alignment of incentives with collective needs. A objected that B's view collapses meritocracy into utilitarianism and erases the moral distinction between earned and unearned advantage. B countered that even A's backward-looking view cannot isolate truly "earned" contribution, since effort itself is shaped by unchosen genetic temperament, upbringing, and luck; therefore A's view also relies on an arbitrary cutoff and merely disguises it as moral purity. A then modified her position, suggesting that desert should track "responsible effort"—effort not deterministically forced by factors outside the agent's control—but conceded she could not specify a non-arbitrary boundary for what counts as "outside control" without either excusing everything or excusing nothing. B claimed this instability proves that any workable meritocracy must abandon pure backward-looking desert for a hybrid that weights social contribution alongside procedural fairness, while A insisted that without some backward-looking anchor the concept of desert loses its normative bite entirely. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate the strength of A's initial claim, B's structural-desert counter, A's charge of utilitarian collapse, B's argument about the unearned nature of effort, and A's "responsible effort" modification—labeling each as clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing, particularly regarding how institutional design might resolve or dissolve their disagreement without settling the metaphysics of responsibility.
