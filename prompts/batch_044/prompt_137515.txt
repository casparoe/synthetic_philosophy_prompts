I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for final-year philosophy students on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgement. Rather than explaining the doctrine itself, I need a teaching plan: the exact order in which the conceptual components must be introduced so that dependencies are respected. In particular, the causal closure of the physical should be secured before the inertness of phenomenal properties is introduced, and the paradox — that an epiphenomenalist seems able to report precisely those causally inefficacious states — should be constructed in stages rather than presented as a single contradiction. For the central example, I want something other than the familiar zombie or pain-to-behaviour link; suggest a concrete scenario, perhaps involving a silent internal alarm that registers danger without triggering any physical response, and specify what features it must have — genuine phenomenal character, complete causal isolation — and what it must lack — any behavioural or neural consequence — if it is to carry the idea without smuggling in interactionism. I also need a misconception map: students will likely conflate epiphenomenalism with eliminativism about consciousness, or suppose that the paradox dissolves if we say reports are merely unreliable; tell me at which point in the sequence each confusion should be intercepted, and why. Provide one precise diagnostic question for the end of the session that reveals whether they have understood the structural tension — the self-stultifying nature of the report — rather than simply repeating that consciousness is causally useless. Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level, such as detailed interactionist physics or contemporary neuroscientific objections, and present the whole plan in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold formatting, or tables, as though composing a letter.
