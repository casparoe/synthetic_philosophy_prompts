I've recently been discussing Simone Weil's critique of political parties—particularly her claim that they suppress individual moral judgment and replace reasoning with collective passion and social loyalty—with a colleague who studies deliberative democracy, and we kept returning to whether institutional design can actually prevent this psychology from emerging. For a fiction-writing exercise, I'd like a short story that tests the idea in an unfamiliar, realistic setting rather than a familiar modern democracy.

Please write a restrained, realistic story—under about eight hundred words—set in a small post-disaster settlement that has deliberately abolished formal parties and governs through sortition and deliberative assemblies with strict term limits and no party labels. The protagonist should be a reluctant assembly member who initially trusts these safeguards to prevent groupthink, but gradually realizes that informal loyalty networks are reproducing exactly the dynamics Weil described: members voting against private conviction to preserve cohesion, treating dissent as moral betrayal, and substituting factional allegiance for independent reasoning. Center the narrative on one concrete decision—perhaps the allocation of scarce medicine or the punishment of a rule-breaker—where her judgment diverges sharply from the network's demand. Include the precise moment she recognizes that the absence of formal parties hasn't eliminated the pathology, and conclude with her choice to comply or break ranks, leaving the outcome unresolved. Avoid modern political references, named parties, or historical analogues, and keep the tone grounded rather than dystopian so the psychological mechanism remains the focus.
