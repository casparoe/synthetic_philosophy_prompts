I'm trying to pin down a move I keep seeing in arguments about who gets to speak, and I dont know whether theres already a name for it or whether Im just over-analysing a weird pattern. Here are two instances that feel like the same thing: a climate scientist is dismissed for talking about energy policy because shes "only a scientist", but then an economist with no climate background is cited approvingly on the exact same policy question. Or a historian of medicine is told to stick to archives when she comments on current health ethics, yet a philosopher with no medical training is welcomed into the discussion because his view matches the audience.

The thing is, its not just "stay in your lane" applied consistently — its selective. The expert's credibility in field A is used as a reason to exclude them from field B, but an outsider from field C is imported into B without the same scrutiny, as long as they agree. So its not a general rule about expertise boundaries; its more like a quarantine that locks people inside their own credibility, but only when its inconvenient.

I know Miranda Fricker's work on epistemic injustice and the testimonial credibility deficit, but this feels different: the speaker isnt being downgraded within their own domain, theyre being barred from leaving it, and outsiders are brought in asymmetrically. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration? How does it differ from testimonial injustice and from hermeneutical injustice? If theres no established term, what should we call it — epistemic siloing, maybe? I want the carving, not just a label: what exactly the phenomenon is, where it stops being this and starts being something else, and what the boundary cases look like.
