I am trying to work out whether it is ever intellectually legitimate to speak of a “national temperament,” and I came across the following summary in an unpublished lecture transcript that was circulating in my department. The speaker argued: “The concept of national character is not only coherent but indispensable for comparative sociology. When cross-cultural psychologists find that, say, South Korean samples average substantially higher on long-term orientation measures than Italian samples, they are measuring a real collective property—call it distributive temperament—rather than merely aggregating individuals. The standard objection that within-nation variation exceeds between-nation variation is mathematically true but conceptually irrelevant, because nobody claims that every Korean is patient; they claim only that the social environment in Korea selects for and reinforces patience in ways that produce a stable macro-pattern. Furthermore, twin studies of migrants show that these orientation scores remain closer to the homeland mean than to the host-country mean across generations, which means the pattern is largely inherited rather than institutional. Therefore, critics who invoke culture, education, or politics to explain these gaps are committing a category error: national character is essentially a biological population parameter, and treating it as a social construct misses the point entirely. Any policy that ignores this underlying distributive reality is therefore doomed to fail.”

Something about this sounds authoritative, yet I suspect it compresses several distinct issues into a single sweeping conclusion. Is the move from survey aggregates to a “real collective property” philosophically sound, or does it smuggle in an ecological fallacy that the speaker never addresses? Does the rebuttal to the within-group-variation objection actually answer the critics’ deeper concerns about practical stereotyping and predictive uselessness at the individual level, or does it change the subject? And does the migrant twin evidence genuinely support the strong claim that these differences are “largely inherited,” or is the inference to biological determinism an overreading that ignores gene-environment interaction and selection effects? Most importantly, does the conclusion—that culture and institutions are basically irrelevant—actually follow from the statistical premises, or does it depend on silently ruling out interaction effects that the speaker never mentions?

Please audit this summary claim by claim. For each step, tell me what a careful philosopher of social science or a defender of cross-cultural psychology would accept, what they would correct or qualify, and whether the speaker’s final policy conclusion survives once those corrections are made. I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of authors—just a conceptual evaluation precise enough that I can use it to structure my own argument. I would also appreciate it if you could say whether the distinction between “distributive temperament” and what we might call “normative character” (shared expectations enforced by social sanctions) is something the literature actually employs, or whether it is a rhetorical framing invented to make the statistical case look stronger than it is.
