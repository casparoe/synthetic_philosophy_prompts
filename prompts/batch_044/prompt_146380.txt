Having retired after a long career in molecular biology and only recently begun to study philosophy with any seriousness, I have become persuaded by Richard Rorty's antirepresentationalism and neopragmatism, in particular by the idea that the value of a scientific framework lies not in its mirroring an independent reality but in its capacity to help us cope with practical problems. I now sit on a regional advisory board that must repeatedly adjudicate between competing explanatory models, and I wish to replace our tacit representationalist habits with an explicit, reusable decision procedure grounded in Rorty's view. Please write this procedure for me in continuous prose, entirely without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write a formal letter. The procedure should be one that a board without philosophical training could actually run: it must list the questions to ask in fixed order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify the precise point where it yields a definite recommendation and the precise point where it must stop and hand the matter to human judgment because the dispute concerns vocabulary choice or values rather than fact. Apply it to two concrete proposals we are currently weighing—whether to endorse a reductionist gene-expression model or an emergent systems-biology framework for predicting ecosystem collapse in a specific watershed—working through both in detail, including the case where the procedure recommends the framework that will feel intuitively wrong to classically trained scientists. Be explicit about where the procedure misfires or risks collapsing into arbitrary preference. Finally, take a definite, committed position, with reasons, on whether Rorty's neopragmatism can genuinely constrain institutional scientific decision-making without dissolving into relativism; do not merely lay out options for me to choose between.
