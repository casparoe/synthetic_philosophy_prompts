Rather than rehearse the familiar Chase and Sanborn coffee-taster intuition pump, I want to examine whether the quining strategy generates parallel conceptual tensions in cases that combine ostensible phenomenal inversion with what we might call 'acquaintance framing error.'

Give an example of a conceptual issue arising in the following kind of scenario: over several years, a subject exhibits a documented reversal in discriminatory behavior and affective response within a specific sensory modality—reliably re-sorting stimuli she previously grouped together, reporting opposite emotional valences, and adjusting her practical judgments—yet she insists, upon sustained introspective examination, that her qualitative experiences have not changed at all, attributing the shift solely to altered 'standards of evaluation' or a changed 'comparative frame.' The conceptual issue is whether this denial should be interpreted as (i) a successful quining of an illusory phenomenal posit, (ii) an introspective failure masked by conceptual conservatism, or (iii) an equivocation between token qualitative states and their functional or type-level roles.

Please construct a concrete, non-obvious instance—do not merely adapt the Maxwell House case, and do not invoke bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Your example should isolate the precise point at which the quining maneuver conflicts with residual appeals to first-person authority. In your response, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view—such as the outright incoherence of qualia or the infallibility of introspective report—as the consensus.
