I've been mulling over Dennett's idea that the self is just a centre of narrative gravitiy, and I've got some gut verdicts on weird edge cases that don't line up with the usual examples. Here they are:

There's an AI that churns out a continuous autobiographical stream, but every 'memory' is generated from noise with no actual past behind it. I feel there's no real self there, just a story-engine.

Then there's a person with severe amnesia who can't lay down new memories but still insists 'I'm still me' and keeps a stable personality. I think they definitely still count as a self.

There's this hive-colony idea where thousands of people share one continuous narrative thread with no private inner monologue at all. I reckon there's one self, not thousands.

A human whose brain gets gradually replaced by synethetic parts that preserve narrative continuity but slowly shift personality over decades. At the end I think it's the same self, just altered.

A fictional character like Sherlock Holmes, written with such psychological richness that readers discuss what he would do. Despite having a narrative centre, I don't think Holmes actually has a self.

Then there's someone who deliberately fabricates a false past and fully believes it, overwriting their real history. I feel like they've either got a different self now, or maybe two overlapping ones? I'm genuinely unsure.

Is there one principle that gets all of these right? And what does it say about the case I'm going back and forth on — a digital upload that copies every memory and narrative pattern exactly, but the original body stays alive and keeps narrating its own life? One self or two?

Also, does my list even hang together, or do I need to drop one of these verdicts to make it coherent?
