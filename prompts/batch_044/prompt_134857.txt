After a career in biochemistry, I have spent my retirement exploring moral philosophy, and I find myself drawn to problems that invite quantitative structuring. In conversation with friends about distributive justice, I recently encountered prioritarianism together with the leveling-down objection: the charge that giving moral priority to the worse-off licenses harming the better-off even when nobody benefits. Rather than accepting the standard linear framing of well-being aggregation, I wish to argue that prioritarianism survives this objection if we reconstruct its priority weights through a saturating, threshold-sensitive function—something like the dose-response curves familiar from pharmacology—where the marginal moral weight of a well-being unit declines sharply above a subsistence threshold but remains steep below it. Specifically, please argue that under such a non-linear weighting scheme, reducing the welfare of those far above saturation yields negligible moral value that can never justify the corresponding loss, so that the leveling-down move is not merely undesirable but structurally unavailable.

Please treat this not as a loose analogy but as a claim about the functional form of moral weighting itself: show whether this reconstruction genuinely dissolves the objection or merely relocates it. I am particularly interested in whether the comparative logic underlying the objection—namely, that equality of low welfare is better than unequal high welfare—persists even after monotonic transformation of the utility scale. If my framing rests on a confused premise, for instance if prioritarianism is necessarily committed to linear aggregation or if the objection targets a comparative structure immune to such functional reshaping, please say so plainly rather than complying politely.
