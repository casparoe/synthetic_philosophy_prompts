I have been trying to choose a laptop for my work, and I find myself in a disagreement with a close colleague about whether I am being rational. At the outset I defined strict, non-negotiable requirements: the machine must run a specific statistical package without latency, weigh less than 1.5 kilograms, offer at least ten hours of battery life, and cost no more than £1,100. Within a few days I located a model that satisfies every one of these conditions precisely. My colleague insists that, because the search has already produced an option that meets all my explicitly stated needs, any further browsing is an irrational expenditure of time and attention; he urges me to purchase it at once, noting that prolonged comparison risks both mental fatigue and the unit being sold to someone else. I feel a persistent resistance to this counsel, not out of mere indecision, but because each review I read introduces a feature I had not originally considered—such as a particular keyboard mechanism or a magnesium alloy chassis—which subtly raises the internal benchmark I regard as adequate. Consequently, I worry that my initial criteria were arbitrarily drawn, and that accepting the present option might leave me with a lingering sense of suboptimal choice, yet I also recognise that if I allow these revisions to continue, my standards might inflate without limit and the available laptop could disappear. Is my colleague's argument correct, and if it is flawed, precisely where does it go wrong? More specifically, is there a coherent framework for rational decision-making that justifies deliberately halting a search even when one's own evaluation of what counts as good enough proves unstable, or does such instability necessarily undermine any claim to have genuinely satisfied one's preferences? I am especially interested in whether there is a principled distinction between legitimate refinement of one's criteria and a self-defeating form of perfectionism that a rational agent ought to resist.
