I serve as the philosopher-ethicist on a clinical ethics committee at a tertiary-care hospital, and we convene twice monthly for 45-minute reviews of cases in which a patient's prior documented preferences conflict with their current expressed preferences following profound cognitive alteration—typically advanced dementia, severe traumatic brain injury, or prolonged dissociative states. The committee includes the attending physician, a chaplain, a legal advisor, and a patient advocate; my deliverable must be a recommendation signed off by the physician and legal advisor, not an open-ended philosophical opinion. I want to operationalize the Buddhist principle of anattā—specifically, the denial of a permanent, unitary, metaphysically substantial self underlying mental continuity—into a reusable decision procedure for these reviews. The procedure must not smuggle in a Western essentialist or narrative-identity model of the person, yet it must yield actionable guidance for clinicians who are not Buddhists.

Write a step-by-step procedure the committee can actually run in sequence, with each step stating: the precise question to ask; what counts as a sufficient factual answer versus what requires a normative judgment; whether the step terminates with a procedural recommendation or hands the decision to human sign-off because the issue is one of values rather than metaphysics; and the specific misfire the step is designed to catch (for example, conflating continuity of consciousness with continuity of autobiographical narrative). The procedure must explicitly distinguish three analytical axes: (1) present suffering versus anticipated suffering for a putative "future self"; (2) internal psychological continuity versus socially recognized relational identity; and (3) the authority of a past mental state over a present mental state when no enduring substrate connects them.

Apply the procedure to three cases:
1. A patient with advanced Alzheimer's who previously executed a directive refusing artificial nutrition, but who now actively seeks food, makes eye contact during feeding, and shows no signs of distress.
2. A patient with severe frontal-lobe injury who has lost all autobiographical memory but retains emotional recognition of family members; the family demands aggressive life-sustaining treatment on the grounds that "this is not who he really is," invoking a core self that survives the injury.
3. A patient with dissociative identity disorder in which one alter authored an advance directive refusing certain interventions, while other alters currently reject that directive and request them; no single state has continuous access to the memories or preferences of the others.

Include at least one case in which the procedure yields a recommendation that runs directly against standard clinical intuition—such as recommending that a clear prior directive not be honored. Then state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle, particularly where Buddhist metaphysical commitments about impermanence and non-self are genuinely incommensurable with Western bioethical frameworks centered on autonomy and personal identity over time.

In your response, separate what is philosophically settled about anattā—such as the rejection of an ātman or permanent soul—from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of Buddhist philosophy and personal identity theory, including debates over whether anattā entails a reductionist, narrative, or relational account of conventional selfhood. Do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.
