At a seminar on moral psychology, two colleagues—Leah and Marcus—debated whether Strawson’s framework of reactive attitudes can illuminate institutional restorative justice. Leah argued that reactive attitudes such as resentment and indignation are essentially dyadic and personal: they require one party to hold another to an expectation of goodwill in direct interaction. In formal restorative circles, she said, facilitators deliberately suppress spontaneous reactive expression and replace it with structured dialogue aimed at reintegration; this is precisely Strawson’s objective attitude in practice—treating the offender as a case to be managed, even if kindly. Marcus objected that Strawson never confined reactive attitudes to private pairs; collective indignation is well documented, and restorative practices explicitly preserve moral demands rather than suspend them—victims are encouraged to voice hurt, offenders are expected to acknowledge guilt, and the community reaffirms membership rather than adopting clinical detachment. Leah replied that channeling emotion through protocol is not the same as feeling it: the institutional framing transforms resentment into a procedural instrument, which makes it an objective stance disguised as participation. Marcus countered that the objective attitude is defined by withdrawal from the moral community—seeing someone as broken, childlike, or outside the realm of responsibility—not merely by the presence of rules; since restorative justice insists on accountability, the reactive structure remains intact. They then turned to a harder case: an offender acted under a severe but then-unknown cognitive bias that undermined rational control, yet the bias was partly maintained by social conditions the offender had previously endorsed. Leah said learning this should shift the community toward the objective attitude, because explanation dissolves the basis for demand. Marcus said Strawson’s view is not about metaphysical freedom but about whether we continue to hold expectations; even with full explanation, we might maintain reactive demands if the offender remains a competent participant in moral life, so the shift is not automatic.
