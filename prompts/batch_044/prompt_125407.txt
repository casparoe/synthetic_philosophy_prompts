I recently joined a remote team at a multinational firm that operates across jurisdictions with sharply different labor norms. In our French and Belgian offices, the thirty-five-hour week is legally enforced and a “right to disconnect” is codified; in our Tokyo office, colleagues routinely work past midnight and the culture treats death-by-overwork as a regrettable but honorable cost of dedication; and in our U.S. headquarters there is no legal cap on hours, yet the company offers “unlimited vacation” while quietly tracking response-time metrics that reward those who never log off. Meanwhile, our onboarding materials frame sustained labor as a moral virtue—language that unmistakably echoes the Protestant work ethic—while our wellness initiatives treat rest as a therapeutic recovery tool rather than as a legitimate claim that can be pressed against work demands.

What implicit theory of the moral and political status of labor and leisure does this institutional patchwork presuppose? Does it presuppose that work has intrinsic value against which leisure must justify itself, or that leisure is a distributive good comparable to health care? Is the practice consistent with the view that individuals hold a positive right to leisure—that is, an entitlement not merely to be left alone, but to have social structures actively protect non-work time—or does it presuppose that leisure is only instrumentally valuable for sustaining further labor? And how should we understand the coexistence of legally enforced work limits, fatal overwork cultures, and meritocratic reward systems: do they share a single underlying conception of who owes what to whom, or do they represent incompatible philosophical commitments that the institution manages to hold simultaneously? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and tell me what a genuinely leisure-rights-based institutional design would have to change about how contribution is measured and rest is allocated.
