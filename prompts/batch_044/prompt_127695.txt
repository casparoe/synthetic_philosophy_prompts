Here is a recurring configuration I have noticed in analogical arguments—both in everyday reasoning and in theoretical modeling—and I do not know whether it carries an established name.

Consider cases where an analogy is drawn between a well-structured source domain and a more ambiguous target domain. The reasoner correctly maps the explicit relational structure—entities, relations, and causal links—but also imports what we might call the “resilience profile” of the source: properties about which parts of the source are hard to alter, which are protected by background rules, and which are “load-bearing” for the structure’s stability. In the target domain, these robustness properties have no clear counterpart, yet they are treated as if they had been mapped too.

For instance, in a debate about institutional reform, someone analogizes a university to a corporation and then treats tenure as structurally protected—not because there is an independent argument that tenure serves a load-bearing function in universities, but because shareholder protections are load-bearing in corporations, and the analogy has smuggled in that modal robustness. In another case, a moral philosopher analogizes ethical reasoning to legal interpretation and begins treating certain ethical “precedents” as binding in ways that mirror stare decisis, not because ethical reasoning possesses an institutional mechanism for binding precedent, but because the source domain’s resistance to overturning prior cases is imported as a structural feature of the target.

The result is not simple overextension—no new entities or relations are invented—but rather a kind of “modal drag,” where the target inherits constraints on changeability that come solely from the architecture of the source.
