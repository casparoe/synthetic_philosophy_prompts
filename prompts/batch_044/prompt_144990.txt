I am a journalist who writes about philosophy, neuroscience, and the politics of knowledge, and I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop titled Agency After Desert: Neuroscience, Law, and the Limits of Blame, to be held in a university auditorium before an audience divided between moral philosophers, cognitive scientists, legal scholars, and several guests who are not specialists. The organizers have asked that my remarks do more than set a pleasant tone: they want the philosophical framing to constrain what follows, not merely decorate it. I would like you to draft an approximately eight-minute speech that takes Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — the claim that true moral responsibility is impossible because no agent can be the ultimate cause of her own mental nature — and makes it do real structural work. The speech should begin by applying the argument not only to individual agents but to the very assembly in the room: none of us chose the cognitive architectures, disciplinary training, funding structures, or institutional invitations that brought us together. From that premise, it should move toward the workshop's practical concerns — how neuroscience and law construct practices of accountability that do not depend on ultimate desert — without ever retreating into false reassurance about free will, self-creation, or personal authorship.

Please ground the abstraction for the non-specialists with one brief, concrete illustration — for instance, a researcher whose commitment to studying decision-making was shaped by unchosen childhood circumstances — and ensure that the speech is accessible to guests who may lack philosophical training, while remaining rigorous enough that philosophers in the audience will recognize that the regress of reasons has not been softened. The remarks should close with an invitation rather than a conclusion, framing the day's papers and discussions as collective attempts to design responsibility practices that acknowledge our shared lack of ultimate authorship. At the end of your response, please append a brief reflective note — no more than one short paragraph — identifying where your speech takes liberties with Strawson's actual position or depends on a contested reading (for example, extending the basic argument from individual to collective or institutional agency), and explicitly state what argument or empirical finding would cause you to revise the framing, such as a compelling defense of agent-causal libertarianism or robust evidence for a form of neuroplastic self-authorship that breaks the causal regress. Write in a formal register appropriate for an opening address, and do not include any bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
