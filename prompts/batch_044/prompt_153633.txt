I would like your help adjudicating a disagreement that arose in my study group concerning Nozick’s tracking theory of knowledge and its well-known tension with epistemic closure.

The first participant argued that Nozick’s conditions—sensitivity and adherence—generate a genuine closure failure: I can track the belief that I have hands, since in nearby worlds where I lack hands I would not hold that belief; yet I fail to track the belief that I am not a brain in a vat, because in nearby BIV-worlds I would still falsely believe that I am not one. From this she concluded that unrestricted closure must be abandoned, but she proposed saving a restricted version she termed “domain-indexed closure.” On her view, deductive transmission of knowledge is legitimate only when both the premise and the conclusion are evaluated against the same modal neighborhood—namely, the set of worlds that are relevant to the specific belief-forming method in use. Thus, within a perceptual domain, I may derive ordinary empirical conclusions, but I may not use those same premises to reach skeptical hypotheses that lie outside the method’s responsive range.

The second participant rejected this as an ad hoc relabeling of the failure. He presented a counter-case: suppose I acquire the belief that a museum is open through reliable testimony. By domain-indexed closure, I should apparently be able to derive that the museum is not a holographic illusion, since illusion-worlds lie far outside the modal neighborhood of testimony-based belief. But he claimed that this misrepresents Nozick’s ontology: modal proximity is not determined by the epistemic “domain” of a method, but by objective similarity among worlds. Consequently, closure is either unrestricted and false, or arbitrarily restricted to save appearances.

She responded by distinguishing objective modal geography from epistemic accessibility. The point of tracking, she said, is not to map metaphysical distance but to register whether a belief-forming process is responsive to the fact in question. If a process—say, visual perception—is not responsive to BIV scenarios, because they do not alter sensory input, then those scenarios should be excluded from the tracking evaluation of conclusions reached within that process. This, she maintained, preserves closure without arbitrariness, because the restriction is fixed by the internal structure of the method rather than by post hoc convenience.

He then introduced what he called an “ascription universality” objection. If knowledge attributions depend on which modal neighborhoods are selected for evaluation, and different agents or contexts might select different neighborhoods, then there is no universal fact about whether closure holds in a given case. He argued that Nozick’s anti-skeptical project implicitly requires universal ascription—there must be one correct set of nearby worlds for each belief—otherwise tracking becomes context-relative and loses its capacity to block skeptical arguments across the board.

She countered that universal ascription is unnecessary. Tracking is always evaluated relative to a specified method and context, and closure should be assessed within that same specification. The disagreement ended with neither side conceding.

Can you go through their exchange point by point? Specifically: (1) Is the closure failure on Nozick’s account a genuine theoretical defect, or is it a feature that correctly distinguishes ordinary knowledge from skeptical hypotheses? (2) Does “domain-indexed closure” solve the problem in a principled way, or is it indeed ad hoc? (3) Is the second participant’s “ascription universality” objection a serious internal difficulty for tracking theories, or does it misunderstand the role of method-relativity? And finally, (4) what—if anything—are both disputants missing about how sensitivity and adherence interact when beliefs are formed not through direct perception but through complex inferential chains? Please do not offer a bibliography or reading list; I am looking for conceptual adjudication of the arguments as actually presented.
