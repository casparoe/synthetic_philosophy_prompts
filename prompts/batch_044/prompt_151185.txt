Here is an argument I constructed for a graduate seminar on comparative value, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to make rational choice impossible without requiring arbitrary precision.

(1) If outcome A is not worse than B, and B is not worse than C, then A is not worse than C. (Transitivity of the "not worse than" relation across outcomes.)

(2) Define a spectrum of worlds W0, W1, ..., W1000, where W0 contains exactly ten people living lives of extraordinary achievement with moderate suffering, and each Wi+1 is identical to Wi except that one life of extraordinary achievement is replaced by one hundred lives that are barely worth living, with the suffering distributed so that no individual in Wi+1 suffers more than anyone in Wi.

(3) For each adjacent pair Wi and Wi+1, Wi+1 is not worse than Wi: the total well-being is greater, the suffering is not increased for any particular person, and the loss of one exceptional life is offset by the addition of many minimally good lives.

(4) Therefore, by repeated application of (1), W1000 is not worse than W0.

(5) But W0 and W1000 differ radically: W0 contains profound achievement and moderate suffering among few, while W1000 contains no profound achievement and minimal suffering spread among thousands. They are not tied; they are incommensurable along distinct dimensions of value.

(6) So (4) is false: W1000 is worse than W0, or at least not comparable in the "not worse" ordering.

(7) The failure of (4) means (1) must fail somewhere in the chain: "not worse than" is not transitive.

(8) But if "not worse than" is intransitive, then "better than" is also intransitive. A rational agent could then be led from preferring W0 to accepting W1 (no worse), W1 to W2, ..., W999 to W1000, and then back to preferring W0 over W1000, paying a cost at each transition, ending with W0 but depleted — a classic money-pump.

(9) Therefore, either rational agency is structurally vulnerable to exploitation, or we must deny (3) and claim that at some point in the chain, replacing an excellent life with barely-good lives makes the world strictly worse.

(10) But denying (3) requires identifying a precise cutoff where the replacement becomes worse, despite the differences between adjacent worlds being imperceptible and structurally identical. This seems to commit us to an arbitrary threshold or to a form of value elitism that privileges exceptional lives regardless of aggregate well-being.

I want to keep (3) if possible, because giving up the idea that more minimally good lives can offset one excellent life feels like a rejection of aggregation altogether. But I also want to avoid (8)'s conclusion that rational choice is impossible. Where exactly does the reasoning slip — in the move from (1) to (4), in the inference to (7), or in the money-pump construction in (8) — and what does each repair cost? If I have to choose, I would rather preserve the coherence of rational agency than preserve unrestricted aggregation, but I want to know precisely what I am paying for that choice.
