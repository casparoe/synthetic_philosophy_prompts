People in social philosophy and evolutionary ethics often compare the emergence of social norms to biological evolution: norms mutate, spread through populations, face selection pressure from institutional environments, and settle into stable equilibria. David Lewis's analysis of convention as a coordination equilibrium — where agents prefer mutual conformity and face alternative possible regularities — is frequently recruited as the formal backbone for this evolutionary picture. How far does the analogy actually hold? Give me the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of genetic material, phenotypic expression, selective environment, reproductive success, and evolutionary drift? Then identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for Lewis's framework: does the evolutionary analogy smuggle in teleology, group-level intentionality, or functional optimization that a purely game-theoretic account of convention rejects? Separate the disanalogies that threaten the evolutionary narrative from those that are merely cosmetic. Finally, assess whether the analogy survives once we distinguish between norms that solve pure coordination problems (where Lewis's model applies cleanly) and norms that emerge from distributional conflict or power asymmetry (where mutual preference for conformity fails). Does this distinction show that convention theory can explain only a subset of normative evolution, or can the evolutionary framing be repaired by supplementing Lewis's framework? Reach a verdict and defend it; if the evolutionary metaphor collapses for the distributional case, say what framework or analogy should replace it there.
