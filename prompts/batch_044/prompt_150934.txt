I've been wrestling with this argument about work, leisure, and overwork and it feels like it eats its own tail. Here it is, with steps numbered so I can see where I'm slipping:

(1) Weber's Protestant ethic treats labor as a calling with intrinsic value, not just as a means to survive.

(2) If labor has intrinsic value, then reducing it without cause is a kind of moral waste—like leaving a good book half-read for no reason.

(3) The 35-hour week was fought for as a social right to leisure, not just as recovery time so you can work harder on monday.

(4) Karoshi—death from overwork—is just the extreme case of treating labor as an unconditional calling: you keep going because stopping feels like a failure of duty.

(5) So if karoshi is genuinely wrong and not just sad, then the duty to work can't be unconditional; fatigue and life outside work must generate real counter reasons.

(6) But if those counter reasons are strong enough to justify a hard 35-hour limit, then leisure isn't just a recovery tool, it's a competing right.

(7) That means work's value is conditional on not destroying the worker, which collapses the Protestant idea that work is good in itself regardless of outcome.

(8) So either karoshi is morally okay (just inefficient), or the Protestant ethic is wrong and leisure is a real right—but then we lose the idea that work has unconditional intrinsic value.

This seems to prove that you can't consistently think overwork is wrong AND think work is intrinsically valuable AND think leisure is a right. Which step is the weak link? I really want to keep (3) and (5)—that leisure is a real social right and that killing yourself with work is wrong—without giving up that work can matter for its own sake. Is there a hidden equivocation in (2) or (7), or do I just have to bite a bullet?
