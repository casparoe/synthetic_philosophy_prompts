During a discussion in my philosophy of practical reason course, two classmates—Jin and Sera—got locked in an argument about whether the internal-reasons thesis can accommodate what Jin called "manufactured motivation." Jin began by describing a modified version of Williams's gin-and-petrol case: imagine an agent who initially has no desire whatsoever to avoid a toxic substance, but a precisely targeted neuro-intervention installs in her a stable, reflective aversion to it. Jin argued that, on a strict internalist view, the agent now has an internal reason to avoid the toxin, because the reason is relative to her actual motivational set, regardless of how that set was causally produced. Sera responded that this makes the distinction between internal and external reasons collapse, since any purportedly external reason could be converted into an internal one by sufficient technological manipulation, and therefore the internalist owes an account of which motivational changes are reason-giving versus reason-distorting. Jin conceded that the causal origin matters for psychological explanation but insisted that normative force comes only from present motivation, so the genesis of a desire is normatively irrelevant. Sera then introduced a "reversibility constraint": she claimed that for a post-intervention motivation to generate genuine internal reasons, the agent must be able to endorse it from a standpoint that survives hypothetical reversal to her pre-intervention state—not because that prior state is metaphysically authoritative, but because without this continuity condition we cannot distinguish rational transformation from arbitrary imposition. Jin accused her of sneaking in an external criterion of personal identity, essentially requiring that reasons be anchored to a "true self" that exists independently of actual desires. Sera denied this, saying the constraint is procedural rather than metaphysical: it checks whether the new motivation can be integrated into a coherent practical identity rather than functioning as an alien implant. They asked me to referee, and I genuinely could not tell whether Sera's reversibility condition saves internalism or silently transforms it into a hybrid view. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess Jin's opening case, Sera's collapse objection, Jin's concession on causation, Sera's reversibility constraint, Jin's charge of hidden externalism, and Sera's procedural defense. Who is right about which specific claim, what are both missing about the relation between the genesis of a desire and its normative authority, and does the reversibility test ultimately belong to an internalist or externalist framework?
