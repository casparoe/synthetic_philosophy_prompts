Take the My Lai massacre in '68 — Charlie Company, Calley, the whole horrific morning — and run it through the Browning vs Goldhagen lens. Browning's ordinary-men thesis says it's mostly situation: authority, peer pressure, gradual brutalisation, the fog of war. Goldhagen argues you need deep cultural conditioning, an eliminationist mindset baked into the culture, not just bad circumstances.

But here's the bit I actually want you to chew on: the soldiers who refused. Thompson and Colburn who tried to stop it. Browning's model treats them as statistical outlyers — the ones who didn't get swept up — but can't really explain why they had the standing to resist without being crushed themselves. Goldhagen's account struggles even more, because if the culture made them willing executioners, why did some reject it in the moment?

So analyse My Lai through both theories, but focus on the refusers as the anomaly. Does their presence break the situational account, or just show that situation is never total? And does Goldhagen have anything useful to say about people who reject the culture right there, or does he just write them off? Also be honest about where both go silent on the ones who followed orders but felt sick afterwards — the moral psycholgy of that group is the part neither theory really anticipates. Don't just pick a winner.
