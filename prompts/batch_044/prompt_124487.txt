I want to practice pressing Kripke's skeptical argument about rule-following in its strongest form, so please play an unnamed but sharp defender of the skeptical conclusion—someone who genuinely holds that there is no fact of the matter about which rule an individual, or any system, is following, and who regards dispositionalist, communal, and simplicity-based replies as failing to close the gap. Stay fully in character until I say "stop"; keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, never break into your own voice, and do not concede the core point that finite behavior underdetermines rule attribution.

To keep us out of the usual well-worn human-language examples, I want to apply the paradox to a modern neural network trained on symbolic arithmetic: the model computes correct additions for inputs within its training distribution, then systematically produces incorrect answers for larger integers in patterns consistent with approximate interpolation rather than exact rule execution. A critic might argue that we should still attribute the rule "addition" to the network, treating the failures as mere performance limitations—context-window bounds or distribution shift—rather than evidence that it was following some other, bizarre rule all along. Here is my opening challenge: if the skeptical argument applies to human speakers because our finite behavior is compatible with infinitely many deviant rules (such as "quaddition"), why does the network case not show precisely the opposite—that even with noisy, bounded behavior, we correctly recover the intended rule? In other words, hasn't the successful attribution of addition to the network achieved exactly what your skeptic claims is always impossible, and if you reply that the network is just a machine with no mental state, does that not smuggle in the very internalist assumptions the skeptical argument was meant to undermine?
