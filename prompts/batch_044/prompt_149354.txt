I have just come out of a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about Russell's theory of descriptions, and I should like a clear-headed explanation of the conceptual knot we tied ourselves in. We were not quarrelling over the familiar quantificational analysis — that a sentence such as 'the present King of France is bald' asserts existence and uniqueness and is false because the uniqueness condition fails. Our disagreement turned on what happens when such descriptions are embedded inside reports of belief or other propositional attitudes.

Consider the report: 'Sarah sincerely believes that the present King of France is wise.' Russell's primary and secondary occurrence distinction gives two readings. On the wide-scope (primary occurrence) reading, there is a false proposition — involving an actual, existing King — which Sarah believes. On the narrow-scope (secondary occurrence) reading, the belief relation connects Sarah to some actual person whom she mistakenly takes to satisfy the description, and the uniqueness clause is part of what she believes.

My friend insisted that this binary overlooks a coherent intermediate position. He suggested that in attributing a belief we might be relating Sarah neither to a Russellian proposition nor directly to an object, but rather to a mental representation that itself contains the descriptive content as an unanalysed constituent. On this view, Sarah's belief is genuinely about nothing, yet the report is true not because she entertains a false proposition, but because her cognitive architecture tokenises a description that lacks a referent. I objected that this sounds suspiciously like smuggling in a Fregean sense without Russell's ontological austerity, but I could not articulate precisely why Russell's framework must reject it — or whether it can reject it without begging the question.

Please explain the conceptual issue at stake: does Russell's distinction between primary and secondary occurrence genuinely exhaust the logical forms available for belief reports containing empty descriptions, or is there room for the intermediate position my friend described, in which the description functions as an internal representational vehicle rather than as either a wide-scope quantifier or a direct object? In particular, clarify whether adopting that intermediate stance forces one to abandon Russell's rejection of non-existent objects, and whether the distinction between 'believing a proposition' and 'believing under a description' can be sustained within a strictly Russellian ontology. I should be grateful if you would also indicate how confident you are in your answer and identify specifically the point about which you are least certain.
