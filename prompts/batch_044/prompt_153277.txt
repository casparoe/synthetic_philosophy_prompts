You are grading a graduate examination in metaphysics. The exam question reads:

Consider a layered ontology in which grounding relations are divided into vertical relations (between distinct ontological layers, e.g., chemical facts grounded in physical ones) and horizontal relations (within a single layer, e.g., one physical fact grounded in another). A proposed view holds that modal facts—facts about possibility and necessity—are horizontally grounded in actual physical properties, but not vertically grounded in them, and that this preserves the full fundamentality of the physical layer. Evaluate this view. Specifically, address (i) whether locating modality within the physical layer via horizontal grounding avoids threatening physical fundamentality; (ii) whether transitivity of grounding applies uniformly across vertical and horizontal relations, and what that implies for chains that include both; and (iii) whether the distinction can be maintained without circularity if modal properties also appear to constrain which physical properties are instantiated.

Below is one student’s answer.

The layered ontology usefully distinguishes vertical grounding, which connects different ontological levels, from horizontal grounding, which connects facts within one level. If modal facts are horizontally grounded in actual physical properties, then they belong to the physical layer itself rather than to a separate modal layer. Because nothing outside the physical layer vertically grounds its members, the physical layer remains fully fundamental.

This arrangement avoids circularity because grounding is irreflexive: no fact can ground itself, which entails that physical properties cannot depend on the modal facts they ground. Transitivity also applies across both kinds of relation. So if physical state P horizontally grounds modal fact M, and M horizontally grounds further modal fact N, then P grounds N. This shows that modality traces back to physics through well-ordered horizontal chains.

One might worry that if possibilities constrain actualities—for example, if the possibility of decay explains why a particle decays—then modality exerts downward influence on physics. But downward influence is by definition vertical, running from higher to lower layers. Since modal and physical facts are in the same layer here, the influence is merely horizontal and therefore does not undermine fundamentality.

Finally, because horizontal grounding operates entirely within actualities, the view avoids commitment to possible worlds as real entities. The modal is simply a pattern among actual physical properties. Hence the layer distinction succeeds, physicalism is preserved, and modality receives a complete ground within the actual world.
