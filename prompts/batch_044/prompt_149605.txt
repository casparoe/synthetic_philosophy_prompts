I hold that lexical threshold negative utilitarianism can be reconstructed without fully abandoning the commensurability of pleasure and pain, by adopting what I call a bipartite value topology: suffering and well-being occupy disjoint ordinal classes below a saturation threshold, so that no quantity of pleasure can offset suffering within that domain; once suffering exceeds the threshold, however, it becomes saturated and can be weighed against pleasure through a non-linear, lexically-constrained transformation that preserves asymptotic priority for suffering reduction. On this view, incommensurability is not solved by denying comparison altogether but by partitioning the value space into non-intersecting regions with a transition rule. What would have to be true for this reconstruction to fail? Please distinguish the decisive objections—whether from the phenomenology of mixed affective states, from formal results showing that non-Archimedean utility representations collapse into standard lexicographic orderings, from the charge that the saturation point is an ad hoc boundary vulnerable to line-drawing paradoxes, or from rival positions such as prioritarianism that accommodate lexical intuition without topological partitioning—from those that are merely awkward adjustments. Include an honest assessment of whether the view risks unfalsifiability if the saturation point is permitted to shift with context or framing, and indicate which of these defeaters a committed defender of the bipartite reconstruction would be forced to concede rather than dismiss.
