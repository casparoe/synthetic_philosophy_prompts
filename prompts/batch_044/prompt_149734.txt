I've been trying to work out for myself when saying that two things both caused a historical outcome is actually saying something important, versus when it's just listing things that happened beforehand. I came up with a rule I call the removal test: if you can take one factor away and the outcome still happens, that factor was only a background condition; if removing it breaks the chain, it was a real cause. But my gut verdicts on actual cases don't line up with it, and I'm not sure whether to fix the rule or overturn some of my calls.

Here are the verdicts:

- The outbreak of World War I in 1914: the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand and the rigid alliance system. I think the alliance system was the real cause; the assassination was just the spark, since any other crisis could have triggered the war. By my removal test, the assassination should be a mere condition.
- The fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989: Soviet economic stagnation and Gorbachev's refusal to send in tanks. Here I think Gorbachev's decision was the real cause, and the economic weakness was just the background pressure. So my test makes the stagnation the condition.
- The extinction of the passenger pigeon: commercial overhunting and widespread deforestation. I think both were fully real causes, not conditions, even though either one might eventually have wiped out the species alone. So my test fails badly here — it would call both conditions, but I want to keep both as causes.
- The success of the Montgomery bus boycott: Rosa Parks's refusal to move and the NAACP's existing organizational network. I don't think these are competing explanations at all; Parks supplied the specific trigger and the NAACP supplied the sustained structure. They feel layered, not like backups for each other.
- The failure of the Kellogg-Briand Pact: the absence of any enforcement mechanism and the economic crisis of the Great Depression. I think the missing enforcement was the real cause, and the Depression was just the stress test that revealed the weakness.

So my removal test handles the first two cases the way I want, but it collapses on the passenger pigeon, and it doesn't tell me how to classify the boycott or the pact. I've also been using an informal distinction between "causal backup" — where one factor could substitute for another — and "joint production" — where each does part of the work — but I can't apply it consistently. Is there a repaired principle, or a small family of principles, that saves my verdict that the assassination was secondary, that Gorbachev was primary, that both hunting and deforestation count fully despite redundancy, and that Parks and the NAACP are parts of one process rather than interchangeable backups? What does the best version predict for a case I haven't listed — say, the collapse of a small manufacturing firm that both lost its only major buyer and suffered a factory fire, where either event alone would have bankrupted it?
