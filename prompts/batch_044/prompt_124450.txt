I am acting as an examiner for a philosophy of long-term governance course and require an assessment of the following student response. The examination question asked students to analyse Derek Parfit's repugnant conclusion within the context of intergenerational settlement planning, specifying the exact principle in population axiology that produces it and explaining why the comparison turns on lives barely worth living rather than on lives of suffering. They were then to evaluate whether a critical-level variant of totalism dissolves the difficulty, and to identify one residual problem that persists even under that revision.

The student answered:
'The repugnant conclusion demonstrates that total utilitarianism is untenable, since it entails that world Z—containing billions of people whose lives are barely worth living—has greater total value than world A, where a far smaller population enjoys excellent lives. The conclusion follows from the principle of mere addition, according to which introducing anyone with positive welfare necessarily improves the world. Parfit regarded this as repugnant because it privileges sheer numbers over quality of existence. Average utilitarianism escapes the difficulty by dividing aggregate welfare by population, so world Z yields a mean near zero whilst world A yields a high mean. Nevertheless, average utilitarianism is vulnerable to the sadistic conclusion, wherein adding an individual with mildly negative welfare can raise the overall average. Critical-level totalism resolves the repugnant conclusion by counting only welfare above a specified threshold, yet it remains exposed to the non-identity problem, because altering our settlement policy changes who comes to exist in future generations.'

Please award a mark with brief justification, and present your assessment as a numbered list of exactly five items. Each item should consist of one or two sentences; include no introductory or concluding paragraphs. Use British English throughout.
