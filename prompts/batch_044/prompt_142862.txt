I was chatting with a colleague over coffee about my course on medieval metaphysics, and we got stuck on Al-Ghazali's occasionalism in exactly the way I see with my grad students every year. Everyone in the room can recite the fire-and-cotton example — God creates the burning at the moment of contact, fire is only the occasion, not the cause — and nobody disputes the basic historical framing. The occasionalist camp insists that science survives intact because all science needs is predictive regularity, not hidden powers or necessary connetions; the opposition, drawing on the Avicennan view of secondary causation and maybe modern interventionist accounts, claims that without real causal production you've just got a chronological list of miracles dressed up as nature. Even when we discuss medicine — does the drug cure the patient or does God cure through the drug as an occasion? — the positions don't budge, and nobody can point to the exact premise they reject.

So I'm wondering: is this stall actually a verbal disagreement about what 'cause' has to do — temporal priority versus modal robustness versus explanatory dependency — or are the two sides answering different questions, one theological about what God directly does and one metaphyscial about what nature contains? Could there be a hidden formal issue, perhaps about whether counterfactual interventon makes sense if every event is directly willed? I don't want a verdict on who wins. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure: is it substantive, a bedrock clash of intuitions about nature's autonomy, or is the question itself ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a formal result about overdetermination, a change in what counts as data — or should we just accept that nothing will move it?
