Exam question: In Philip Goff's version of constitutive panpsychism, macro-conscious subjects are constituted by arrays of micro-conscious subjects rather than being emergent from them. The combination problem asks how micro-experiences integrate into a unified macro-experience. Some defenders propose a direct constitutive integration relation—call it phenomenal bonding—rather than causal aggregation. Critics respond that this either introduces an emergent macro-property (thereby abandoning pure panpsychism) or collapses into cosmopsychism, in which only one universal subject exists. In your answer, specifically address whether phenomenal bonding requires universal mutual accessibility among all micro-subjects, or whether a transitive chain of accessibility suffices, and evaluate whether either option avoids both horns of the dilemma. Write approximately 400 words.

Student answer: The combination problem asks how many small minds make one big mind. Goff's constitutive panpsychism holds that macro-subjects are constituted by micro-subjects, but not by aggregation like bricks. Instead, there is phenomenal bonding: the micro-subjects are related in a way that directly constitutes the macro-subject's experience. This is not emergentism, because emergentism says that properties appear at higher levels that are not present in the parts. Here, the macro-experience is present in the parts, just organized through the bonding relation. Goff argues that if we accept this bonding relation, we do not need a new fundamental law of nature, unlike Chalmers, who thinks psychophysical laws are necessary. So bonding solves the problem without extra laws.

As for accessibility, universal mutual accessibility requires every micro-subject to be directly open to every other, while a transitive chain only requires each to be linked to the next. The chain option is sufficient for bonding, because if A is linked to B and B to C, then A is part of the same macro-subject as C. This avoids cosmopsychism, because the chain can be broken between different brains—my micro-subjects form one chain, yours form another—so we preserve individual subjects.

However, critics note that even with chains, the macro-quality is still just many small qualities arranged together. If each micro-subject has a minimal phenomenal field, bonding them in a chain should still yield only a collection of minimal fields, not one unified macro-field. Goff can reply that the macro-quality is not the sum but the unified structure of the chain itself. Therefore, phenomenal bonding with transitive accessibility solves the combination problem without falling into emergentism or cosmopsychism.
