As a journalist who writes about science and civic institutions, I am caught in a local decision that feels like a philosophical case study rather than a routine ballot. Our municipality runs an advisory climate board selected through a hybrid mechanism: residents either cast a direct vote (all choices are published in an open ledger) or delegate to a registered expert, whose public mandate list displays every delegator by name. The board has no binding power over the industries it advises, so I view participation as lending credibility to a largely symbolic body. Still, I must choose among four paths: delegate to the one qualified scientist on the ballot, which puts my name on her mandate list and, I feel, endorses the board's legitimacy; vote directly for the strongest candidate, who is almost certain to lose, thereby registering preference without influence; vote for the least harmful viable contender to block a genuinely damaging alternative; or abstain, avoiding complicity but surrendering all leverage. Because ballots are public and delegation is visible, anonymity is impossible, and because the district is large, my individual contribution is statistically negligible. I am not asking for a campaign strategy; I want to know whether any of these paths can be defended on principle—whether there is a coherent distinction between expressing judgment, preventing harm, and refusing to legitimize a flawed process—or whether the tension is irreducible.
