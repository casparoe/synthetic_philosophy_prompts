I have been working through an argument about predictive algorithmic systems and republican freedom, and I find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve the very distinction it starts from.

(1) Republican liberty, as distinct from negative liberty, is violated not merely by actual interference but by domination: the presence of an arbitrary power to interfere, even when that power is never exercised.

(2) A power is arbitrary in the relevant sense only if its exercise is not compelled to track the interests or considered judgments of those subject to it.

(3) Consider a predictive curation system that removes options from a user's environment—filtering information, products, or pathways—before the user ever encounters them. The filtering is driven entirely by statistical models calibrated to the user's past behavior, with no particular human agent selecting what is hidden.

(4) Because the system's outputs are optimized to align with the user's revealed preferences, there is no specific human will being exercised arbitrarily against the user's interests.

(5) Therefore, such predictive restructuring does not constitute domination in the republican sense, even though it reduces the user's effective options as thoroughly as explicit censorship.

(6) Yet if republican liberty is genuinely distinct from negative liberty, it should be able to identify a form of unfreedom that negative liberty misses—namely, unfreedom arising from subjection to another's arbitrary will rather than from actual obstruction.

(7) Here, however, both conceptions seem to miss the harm: negative liberty misses it because no actual interference occurs (the hidden options are never presented, so nothing is blocked), and republican liberty misses it because no arbitrary will is present.

(8) So either the republican/negative distinction fails to cover an important case of diminished freedom, or we must expand the notion of arbitrary power to include non-intentional, statistically calibrated restructuring of options.

(9) If we take the latter route—treating algorithmic calibration as a form of arbitrary domination—then every personalized recommendation, from navigation suggestions to playlist ordering, becomes a dominating power, which is implausible.

(10) Hence the distinction is either empty in this domain or applies far too broadly to be useful.

I want to keep both the intuition that predictive curation genuinely restricts freedom and the intuition that republican liberty captures something negative liberty does not. Which step should I reject? Identify the step precisely, explain why it initially looks sound, and then price the repair: what does rejecting it cost, and can the republican/negative distinction be preserved without treating every algorithm as a tyrant?
