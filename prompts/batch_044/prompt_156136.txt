I'm currently working through some primary texts in transcendental philosophy for a graduate seminar, and in discussions with my peers I've noticed a recurring argumentative configuration that I can't place within the standard taxonomy of fallacies or transcendental strategies. The pattern runs like this. First, an arguer demonstrates—convincingly, I think—that some condition C is necessary for the possibility of a phenomenon we all accept: perhaps the unity of apperception for empirical knowledge, or the normativity of reason-giving for scientific practice, or the presupposition of agency for practical deliberation. This first step is orthodox transcendental reasoning. But then comes a second move: the arguer treats the acceptance of C as insufficient unless it is accompanied by a specific, usually "thick," metaphysical interpretation—say, that C requires non-natural normative properties, or libertarian free will, or a transcendental subject that is not merely functional but substantively real. Crucially, the arguer does not show that rival interpretations—naturalist, compatibilist, deflationary—actually deny C; rather, they deny C only once C has been read through the arguer's preferred hermeneutic lens. I have observed this in debates over Kant's deduction of the categories versus empirical realism, in arguments that move from the concept of a person being necessary for self-ascription to the metaphysical reality of enduring substances, and in recent defenses of moral agency that slide from "freedom is presupposed in practical reasoning" to "therefore determinism is false" without engaging compatibilist accounts of that very presupposition.

What interests me is not the transcendental argument itself, but this particular slide—the move from transcendental necessity to metaphysical exclusivity via an unstated principle that transcendental conditions must be interpreted in an ontologically loaded rather than regulative or functional register. Is there an established term for this configuration? I am not satisfied with a bare label: I want to know whether it is genuinely a distinct phenomenon or merely a variant of equivocation, of the gap between constitutive and regulative principles in Kant, or of what we might call an interpretive charity failure. Specifically, I want the boundaries drawn: in which cases does the metaphysical interpretation genuinely follow from the transcendental claim, and in which cases does the slide depend on smuggling in premises that the transcendental step does not license? If there is no standard name, I would welcome a coined term with a precise definition and boundary conditions, because I find myself reaching for this distinction repeatedly and having to reconstruct it from scratch each time.
