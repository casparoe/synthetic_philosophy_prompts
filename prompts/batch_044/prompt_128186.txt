Exam question: Explain how Nāgārjuna deploys the catuskoti (fourfold negation) in the first chapter of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā to undermine the claim that entities possess svabhāva and arise from intrinsic conditions. Then address the reflexive problem: if emptiness (śūnyatā) is the absence of svabhāva in all things, does this absence itself have svabhāva? Clarify how Madhyamaka answers this reflexive challenge, and specify the respective roles of saṃvṛti-satya and paramārtha-satya in preventing the doctrine from collapsing into nihilism. Be precise about whether emptiness functions as a metaphysical thesis or as a therapeutic deconstruction of all theses.

Student answer: Nāgārjuna’s analysis in the opening chapter applies the four positions—arising from self, from other, from both, and from neither—to show that no entity can have an inherent nature (svabhāva). If something arose from itself, its arising would be redundant; if from an utterly other nature, causation would be arbitrary and unconditioned; both options combine these defects; and arising without any cause would undermine the very conditions (pratyaya) that make conventional regularity intelligible, which is incompatible with dependent origination. Turning the analysis on emptiness itself, we find that emptiness too lacks svabhāva: it does not arise from itself, from non-emptiness, from both, or causelessly. In Chapter XXIV, Nāgārjuna presents emptiness as the remedy for all philosophical views; once it is properly grasped, every other view is eliminated, leaving emptiness as the sole correct metaphysical position. This reflexive move blocks nihilism, because conventional phenomena retain a kind of borrowed being (upādāya) through pratītyasamutpāda—their interdependence gives them a functional, derivative reality rather than sheer nothingness. This structure is analogous to the Sautrāntika position that external objects exist only as mental representations that depend on consciousness, except that for Madhyamaka the dependence is reciprocal rather than one-sided. Consequently, conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya) sustains these interdependent phenomena as practically effective, while ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya) denies them any intrinsic nature, allowing the school to avoid both eternalism and annihilationism.
