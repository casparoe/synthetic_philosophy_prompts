I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science. I am trying to understand the problem of verisimilitude—the challenge of defining a formal measure of how close a false scientific theory lies to the truth, together with the well-known difficulty that any such measure seems to reward theories that accumulate large numbers of true but irrelevant consequences while penalizing theories that are broadly correct but narrowly scoped.

Please restate this philosophical problem entirely in the vocabulary of statistical inference and experimental measurement: true parameters versus misspecified models, predictive risk, calibration curves, bias-variance decomposition, loss functions, structural risk minimization, and the distinction between random and systematic error. Map truth onto the true data-generating process, false theory onto a misspecified estimator, and closeness to truth onto an appropriate approximation metric. Be as precise as possible: in particular, translate the counterintuitive result that conjoining a false theory with an independently true statement can increase its verisimilitude score into a corresponding phenomenon in statistical practice. Do not use philosophical jargon except where necessary to identify the original concept being translated.

Then provide a residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate survive this translation intact, which parts require distortion or analogical stretching, and which parts have no counterpart in statistical reasoning whatsoever. Specifically, address whether the philosophers' distinction between truth content and falsity content corresponds to anything deeper than a particular choice of loss function or weighting scheme; whether the failure to produce a satisfactory formal definition indicates a genuine limitation in statistical thinking or merely reveals that philosophers were attempting to formalize an intuitive notion that working scientists never actually need to quantify; and whether my suspicion—that the entire problem is essentially about selecting the right error metric for misspecified models—captures the debate or overlooks something essential that statistical vocabulary cannot express. I want an honest account of what does not survive, not a claim that the two frameworks are merely saying the same thing in different words.
