I am preparing a single fifty-minute seminar for third-year philosophy students who have just finished a unit on classical Chinese thought. Next session I need to introduce them to the dialogue attributed to Gongsun Long—commonly called the White Horse Dialogue—and to the broader concerns of the School of Names (Mingjia). The students have read a translation of the dialogue, but they are consistently misreading it: some treat it as a proto-Zenonian paradox about motion and division, others reduce it to a purely linguistic claim about how the predicate white modifies horse, and a few import Quinean ontological relativity to argue that the dispute is merely about how we choose to divide the world into categories. I do not want you to explain the dialogue’s arguments to me; I want a teaching plan. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual distinction—perhaps between universal form (xing) and particular color-determination (se), or between the name (ming) and the actuality (shi)—must be secured before the claim that a white horse is not a horse can appear as a substantive ontological thesis rather than a flat contradiction? Second, identify the one carrying example or analog that should bear the full explanatory load for this session, and specify exactly what it must include and what it must lack: for instance, why comparing it to Aristotelian accidental properties fails, and what modern or cross-cultural analog might work better without distorting the Mingjia concern with correct naming. Third, map the misconceptions I should anticipate—the Zeno confusion, the linguistic-reductionist reading, the Quinean framing—and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be defused. Fourth, propose one concise check for understanding at the end that reveals whether students have grasped the School’s specific worry about the relationship between names and actualities, rather than just parroting the dialogue’s conclusion. Finally, state explicitly what I should leave out at this level—perhaps the Mohist logical rebuttals, the full five-part structure of the dialogue, or the later Han synthesis—and confirm that I should openly tell the students we are bracketing it.
