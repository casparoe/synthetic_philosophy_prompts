You serve as an editorial referee for an interdisciplinary policy journal that publishes rapid-response briefs on emerging scientific and historical controversies. Your task is not merely to recommend acceptance or rejection, but to produce a standardized "assertion audit" for every brief: a reusable decision procedure that applies the three major norms of assertion—knowledge, truth, and belief—to determine whether the brief's central empirical claim is assertible in its published form. Policymakers will cite these briefs without reading the underlying studies, so the audit must be executable in under fifteen minutes, must not require direct access to the author's mental states or to ground-truth databases, and must document every point where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Design an eight-step (or fewer) procedural checklist with explicit branching conditions. For each step, specify the test to run, the information required, and the judgment call (if any) that must be signed off by the referee. The procedure must handle three hard cases: claims supported only by unpublished preprints or single small-sample studies; claims that are probably true but depend on modeling parameters that other experts dispute; and claims that the author sincerely believes but admits are not yet established by the available record. For each case, the procedure must generate one of four outputs: "assertible without qualification," "assertible only in conditional or hedged form," "not assertible—recommend revision to a weaker claim," or "not assertible—recommend withholding publication until further evidence is available."

In addition to the immediate audit, include a "downstream liability" check: if the claim is asserted now, what specific update, correction, or retraction obligation is triggered should contradictory evidence emerge within twelve months? This check must be operational—specify the trigger condition, the responsible party, and the form of the correction.

Apply your procedure to three hypothetical briefs:

1. A forty-subject pilot study finding that a common dietary supplement improves working memory in older adults, with no replication and no registered protocol.
2. A climate-modeling brief asserting that a specific ocean-feedback loop will accelerate warming by 0.4°C within fifteen years, where the parameter governing the loop is disputed by three other modeling groups.
3. A brief claiming that a particular indigenous burial practice was widespread in the region during the pre-contact period, where the author has strong ethnographic belief and partial oral-historical support, but the archaeological record is ambiguous and a competing interpretation exists in the literature.

For each brief, state the step-by-step output of your procedure, identify the exact step where an unavoidable judgment call occurred, and explain one specific way the procedure could misfire if applied mechanically without that judgment. Then state clearly what the procedure cannot do—for example, whether it can resolve the underlying philosophical disagreement between the knowledge norm and the belief norm, or whether it can guarantee that policymakers will attend to the qualifications.

Keep the language formal, the distinctions sharp, and the practical constraints explicit. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
