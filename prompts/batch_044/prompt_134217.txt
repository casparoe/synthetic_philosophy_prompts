Please write the opening remarks — about seven minutes — for an interdisciplinary workshop on institutional failure, delivered to an audience split evenly between philosophers of collective action who are well-versed in multipolar-trap dynamics and practicing urban-policy managers who are not. The remarks should center on what I will call cooperative exhaustion: a failure mode that inverts the tragedy of the commons, in which competing actors race to over-contribute to a shared system — redundant safety protocols, overlapping carbon-credit registries, parallel data infrastructures — until the system's absorptive capacity collapses. This idea should do real structural work in the speech: you must explain why the standard remedies for races to the bottom — transparency, binding commitment devices, and hierarchical coordination — often worsen cooperative exhaustion rather than cure it; you must defend the counter-intuitive normative claim that strategic restraint, and even deliberate non-participation, can be ethically obligatory in such contexts; and you must frame that restraint as a form of public-spiritedness rather than as free-riding. The tone should be formal but accessible, precise enough that the philosophers recognize the underlying logic, and concrete enough that the managers see immediate relevance to their own coordination problems. Do not cite specific authors, papers, or texts by name, and do not include a bibliography or reading list. The closing image should be specific and non-obvious: perhaps the image of a bridge that collapses not because too many vehicles drive across it, but because too many engineers simultaneously reinforce different sections according to incompatible standards.
