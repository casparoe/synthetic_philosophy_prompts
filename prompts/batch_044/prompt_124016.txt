I am presenting in my philosophy of mind seminar next week on a thesis I have developed for the course: that Hofstadter’s Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach, when read literally rather than heuristically, collapses into a category error between syntactic self-reference and semantic self-modeling. I distinguish strong self-reference, in which a formal system constructs a fixed-point formula asserting its own unprovability within a predetermined axiomatic framework, from weak self-reference, in which a representational system merely includes a model of itself among its objects without locking its own epistemic boundaries. Consciousness, I argue, exhibits weak but not strong self-reference: it involves open meta-representation, meaning representational frameworks can be continuously revised rather than mapped onto a static Gödel numbering scheme. Consequently, treating the strange loop as a Gödel-style fixed point forces an implausible computational functionalism that Hofstadter himself rejects, and the analogy explains at best a closed computational self-model rather than conscious self-awareness.

The audience is a philosophy-of-mind reading group of about twelve graduate students and two faculty members. They care whether analogical reasoning can carry genuine explanatory weight, and they are especially suspicious of arguments that slide from mathematical structures to phenomenological conclusions without specifying an intermediate mechanism.
