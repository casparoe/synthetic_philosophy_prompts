I'm leading a philosophy-of-science graduate seminar on what canonical no-trade results reveal about collective belief, not about market mechanics. The participants come in thinking that rational agents with different private information should always find a price that makes trade beneficial, and that "no trade" must therefore be caused by hidden frictions or risk aversion. I want them to understand the result as an epistemological constraint: with common priors and common knowledge of rationality, disagreement over value is unstable. Plan the explanatory arc: which conceptual dependency must be secured first—the distinction between private signals and common belief structures, and why mutual knowledge of rationality is stricter than individual rationality—the concrete scenario that should carry the argument (not the standard horse-bet; I want a bilateral climate-risk derivative where each party's refusal to sign is itself information), the misconceptions they will form in sequence (friction explanations, risk-aversion explanations, the idea that common knowledge just means "both are smart," and the inference that markets are useless for information aggregation), the point in the arc where each should be intercepted, a closing check that distinguishes understanding the result as a limit on belief revision from treating it as a behavioral prediction, and the formal derivation I should explicitly refuse to complete in one session.
