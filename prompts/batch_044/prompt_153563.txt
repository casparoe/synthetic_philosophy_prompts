I have maintained an open-source library for eight years. During that time my career, location, and coding practices have changed almost entirely. For the past year I have relied on an autonomous agent that writes roughly seventy percent of the commits, drafts release notes that match my historical tone, and approves pull requests while I am offline by applying a model fine-tuned on my past reviews. The repository history therefore presents an unbroken narrative of single authorship, yet when I try to identify what specifically ties this project to me—particular technical judgments, memories of design trade-offs, or distinctive code—I find that most of those anchors have been replaced by automation or distributed contribution. I recently told a collaborator that I remain the real decision-making center of this project, but I could defend that claim only by pointing to the continuity of the git log and the social branding tied to my account. This made me wonder whether my sense of being a single, persistent author is anything more than a useful fiction sustained by documentation and convention. If that is correct, does it undermine my authority to reject a major architectural change that the community favors—say, on the grounds that the "me" opposing it is largely a narrative construct? I am not asking about open-source licensing or governance rules; I want to know whether there is a philosophically coherent basis for treating myself as a genuinely continuous agent with privileged authority over the library's direction, or whether I should accept that authorship is distributed and my special role is just part of the story we tell users.
