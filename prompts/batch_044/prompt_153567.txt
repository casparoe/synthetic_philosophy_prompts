I have spent the last year reading Wittgenstein's remarks on language games and family resemblance without any formal philosophical training, and I am now preparing to interview, for a small podcast, a philosopher who extends that framework to scientific explanation itself. She holds that explanation is not a unified concept with necessary and sufficient conditions—like the covering-law model might suggest—but rather a family-resemblance term whose instances share only an overlapping network of similarities: causal narratives, mathematical unifications, and pragmatic answers all count without sharing one essential feature. I need a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond having her restate this view. Specifically, I want one question that presses her on the standard objection that family resemblance merely pushes essentialism back to the level of similarity judgments; one that asks how her view distinguishes genuine scientific explanation from pseudo-explanatory storytelling if there is no core criterion; one that connects this to artificial intelligence—whether a language model producing a causal narrative participates in the language game of explanation or only mimics its surface grammar; and one that asks what it means to teach the concept of explanation to a child if there is no hidden essence to grasp. For each of these four, please provide a follow-up tailored to the reply she is most likely to offer, particularly the reply that appeals to use in practice or to the form of life to dissolve the difficulty. The remaining questions should probe whether family resemblance collapses into relativism about scientific standards, and whether any formal structure—perhaps a network or cluster model—could capture these overlaps without smuggling an essentialist backbone back in. The tone should reflect an interviewer who is genuinely puzzled by these texts, not an academic expert, yet who is capable of pressing precisely where the literature presses.
