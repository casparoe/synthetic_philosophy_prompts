The exam question was as follows:

“In his letter to d’Alembert, Rousseau argues that introducing a public theater into Geneva would corrupt republican morals. First, explain his moral-psychological objection: what does the structure of theatrical spectatorship do to the citizen’s capacity for direct moral action? Second, explain his socio-ontological objection: what is problematic about the actor’s social being, and about the relation between represented appearance and real civic presence? Third, apply both objections to private, amateur theatrical performances staged inside a single household with no admission fees, no professional performers, and no public audience beyond family members. Would Rousseau’s critique still apply with full force, or does the removal of commercial spectacle and professional deception dissolve his concerns? Defend your answer.”

The student submitted this response:

“Rousseau’s first objection is moral-psychological. Theater turns citizens into passive spectators. Instead of acting virtuously themselves, they sit and watch actors pretend to suffer or display virtue. This weakens civic character because people learn to feel pity without being required to do anything about it. Over time, the republic fills with people who are emotionally satisfied but politically inactive.

His second objection is socio-ontological. The actor is essentially a fake person who stands before the public and pretends to be what he is not. This creates representations that are copies of copies—just like Plato argues in the Republic—so the audience is relating to beings that are twice removed from reality. In a republic, citizens must deal with one another as they really are, not through theatrical masks. That is why the actor’s presence corrupts the social fabric.

As for private household performances, Rousseau’s critique does not apply at all. Because there are no professional actors being paid to deceive, the socio-ontological problem disappears. And because everyone present knows it is only family play-acting, the moral-psychological problem also vanishes—there is no public spectacle consuming civic energy. So private theater is harmless, and Rousseau would have no reason to object to it.”
