I lead a philosophy of language seminar for about ten graduate students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class structured deliberation that lets them experience the instability of the analytic-synthetic boundary before anyone names Quine or holism. Give the group a miniature artificial language with eight interdefined terms and five observation reports that appear to conflict with one definitional link. Their task: collectively revise the system by choosing which beliefs to abandon—redefining a term, discarding an observation, or altering an auxiliary bridge rule—under the constraint that they must preserve at least six terms unchanged. Assign roles (e.g., a terminologist who guards definitions, an empiricist who guards observations, a bridge-builder who holds auxiliary rules) so that rational self-interest drives different members to protect different nodes. Include precise rules for proposing revisions, a voting mechanism that requires consensus on each change, materials that make the interdependence visible, and a timeline for the 90 minutes. Then provide a debrief that maps each kind of revision they actually made onto Quine's claim that no statement is immune to revision, explains what coordination failure will predictably occur in the first round, and honestly states which element of Quine's argument—likely the global underdetermination of theory choice—this localized game cannot reproduce and must be added by the facilitator.
