I have no formal background in philosophy, but I've been working through the problem of punishment on my own, and I've reached a point where I need to compare two positions that seem to sit in direct tension. On one side is the view associated with Daniel Dennett: that determinism does not erase moral responsibility, because what matters for "just deserts" is not some metaphysically free origin of action but the functional role that holding people accountable plays in regulating behavior and sustaining social cooperation. On the other side is the view associated with Gregg Caruso: that "just deserts" is a moral fiction, that no one genuinely deserves suffering in a retributive sense given that our actions are the products of causes outside our control, and that punishment should therefore be stripped of its retributive element and rebuilt entirely around public-health goals like deterrence, rehabilitation, and containment.

What I want to do is push this comparison past the usual classroom examples—shoplifting, traffic accidents, and so on—into a scenario that forces the disagreement into sharper relief. Imagine a society that has developed a near-perfect predictive-intervention system: it can identify, with extremely high reliability, which individuals are about to commit serious harm, and it can stop them in advance through non-punitive means—therapy, temporary restraint, environmental restructuring, neurological adjustment—without ever allowing the harm to occur and without inflicting suffering for its own sake. In such a world, punishment is practically unnecessary; the practical functions that Dennett might cite to justify it have been replaced by prevention.

Yet suppose that citizens in this society still feel, powerfully and persistently, that when harm does slip through, the perpetrator should suffer "to balance the scales," even if that suffering prevents nothing and serves no rehabilitative purpose. The feeling is not about deterrence or safety; it is about a perceived moral ledger that demands payment.

How would Dennett and Caruso each diagnose that residual feeling? Would Dennett treat it as a useful evolutionary or social mechanism that should be preserved in some symbolic or ritualized form, perhaps as a way of reaffirming the norms that make the predictive system legitimate? Or would he see it as a cognitive leftover that should be gradually replaced by more functional reactive attitudes? Conversely, would Caruso regard the feeling as precisely the kind of dangerous illusion that must be actively dismantled, even if it feels intuitively satisfying, and if so, what practical mechanisms does he propose for weakening it without destabilizing social order?

More specifically, I want to know which of these two frameworks better explains why the "balancing" impulse persists even when every practical justification for it has been removed, and whether either view can account for the possibility that this impulse might become actively destructive—say, if citizens begin to sabotage the predictive system in order to create opportunities for deserved suffering. Does Dennett's functionalism have the resources to explain why a socially useful mechanism might turn pathological, and does Caruso's skepticism have the resources to explain why people cling to the illusion so tenaciously despite its practical uselessness?

Finally, I am trying to understand whether this thought experiment reveals that "just deserts" is best understood as a useful fiction, a harmful error, or something that occupies a more complicated middle ground—perhaps a social technology that was once necessary but becomes obsolete, and whose emotional residue must be managed rather than either celebrated or simply ignored. I would like a comparison that takes both thinkers seriously on their own terms, applies them rigorously to this predictive-prevention scenario, and concludes by saying what this particular edge case adds to the philosophical debate that arguments confined to ordinary criminal justice do not.
