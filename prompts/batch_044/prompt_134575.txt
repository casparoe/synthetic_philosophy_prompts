I've been trying to understand whether Buddhist ethics is really just a kind of consequentialism in disguise, since both traditions seem centrally concerned with reducing suffering. Most discussions I encounter treat karma like a cosmic points system: perform good actions, accumulate merit, and eventually receive better rebirths. That framing makes the comparison with consequentialism almost trivial—either Buddhism is long-term utilitarianism with rebirth added on, or it rejects outcomes altogether in favor of pure intention. I think both sides miss a deeper tension. In consequentialism, the moral quality of an act is fixed by the actual states of affairs it produces, which requires the agent to maintain a kind of mental ledger: tracking probabilities, weighing future harms and benefits, and adjusting behavior to maximize some aggregate measure of welfare. Even when this reasoning is motivated by genuine compassion, it seems to demand a calculative attachment to results. In Buddhist practice, however, the purification of mind depends on acting from compassion (karuna) without clinging to the fruits of action; attachment to outcomes, even beneficial ones, is treated as a form of craving (tanha) that perpetuates the very suffering ethics aims to eliminate. So my half-formed idea is that consequentialist reasoning is not merely different from Buddhist ethics, but actively corrosive to it: the mental habit of optimizing welfare is structurally analogous to the mental defilement of craving, because it makes one's moral stability depend on controlling and predicting an external future rather than on the present purity of intention (cetana).

Could you argue for this position in detail? Specifically, defend the claim that consequentialism undermines Buddhist moral development not by producing wrong actions, but by requiring a form of outcome-tracking that functions as a mental defilement. Please also address the obvious objection that Buddhist teachers often advise practical foresight—avoiding harm, considering consequences for others—without letting that practical wisdom collapse into consequentialist reasoning. I am not asking for citations or a survey of scholars; I want a focused conceptual argument about whether the distinction between prudent foresight and calculative attachment can be drawn sharply enough to sustain this anti-consequentialist reading.
