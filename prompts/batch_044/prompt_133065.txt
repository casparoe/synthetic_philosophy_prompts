In evolutionary debunking debates I keep noticing a particular asymmetry, and I am unsure whether it has a specific name. A debunker explains a first-order moral belief—say, that harming the innocent is wrong—by tracing it to evolutionary pressures that promoted cooperation, and takes this origin story to undermine the belief’s justification, on the grounds that those pressures do not track moral truth. Then, in the same argument, the debunker explains why people resist the debunking conclusion—perhaps by saying that maintaining shared norms is adaptively valuable—without treating that second explanation as undermining belief in evolutionary theory itself, or the normative premise that we ought to avoid unjustified beliefs. The evolutionary mechanism is wielded as a debunker for moral content, but treated as a neutral, truth-tracking account when applied to epistemic or psychological responses; I have seen versions of this pattern in discussions of incest taboos and of fairness norms. Is there an established concept for this selective deployment—where evolutionary explanation discredits beliefs in one domain yet is left untouched in the adjacent domains that support the argument? If not, how exactly would you distinguish it from a generic self-defeat problem and from ordinary explanatory pluralism, and what boundaries would you draw around the phenomenon?
