I have been sitting in on a philosophy of logic reading group, and last week two members—Mara and Leo—became stuck in a disagreement about Bradley's regress and whether it genuinely undermines the reality of relations or merely forces us toward a different ontology. The exchange began when Mara argued that Bradley's reasoning in Appearance and Reality demonstrates that if a relation R genuinely connects a and b, then a further relation R' is required to link R to a, and R'' to link R' to a, producing an infinite regress that proves relations are internally contradictory and therefore unreal. Leo conceded that the regress works for what he called "external" relations—those that stand between terms without being part of their nature—but he insisted that Bradley illegitimately assumes every relation must be an independent term requiring further linkage. Leo proposed that if relations are "internal," in the sense that they are not separate entities but part of the very nature of the terms they relate, then the regress stops at the first step, since there is no free-standing R that needs connecting. Mara replied that this move collapses relations into qualities of an undifferentiated whole, making it impossible to distinguish "Socrates is mortal" from "Socrates is wise" without smuggling relational distinctions back in as distinguishable parts, and that Bradley's appeal to an undifferentiated "immediate experience" to ground the whole does not solve the problem but merely renames it. Leo then sharpened his objection around the unity of the proposition: he claimed that Bradley's rejection of relational structure makes it impossible to explain how a single judgment can contain distinguishable elements at all, since without relations or logical form a judgment becomes an indistinguishable qualitative blob. Mara countered that Bradley can differentiate judgments by their internal qualitative content without needing relational scaffolding, and that Leo is importing Russellian assumptions about logical form that Bradley explicitly rejects. Leo pressed further that, even granting Bradley's monism, there remains the problem of error: if all reality is one Absolute and relations are merely appearances, there is no false relation for a mistaken judgment to misrepresent, which seems to make error inexplicable. Mara responded that error is itself an appearance that gets dissolved in the Absolute, not a relation that needs to be false.

Neither asked me to settle it, but I am trying to understand the logic of their exchange rather than pick a favorite. Can you adjudicate point by point? Regarding Leo's point about error, does Bradley's monism provide a coherent account of false judgment, or does the rejection of relational structure indeed render error inexplicable? Finally, what are both parties missing—perhaps a misreading of how Bradley deploys the appearance-reality distinction, or a middle position that preserves the unity of the proposition without accepting either Bradley's monism or a fully Russellian logic?
