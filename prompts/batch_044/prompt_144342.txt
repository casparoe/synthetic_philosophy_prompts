In debates over whether societies owe a duty of remembrance to historical injustices or may legitimately cultivate forgetting—a tension often traced to Margalit’s defense of shared moral memory and to Rieff’s diagnosis of a therapeutic culture that treats the past as an emotional burden to be dissolved—one influential move analogizes collective remembrance to an immune response. The claim runs that, just as an immune system retains pathogen-specific memory to prevent reinfection yet must also develop tolerance to avoid autoimmune self-destruction, a polity should preserve “mnemonic antibodies” against past wrongs while practicing selective forgetting to prevent chronic social inflammation. Advocates use this to license both commemorative obligation and institutional amnesty: remembrance is protective, but obsessive remembrance is pathological, like lupus.

Audit this analogy in full. First, lay out the explicit mapping—past injustice to pathogen, collective commemoration to immune memory, paralyzing grievance culture to autoimmune disorder, and mechanisms of amnesty or archival suppression to immune tolerance. Second, identify which biological features are quietly imported into the social domain that lack any genuine counterpart: for instance, the immune system’s absence of moral agency, its inability to distinguish deserved from undeserved suffering, and the fact that “tolerance” is metabolically regulated rather than ethically justified. Third, determine which of these disanalogies actually undermine the normative conclusion—that societies must calibrate remembrance and forgetting like immune homeostasis—and which are merely decorative. Does the analogy support both the duty to remember and the right to forget with equal force, or does it collapse one side into the other? Fourth, assess whether the conclusion survives once the hidden commitments are exposed: in particular, does framing forgetting as “tolerance” smuggle in a medicalized, value-neutral description of what may actually be a moral or political choice about whose suffering counts? Finally, propose whether an alternative analogy—perhaps archival curation with retention schedules, scar tissue that marks but also constrains movement, or debt accounting with statutes of limitation—would do the work better without importing the same covert normative machinery.
