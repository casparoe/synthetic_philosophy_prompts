In my graduate seminar on rational choice theory, we have reached an impasse that has lasted several sessions without any visible convergence. The topic is whether superrationality—cooperating with a perfect copy of oneself in a one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma—is a legitimate extension of practical rationality or merely an evidentialist confusion projected onto decision-theoretic machinery. Every participant accepts the formal payoff structure, concedes that causal decision theory licenses defection, and acknowledges that evidential correlation can license cooperation. Yet the causalists continue to object that reasoning about a “copy” violates the causal independence required for agency, while the superrationalists reply that identity of reasoning procedure, not causal linkage, is what justifies the correlation. I have come to suspect that the stall is not substantive but structural: one camp may be analyzing the relation synchronically, as two simultaneous decision nodes in branching worlds that share an epistemic origin, while the other analyzes it diachronically, as a single strategy persisting across temporal stages of one agent. If so, they may be answering different questions—one about simultaneous agents, one about the unity of practical reason over time—while treating both as “superrationality.” Is this diagnosis correct? Or does the disagreement run deeper, perhaps about whether rationality can ever be responsive to correlations that lack causal efficacy, regardless of how the “copy” relation is parsed? I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication: determine whether this is a verbal dispute about “copy” or “agency,” a hidden formal disagreement about the scope of decision rules, two distinct questions masked by shared examples, or a bedrock clash of intuitions about what it means to be one reasoning subject. And given your diagnosis, specify what would actually count as progress—whether a rigorous distinction between synchronic and diachronic reasoning frames, a revised account of agency that accommodates correlation without causation, or an argument that the original question is ill-posed because it conflates epistemic dependence with practical authority.
