Read Cormac McCarthy’s novel The Road through Michel de Montaigne’s essay on cannibals and the problem of cultural relativism he develops there. Montaigne argues that Europeans call barbaric whatever differs from their customs, and he distinguishes a natural violence—direct, ritualized, and embedded in social life, such as the cannibalism he describes among Brazilian peoples—from an artificial violence refined by law, theology, and technology, such as torture and religious warfare. In The Road, cannibalism has become institutionalized: organized bands keep captives in cellars, maintain supply chains of human flesh, and operate with a brutal rationality, while the father and son cling to a minimal code—do not kill except in defense, share scarce resources, and refuse to eat people even to survive, insisting instead that they carry an inner moral fire. Examine whether this inverts Montaigne’s hierarchy. Are the novel’s cannibals practicing a stripped-down natural survival instinct, or have they constructed a new artificial barbarism—complete with hierarchy, storage, and rationalized predation—that makes European civilization’s sins look mild by comparison? At the same time, consider whether the father’s code is itself an artificial remnant, a civilized luxury that depends on the very institutions and social worlds that the catastrophe has erased. Does the novel suggest that once those supporting structures vanish, the distinction between natural and artificial barbarism collapses, and with it the plausibility of the cultural relativism Montaigne can afford only because he writes from within a stable society?

Treat both texts with equal seriousness: do not reduce the father to a simple universalist mouthpiece, and do not distort Montaigne’s skepticism into a blanket approval of all practices. Pay particular attention to the cellar scene and to the father’s repeated claim about carrying the fire, asking how these moments function in relation to Montaigne’s observation that the observer’s horror often reveals more about the observer’s culture than about the observed practice. End by stating what the novel adds to the philosophical debate—perhaps about whether moral identity can survive the loss of the social institutions that make it meaningful, or about the conditions under which relativism collapses into nihilism—that argument alone does not capture.
