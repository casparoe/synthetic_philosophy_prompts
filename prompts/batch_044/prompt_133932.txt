Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning to philosophy, I am struck by how rarely moral psychology employs the kind of rigorous nuisance-parameter control that we take for granted in the natural sciences. This leads me to a precise proposition. Define a moral preference as a judgment that remains invariant under a full factorial rotation of framing conditions, emotional priming, and presentation order—analogous to isolating a true signal by averaging over all nuisance variables in an ANOVA design—while a bias is any judgment that collapses or reverses under such rotation. The claim is that, within the next half-century, we will possess a validated, non-circular instrument capable of classifying any particular moral judgment as preference-driven or bias-driven with accuracy exceeding eighty-five percent, without appealing to an independent normative ground truth.

Please state your actual credence in this claim, decomposed into the following terms, each with a number between zero and one and a single line of justification: first, the probability that moral judgments possess a stable, context-independent preference structure analogous to revealed preference in economics; second, the probability that, if such structure exists, experimental methods can isolate it despite the absence of an objective calibration standard for morality; third, the probability that the preference-bias distinction does not ultimately collapse into a purely normative stipulation rather than an empirical discovery; and fourth, any additional factor you consider indispensable. Combine these into an overall figure, and identify which term is both the most uncertain and the most decisive for your final number—specifically, what kind of philosophical argument or empirical finding would shift it by at least twenty points.

Finally, briefly indicate whether you think the distinction is worth preserving even if it proves largely normative.
