Write an essay on whether mandatory cooling-off periods for high-risk financial contracts entered into by elderly clients constitute justified paternalism or illegitimate choice architecture. I have recently been appointed to an ethics review board tasked with evaluating these mandates, and our deliberations are stalled by a disagreement that goes deeper than the usual present-bias literature. One faction treats the waiting period as a neutral correction that reveals stable preferences; the other contends that delaying the decision actively reconstructs the client's mental representation of their future self, so that the preference expressed after the delay belongs to a psychologically distinct temporal agent rather than the original decision-maker. Weigh these positions carefully, but do not end non-committally: defend a specific verdict on whether such mandates are ethically permissible, and justify it. In doing so, address whether an identity-sensitive revision of paternalism—one that permits interference only when it protects the interests of a single, diachronically continuous self—can salvage the justification, or whether the preference-drift phenomenon undermines paternalist reasoning altogether.
