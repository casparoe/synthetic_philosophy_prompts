I'm preparing the final meeting of my graduate seminar on evolutionary approaches to mind, and we've been working through Dennett's taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—though I worry my students have begun to treat the four kinds as a neat ladder rather than a philosophical problem. Rather than ending with a standard discussion, I'd like to close with an original poem—roughly twenty to twenty-four lines, arranged in four quatrains—that takes the taxonomy seriously but complicates it through form. Each quatrain should be voiced from the perspective of one creature in ascending order. The first (Darwinian) must use a rigid, unvarying meter and strict end-rhyme, as if behavior were fully hard-wired; the second (Skinnerian) should keep the rhyme scheme but introduce repetition and slight metrical variation, like reinforcement reshaping response; the third (Popperian) should contain an embedded internal rhyme or parenthetical clause that functions as a mental model being tested offline, before any action is taken; and the fourth (Gregorian) should borrow and transform a phrase or structural pattern from one of the earlier stanzas—indicating cultural transmission—while breaking the previous meter entirely. The philosophical work should happen in the progression: the Gregorian speaker understands the lower creatures through language and tools they lack, yet that understanding depends on capacities continuous with theirs. The final two lines should hold that asymmetry without resolving it—perhaps stating that one can possess the words for a life that has no words for itself. Please do not include citations or bibliographic references; I'll supply those for the handout. Write it as a serious poem, not a mnemonic rhyme, so that someone familiar with the taxonomy recognizes the accuracy and someone unfamiliar still finds it coherent.
