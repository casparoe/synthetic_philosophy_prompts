In my philosophy of computation seminar, the instructor has adopted a recurring maxim whenever discussion turns to so-called hypercomputational models: "The Church-Turing thesis doesn't describe the limits of nature; it fixes the meaning of computation itself." She invoked it most recently to dismiss a classmate's proposal that certain continuous analog physical processes—or theoretical oracle-augmented architectures—might compute functions that lie outside the class of Turing-computable ones. Rather than engaging with the physical or mathematical details, she treated the proposal as conceptually muddled, as if asking whether a bachelor could be married. I am trying to determine whether that dismissal is genuinely supported by the slogan, or whether it relies on sliding between different interpretations of the thesis.

Could you disambiguate the precise claims that one-liner might be expressing? I would like a menu of readings: the purely stipulative or definitional version, the empirical-physical version, the mathematical version that treats the thesis as an informal convergence result, and any other substantive precisification that philosophers of computation actually distinguish. For each, please indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or contested, and—most importantly—whether that particular reading actually licenses treating hypercomputational questions as category errors rather than as open empirical or theoretical inquiries. I am also curious how these readings intersect with debates about the thesis's epistemic status—whether it is best understood as an empirical conjecture about the physical world, a mathematical claim in need of proof, or a conventional boundary on the concept of effective calculability—since my classmates seem to conflate these dimensions when they repeat the line.
