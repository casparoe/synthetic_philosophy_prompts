Consider a public pension board's "Preference Stability Clause." The clause provides that if a beneficiary revises her investment risk profile more than twice within any rolling twelve-month window, her account is automatically reverted to a conservative default and further changes are suspended for six months, on the stated rationale that rapid reversals either signal cognitive decline or expose the beneficiary to exploitation through money-pump dynamics. However, the same charter permits unlimited, immediate reallocation whenever the beneficiary documents a qualifying life event—marriage, divorce, disability diagnosis, or inheritance—without triggering the stability mechanism.

What philosophical commitments about diachronic rationality does this institutional design presuppose? Does it assume that rational preference orderings must be transitive and stable across time, or can it be interpreted as protecting autonomy rather than enforcing consistency? How does the life-event exemption affect the claim that the board is targeting money-pump vulnerability specifically, as opposed to mere volatility of taste? And is the practice consistent with a view that permits intransitive preferences across temporal stages so long as no single stage is exploited?
