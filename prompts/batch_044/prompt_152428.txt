I'm constructing a diagnostic instrument for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar that treats illusionism about phenomenal consciousness not as a monolithic denial but as a family of positions diverging on three axes: what exactly is illusory, what mechanism generates the appearance, and whether any residual non-illusory substrate remains. I need a decision-tree taxonomy—functioning as a sorting quiz—that begins with a respondent's commitment that phenomenal consciousness, as ordinarily conceived, fails to match reality, and then guides them through concrete diagnostic branches to a named leaf position.

The root should distinguish between total denial of phenomenal properties and denial only of our standard conception of them (for instance, as intrinsic, immediately given, or essentially private). From there, branch by explanatory mechanism: is the appearance best accounted for as representational misrepresentation of physical or functional properties; as a conceptual error that applies an empty or incoherent category; or as a pragmatic fiction sustained for social coordination? Next, ask whether the illusion's scope is global—encompassing all seeming phenomenal states—or local, restricted to specific features such as the transparency of experience, the unity of consciousness across modalities, or the presence of an irreducible 'what-it-is-like' quality. At each leaf, place an actual named view (e.g., strong eliminativist illusionism, representational-error illusionism, conceptual-confusion illusionism, pragmatic-fiction illusionism, or hybrid local-illusionist positions), and explicitly mark any logically available combinations of answers that remain unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The tree should also flag any named positions that it cannot genuinely separate on these three axes, rather than manufacturing false distinctions.

Please phrase every node as a concrete claim or scenario answerable without prior knowledge of the taxonomy's labels, and keep the instrument free of bibliographic requests, reading lists, or citation demands—the audience is graduate students who understand the basic debate but should navigate entirely from reasoning. To keep the taxonomy out of encyclopedia-level territory, include one branch that asks whether adopting a predictive-processing or hierarchical-inference architecture as the underlying cognitive mechanism forces a particular answer on the mechanism or scope questions above, so that the tree captures a genuinely under-discussed junction in the logical space rather than rehearsing standard textbook divisions.
