Here is an argument my seminar group built by extending Knobe-effect reasoning into omissions, and I find it suspicious because it seems to prove that negligence is conceptually impossible. I want you to tell me exactly where it breaks, what that rejection costs, and whether the flaw is an equivocation or a modal slide.

(1) The standard Knobe effect shows that people judge a harmful side-effect of an action to be brought about intentionally, while judging an otherwise identical beneficial side-effect not to be intentional.

(2) If the same experimental design is applied to omissions—cases where an agent fails to perform some available action—subjects judge that an agent who fails to rescue a drowning child intentionally failed to rescue when the child dies, but do not attribute intentionality to the identical failure when the child is saved by a bystander.

(3) So the attribution of intentionality to omissions, like to actions, tracks the moral valence of the outcome rather than tracking any independent feature of the agent’s mental states.

(4) Call the view that intentionality is determined solely by internal mental-state features—plans, beliefs, and relevant desires—the internalist account of intentional omission.

(5) If internalism is true, then the moral valence of an outcome should not systematically shift judgments about whether an omission was intentional, since mental-state presence or absence does not depend on what actually happens in the world.

(6) But (2) and (3) show that outcome valence does systematically shift those judgments.

(7) Therefore either internalism about intentional omission is false, or these judgments are systematically erroneous across contexts.

(8) The widespread-error option is implausible: these judgments are stable, predict legal reasoning, and guide moral evaluation, so treating them all as mistakes would undermine too much of our practical and institutional life.

(9) Hence internalism is false: for omissions, intentionality is constituted at least in part by the normative status of the outcome, not merely by internal mental states.

(10) But if the normative status of the outcome partly constitutes intentionality, then whenever an omission produces a bad outcome, that very badness makes the omission intentional.

(11) Conversely, whenever an omission produces a good or neutral outcome, that very neutrality or goodness makes the omission unintentional.

(12) This collapses the distinction between intentional omission and mere negligence or accident: negligence becomes impossible, because any harmful result of an omission automatically renders the omission intentional.

(13) Since negligence is a robust category in law and morality, (12) is absurd.

(14) Therefore the extension of the Knobe effect to omissions in (1)–(3) must be mistaken, and there is no genuine asymmetry in how we attribute intentionality to omissions.
