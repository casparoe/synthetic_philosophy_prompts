I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop called "Who Decides the Scale?" — about eight minutes, then Q&A. The room's split: half are EU lawyers and federalism scholars who can cite Article 5 TEU in their sleep, half are organizers from small Swiss cantons and German Länder who are tired of being told Brussels or Berlin "knows best." I want the whole talk built around one idea I think people miss: subsidiarity isn't about physical proximity or "closer is always better," it's about reverse delegation, where lower bodies don't just recieve authority from the center but actively generate the conditions that make the center's claims legitimate. But there's a tension — in practice the EU uses subsidiarity as a blame-shifting device (if the policy fails, it's the member state's implementation), while Swiss fiscal equalization means rich cantons subsidize poor ones, which breaks the usual "local autonomy" story. So the philosophical point has to do real structural work: because legitimacy flows upward from these agreements rather than downward from a unified sovereign people, I can't promise anyone that decentralization is automatically more democratic, and I can't treat the EU like a big nation-state that just needs to be trimmed back. The lawyers should hear a real argument about competence-competence and the missing middle in EU regional policy, but the organizers shouldn't feel lectured. Include one slightly awkward analogy that lands for half the room and confuses the other half in a friendly way. Please don't make it sound like a textbook — there's a typo in my workshop invite ("subsidiarty") and I want to keep that energy. No bibliography, just the speech.
