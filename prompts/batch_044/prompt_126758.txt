I am preparing a graduate seminar on diachronic rationality, and I have become convinced that the standard absent-minded driver scenario—where a single agent, unable perceptually to discriminate between two successive highway exits, must commit in advance to passing a dangerous first junction in order to reach a correct second one, yet risks precisely the irrational continuation that commitment was meant to prevent—derives its paradoxical force from an implicit model of the agent as a unified planner with perfect recall of intentions but imperfect local discrimination. I want you to translate this entire normative structure into the vocabulary of distributed state-machine replication and fault-tolerant consensus protocols: treat each temporal stage of the driver as an independent node or epoch, the highway exits as state transitions that must be agreed upon despite message loss, and the planning commitment as a pre-committed log entry that subsequent nodes may fail to honor. Be precise about which concepts—ex ante optimality, causal versus evidential conditioning, the strategic value of precommitment—map onto analogues such as leader election, Byzantine fault tolerance, or split-brain resolution, and which must be distorted to fit this architecture.

Then provide the residue report: specify exactly what does not survive translation. Does the tension between planning and execution vanish once we eliminate a central controller? Is the paradox reproduced as a coordination failure among replicas, or does it depend on a metaphysics of personal identity that permits one epoch to bind another? Finally, indicate whether the residue—particularly any remnant involving first-person authority over future actions—reveals a genuine expressive limitation in the distributed framework, or whether it exposes the original philosophical formulation as smuggling in assumptions about unified agency that are less substantive than they initially appear.
