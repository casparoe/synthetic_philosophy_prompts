Construct a diagnostic taxonomy—formatted as a branching decision tree—for philosophical positions on Kuhnian paradigm shifts and incommensurability, intended for use in a philosophy-of-science seminar. Begin the tree with the root commitment that scientific change is not purely cumulative: there are episodes in which core theoretical terms, evaluation standards, and ontological commitments shift together in ways that resist simple translation. The first branch should distinguish scope: does incommensurability affect only a localized cluster of interdefined theoretical terms, or does it extend globally across an entire disciplinary matrix? From each of those nodes, split by the primary kind of discontinuity—semantic (reference and meaning shift), ontological (the entities or structures posited change), or methodological (standards of explanation, confirmation, and problem-definition diverge)—noting that hybrid combinations are possible but that some pairings may be unstable. The next level should ask whether rational comparison across paradigms remains viable despite the discontinuity: can practitioners still weigh predictive accuracy, problem-solving efficacy, explanatory coherence, or values like simplicity and consistency, or is paradigm choice ultimately resolved by sociological dynamics, pragmatic conversion, or non-cognitive preference? Then include a branch on relativism: does incommensurability entail that successive paradigms are incommensurable with respect to truth, or is it compatible with a realist view that different paradigms approximate the same reality through distinct conceptual schemes? At each leaf, name the position if it is recognized in the literature—such as local semantic incommensurability with retained rational comparison; global ontological incommensurability resolved through sociological consensus; methodological pluralism without ontological commitment—or label it unnamed if the logical space is unoccupied, and provide a brief diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. To keep the instrument genuinely diagnostic rather than a label-matching exercise, phrase every node as a substantive claim or concrete case judgment that a respondent could endorse without knowing which interpretation it corresponds to. The tree should contain about seven branch points and eight to ten leaves. Inject one niche distinction—specifically, whether contemporary practices of large-scale computational simulation and data-driven model selection function as a paradigm-independent arbitration mechanism, or whether they are themselves embedded within and shaped by the very paradigms they purport to evaluate—so that standard positions must reveal commitments they rarely make explicit. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that the tree's branching logic cannot genuinely separate, explaining whether the overlap is merely verbal or substantive, and ensure that no branch asks for a bibliography, citation, or identification of a specific author or text.
