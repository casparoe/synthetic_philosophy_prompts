Write a dialogue between a Confucian role ethicist and a moral universalist who reject the idea that obligations are role-relative. The Confucian interlocutor should defend the view that personhood is fundamentally relational and that moral duties arise from specific, asymmetrical positions—parent and child, ruler and subject, elder and younger friend—cultivated through ritual propriety and expressed as graded care rather than as applications of an impartial principle. The universalist should defend the view that any morality indexed to social roles risks rationalizing partiality and that true moral respect requires treating every person as an equal bearer of dignity, with obligations derived from universal reason or contract rather than from kinship or station.

Set their exchange in response to a particular case: a physician-researcher who has developed a treatment that can save only one life, and must choose between her own adopted child and five strangers who are members of a distant community with no prior relation to her. The twist is that she understands her professional identity—her capacity to heal at all—as having been formed within the very relational network that defines her parenthood, so that abandoning the child is not merely a failure of kin-duty but, on her view, a dissolution of the self who practices medicine. The Confucian should press the claim that the universalist's demand for impartial choice presupposes an atomic, pre-social self that is itself an abstraction, and that role-constituted identity is not a bias to overcome but the ground of moral agency. The universalist should reply that identifying the self with its relational roles makes moral criticism impossible—if every role-bound preference is self-constituting, then nepotism becomes unfalsifiable—and should insist that the physician's obligation to strangers is precisely what tests whether her healing role is genuinely moral or merely tribal.

Both characters must be equally competent, responding directly to each other's arguments rather than talking past one another, and neither should be a strawman. The dialogue should be written in American English as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting, as if composed in a single letter. Finally, please state explicitly how confident you are in your reconstruction of the Confucian position, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure—particularly whether the argument from role-constituted identity avoids circularity when it is used to justify partial care.
