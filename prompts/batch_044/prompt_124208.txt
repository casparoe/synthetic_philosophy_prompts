Write an essay on whether moral status, as it tracks sentience, should be understood as genuinely gradational across entities ranging from simple feedback loops (thermostats, basic control systems) through organisms with distributed but non-centralised nervous architectures (cnidarians, certain insects) to fully integrated mammals, or whether any workable normative framework must ultimately rely on sharp thresholds despite the appearance of continuity. Weigh the arguments carefully: on one side, the phenomenological and functional continuity that makes binary distinctions seem arbitrary and potentially speciesist; on the other, the practical and theoretical need for decisive action-guidance in ethical reasoning, which risks paralysis if obligations scale smoothly with every incremental increase in neural complexity. In particular, examine whether a multi-dimensional 'capacity-vector' model—where sentience is decomposed into separable dimensions such as integrative processing, valenced experience, temporal persistence, and self-modelling, each carrying independent moral weights—can preserve the intuition of gradation without collapsing into an unworkable calculus of micro-obligations. Reach a definite conclusion: either defend a scalar framework accompanied by a principled decision rule for practical action, or argue that gradation is epistemically real but normatively inert, and specify which dimensions, if any, ought to serve as operative thresholds. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; construct the argument from first principles and conceptual analysis alone.
