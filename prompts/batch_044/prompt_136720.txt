You are the governance architect for a regional research consortium. Every month you evaluate proposals to replace a committee decision with a futarchic mechanism—stakeholders wager on outcomes, and the option with the best predicted performance on an agreed metric is adopted. Write a reusable decision procedure your staff can execute in forty-five minutes: an ordered sequence of gates, each with a precise question, the form of answer that permits passage, the conditions that trigger a hard stop (rejection), and the conditions that require escalation to a values committee because the issue is normative rather than empirical. Ground the gates in the actual mechanics of prediction-market governance—metric agreement, counterfactual resolution integrity (whether the outcome can be measured for the path actually taken without contamination from the betting mechanism itself), liquidity and expertise availability, Goodhart corruption risk, and the distinction between selecting an action to maximize a value and defining the value to be maximized—without requiring philosophical citation. Mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated by procedure.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) selecting one of three climate-adaptation infrastructure grants by predicted ten-year flood-damage reduction in dollars; (b) adopting a mandatory negative-results publication policy evaluated by a predicted five-year peer-reputation index; and (c) procuring an internal data-platform vendor by predicted two-year system-downtime hours. One of these must yield an unwelcome result—either a hard stop or an escalation—explain which gate produces it, and show why the sponsor's preference cannot override that gate. Conclude by identifying two classes of institutional decision for which the procedure is structurally unsuited regardless of data quality.
