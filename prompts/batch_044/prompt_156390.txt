Having spent decades in condensed-matter physics before turning to philosophy of mind, I am struck by how often split-brain cases are interpreted through binary metaphors—either one consciousness or two—without attention to the graded, dynamical nature of physical order. I ask you to construct a formal argument for the position that complete corpus callosotomy does not bifurcate consciousness into two distinct subjects, but instead produces a single conscious system undergoing a form of functional decoupling analogous to symmetry breaking in a physical ensemble: locally ordered domains emerge, yet they remain constituents of one globally defined system whose unity parameter—interhemispheric information integration—has merely fallen below a critical threshold rather than disappeared entirely. Defend the view that the dissociable behaviors observed in these patients are best explained not by the presence of a second, rival stream of experience, but by the dominant hemisphere's failure to access, reconcile, and update its own predictive models with those generated in the decoupled domain. You should treat unity of consciousness as a continuous, measurable property of integrated functional architecture rather than an all-or-nothing metaphysical status. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or author-specific citations; build the argument entirely from conceptual analysis and the internal logic of predictive integration, keeping the tone rigorous and suitable for an audience of philosophers skeptical about importing physical analogies into consciousness studies.
