I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind writing a dissertation chapter on mental causation, and I've noticed a recurring unstable posture in debates over the causal exclusion argument that I can't quite map onto the standard taxonomy. Here is the pattern, described concretely. A philosopher wants to defend that my deciding to leave a lecture genuinely causes my walking out, without reducing that decision to the neural event that also causes the leg movement. They begin by conceding that the neurons are fully sufficient for the motion—replay the exact firing pattern in a zombie and the legs still move. But they insist the decision still does causal work because it "structures," "organizes," or "programs" which neural pattern occurs: it is like an architect's plan that determines the arrangement without pushing any brick. Then a critic asks: if the bricks are sufficient for the wall, and the plan doesn't push any brick, what causal work remains for the plan? At this point the defender usually says the plan explains why those particular bricks fired—making the mental state a cause of the cause, not of the movement itself. Immediately, however, they add that we shouldn't treat the plan and the bricks as two competing things; they are one reality seen at different levels. Once they've said that, though, they revert to treating mental explanations as indispensable and non-redundant—exactly the claim they seemed to have surrendered.

I see this oscillation repeatedly: mental properties are assigned an architectural role that sounds causally potent, but whenever you try to pin down whether that potency affects the physical outcome, affects only the prior physical cause, or is merely descriptive, the answer slips among all three without settling. Is there an established name for this specific unstable role—this "architectural" or "structuring" causation that keeps sliding between being a prior cause of the physical, being identical to the physical arranged a certain way, and being causally redundant for the behavior? Or for the argumentative pattern itself, in which a defender of mental efficacy keeps retreating to a structural metaphor and then, under pressure, collapsing it into either reduction or epiphenomenalism, only to resurrect it in the very next move? I don't think it is just the standard exclusion argument, nor ordinary non-reductive physicalism, nor straightforward overdetermination, though it touches each. If there is no precise term, I'd like one coined with clear boundary conditions: when does a structural explanation count as genuinely causal in this domain, when is it merely redescriptive, and what distinguishes this particular slide from an ordinary motte-and-bailey? I'm preparing this for a seminar and want to avoid mislabeling what might actually be a recognized failure mode of mental-causal theories.
