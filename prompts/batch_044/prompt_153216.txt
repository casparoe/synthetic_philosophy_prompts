I've come to accept a particular framing of the cognitive penetration debate: that what philosophers typically classify as "beliefs altering perceptual experience" is in fact nothing more than the subpersonal calibration of sensory gain parameters—functionally continuous with ordinary adaptation and attentional modulation—rather than genuine doxastic influence on phenomenal content. On this view, there is no principled boundary between "cognitive penetration" and the routine top-down tuning that allows a radiologist to resolve an ambiguous shadow or a musician to parse a chord; the appearance of penetration arises only when we illegitimately project personal-level mental states onto subpersonal predictive-coding loops. I take this to dissolve the standard epistemic worry—that if beliefs can change what we see, perception loses its evidential privilege—because parameter calibration tracks environmental statistics rather than representing propositional commitments.

What would actually count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, not just the standard objections reheated.

Specifically: which empirical findings in perceptual neuroscience—say, about direct prefrontal-to-early-visual-cortex projections carrying semantic rather than purely attentional signals—would force me to concede that something genuinely doxastic is altering phenomenal character rather than merely modulating sensitivity? Which conceptual distinctions—perhaps between parameter tuning and content determination—would have to collapse for my framing to fail, and are there formal results from Bayesian modeling or information theory showing that predictive coding necessarily collapses into belief-influence at some level of description?

Also, what does this view genuinely risk predicting that rival accounts do not? If I am right, then controversial cases—such as emotional state altering color discrimination or expertise restructuring object recognition—should never involve a change in what it is like to see the stimulus, only in resolution or salience weighting. Is there an observation that would clearly violate that expectation? And have I qualified the view into unfalsifiability—by allowing any apparent case of belief-driven seeing to be redescribed as "just calibration"—and if so, is that insulation a problem for a view that claims to be making a substantive empirical claim rather than a terminological stipulation?

I would like you to distinguish what defenders of this "calibration-only" framing would themselves concede as damaging—perhaps a demonstration that personal-level beliefs can bypass predictive mechanisms entirely and still alter phenomenal content—from what critics say but defenders could dismiss as category mistakes. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the position has become well-insulated in a way that undermines its philosophical interest.
