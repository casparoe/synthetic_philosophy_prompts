Consider a diachronic rationality framework in which an agent’s preferences are fully transitive and acyclic at every single time-slice, but in which the agent also bears a “consistency cost”—a disutility incurred whenever she revises a prior plan without an overriding reason. A strategic interlocutor can now construct a sequential betting scheme that never exploits intransitive preferences directly. Instead, at each stage the agent faces a choice between honoring an earlier commitment (avoiding the consistency cost but accepting a slightly worse outcome) and revising the plan (avoiding the worse outcome but paying the consistency cost). Because the consistency cost is calibrated to the agent’s own prior valuations, she rationally revises at every step; yet over a closed loop of such revisions she ends up back at her initial state minus the accumulated costs, with no net gain in welfare. This is not the classic Dutch-book for cyclical preferences, but a “commitment-cycle money pump” that targets diachronic coherence itself.

What are the most important implications of this possibility for theories of diachronic rationality? In particular: does it show that diachronic consistency is not a rational virtue but a strategic vulnerability; does it require us to distinguish between local rationality at each decision node and global rationality across the sequence in a stronger way than standard planning theories allow; and does it undermine the use of precommitment devices, or instead reveal that any such device must be designed with an explicit exit tax that prevents looping? Address these, and identify any further structural feature—for instance, about the nature of the consistency cost, the role of foresight, or the difference between causal and evidential reasoning in sequential contexts—that determines whether the pump can actually be executed.
