I have been thinking on my own about what John Maynard Keynes believed makes life genuinely good, particularly in that essay where he imagines future generations having solved the economic problem and working only a few hours a week. Most discussions I have seen treat this as a simple prediction about leisure and consumption, but I think Keynes was describing a deeper moral transformation. He distinguishes between absolute needs and relative needs, and he warns that the love of money as a possession is a kind of morbid obsession. From this I draw a principle: in Keynes's vision of the good life, economic comparison with others becomes morally irrelevant. I want to argue that this makes his view essentially Stoic — not in the sense of suppressing emotion, but in the sense that self-worth is detached from external goods like income or status.

Yet there is a tension I cannot resolve. Keynes moved in the Bloomsbury circle, and he was clearly influenced by G. E. Moore's idea that certain states of mind — aesthetic experience, intimate friendship — are intrinsically valuable. So the good life for Keynes cannot be purely negative (merely being free from economic anxiety); it must also be positively cultivated through art, conversation, and affection. This raises a problem: if one must actively cultivate these goods, does that not reintroduce a kind of purposefulness and even competition — for example, seeking the best companions or the finest art — that conflicts with the Stoic indifference I described? I am not sure whether Keynes intended these two strands to coexist, or whether one should be read as primary.

Could you please comment on these notes for an essay? I would particularly value your view on whether my Stoic reading is accurate to Keynes's text, whether the tension with Bloomsbury aestheticism is real or only apparent, and whether the overall interpretation is interesting enough to pursue. Please respond in British English, and do not provide a bibliography or list of further reading.
