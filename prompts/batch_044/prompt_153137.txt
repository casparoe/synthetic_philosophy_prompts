I'm preparing a long-form feature on how scientific belief actually gets revised, and I've become stuck on a tension inside Bayesian epistemology that popular accounts tend to flatten. The familiar problem is "old evidence": data you already possessed before formulating a theory should not, by Bayes's rule, raise your credence in that theory, yet scientists clearly treat long-known observations as confirming. The standard philosophical reply distinguishes genuinely predictive success—where the theory assigns high prior probability to data not yet used in its construction—from mere post-hoc accommodation, sometimes by insisting that the old evidence played no role in generating the theoretical framework.

But I'm interested in a more granular version of this puzzle that arises in hierarchical modeling. Consider a cosmologist who updates her beliefs about both the noise parameters of her instrument and the parameters of her cosmological model using the same dataset—say, cosmic microwave background measurements. At the first level, she conditions on those measurements to update hyperparameters governing instrument noise. At the second level, she uses the noise-corrected measurements to update her credence in competing cosmological theories. Formally, the joint posterior over instrument-level and theory-level parameters factorizes in a specific conditional structure. Conceptually, however, the very same measurements are doing double duty: they are "old" relative to the cosmological theory if she already relied on them to calibrate the instrument, yet they are "new" relative to the hyperparameters if the noise model was initially unspecified.

Please explain the conceptual issue here. Does this hierarchical partition genuinely resolve the old-evidence problem by relocating the belief update to a different level of the model, or does it merely displace the bootstrapping worry upward? If the calibration step and the theory-testing step are both embedded in a single joint inference, in what precise sense—if any—can the cosmologist claim that the data provide predictive rather than merely accommodative support for her cosmological model? And more generally, what does this nested case reveal about whether Bayesian coherence, by itself, can draw a normative line between genuine predictive success and post-hoc fitting when an agent's belief state is structured across interdependent hypotheses?
