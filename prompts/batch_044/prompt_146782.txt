I cover the history of economic ideas for a British magazine, and in recent discussions with colleagues about Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomia and chrematistics, I have become interested in how these ancient categories might illuminate entirely modern forms of exchange. For an upcoming feature, I would like you to write a short story—roughly 600 to 900 words—that translates these concerns into a fictional setting without simply rehearsing the familiar textbook examples. Specifically, imagine a closed coastal settlement in the near future where conventional currency has been abolished and replaced by an algorithmic system of ‘attention credits’ that accrue through social interaction and are traded for necessities. The protagonist should be someone who initially believes this system restores exchange to its natural purpose—meeting household needs—only to realise that the credits have become an object of unlimited accumulation, decoupled from use and governed by a logic of perpetual growth that Aristotle would have recognised as chrematistic. Please render this realisation through concrete scenes and character relationships rather than explicit philosophical exposition, maintaining a contemplative, literary tone throughout. The piece should be suitable for an educated general audience and must be composed in British English. I do not require any scholarly apparatus, citations, or reading suggestions—only the narrative itself.
