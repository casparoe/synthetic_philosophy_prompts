I am a journalist covering science and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I am pitching our editorial board to commission a concise, standalone research proposal—approximately fifteen hundred words, deliverable within one semester—for an independent philosophical project. The question I want precisely framed is this: Bernard Williams's integrity objection, particularly the scenario in which a moral agent introduces a deliberative step that seems to betray personal attachment—the situation commonly summarized as involving one thought too many—is standardly read as a critique of utilitarian reasoning in intimate, face-to-face contexts. Yet the objection is rarely tested against institutional or bureaucratic settings, where emotional detachment is not merely tolerated but structurally required. Does Williams's critique retain its normative force when the decision-maker is an institutional agent—such as a public-health allocator or a governance architect—who is expected to suppress precisely the emotional attachments that Williams treats as constitutive of integrity? More specifically, can we draw a principled distinction between institutional roles in which the absence of that extra deliberative step represents a failure of integrity, and roles in which its absence is the correct fulfillment of professional duty?

The proposal must state this research question with enough precision that it admits of a determinate answer; explain why existing treatments of Williams's integrity objection leave the institutional dimension unresolved; outline a conceptual rather than empirical approach—specifically, an analysis of role morality, the conditions for integrity, and the normative status of emotional distance in bureaucratic structures; describe what a successful result would look like (for example, a typology of institutional roles mapped against integrity-preserving and integrity-abandoning emotional postures), what would count as failure (for instance, a collapse of the distinction into unexamined intuition), and provide an honest assessment of scope: what can be achieved in one semester, where the argument risks becoming unwieldy, and which extensions should be marked for future work. The deliverable should be useful to readers who design or evaluate institutional decision procedures, not only to academic moral philosophers. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation inventory; refer to arguments and positions by their conceptual content, and note that full references will be appended separately. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for an editorial board that includes scientists and policy professionals without specialist training in philosophy.
