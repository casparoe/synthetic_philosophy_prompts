As a retired physicist who has recently turned to the philosophy of education, I have been reading Locke's writings on education in parallel with modern literature on innate cognitive architecture. In a recent seminar, a participant dismissed objections about genetic predispositions in learning with the line that the mind is born a blank slate—invoking Locke to suggest that education can, in principle, reshape any disposition without biological constraint. I am convinced the phrase is carrying more weight than it ought, but before I reply I should like its commitments separated with the precision I once applied to physical models.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that the slogan tabula rasa (or the mind is born a blank slate) might actually be expressing in this educational context? For each reading, indicate whether it amounts to a platitude, a substantive and defensible interpretation of Locke, a contentious modern extrapolation, or something plainly false. Please include at least:
(i) the epistemological claim that there are no innate ideas;
(ii) the psychological claim that there are no innate behavioural tendencies or temperamental differences;
(iii) the pedagogical claim that environmental input is the sole determinant of character;
(iv) the metaphysical claim that the self possesses no pre-social structure;
and any further formulations you judge belong on the menu.

Then tell me which of these readings was likely being invoked to dismiss the genetic-predisposition objection, and whether that reading survives contact with Locke's own recognition of innate active powers, his insistence on physical hardiness and observable temperamental variation among children, and his warning—often neglected—that associative learning can corrupt just as readily as it can cultivate. I should be grateful for a formal reply in British English, structured as a disambiguation with verdicts rather than a single authoritative interpretation.
