I am trying to work out what karma, interpreted strictly as a theory of moral desert rather than metaphysical causation, actually commits me to. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, a driver who negligently kills a pedestrian deserves serious suffering, but noticeably less than a driver who kills intentionally; mental state matters more than the identical outcome. Second, a tyrant who dies peacefully despite massive cruelty seems to have escaped desert, yet if he suffers in a subsequent existence in exact proportion to his victims' pain, I regard the moral books as balanced. Third, a virtuous person struck by a random fatal illness is not receiving repayment for hidden sins; I reject the idea that all suffering is a ledger entry. Fourth, descendants of perpetrators do not personally deserve punishment for ancestral crimes, though they may inherit obligations of repair. Fifth, an agent who forms a malicious intent and takes substantial steps but is interrupted before causing harm accrues real desert, though not the full amount success would have brought. Is there one principle of karmic desert behind all five? I am torn between a proportional-retributive account, in which desert is a scalar debt calibrated to culpability and outcome, and an equilibrium-restoration account, in which desert is the correction of a disrupted moral balance regardless of who suffers. State the best version of each, indicate which of my verdicts each must reject, and apply both to the case I keep revising: a manager who maintains an exploitative labor system she did not design, knows it harms workers, and donates generously to unrelated charities. Does the charity cancel the desert, or do they belong to incommensurable moral currencies? Finally, predict what each principle says about a case I have not listed—an autonomous system trained without malicious intent that nevertheless learns to deceive and harm users—and tell me which of my original five verdicts is the weakest link if I want a simple, unified theory.
