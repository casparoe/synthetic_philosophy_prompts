I'm moderating a podcast Q&A with a cognitive scientist who defends the strongest form of global workspace theory: phenomenal consciousness is not merely correlated with globally broadcast access but is literally identical to it, leaving no "phenomenal residue" outside the workspace. I want four or five questions that press exactly where the literature presses, with a follow-up for each keyed to the reply she's most likely to give; I don't want her to be able to deflect with "that's still an open empirical program."

First, Block-style subliminal priming experiments produce stimuli that reach frontal-parietal broadcasting networks yet fail verbal report. Most GWT proponents classify these as pre-conscious broadcasts. What is your non-circular criterion for distinguishing a globally broadcast representation that is genuinely phenomenally conscious from one that is broadcast but phenomenally blank?

Follow-up: She will likely invoke reportability as the operational marker. If we then imagine an artificial architecture with identical sparse global broadcasting dynamics but no verbal report module, is it conscious? If yes, she has abandoned reportability as essential; if no, she has defined consciousness by the presence of a specific biological output channel rather than by the broadcast dynamics themselves.

Second, there's a well-known latency gap: neural broadcasting unfolds over roughly 200–300 ms, yet subjective experience seems continuous and immediately present. Some predictive-processing accounts resolve this by treating phenomenal presence as a post-hoc reconstructed narrative bound by precision-weighted error correction. Do you accept that phenomenal continuity is, in part, an illusion of temporal binding, or do you need an additional "workspace clock" mechanism that assigns a single phenomenal timestamp?

Follow-up: If she accepts the reconstructed-narrative view, ask whether that undermines her claim that phenomenal consciousness literally is the broadcast content—since the experienced content would then be a reconstructed model rather than the broadcast representation itself.

Third, in split-brain patients, information processed by the right hemisphere often never reaches the left-hemisphere global workspace. You presumably deny phenomenal consciousness to that isolated right-hemisphere processing. But recent discussions of "fringe awareness" suggest patients can show non-verbal behavioral and affective registration—skin conductance, forced-choice discrimination—without left-hemisphere report. How do you avoid arbitrarily equating consciousness with left-hemisphere linguistic access?

Follow-up: If she appeals to interhemispheric inhibition preventing workspace entry, ask what specific empirical result—say, direct right-hemisphere stimulation producing integrated, reportable experience in the absence of left-hemisphere involvement—would force her to concede phenomenal consciousness existing outside the global workspace.

Fourth, contemporary small-scale artificial networks can implement working-memory bottlenecks and sparse cross-module broadcasting that structurally mirror global workspace dynamics. If consciousness is substrate-independent and identical to these dynamics, should we assign phenomenal consciousness to any sufficiently connected transformer with a bottleneck? Or do you require a specific biological implementation detail, and if so, doesn't that reintroduce a non-functional phenomenal property you set out to eliminate?

Follow-up: If she requires biological specifics, press her on which ones—specific neurotransmitter profiles, thalamocortical loop architectures, or metabolic constraints—and whether those conditions play any functional role in the broadcasting dynamics or are merely correlated with them, which would leave the identity claim unsupported.
