I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, currently working on causal reasoning in distributed debugging tools. I've been trying to understand the long-standing philosophical disagreement between counterfactual theories of causation—roughly, the view that C causes E if, had C not occurred in the closest possible worlds, E would not have occurred—and interventionist theories, which analyze causation through structural equations and an explicit do-operator that represents external manipulation of variables.

In the literature, the debate appears stalled. Counterfactualists object that interventionist accounts fail to capture preemption and overdetermination without ad hoc restrictions, while interventionists respond that counterfactual dependence is either circular or too metaphysically loaded to be operationalized. Meanwhile, in computer science and engineering, the interventionist framework is treated as a settled formalism, and the metaphysical objections are rarely addressed. This makes me wonder whether the stall is genuine or an artifact of two groups answering different questions.

My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about empirical facts or logical inconsistencies, but about what a causal account is required to deliver: ontological grounding, predictive control, or algorithmic identifiability. Specifically, I suspect counterfactualists are asking what makes one event depend on another in reality, while interventionists are asking what procedure lets us compute the effect of an action—and that these are not competing answers but answers to distinct problems that happen to share vocabulary. I also wonder whether a hidden formal divergence explains the impasse: for instance, whether the two frameworks implicitly disagree about the transitivity of causal chains in asynchronous, non-intervenable systems where confounders cannot be experimentally isolated, a situation common in distributed logs but rarely modeled in philosophical examples.

Could you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement? Is it verbal, with causation functioning as a homonym across disciplines? Is it substantive but masked, with one framework denying conditions that the other takes for granted about unobserved variables? Or is it a normative divergence about whether causation must be reducible to non-causal facts?

Furthermore, given your diagnosis, what would count as actual progress? Would it require a formal bridge linking possible-world semantics to structural equation models under specific boundary conditions? A conceptual distinction that separates metaphysical dependence from epistemic intervention? Or should the field accept a principled pluralism, recognizing that the two frameworks serve incompatible but legitimate purposes?
