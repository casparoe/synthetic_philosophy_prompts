I chair an emergency resource-allocation committee for a regional disaster-response agency. Every week we receive more clinic aid requests than we can evaluate thoroughly; we have ninety minutes per file, incomplete post-event data, and a hard deadline before the next storm season. Rather than attempting an optimal allocation, we want a reusable decision procedure grounded in satisficing and bounded rationality—using aspiration-level thresholds for “good enough” demonstrated need, a recognition-primed stopping rule for information gathering, adaptive reference points drawn from the prior three allocations, and explicit hand-off points where value judgment must replace calculation. Write the procedure: the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it halts with approval or rejection, and where it requires human deliberation because the issue is one of values rather than facts. Then apply it to three requests: a rural clinic asking for backup generators after a mild winter storm that caused no infrastructure damage; an urban clinic with adequate current stock requesting preemptive trauma-kit upgrades justified only by projected future climate risk; and a clinic that clearly meets every aspiration threshold but serves a population with lower documented mortality risk, so that approval feels inequitable relative to past distributions. Include the case where the procedure yields a recommendation the committee is likely to resist, and state precisely what the procedure cannot settle—especially regarding opportunity costs across unknown future requests and equity adjustments that fall outside the aspiration levels.
