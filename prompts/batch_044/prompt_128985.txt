I am preparing a session for a postgraduate philosophy seminar — twelve students, ninety minutes — and I want them to feel, before I ever name it, the precise friction that arises in interpersonal utility comparisons when the metrics of well-being are not merely quantitatively different but structurally incommensurable. Rather than delivering a lecture on aggregation problems, please design a structured deliberation or simulation in which each participant is assigned a hidden 'normative profile' (pure hedonism, preference-satisfaction, an objective-list account, or a capabilities-based framework) and must collectively allocate scarce resources across four fictional individuals whose welfare reports are rendered exclusively in the vocabulary of that profile. The rules should compel the group to make forced comparative judgements — for example, deciding whether to fund a treatment for Person A or Person B — while explicitly forbidding any translation of the reports into a shared scale or common currency. Include clear roles, a payoff or decision structure that does not require real money but creates genuine tension, a timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments of deadlock or arbitrary resolution onto the philosophical problem of comparison. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I should manage it, and be frank about which feature of the comparison problem — perhaps the absence of any neutral arbiter — the exercise cannot reproduce and I must supply verbally.

Before drafting the activity, please think carefully about the pedagogical dynamics and take a moment to brainstorm the structural conditions that make the failure of comparison feel unavoidable rather than merely theoretical. You are an expert in philosophical pedagogy and experiential learning design; respond in American English, even though this request is written in British English. The group should come away seeing why a common metric is not just hard to find but conceptually contested, and the debrief should name the problem only after the participants have already lived through its pressure. Make sure the facilitator does not reveal the formal label until the final ten minutes, and note which aspect of inter-theoretic value comparison — for instance, the question of whether one framework is privileged — remains outside the reach of the simulation.
