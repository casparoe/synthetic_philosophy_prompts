I've been working through population ethics with a reading group, and we keep running into the same frustration: every proposed resolution of the nonidentity problem seems to dissolve one intuition by sacrificing another. Before I evaluate any more accounts, I want the full specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate resolution must satisfy — not the resolution itself, but the checklist of what it has to deliver. The framework must account for the classic comparison cases: the choice between conceiving now versus later under conditions that affect welfare, and the intergenerational policy choice where a riskier path produces one population and a safer path produces another with no overlap in membership. It must preserve the distinction between harming a particular person and failing to confer a benefit, while explaining why the intuition that existence under hardship can be wrong survives even though the alternative for that specific individual is non-existence. Crucially, include the replacement problem as it arises in long-term climate ethics: if our carbon policies determine not just welfare levels but which specific individuals come to exist centuries hence, any resolution must specify whether changing the identity pool counts as harming anyone, and whether that verdict depends on adopting a four-dimensionalist versus endurantist view of personal identity over time. For each requirement, give the case or conceptual pressure behind it; flag any pair you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, preserving a strictly person-affecting restriction while also condemning climate inaction that reshuffles future identities. Then, briefly, score how a person-affecting restriction, a wide impersonal consequentialist account, and an actualist view of harm fare against this list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating tensions rather than on the scoring; I'm trying to see whether the problem lies in the solutions or in the list itself.
