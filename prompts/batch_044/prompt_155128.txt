Two members of my study group—Jordan and Casey—got into it after I mentioned Hilbert's hotel in passing. The disagreement turned on whether the hotel is a legitimate intuition pump for actual infinity or a disguised appeal to potential infinity, with Cantor's results brought in to settle the matter. Here is the exchange as it unfolded.

(1) Jordan said Hilbert's hotel demonstrates that actual infinity is conceptually coherent. A fully occupied countably infinite hotel can still accept one more guest by shifting each occupant from room n to room n+1. The occupancy is actual—every room is filled—yet there is room for more, which is precisely the non-paradoxical behavior we should expect from a completed infinity.

(2) Casey replied that this is sleight of hand. The shift describes an infinite sequence of discrete actions. If you take actual infinity seriously, there is no last move to complete, so the scenario implicitly treats infinity as potential—always in the process of being extended—not as a finished totality. The coherence Jordan sees comes from smuggling in potentialist dynamics.

(3) Jordan countered that the hotel's state at any given time is fully actualized: every room n is occupied. There is no temporal construction. The shift is a static mapping, not a process executed in time. Potentialism concerns becoming; Hilbert's hotel concerns being.

(4) Casey then introduced what they called the assignment-gap argument. Even granting the static mapping, Cantor's theorem shows there is no surjection from the natural numbers onto the power set of the natural numbers. Applied to the hotel: there are subsets of guests—uncountably many—such that no room-assignment scheme can place exactly that group in the hotel. So if the manager promises that any group can be accommodated, the promise fails. This, Casey said, undermines the idea that an actual infinite totality is fully manageable or complete.

(5) Jordan responded that Casey is conflating cardinality with administrative feasibility. Cantor's result is a structural feature of sets, not a managerial failure. The finitist, by contrast, cannot even make sense of why some infinite collections are strictly larger than others, since denying actual infinity collapses all talk of larger infinite sets into confusion about potential processes. The assignment gap actually supports actualism by showing that infinite structures have definite, non-arbitrary properties.

(6) Casey's final move was decision-theoretic. Suppose the manager must accommodate an infinite sequence of buses, each with countably infinite passengers, and each shift takes a fixed unit of time. Because there is no final bus, the manager never reaches a state where all are settled. Jordan said this confuses physical execution with mathematical existence; Casey said that if actual infinity is to be more than a formal game, it should be able to guide rational action, and an infinite task with no termination point is exactly the kind of practical incoherence that should make us suspicious of treating infinity as actual.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both are missing about the relationship between Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's theorem, and finitist objections. Do not split the difference; take a position on whether the assignment-gap argument actually damages actualism or is a category error.
