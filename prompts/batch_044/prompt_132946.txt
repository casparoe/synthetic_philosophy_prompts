I am drafting an essay for a philosophy of action seminar that takes up the classic case of Sphex ichneumoneus—the digger wasp that, when its prey is moved during its burrow-inspection routine, will repeat the inspection cycle indefinitely rather than adapt its sequence. Rather than treating this as a simple parable of instinct versus reason, I want to distinguish what I call "recursive protocol lock" from mere behavioral repetition. On my view, the wasp is not just stuck in a loop; it is stuck in a loop that it cannot represent to itself as a loop. I then argue that human analogues—compulsive checking, bureaucratic ritual, or even the habit of refreshing an already-empty inbox—are not less sphexish, but sphexish at a different layer: where the wasp locks at the level of motor sequencing, the human typically locks at the level of goal-interpretation, subtly redefining the task (for example, "making sure nothing was missed") so that the protocol remains intact. From this I draw a tentative conclusion about free will: agency is not the absence of deterministic loops, but the presence of a specific meta-cognitive capacity—what I call second-order loop detection—that allows an agent to suspend a protocol without dissolving the underlying goal that made it rational in the first place.

I am concerned that I am smuggling in a computational metaphor—protocols, loops, meta-cognitive detection—that does not do real philosophical work, and that my distinction between motor-level and interpretive-level lock may collapse once we consider cases like severe obsessive-compulsive disorder, where a human subject seems genuinely unable to break a motor-level ritual despite recognizing its futility. I also worry that my conclusion risks tautology: if agency just is second-order loop detection, then defining free will in those terms makes the argument circular rather than substantive. I would appreciate comments on three points: first, whether the layer-distinction between wasp-like and human-like rigidity holds up, or whether there are clear counterexamples that blur it; second, whether the computational framing genuinely illuminates the free-will problem or merely re-describes it in technical language; and third, whether my tentative conclusion—that free will should be analyzed as a specific kind of meta-cognitive break in recursive execution—survives once we separate behavioral flexibility from representational capacity. If the framing seems forced, I would also welcome suggestions for a less metaphor-laden way to articulate the difference between being trapped in a routine and being able to step outside it while preserving one's ends.
