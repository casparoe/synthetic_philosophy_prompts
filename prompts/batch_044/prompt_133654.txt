I have been studying Douglas Hofstadter's Person Paper on Purity in Language, and I am struck by how the satirical inversion—systematically substituting racial markers for gendered pronouns—seems to establish a formal isomorphism between sexist and racist exclusion in language. If this structural correspondence holds generally, then the critique should extend necessarily to any linguistic system that encodes social categories grammatically. I am particularly trying to determine whether Hofstadter's framework implies that languages with obligatory grammatical gender, such as French or German, embed the same kind of normative default that he identifies in English generic masculinity. Or does the satire depend on a hidden premise—perhaps tied to the specifically semantic rather than morphological nature of English pronouns—that prevents the analogy from traveling across linguistic structures? More broadly, if one accepts that unmarked categories carry implicit normative authority, must one reject all grammatically gendered agreement systems as equally exclusionary, or is there a principled way to distinguish morphological convention from the social choices Hofstadter targets?
