I am a retired biochemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons and the subsequent literature on distributive justice. Next Thursday I present at an interdisciplinary moral-philosophy seminar—mostly analytic ethicists specializing in distributive justice, plus two welfare economists—on what I call "saturating prioritarianism." The thesis is that the moral weight of an increment of well-being should not be a constant declining linear weight (the standard prioritarian curve), but a sigmoidal saturation curve analogous to enzyme-substrate kinetics: near-maximum weight at the extreme lower tail, steep decline through the middle range, and asymptotic near-zero weight above a saturation threshold. I argue this blocks the leveling-down objection without collapsing into pluralist egalitarianism, because leveling everyone downward eliminates the high-weight gains that would justify downward redistribution, while any downward leveling that actually benefits the worst-off can be evaluated directly on the weighted sum. I need a speaker's prep sheet: the objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and damaging they are, phrased in the vocabulary an analytic ethicist or welfare economist would use; for each, whether I should concede ground, hold firm, or reframe; the one reply that has to land for the proposal to survive; and—crucially—the weakest structural point in my position, namely the risk that the saturation parameter looks normatively arbitrary (like picking a Michaelis constant by convention) and how to defend it without appealing to empirical biology. Please also include the objection I am hoping nobody asks: whether my view licenses withholding life-saving treatment from those who have exceeded the saturation threshold if the resources could instead lift someone from near-zero well-being to the steep part of the curve, and how to answer it under time pressure without sounding like a threshold-utilitarian.
