The debate over Mary’s room—that a scientist who knows every physical fact about color vision nevertheless learns something new upon seeing red—has remained largely static for decades, with both sides recycling familiar moves about phenomenal concepts, acquaintance knowledge, and the completeness of physics. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a survey of positions. Specifically, is the deadlock sustained by an ambiguity in what counts as physical information, where one side means information stateable in the vocabulary of a completed fundamental physics and the other means information capturable by any third-person structural description, even one that includes relational or perspectival features? Are they therefore not genuinely disagreeing about whether phenomenal properties exist, but about whether a particular representational format can be saturated without first-person access? Reconstruct the implicit disagreement, distinguish what is substantive from what is merely verbal or framing-dependent, and propose a precise conceptual distinction—say, between ontological completeness and representational saturation—that might either dissolve the impasse or reveal that the sides are answering different questions under one heading. Finally, tell me what kind of result could actually move this debate: a formal theorem about descriptive completeness, an empirical finding about concept acquisition in sensory-deprived subjects, a disciplined phenomenological report, or nothing at all, and justify your answer with reference to the diagnosis you have given.
