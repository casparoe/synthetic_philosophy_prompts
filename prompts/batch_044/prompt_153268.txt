Student answer: The symbol grounding problem arises when the meanings of symbols in a formal system are defined entirely by their relations to other symbols, generating an infinite regress that can only be halted by anchoring some representations to the world through non-symbolic, direct means. This regress was first clearly articulated by Searle in his critique of strong artificial intelligence, though he did not develop the remedy. Harnad’s contribution was to propose that the necessary anchoring comes from direct sensorimotor capacities—the ability to discriminate, identify, and describe objects through bodily interaction rather than symbolic mediation. He formalized this anchoring mechanism in what he called the Total Turing Test, which requires that a system exhibit categorical perception and robotic interaction as a precondition for genuine semantic content. A predictive language model trained solely on text distributions clearly fails this requirement: it manipulates syntactic patterns without any proprioceptive or perceptual contact with referents, functioning essentially as an enlarged Chinese Room in which statistical correlation substitutes for intentionality. Nevertheless, if such a model is embedded within an architecture that integrates continuous proprioceptive feedback—what has recently been termed embodied tokenization—the situation changes. In these hybrid systems, multimodal prediction-error minimization creates a functional dynamics that is equivalent to direct sensorimotor categorization. The symbols are therefore grounded not through explicit one-to-one pairing with objects, but through the continuous flow of cross-modal inference. Hence, Harnad’s demand for direct coupling is satisfied in all but name, and a text-trained model within such an architecture can legitimately be said to possess grounded symbols.
