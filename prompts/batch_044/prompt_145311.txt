Consider the institutional practice—common in government agencies, corporations, and large administrative bodies—of evaluating employee conduct after harmful outcomes through a protocol-adherence audit. In this audit, investigators ask primarily whether the actor followed approved standard operating procedures and whether they held formal authorization to deviate. If protocol was followed, the actor is typically cleared regardless of whether they exercised independent moral judgment about the human consequences of their actions; if they deviated without authorization, they face severe discipline, even when the deviation was intended to prevent harm. Concurrently, these organizations often mandate ethics-training modules that treat moral competence as mastery of compliance checklists rather than as the cultivation of judgment, imagination, or attentiveness to others. Some organizational ethicists have recently labeled the implicit standard here procedural innocence: the idea that conformity to internal rules functions as a complete moral shield, producing what might be called bureaucratic moral immunity.
