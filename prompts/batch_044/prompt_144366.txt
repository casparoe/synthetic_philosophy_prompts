You are grading a take-home exam for an upper-level seminar on historical forecasting and institutional epistemology. The course instructor posed the following question:

"One debate in forecasting concerns whether the 'outside view' derived from imperial lifespans can be applied to modern democratic federations. A researcher proposes estimating the remaining lifespan of such a federation—defined as a multi-state polity with a common currency, distributed military sovereignty, and representative governance at both federal and state levels—by constructing a reference class from the 26 longest-lived imperial and compound polities in standard historical datasets (Rome, Byzantium, the Holy Roman Empire, Qing China, the Ottoman Empire, etc.). The researcher computes a mean lifespan and median time-to-collapse, then applies an adjustment factor for modern information technology and nuclear deterrence, treating the result as a base-rate prior.

Critically evaluate this proposal. Your answer should address: (i) whether imperial polities and democratic federations belong in the same reference class; (ii) how base-rate adjustments for technological and strategic differences should be performed without the reasoning collapsing entirely into an inside view; and (iii) whether an alternative reference class—specifically the seven 18th- and 19th-century compound republics with asymmetric fiscal authority (e.g., the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Dutch Republic, the Republic of Venice)—would yield a more appropriate forecast, and what using that class would change about the prior."

Below is the answer submitted by a student (identified only as "Student M."):

"The proposal is mostly sound. The 26 empires form the right reference class because they are the best-documented long-lived polities, with a mean lifespan near 220 years. If a democratic federation was formed around 1950, the raw base rate suggests a likely collapse window around 2170. The proposal's adjustment for nuclear deterrence should be upward rather than downward—because nuclear weapons reduce the probability of total war, they extend expected lifespan—so applying a downward factor is an error.

Regarding the compound republics, these are poor substitutes. The Dutch Republic lasted roughly two centuries, Venice about a millennium, and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth about 226 years, which is actually close to the imperial mean. Because their lifespans overlap so much with the imperial distribution, switching reference classes does not materially alter the base rate. Moreover, none of these polities was democratic in the modern representative sense, so they share even less with the target entity.

The deeper problem is that the proposal mixes outside and inside reasoning. Once you begin adjusting for currency unions, nuclear arsenals, or information technology, you have abandoned the outside view. As Philip Tetlock argues, the correct forecasting method is to adhere strictly to the outside view and never update the base rate with inside details; otherwise the exercise becomes subjective speculation. Therefore the researcher should either use the raw imperial mean without adjustment or abandon the reference-class method entirely."
