I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for first-year internal-medicine residents. They have substantial clinical experience but no formal philosophy training. The concept I need them to grasp is not the generic killing-versus-letting-die distinction, but a specific structural variant I will call the maintained-omission problem: an omission becomes morally active in the same register as a killing when an agent continuously sustains the institutional or physical conditions that prevent rescue, rather than merely failing to intervene in a one-off situation.

Plan the explanatory arc for the session. First, state the dependency order: which idea must be secured before the residents can recognize a maintained omission (for instance, the distinction between a baseline that is naturally given and one that is engineered by prior choices), and which idea must come only after that is stable. Second, specify the one carrying case—realistic, clinical, and not the familiar ventilator-withdrawal scenario—describing exactly what features it needs (e.g., an ongoing resource-allocation protocol the physician co-authored and continues to enforce) and what it must lack (e.g., a sudden, unanticipated emergency decision made in isolation). Third, map the misconceptions they will form in sequence—likely beginning with “if my hands do not touch the patient, my agency is not involved,” then “institutional rules diffuse personal responsibility,” then “withholding care is always less active than withdrawing it”—and indicate the precise point in the arc where each misconception should be headed off, together with the specific distinction or question you would deploy at that point. Fourth, design the final check for understanding: one brief judgment task or modified scenario that reveals whether they have grasped the structural claim rather than merely feeling moral discomfort. Fifth, say explicitly what standard frameworks—such as the four-principles approach or a simple utilitarian calculus—should be named and set aside at this level, with a brief reason why each obscures the maintained-omission point. Finally, identify where your reasoning about this sequence is most likely to fail: what clinical objection or feature of real-world triage would undermine the claim that maintained omissions belong in the same moral category as killings, and what specific consideration or counterexample would change your mind about either the suitability of this case or the coherence of the maintained-omission framework itself.
