Analyze the emergence and sustained operation of Bitcoin since its 2009 introduction as a case study in the social ontology of money. Standard social-constructionist accounts—particularly those that treat monetary kinds as institutional status functions maintained by collective intentionality—presume some form of centralized or socially recognized authority that assigns and enforces the status. Bitcoin appears to function as a store of value and medium of exchange without any such authority, relying instead on distributed cryptographic verification and peer-to-peer consensus mechanisms. Examine the episode through the lens of a distinction between "institutional kinding," where a social kind is stabilized by explicit collective acceptance of assigned functions within a recognized institutional framework, and "protocol kinding," where the kind is stabilized by algorithmic rules and distributed verification that do not depend on collective intentionality about status assignment. What does Bitcoin's persistence and partial monetary acceptance show about whether institutional backing is necessary for a social kind to achieve stability? Where does the protocol-kinding framework misdescribe the social practices surrounding Bitcoin—such as exchange platforms, regulatory recognition, and user conventions—and what does that reveal about hidden institutional dependencies in apparently decentralized kinds? Conclude by saying whether this case strengthens or undermines social-constructionist theories of money that require collective intentionality, and identify one feature of the episode that neither framework explains well.
