The debate over whether transformative experiences—such as becoming a parent or undergoing deep religious conversion—undermine rational choice has stalled in a peculiar way. One side argues that the problem is purely epistemic: before the change, you cannot know what the experience is like, so you cannot assign accurate subjective values to outcomes. The other side replies that knowledge is not the issue; even with perfect information, your preference architecture is replaced, meaning the pre-transformation agent is evaluating outcomes for a psychologically or numerically different subject. Despite years of exchange, neither camp has revised its core position. My hypothesis is that the stall is not about decision theory but about an unargued metaphysical commitment: the first camp implicitly requires diachronic psychological continuity for rational agency, while the second camp treats identity discontinuity as compatible with rational evaluation, and neither side has defended that premise. Is that correct, or is there a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about whether intertemporal choice theory needs an identity-stability axiom—masquerading as an epistemic dispute? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually claims about value assignment and personal identity, say whether it is verbal, substantive, or bedrock, and tell me what—if anything—would count as progress: a conceptual distinction between phenomenal ignorance and axiological discontinuity, a formal model of choice across identity stages, or a reframing that dissolves the problem.
