After retiring from statistical physics and turning seriously to moral philosophy, I have found myself returning repeatedly to consequentialist cluelessness—the problem that bounded agents lack the predictive access required to maximize expected good across long causal chains. In physics we often work with effective theories that are valid within a specific scale of ignorance; I am trying to understand whether moral reasoning can be structured analogously, without pretending to omniscience about microstates.

Please rank the following five responses to consequentialist cluelessness in tiers from S (most philosophically robust for an agent who must actually choose) down to D (least robust), and in one or two sentences justify what specifically earns or costs each strategy its tier:

- The Bayesian credence strategy: form explicit subjective probabilities over distant outcomes and maximize expected value despite severe epistemic gaps.
- The rule-consequentialist fallback: abandon act-level optimization in favor of rules that have demonstrated historical or evolutionary correlation with welfare.
- The satisficing-default strategy: intervene only when a high-confidence lower bound exceeds a harm-avoidance floor; otherwise default to non-action.
- The evolutionary-heuristic deference strategy (a framing I am testing and want taken seriously): treat culturally and biologically evolved moral intuitions as scale-invariant approximations—much like renormalized constants in physics—that remain robust precisely because they do not depend on fine-grained causal forecasting.
- The particularist dissolution: deny that general action-guiding principles exist and rely entirely on direct, context-sensitive moral perception.

I want a direct assessment rather than a literature survey. I am especially interested in whether the evolutionary-heuristic proposal avoids the impossible epistemic demands of the Bayesian approach without collapsing into uncritical conservatism, and where it lands relative to the rule-based and particularist alternatives.
