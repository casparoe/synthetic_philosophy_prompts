I'm working through some decision-theoretic material in a philosophy seminar, and we've been discussing I.J. Good's classic result that for a rational agent with an unrestricted choice of action, acquiring additional information before deciding has non-negative expected value — in other words, information can never harm you if you remain free to act on it optimally. The result seems straightforward until you encounter institutional cases where information is not merely instrumental but also carries expressive or dignitary weight. I want to apply the theorem to a real policy decision and see exactly where it holds up and where it falls silent.

Consider the 2020 English A-level grading controversy. After COVID-19 cancelled exams, Ofqual deployed an algorithm that incorporated historical school-level performance data to moderate teacher-submitted grades, producing widespread downgrades for students at schools with weaker track records; after sustained public protest, the government reversed course and allowed the teacher predictions to stand. Analyze this episode through Good's framework: identify the relevant decision-makers and the actions available to them upon receiving different information streams — the center-assessed grades, the historical school data, the teacher predictions, and the public reaction itself. Does the reversal indicate that the algorithm's information had negative value in some dimension that Good's theorem fails to register? In particular, inject the following distinction: sometimes information is instrumentally valuable for reaching a correct outcome, but sometimes its disclosure or use expresses a judgment about the moral status of the people it concerns — here, that students from high-performing schools were more credible than those from disadvantaged backgrounds. Does Good's result have anything to say about this expressive dimension, or does the case reveal that the theorem's scope is strictly limited to instrumental decision-making under conditions of uncoerced agency? And what does the episode show about whether the theorem depends on a hidden assumption that epistemic evaluation can be cleanly separated from moral evaluation?

Please separate what is settled about Good's result and its standard conditions — for instance, that it applies to agents with well-defined utility functions and genuinely free choice — from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of decision theory, such as whether there are robust counterexamples involving coercion, framing effects, or moral side-constraints, and do not present any contested interpretation of the theorem's boundaries as if it were the consensus view.
