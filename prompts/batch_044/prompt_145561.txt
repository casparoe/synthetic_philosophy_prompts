For a seminar on logic and its limits, I'm trying to fix my own view on what Gödel's theorems do and don't establish beyond arithmetic. Here is where I stand: (1) The first theorem applies only to formal systems that are consistent, recursively axiomatizable, and strong enough to represent primitive recursive arithmetic; within those bounds it proves there are true but unprovable sentences. (2) Arguments that invoke Gödel to claim human minds are non-computational, or that artificial intelligence faces principled barriers, confuse syntactic unprovability with functional or cognitive limitation and should be rejected. (3) Still, the separation of truth from derivability strikes me as revealing a structural feature of any rich, finitely specifiable framework—so I think ambitious programs to fully axiomatize physics or ethics will always leave genuine truths outside the system. (4) I also reject analogies between Gödelian incompleteness and empirical underdetermination, since the latter involves evidential insufficiency rather than syntactic limitation. (5) Finally, I hold that the second theorem doesn't undermine mathematical reliability, because consistency can be recovered in stronger meta-systems, and I think a parallel hierarchical strategy could address analogous gaps in formalized science.
