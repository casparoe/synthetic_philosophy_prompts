You have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on long-range urban planning and moral trade-offs, attended by city planners, architects, and philosophers who study population ethics. The audience is mixed: half work with aggregate welfare metrics and cost-benefit frameworks; half are skeptical that such metrics capture what matters about future lives.

Your task is to frame the day’s central tension through a concrete planning dilemma that has not yet been resolved in public debate. A proposed regional development could be built to accommodate ten million residents at a living standard that is, by conventional measures, adequate but not flourishing—stable shelter, sufficient nutrition, modest leisure—or it could be scaled to accommodate one million residents at a standard that permits genuine excellence: deep community attachment, rich cultural access, and substantial autonomy. The planners in the room will recognize this as a question of density, infrastructure cost, and aggregate output. Your remarks must show them, without alienating them, that it is also a live instance of a problem in population axiology.

Specifically, you should articulate—without jargon that loses the practitioners, and without false simplification for the theorists—why a framework that maximizes total well-being seems to favor the larger, merely adequate population over the smaller, excellent one, and why that result strikes many people as deeply wrong. The philosophical structure should constrain what you can say: you must not treat aggregate metrics as ethically neutral, you must not pretend the problem is solved, and you must not offer a hollow compromise that papers over the disagreement. At the same time, you should note that rejecting the totalist framing raises its own difficulties—such as whether we can coherently value potential lives, whether quality thresholds avoid arbitrariness, or whether averaging principles escape the pressure entirely—without settling which difficulty is fatal.

Please write these remarks. In doing so, separate what is well-established in the relevant philosophical literature (for example, the logical derivation of the repugnant conclusion within total utilitarian frameworks) from what remains genuinely contested among informed specialists (for example, whether critical-level utilitarianism successfully avoids the conclusion, whether the intuition against barely-worth-living populations is defensible, or whether person-affecting restrictions can be consistently maintained). Do not present any particular axiological position—whether totalist, average, critical-level, or person-affecting—as if it were the consensus view.
