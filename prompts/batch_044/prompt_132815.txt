As a doctoral candidate in comparative poetics, I have repeatedly noticed a configuration in translations of formally constrained verse—particularly in traditions where rhyme and syllabic architecture are structurally load-bearing, as with the problems raised by Marot's ton beau—yet I lack the precise vocabulary to isolate it. The pattern is this: a translator reproduces every formal feature—rhyme scheme, metre, stanzaic shape—yet in accommodating the target language's syntactic linearity, the logical priority of the original argument is silently inverted. For instance, where the source distributes a conditional denial across a quatrain whose rhyme binds the negation to its conclusion, an English version forced to front-load subordinate clauses in order to preserve the rhyme ends up asserting the very condition it ought to reject. Lexically each line remains defensible; structurally, the poem now argues the opposite of itself. The result is neither domestication nor foreignisation, nor an ordinary syntactic shift, but a macro-level misprision in which formal fidelity produces argumentative reversal.

Is there an established term in translation theory or the poetics of constraint for this specific interaction between preserved prosody and inverted logical architecture? If an approximate concept exists, how does it differ from macro-structural distortion and from intentional hermeneutic reinterpretation? And if there is no precise term, how ought one delimit the phenomenon—say, by requiring that every local choice remain justifiable while the global argument is reversed—so as to distinguish it clearly from plain mistranslation and from deliberate adaptation? Please reply in British English.
