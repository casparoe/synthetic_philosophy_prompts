At a seminar on metaphysics and engineering, two colleagues, Marcus and Elena, debated the identity of the RV Tethys, an oceanographic vessel that has operated continuously for forty years with every physical component gradually replaced, including sensors and propulsion modules that now allow it to dive to depths the original design never permitted. Marcus argued that the vessel remains the same ship because institutional identity tracks functional role and documented continuity: it carries the same registration number, serves the same research program, and the crew treats it as one enduring entity, so replacing parts is analogous to repairing a living organism that survives cellular turnover. Elena replied that this mistakes administrative convenience for metaphysical fact: since every original atom-level component has been removed and stored, and those stored parts have now been assembled into a second vessel in a dry dock that performs the original shallow-water surveys, there are two candidates with equal claim, and labeling one the true Tethys is arbitrary. Marcus countered that the reconstructed dry-dock vessel is merely a replica, because genuine identity requires spatiotemporal continuity of the functioning whole rather than mere material provenance, and gathering old parts in one place breaks the causal chain of the ship's lived operational history. Elena answered that this begs the question by assuming continuity must be spatial and temporal, when the old parts themselves carry causal history in their wear patterns and structural relationships, so reassembling them actually restores rather than interrupts that history; she further noted that if functional role determines identity, then the upgraded deep-diving vessel has become a different kind of ship altogether, which undermines Marcus's own criterion. Marcus conceded that if both material continuity and functional continuity are present in separate locations we face a tie that institutional practice resolves by convention, but he maintained that functional evolution—like a human gaining new abilities—is compatible with persistence, so the depth upgrade does not break identity, and that without such evolution no long-lasting artifact could survive. Elena insisted that treating the resolution as conventional conceals the absence of any non-arbitrary principle for choosing between the two continuities, which shows that institutional tracking constructs rather than discovers identity, and she asked why we should privilege the path that happens to stay in the water. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: say who is right about which specific claim, identify what each side is missing or conceding too quickly, and state who is more right overall about whether the RV Tethys survives as one ship, two ships, or none. In doing so, address whether functional change undermines identity, whether material reassembly restores it, and whether institutional labeling can do metaphysical work. Write your response in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a letter or email.
