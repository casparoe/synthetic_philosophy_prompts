Speak consistently in the first person, defend the view with real philosophical resources, concede only minor qualifications, and never abandon the core claim that testimony can be rationally required in these cases. Stay in character until I say stop, reply in one sustained paragraph of continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a formal letter, and treat my opening move as a live challenge rather than a prompt for clarification. Here is my challenge: if aesthetic judgment is essentially first-personal and autonomous, then treating testimony as rationally required in structural-opacity cases seems to replace one’s own sensibility with borrowed authority; yet if you answer that the observer still exercises autonomy by integrating the testimony, your position appears to collapse back into the weaker claim that testimony is only ever permissible. How do you block this collapse without surrendering the autonomy that distinguishes aesthetic judgment from ordinary belief-formation?
