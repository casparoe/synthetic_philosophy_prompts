I have been trying to clarify my own thinking about where scientific planning ends and irreducible political value-judgment begins, partly after a seminar discussion of Carnap’s internal and external questions applied to social policy. Here are my intuitive verdicts on concrete planning disputes, and I want to know whether any coherent principle—perhaps along Carnapian lines—captures them, or whether some of my judgments must be abandoned for consistency.

First, when two hydraulic engineers disagree about predicted sediment accumulation behind a proposed dam, I treat this as a straightforward empirical dispute resolvable by further measurement and model refinement. Second, when a planning board agrees on every predicted economic and ecological effect of a zoning reform but splits over whether equality or property rights should be weighted more heavily, I think there is no further rational resolution: the conflict is practical, not cognitive. Third, if a planner adopts a specific welfare-function framework—defining social welfare, say, as aggregate preference satisfaction—to derive optimal resource allocations, I view the derivation as internal to the adopted framework, but the adoption of that framework itself as an external practical decision that empirical science cannot justify. Fourth, when citizens debate erecting a monument to a controversial historical figure, I treat tourism-impact studies as genuinely empirical, while the decision to honor the figure belongs to the realm of values and should not be disguised as technical planning. Fifth, disagreements about forecasting methodology—using exponential versus logistic population models—feel mostly like empirical questions of predictive fit, though I also sense they involve conventional choices about simplicity and robustness that observation alone does not settle. But here is the tension I keep revisiting: a government proposes a comprehensive five-year plan that uses sociological theory to specify not only production targets but also preferred family structures and cultural norms, claiming these prescriptions maximize welfare within the adopted framework. Part of me wants to say that once the welfare framework is fixed, the cultural prescriptions are just internal calculations; another part insists that prescribing cultural norms is inherently a value-laden imposition that no framework choice can render purely scientific. Is there a unified account—perhaps refining Carnap’s distinction between theoretical language and practical framework-choice, together with his view that value statements are not theoretical assertions but expressions of practical attitude—that consistently delivers the first five verdicts? If so, what does it say about the sixth case, and about a scenario I have not listed: a city council that rejects all quantitative welfare modeling and insists that every planning decision must be resolved by direct democratic vote, on the grounds that any framework choice is irreducibly political? Please also state which of my original verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to drop, and whether there is a simpler competing principle that saves more of them at the cost of added complexity.
