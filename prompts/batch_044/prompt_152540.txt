I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on Hellenistic ethics and its contemporary revival, and I'm trying to develop a critical reading of Albert Camus's The Stranger that takes both the novel and Stoicism seriously rather than reducing one to an illustration of the other. The modern revival—especially in popular philosophy and cognitive-behavioral contexts—often treats Epictetus's dichotomy of control as a therapeutic technique for emotional regulation: distinguish what depends on you from what does not, and cultivate resilience toward the latter. But Epictetus's original formulation ties that same distinction to prohairesis (moral character and rational choice), to active virtue, and to a cosmopolitan duty to act justly within a rational cosmic order. Read The Stranger through this precise tension. Where does Meursault's emotional flatness—his refusal to perform grief at his mother's funeral, his passive acceptance of the trial's machinery, his calm before execution—resemble the modern Stoic ideal of controlling one's internal responses to external events? And where does he fail the ancient standard, since he displays almost no concern for moral virtue, social obligation, or the welfare of others? Point to specific scenes (the funeral, the beach shooting, the trial, the chaplain's visit) and say whether the novel is dramatizing Stoic detachment, parodying it, or revealing what is lost when the dichotomy is separated from virtue ethics. Does Camus's protagonist inadvertently demonstrate that emotional control without moral purpose is not wisdom but a kind of emptiness? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of scholarship; I want a reading that asks what The Stranger adds to the debate between modern and ancient Stoicism that a purely philosophical argument cannot capture on its own.
