After a long argument with a friend last night, I'm trying to understand why we cannot move past a disagreement about corruption, clientelism, and whether Western standards are genuinely universal. We were debating cases where local officials sustain long-term, non-transactional reciprocal obligations with community members—receiving regular support, gifts, or loyalty that predate any specific contract—versus one-off bribery for a discrete official act. We agree on every empirical detail: who gave what, when, and whether a particular decision followed. My friend claims Western anti-corruption law treats the official as a pre-social agent with no legitimate prior obligations, so any sustained loyalty network automatically registers as "undue influence," whereas in many contexts these relationships are the background condition of governance rather than its corruption. I responded that without a universal distinction—perhaps between relational maintenance and transactional exchange—we lose the capacity to classify anything as corruption at all. Yet we have circled this for months without resolution. Is our impasse actually a hidden empirical question about how institutions function, a verbal disagreement about what "corruption" denotes, a dispute over which cases count as data, or are we defending bedrock intuitions about whether social bonds can ever justify preferential treatment? And if the problem is that we are answering different questions under one word—say, one normative and one descriptive—what kind of move would genuinely advance it: a formal distinction, a comparative institutional analysis, or an acceptance that the concept is irreducibly plural?
