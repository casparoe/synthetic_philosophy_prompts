I am preparing a discussion module for an advanced seminar in practical reason, and I would like your help adjudicating a debate I witnessed last week between two doctoral students—Jordan and Riley—who were arguing about whether undergoing a genuinely transformative experience can ever be rationally chosen when the agent lacks first-person access to what that experience will be like. Their exchange became quite conceptually dense, and I want to use it to test whether students can separate predictive evaluation from procedural commitment. Please approach this as a professional philosopher with expertise in rational choice theory and the philosophy of transformative experience. Before offering your verdict, take a moment to clarify the distinction between forecasting a specific phenomenological outcome and committing to a structural decision-procedure; then proceed carefully.

Jordan opened by claiming that deciding to enter a transformative state—say, committing to a permanent contemplative practice or becoming a parent—is irrational unless one can evaluate it from the inside, but ex hypothesi that is impossible. Therefore, Jordan argued, the rational agent should avoid such commitments and favor options where current preferences retain authoritative weight; to choose otherwise is to substitute hope for judgment. Riley countered that this presupposes rationality always requires predictive evaluation of outcomes. For transformative choices, Riley said, rationality is procedural: one assesses the robustness of one's decision-framework—openness to revision, resilience, capacity for post-hoc integration—rather than forecasting the phenomenology. The commitment is to a rational procedure, not a gamble on a specific future state.

Jordan then replied that calling it "procedural" does not solve the problem; it merely renames ignorance. Without specifying what counts as successful "integration," Riley has no criterion to distinguish a wise transformative commitment from reckless self-abandonment. Jordan insisted that one needs at least a substantive threshold for when the post-transformation self's endorsement validates the prior choice. Riley responded that the criteria are structural—coherence with core values, absence of coercion, maintenance of reflective capacity—not representational previews. Demanding a substantive preview is conceptually impossible in these cases, Riley argued, like asking someone to imagine a color they have never perceived. Jordan's insistence on predictive authority, Riley added, treats a constitutive achievement of the self as if it were a predictive target.

Jordan pushed back: if the criteria are purely structural, then any leap can be rationalized post hoc by redefining "coherence" after the fact. Without a pre-committed substantive constraint, Riley's framework collapses into the claim that transformative choices are rational whenever one survives them with a coherent narrative. That, Jordan said, removes the normative bite entirely. Riley's final move was to deny that any leap qualifies—only those satisfying the structural conditions she named. The error, Riley concluded, lies in treating future self-endorsement as something one must predict before acting, rather than as something one constitutes through the process of reflective integration itself. Jordan is applying a predictive model where a constitutive one is required.

Please go through their exchange turn by turn—rating each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Do not simply declare an overall winner; specify exactly where Jordan is correct, where Riley is correct, and where each overreaches. In particular, address whether Riley's appeal to procedural rationality genuinely escapes Jordan's charge of normative emptiness, or whether it risks collapsing into post-hoc rationalization. Finally, identify what both parties are missing about the relationship between structural conditions and the authority of the present agent's values.
