I'm writing a chapter on the justification of deductive inference and I'm trying to isolate exactly what Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise demonstrates about the limits of formalization. The familiar reading is that the tortoise generates an infinite regress by demanding that modus ponens itself be added as a premise every time Achilles applies it. But I'm interested in whether the regress is specifically an artifact of treating inference rules as object-language premises rather than as structural features of the proof framework—say, in the manner of sequent calculus, where certain rules operate at the meta-level rather than within the derivation itself.

Could you explain, at a conceptual rather than fully technical level, whether Carroll's regress genuinely establishes that deduction requires an irreducible, non-premise act of reasoning, or whether it only shows that you cannot internalize every license to infer as an explicit premise within a single object-language without generating an infinite hierarchy? In particular, I want to understand whether the tortoise's refusal should be interpreted as a confusion about logical consequence, or instead as a legitimate demonstration that the gap between syntactic derivability and semantic consequence can never be fully closed by enriching the premise set—especially once we distinguish object-language rules from meta-language authorizations to apply them. Please don't just summarize the dialogue; I'm trying to determine whether there is a coherent position on which the tortoise is correct that every application of a rule requires a separate normative authorization, without that position collapsing into a global skepticism about deduction itself.
