My friend and I argued until late about Parfit’s hitchhiker and the claim that rationality can license what Parfit calls rational irrationality—binding yourself now to do what will then be against your best reasons. I tried to turn our back-and-forth into a strict argument, but it runs away to a conclusion I cannot stomach: that the fully rational agent should progressively dismantle her own future capacity for deliberation. I need to locate the fracture.

(1) Rationality is evaluated agent-relatively and time-indexed: at any moment, an agent should act on the strongest reasons available to her then.

(2) In the hitchhiker case, at t₁ (before departure) the driver has decisive reason to commit to delivering the passenger at t₂, since without that credible commitment the passenger will not enter and the cooperative gain is lost.

(3) At t₂ (near town), the driver has decisive reason to drive past: the passenger is already in the vicinity, the marginal cost of stopping is positive, and no further benefit depends on completion.

(4) So the rational driver at t₁ intends an action that she fully expects her rational self at t₂ will have decisive reason not to perform.

(5) A present commitment is credible only if the agent can causally guarantee that the future defection-reason will not govern behavior; mere intention is insufficient when the future self will deliberate independently.

(6) The only fully reliable guarantee is to restructure the future agent’s psychology—by irreversible disposition-installation, selective information-destruction, or impairment of the deliberative faculties that generate the defection—so that the arrival-time self functions more like an automaton executing the prior plan than like an independent reason-weigher.

(7) Generalizing: whenever present and future temporal stages of an agent face intertemporal conflict, the present stage should apply the same binding strategy to protect its interests.

(8) Therefore the rational life-plan is a sequence of self-immobilizing commitments that eventually eliminate all future independent deliberation, leaving the agent a mechanism with no remaining capacity for rational revision.

(9) But an entity lacking deliberative capacity is not a rational agent at all; rationality presupposes the very capacity that the plan annihilates.

(10) Hence rationality demands both the preservation and the systematic destruction of future reasoning capacities.

The chain feels valid, yet (10) is absurd. I want to preserve (2) and (4)—the hitchhiker intuition that credible commitment requires some form of self-binding seems right—but I am open to revising (1). Is the error in (5), with its hidden assumption that credibility requires internal psychological restructuring rather than reputation, external enforcement, or habit? Or is there an equivocation between “having reason to defect” and “being able to act on that reason”? Or does the slide from (6) to (7) illicitly universalize a local remedy? Tell me which step to reject, why it looked plausible, and what each standard response—the plan-based conception of rationality, the dispositional-commitment view, and the view that treats temporal parts as separate agents—must pay to deny it.
