I spent much of last night arguing with a friend about Samuel Scheffler's claim in Death and the Afterlife that human extinction would be bad mainly because it destroys the afterlife—the continuing network of human culture, projects, and descendants through which individual lives gain meaning beyond death. My friend summarized Scheffler as holding that this collective afterlife functions like a Parfitian reductionist identity relation, so that humanity is effectively a single diachronic person and extinction amounts to collective suicide; she added that Scheffler restricts this value to beings capable of generational overlap, thereby excluding posthuman successors, and that the badness is intrinsic yet collapses under a presentist ontology because no future people exist to stand in the relation. She further insisted that Scheffler replaces religious personal immortality with this secular collective survival, and concluded that the value is purely relational: if we stopped caring about descendants, extinction would cease to be bad. I suspect she has conflated Scheffler's modest relational thesis with stronger metaphysical and ontological commitments he does not hold, but I cannot locate every distortion. Is this a fair summary? Please respond as a numbered list with exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, what Scheffler explicitly denies or a defender would reject, and whether the conclusion that the badness is purely relational survives correction.
