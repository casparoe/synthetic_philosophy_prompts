I have been thinking about what scientists mean when they call a trait "heritable," especially after a conversation with a coworker who follows popular genetics writing. He summarized the current research for me in three points. First, he said that a heritability coefficient of, say, 0.50 for a behavioral trait means that exactly half of the differences among people in that trait are caused by genetic differences, and the other half by environmental ones, with the split being fixed for that trait regardless of context. Second, he pointed to studies that report lower heritability for educational attainment in American samples compared to Scandinavian registry studies, and he argued that this gap proves Scandinavian societies are more equal in opportunity: when environments become uniform, he claimed, genetic differences become the only remaining source of variation, so heritability should rise toward 100 percent. Third, he concluded that because heritability reflects an underlying biological reality, broad social interventions—like universal high-quality tutoring—cannot substantially reduce group differences in outcomes, since they can only change the environment and not the genetic component he thinks is locked in place.

I am skeptical, particularly about whether heritability describes causes for individuals rather than statistical patterns across populations, and about whether comparing heritability across countries actually measures equality rather than something about how environments are distributed. But I lack the technical background to identify the precise errors. Could you examine his three claims carefully: for each one, indicate what a quantitative geneticist would accept, what is a common misinterpretation, and whether there is a hidden assumption—perhaps about additive variance, gene-environment correlation, or the distinction between narrow-sense and broad-sense heritability—that makes the reasoning fail? I am especially interested in whether there is any coherent scenario in which a large-scale social change could cause the heritability of a trait to drop sharply within a single generation, or whether that possibility is ruled out by the mathematics itself, and whether his comparison between Scandinavian and American samples can support any conclusion about equality of opportunity without smuggling in an unstated premise about how environmental variance is structured.
