I'm working through Rorty's later writings on antirepresentationalism, and I've become fixated on a tension that standard expositions tend to paper over. Rorty rejects the idea that language or thought represents reality in any philosophically interesting sense, yet he insists that scientific inquiry makes genuine progress and that some vocabularies are demonstrably better at predicting and controlling nature. The usual resolution treats these claims as separated by domain: representation is dead in philosophy but survives in science only as a pragmatic instrument. I find that separation unstable.

Please argue that Rorty's antirepresentationalism is actually incompatible with his own account of scientific success unless we smuggle in a minimal representational thesis—specifically, that predictive success requires some form of structural correspondence between theoretical vocabulary and causal regularities, even if that correspondence is not a 'mirroring' relation in the traditional Cartesian sense. Defend the claim that Rorty needs what we might call instrumental representationalism to prevent science from collapsing into mere rhetorical redescription. Make the case that without this minimal thesis, the distinction between normal and abnormal discourse loses its normative bite, because there would then be no non-circular reason to prefer one vocabulary over another except social consensus. Be precise about what kind of correspondence survives—do not simply invoke truth-as-correspondence—and show exactly how it differs from the mirror-model epistemology Rorty rejects.
