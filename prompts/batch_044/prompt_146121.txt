After a long argument with a friend about whether harms inflicted in persistent virtual worlds are genuinely real, I sketched the following idea for an essay. I want to distinguish mere psychological disappointment from what I will call borrowed modal authority: a virtual object lacks intrinsic physical properties, yet it can inherit harm-eligibility from a normative frame that the interacting community implicitly ratifies as authoritative within that world. On this view, destroying a long-cultivated virtual artifact counts as a real harm only when the act violates a counterfactual-dependence relation that the frame treats as protected—roughly analogous to breaking a promise, where the binding force comes from collective recognition rather than physical causation. I am asking for comments specifically on coherence: does this distinction actually separate virtual harm from disappointment without smuggling a physicalist counterfactual test back in through the back door? And is there an obvious objection—perhaps about voluntary frame exit or the absence of consent—that undermines the borrowing relation altogether?
