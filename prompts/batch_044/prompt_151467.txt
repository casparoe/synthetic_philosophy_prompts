hey, act like a philosophy of language prof who's taught Austin and Searle for years and is tired of surface-level takes. Before you dive in, do a quick brainstorm first: lay out the correspondences in a table or list, then tear them apart. Think carefully about each step.

There's this analogy people keep leaning on -- that performative utterances (like saying I promise, or the wedding pronouncment) are basically moves in chess. You speak the words according to rules, the social board updates, and that's the whole story. A friend used this in seminar to argue Austin was overhyping performatives: they're not special, just conventional updates, like sliding a rook to a new square. 

Audit this for me. What maps onto what exactly? The utterance itself, the felicty conditions, the institutional result, the rules of the game, the players? Then show where the mapping cracks. Specifically, does the chess picture smuggle in the idea that there's always a pre-existant board and rule-set that the speaker just navigates? And does that miss the way some performatives seem to help constitute the very instititutional framework they're operating in? Also, where does direction of fit sit -- beliefs aim at the world, desires aim to change it -- do performatives fit either, and does chess have anything analogus?

Finally, does the conclusion survive? Once you account for the disanologies around brute facts vs institutional facts, and the way language can create conditions for its own success, is there still something mysterious Austin was right to flag, or does the chess analogy dissolve it completely? No biblography needed, just work through it slowly.
