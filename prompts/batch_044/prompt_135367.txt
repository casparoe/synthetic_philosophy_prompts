I'm in this philosophy of education seminar and we got into a weird argument about how universities evaluate volunteer work. There's this practice—common at a lot of schools, mine included—where service-learning courses make you log hours at a nonprofit, but then there's a reflection essay that's graded partly on whether you frame your motivation as 'authentic commitment' versus just 'fulfilling a requirement.' The rubric literally gives more points if you claim you'd have volunteered even without the course credit.

At the same time, the financial aid office runs work-study jobs in dining halls and libraries for students who just need tuition money, and nobody asks whether they're working 'for the right reasons'; the labor counts the same either way. So what philosophical commitments about moral worth does that grading practice preseuppose? Is it basically Kantian—actions only have value if done from duty and not for external reward—or could it be something like virtue ethics about character? And is it even consistent with the work-study policy, which treats labor as valuable regardless of internal motivation? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it's actually required or if the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what that reflection esssay would have to look like if we replaced the underlying view with a consequentialist account of civic engagement.
