At a dinner following my philosophy seminar's session on population ethics, two of my classmates—let's call them Mara and Leo—fell into a sustained disagreement about lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, specifically whether pleasure and suffering remain commensurable once a lexical priority for suffering reduction is in place. Mara opened by defending a strict version: once suffering is pushed below a critical lexical threshold, no amount of pleasure can justify allowing suffering back across that line, which she took to entail that pleasure and pain are incommensurable across the boundary—there is no meaningful exchange rate, and treating them as comparable is a category mistake. Leo responded that this confuses normative weighting with metaphysical incommensurability; even on her view, once both worlds sit below the threshold, we still need to rank states that differ in both residual suffering and pleasure, and that ranking presupposes some form of comparison. Mara then pressed a boundary case: compare a world with suffering just barely below the threshold but astronomical pleasure against a world with exactly zero suffering and zero pleasure. She argued we reject the first world not because pleasure failed to outweigh suffering on some scale, but because crossing the threshold violates a categorical constraint—like a deontological side-condition—so no cross-dimensional weighing occurs. Leo countered that within-tier comparisons still require measurement, and he proposed borrowing a non-compensatory lexicographic preference structure from multi-criteria decision analysis: suffering dominates as the first criterion, pleasure operates only as a tie-breaker within the permitted zone, arranged in a strict hierarchy that avoids cardinal aggregation altogether. Mara replied that this merely relocates the problem: defining the threshold itself requires comparing magnitudes of suffering to determine where the cutoff lies, and ordering worlds within the same tier still demands cardinal assessment of both dimensions separately. Consequently, she claimed, Leo's framework smuggles commensurability back in through the ordering rules rather than eliminating it.

Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important both are missing—particularly regarding whether their dispute is genuinely about metaphysical incommensurability, about normative structure, or about whether a lexicographic model can be consistently applied to continuous welfare dimensions without implicit cardinal comparison.
