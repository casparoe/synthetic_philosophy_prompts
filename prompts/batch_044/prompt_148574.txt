I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've been working through Daniel Dennett's proposal that the self is best understood not as a Cartesian substance but as a center of narrative gravity — a kind of fictional protagonist that emerges from the brain's ongoing autobiographical storytelling. The basic image is clear enough: just as the center of mass of a physical object is a useful abstraction rather than a separate part, the self is a useful abstraction that pulls together dispersed mental states into a coherent biography. But I keep returning to a granular conceptual tension that the standard presentations gloss over.

If the boundaries of the self are fixed by the coherence and gravitational pull of the present narrative rather than by any underlying psychological substrate, then it seems the self can undergo what we might call motivational retrofitting. Imagine a case in which, over the course of decades, a person gradually reinterprets a past act of serious betrayal not by denying its occurrence, but by embedding it in a radically new explanatory frame — perhaps reconceiving the act as an unavoidable behavioral manifestation of an undiagnosed neurological condition that the present self now "discovers" was always the true cause. The old narrative center, anchored in malice and full agency, is replaced by a new center anchored in pathology and diminished culpability. The memory traces remain intact, yet the interpretive gravity that binds those traces to a responsible agent has shifted.

My question is whether Dennett's framework can distinguish, in principle, between benign narrative growth and this kind of autobiographical retrocausation — a restructuring that severs moral accountability without severing memory. Specifically, if the self is genuinely nothing more than the present center of narrative coherence, does a radical revision of past motives dissolve the continuity required for diachronic moral responsibility? Or does the framework collapse into a kind of narrative solipsism in which the present self can always rewrite the past out of moral reach by relocating the center of gravity? I'd like an explanation that gets into the mechanics of how narrative gravity binds actions to agents through interpretive coherence rather than through causal-psychological continuity, and that addresses whether the view has internal resources to block this responsibility-evading maneuver without smuggling back in a substantive, non-narrative self.
