I've been thinking about this pattern I see in small groups - like a community garden board or an open source project - where people collaborate and talk in "we" language the whole time, but if the project goes sideways or someone faces blowback, suddenly everyone insists they were only ever acting as individuals and never really bought into the collective outcome. Its not free-riding, since they actually contributed labor and resources. Its also not just hidden disagreement about goals, because they genuinely seemed aligned when things were going well. The strange part is that each person seems to treat the joint commitment like it has a hidden expiration clause tied to thier own interpretation of success: they feel part of the "we" right up until the result conflicts with thier private view of what was intended, and then they retroactivly deny that any real shared commitment existed. Is there an established name for this? I know Gilbert writes about plural subjects and joint commitment, but those frameworks usually treat commitment as present or absent from the start, not as something that can be revoked after the fact depending on outcomes. Could this be folded into existing collective intentionality theory, or is it a distinct failure mode? If its distinct, can you name and define it precisely, including how it differs from strategic ambiguity, weak shared intentionality, and plain groupthink? Make clear what counts as the phenomenon and what doesnt, especially around cases where people genuinely change their minds versus cases where they were always holding that escape clause.
