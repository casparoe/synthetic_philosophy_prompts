For an institutional ethics framework that must classify actions in hybrid human-algorithmic systems—where a clinical protocol may withhold ventilation via automated risk scoring or a physician may manually discontinue care—write the full desiderata that any adequate theory of the doing/allowing distinction (and by extension killing versus letting die) must satisfy before it can be applied. Specify the cases it must get right: direct physical intervention, authorization of automated execution, failure to rescue when rescue is assigned to a machine, and redirection of scarce resources that results in death. Specify the distinctions it must not collapse—between causal contribution and normative responsibility, between intending and foreseeing, between individual and distributed agency, and between an omission that violates a duty and one that merely allows a natural course. Identify the findings from moral psychology and organizational cognition it must remain consistent with, particularly regarding how agents represent their own causal role in distributed systems. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable—for example, that the theory must not classify every automated outcome as a doing by the human authorizer—and which reflect camp-specific preferences, such as the insistence that only bodily motion can constitute doing. Then say whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, especially if demands for precise agent-level causation conflict with scenarios of distributed execution. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing theories, though a brief indication of how causal, normative-role, and counterfactual-dependency accounts fare against the list is welcome.
