In the novel I am presently revising, one of my central characters is a philosopher of science who finds herself caught between a mechanistic biologist and a systems ecologist, and I want their disagreement to be philosophically sharp rather than merely atmospheric. To that end, I am trying to understand how the classical logical-empiricist account of inter-theoretic reduction—wherein a higher-level theory is derived from a lower-level one through bridge laws that precisely coordinate the vocabularies of each domain—compares with what I have begun to call “structural-absence emergence.” By the latter I mean the view that certain organizational properties, such as the stability of an ecosystem or the coherent dynamics of a distributed neural network, are not just complex aggregates of constituent parts but depend essentially on the specific absence of particular connections among those parts; on this view, the explanatory work is done not by what is present and interacting but by relational voids that lack any direct physical counterpart at the component level, so that a complete lower-level inventory of entities and forces still fails to capture the organizational logic in question.

How do these two frameworks diverge in their criteria for explanatory success? Can the bridge-law model accommodate reference to absent relations by treating them as negative boundary conditions within the lower-level description, or does structural-absence emergence require a genuinely distinct form of explanation that resists such assimilation? Furthermore, if one adopts the second view, must one commit to an ontologically layered reality in which emergent organizational properties exert downward influence, or can it be interpreted as an epistemic limitation—an admission that our lower-level descriptions are necessarily incomplete rather than that nature itself is stratified? I would like to know which of these interpretations is more defensible, where the deepest tension between reduction and this particular form of emergence actually lies, and whether a consistent middle position is available without collapsing into either pure eliminativism about higher-level sciences or an implausible vitalism about organizational patterns.
