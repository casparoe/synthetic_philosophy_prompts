Edward Gibbon’s History of the Decline and Fall treats the collapse of Rome as driven chiefly by internal institutional decay—luxury, the erosion of civic virtue, and the breakdown of military discipline—while treating external barbarian incursions as accelerants rather than root causes. Modern practitioners of reference-class forecasting apply an outside view to geopolitical risk: they assemble exchangeable sets of past empires, compute base-rate frequencies of regime collapse, and model survival through competing-risk hazard functions that separate internal fragmentation from external conquest. A specific methodological tension arises here. These statistical frameworks treat each empire as a draw from a common distribution, yet Gibbon insisted on Rome’s constitutional singularity—its unprecedented fusion of republican tradition, imperial bureaucracy, and later Christian transformation—which seems to violate the exchangeability required for valid base-rate inference. Imagine Gibbon is shown a contemporary forecasting model that assigns a 65 percent probability of systemic crisis within two centuries for agrarian-bureaucratic empires of comparable scale, with distinct hazard curves for endogenous decay and exogenous invasion. How would he likely assess this approach? Would he reject the reference class as a category error that flattens historically specific causation into statistical noise, or would he grant the base rate provisional authority as a prior to be updated with Rome’s particular institutional details? Furthermore, if he were to incorporate competing-risk logic into his own narrative method, how might that reshape his explanatory emphasis on contingent human agency without reducing it to a coefficient in a survival curve?
