Analyze Kevin Rudd’s 2008 parliamentary apology to Australia’s Stolen Generations through the lens of institutional moral repair, treating the apology as an illocutionary act whose conditions of success include uptake by the wronged party. The standard speaker-hearer model assumes a contemporaneous recipient with institutional standing to accept or reject the apology; here, however, the direct victims were largely deceased or dispersed, and the intended recipient was a collectively constituted but organizationally diffuse group—Indigenous Australians—lacking a single authoritative voice. Map precisely who counts as the speaker (the Commonwealth of Australia as a continuing legal person), what the wrong was (state-legislated forced removal of children), and who was positioned to grant or withhold acceptance. Then examine where the model strains: does temporal distance destroy the felicity conditions for apology, or does the state’s continuity as agent allow it to address a diachronic collective? Consider also that the apology was paired with an explicit refusal to consider compensation, which some theorists of moral repair treat as incompatible with genuine apology and indicative of mere performative closure. Tell me what features of this episode the institutional-repair framework explains well, which features it misdescribes or leaves silent—particularly regarding the absence of a unified hearer and the separation of symbolic from material repair—and what the case suggests about whether state apologies to historical wrongs are genuinely reparative acts or primarily mechanisms of national narrative management.
