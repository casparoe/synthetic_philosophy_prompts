Construct a thought experiment that isolates a tension internal to the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem: specifically, a case in which an agent has epistemically sufficient evidence to form belief B, yet forming B for evidential reasons would cause severe harm, while forming B for pragmatic (wrong-kind) reasons—without weighing the evidence—would prevent that harm and is morally required. The scenario must be set in an ordinary institutional context (for example, a hospital administration office, a school board meeting, or a family business negotiation), not a trolley-style dilemma; it should be confined to one paragraph; the belief B must concern a specific, checkable non-moral fact (such as the safety of a procedure, the solvency of an account, or the structural integrity of a building); and the harm prevented must flow solely from the agent’s own subsequent actions or omissions being guided by B, not from anyone else being deceived by the agent’s belief. After presenting the scenario, map out precisely which considerations count as right-kind and which as wrong-kind reasons for belief B; explain why the evidential path is ethically blocked despite its epistemic merit; and show why this does not collapse the wrong-kind-of-reasons distinction into a generic conflict between epistemic duty and moral duty. Finally, identify the feature of your case that makes the pragmatic belief genuinely belief-like rather than a mere decision to act as if B were true, and discuss how a defender of pure evidentialism could attack that feature.
