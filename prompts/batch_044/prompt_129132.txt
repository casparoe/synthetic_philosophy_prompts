Move 2 (Me): That forces a false dichotomy. The nematode lacks consciousness and integrated neural processing altogether; we distinguish it on functional grounds, not by weighing tissue. Size is at best a correlation, not a criterion.

Move 3 (Friend): But correlation is enough for practical moral reasoning. In clinical ethics we treat anencephalic infants differently partly because they lack substantial cortical mass. And across species, larger brains generally track richer subjective lives. So size is relevant, even if not decisive.

Move 4 (Me): Anencephaly is about the absence of cortical architecture, not about milliliters of tissue. Meanwhile, macrocephalic patients with severe hydrocephalus have enlarged cranial volume but sharply reduced neural tissue, yet no one doubts their full moral status. Size misleads in both directions.

Move 5 (Friend): You keep treating size as sufficient, which I never claimed. I only say it is part of the evidence. But then consider whales: a sperm whale’s brain is roughly eight times the mass of a human brain. If size is part of the calculus, should we not grant whales greater moral weight than humans, all else being equal?

Move 6 (Me): That reductio shows exactly why size cannot be an independent factor. You would have to smuggle in a species-normal baseline—“relevant for its kind”—which means species membership or functional equivalence is doing all the normative work, not absolute size.

Move 8 (Me): Then consider a hypothetical synthetic system—a data-center-scale artificial network with a hundred trillion synthetic neurons arranged in a purely feedforward grid, with no recurrent loops, no global workspace, and no integrated information. If it means “should be weighed independently in moral calculus,” it risks justifying discrimination against small-brained humans—infants, people with microcephaly—based on a metric that does not track their actual capacities.
