I am advising a small interdisciplinary research group—three philosophers of action and two theoretically oriented cognitive scientists—with three years of funding and no empirical laboratory. They want to concentrate on collective intentionality and joint action, but specifically on conceptual problems that shape how distributed agency works in settings where human and non-human participants coordinate without fully shared mental representations. I am trying to keep them out of well-rehearsed textbook debates unless those debates are genuinely upstream of newer concerns, and I want the deliverable to be a defended research agenda rather than a literature survey. Among the live issues I see are the ontology of plural subjects versus reducible we-intentions; the normative structure of joint commitment when participants are bound by obligations they did not individually authorize; and a more specific problem I will call fragmented collective intentionality, in which subgroups within a collective maintain incompatible representations of the shared goal—differing, for instance, over whether the aim is instrumental success, procedural fairness, or relational solidarity—yet still produce stable coordinated outcomes. There is also the longstanding question of whether joint action requires shared attention or can be sustained by purely external coordination mechanisms, but I am unsure whether resolving it actually changes anything downstream for social ontology or for practical design of distributed systems.

Given this framing, which two or three of these open questions should the group actually prioritize, and in what order? I want the reasoning, not just the list: for each choice, specify what makes it tractable for philosophers without access to large-scale behavioral experiments, how many other questions depend on its resolution, and what kind of conceptual or formal result over the next two years would cause you to upgrade or downgrade it. In particular, defend whether the problem of fragmented collective intentionality deserves a central place or is merely a special case of more general disagreements about shared intention. Also indicate which widely discussed lines of work in this area you would actively deprioritize—questions that absorb disproportionate attention but whose answers, whether positive or negative, would not substantially alter how we understand joint commitment, plural agency, or the conditions for distributed coordination. What criteria are you using—dependency, tractability, neglect, or practical leverage—and where do they conflict in this particular allocation?
