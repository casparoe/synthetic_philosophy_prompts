I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind serving on my university’s interdisciplinary biomedical research ethics board, and I need a formal decision memo for Thursday’s meeting. We are reviewing a protocol titled “Hemispheric Autonomy in Post-Callosotomy Patients.” The study uses split-field tachistoscopic presentation paired with independent manual-response channels—left-hand drawing versus right-hand pointing—to present study information separately to each visual field and to record independent responses. The investigators have designed what they call a “dual-channel assent protocol”: either hemisphere can register withdrawal through its assigned motor modality, and they argue that standard unitary informed consent risks suppressing the autonomous preferences of a potentially divided consciousness. Half the board regards this as an ethically necessary refinement that takes split-brain cases seriously; the other half sees it as a category error that fractures a single legal person into two unregulated research subjects without statutory basis. The philosophical issue at stake is whether the unity of consciousness is a metaphysical prerequisite for singular moral and legal personhood, or whether empirical hemispheric independence can justify partitioned consent procedures. Please draft the board’s memo recommending a concrete course of action—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—with an implementation timeline. The memo should treat the investigators’ divided-mind argument with genuine philosophical seriousness rather than simply restating the legal definition of personhood, and it should assess whether the dual-channel assent mechanism can be separated from the study’s scientific aims or whether it inextricably commits the protocol to treating one patient as two subjects. Include a clear bottom line, reasoning the board can defend to the investigators, and an honest account of the strongest consideration favoring the position you reject. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to specific named theories that would require verification.
