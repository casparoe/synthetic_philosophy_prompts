Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 12 upper-level philosophy students that makes them feel the conflict between the luminosity thesis and the KK principle through direct participation rather than lecture. Create a "gradient self-knowledge" simulation: assign each student a hidden numeric value on a continuous mental-state spectrum representing, for example, the felt intensity of a belief or sensation, with adjacent values being phenomenally indistinguishable. Give the group three binding rules: (1) the Luminosity Rule—if a student genuinely feels they occupy a specific value, they must publicly declare "I know I am at exactly X"; (2) the KK Rule—after any such declaration, an assigned auditor must ask whether the student knows that they know it, and the student must answer consistently without hedging; (3) the Shift Rule—between rounds, the facilitator silently adjusts some students' values by a small increment that remains below the threshold of phenomenal difference. Structure specific roles (value-holder, auditor, contradiction-tracker), a 20-minute setup and rule explanation, three 15-minute declaration rounds with silent shifts between them, a 20-minute collective mapping session in which the group traces which declarations collapsed, and a 10-minute debrief. The debrief should map the moments of contradiction onto the idea that luminosity fails when iterated knowledge claims are applied across a continuous domain with imperceptible transitions, should explain precisely which feature of the standard anti-luminosity argument—such as the objective reliability conditions of the underlying cognitive mechanism across the gradient—the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally, and should be frank about what will predictably go wrong the first time (students will resist retracting early declarations; auditors will apply the KK Rule inconsistently out of sympathy) and how to correct it. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; the activity should stand entirely on its own rules, roles, and incentives.
