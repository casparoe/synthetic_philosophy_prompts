I am drafting a novel that moves between three episodes commonly narrated as societal endings: the Eastern Mediterranean interconnection crisis around 1177 BCE, the Classic Maya lowland polity fragmentation of the ninth century, and the ecological and political transformation of Rapa Nui after the peak of moai construction. To shape the narrative architecture, I need to adjudicate between two conceptual frameworks. The first, which I call the rupture thesis, holds that collapse can be a genuine cascading failure of interdependent institutions, demographics, and ecological management, producing discontinuous worlds in which writing, monumentality, and centralized authority truly vanish for generations. The second, which I call the palimpsest hypothesis, proposes that such episodes are better read as redistributions of complexity into smaller-scale, perishable, and archaeologically faint forms: populations and practices persist, but shift to materials and settlement patterns that leave thin traces, so that the impression of disappearance is largely an artifact of evidentiary visibility rather than an ontological break.

Compare these two models with specific attention to the Bronze Age transition, the Maya downturn, and Easter Island. How does each account for the co-occurrence of severe ecological stress, the cessation of large-scale monument building, and regionally uneven demographic signals? Which framework more coherently distinguishes cases where literacy and state structures genuinely disappear from cases where they appear to dissolve into dispersed local networks that our record simply fails to resolve? I do not need bibliographies or named citations; I need a conceptual verdict on which model should govern my novel’s underlying logic.
