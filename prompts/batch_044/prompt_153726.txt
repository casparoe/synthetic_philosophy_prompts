I'm presenting a seminar paper defending a reconstructed reading of Rawls's treatment of religion in Political Liberalism. My thesis is that the proviso — the condition that citizens may advance comprehensive religious reasons in public debate only if they eventually support their positions with public reasons — should be interpreted not as a temporal sequencing rule but as a structural modal constraint on justification itself. I call this condition justificatory bifocality: a religious citizen's political claim must be articulable simultaneously through both a theological frame and a public-reason frame, with neither reduced to the other. On this reading, maintaining both frames is not a transitional step toward secular translation but a permanent eligibility condition for participation in the overlapping consensus. Consequently, the boundary between constitutional essentials and ordinary legislation becomes stricter for religious doctrines, because continuous dual articulation is required rather than a one-time translation at the legislative threshold.

The audience is a joint workshop of political philosophers specializing in liberalism and religion, constitutional lawyers focused on Establishment Clause neutrality, and theologians in public theology. Roughly half have published on Rawls's distinction between reasonable and unreasonable comprehensive doctrines and defend the standard sequential reading of the proviso. The lawyers worry that blurring the public-comprehensive boundary risks collapsing neutrality into sectarian endorsement. The theologians welcome religious voice but distrust formal structural conditions that might subordinate theological integrity to liberal proceduralism. Several attendees view my reconstruction as either an unnecessary complication or a disguised form of secular hegemony.
