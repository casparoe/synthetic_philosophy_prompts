I was talking with friends last weekend about whether a heart grown in a lab from someone's own cells is really "their heart" in the same sense as the one they were born with. We went through every feature we could think of—same tissue structure, same genetic markers, same function—and agreed they matched completely, yet half of us still felt there was a genuine difference in what kind of thing each one was. Nobody could point to a hidden property that was missing; the disagreement seemed to come from a kind of resistance to collapsing the two into one category. Later I noticed the same split happening with digital images that are pixel-for-pixel identical to well-known paintings, and again with text produced by artificial systems that copies an author's style so precisely that even careful readers can't distinguish it from the original. In each case people agree on all the observable characteristics, but still divide the world into "the real kind" and "the replica" without being able to name what distinguishes them. I am wondering whether philosophers have a specific, narrower term for this exact pattern—where full agreement on properties coexists with an insistence that two items fall under different kinds, and the insistence is not defended by citing a missing feature but by appealing to the category itself. I know this touches on the problem of universals, but I am not asking for a general lecture on that; I want to know if there is an established concept for this particular disagreement structure, how it relates to essentialism and nominalism, and whether it is usually seen as a difficulty for realism about kinds or for the view that denies them. Please answer in American English.
