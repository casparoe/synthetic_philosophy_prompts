I'm working through debates in value pluralism and keep returning to a specific tension in Ruth Chang's account of hard choices, where options are on a par—neither better, worse, nor equal—rather than strictly incommensurable. The conceptual knot I want to untangle concerns the status of rational regret after such a choice. If there genuinely is no fact of the matter about whether career path A was superior to career path B, then comparative regret—regretting that one chose A over B specifically because B was the better option—seems conceptually misplaced. Yet agents routinely experience a form of regret that is not merely causal disappointment, wishing the world had turned out differently, but appears evaluative: they feel they have lost something of genuine, unrecoverable value. This suggests a hidden structural feature in the choice situation.

Can you explain precisely where the conceptual pressure lies? Specifically, I want to understand whether this post-decisional regret reveals a covert commitment to a hierarchical ordering of values—what we might call an implicit meta-axiological coherence condition—or whether it can be accommodated within a pluralist framework without collapsing the relation of being on a par into hidden equality or hidden superiority. How should we distinguish the kind of regret that remains rationally permissible under value pluralism from the kind that smuggles back in a monistic comparison? Furthermore, does the very possibility of constructing a retrospective narrative that justifies one's choice—what I will call axiological self-constitution—function as a second-order resolution of the hard choice, or does it merely relocate the incommensurability from the first-order options to the second-order principles of self-formation? Please map out the conceptual architecture clearly, separating the disanalogies between first-order value conflict and second-order identity construction that matter for this question from those that do not.
