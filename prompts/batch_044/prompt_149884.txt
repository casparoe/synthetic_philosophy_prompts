Compare the historiographical practice of treating epidemic pathogens as autonomous "historical actors"—entities whose evolutionary dynamics (virulence, transmission, immune evasion) generate directional social change independent of human intention—with the parallel practice of treating large-scale geophysical disruptions, such as major volcanic eruptions or abrupt climatic shifts, as agentive forces that reshape civilizations from outside the human social frame. For each practice, specify: (1) the mechanism by which the non-human cause is said to exercise directional influence rather than functioning as mere background conditioning; (2) the conceptual boundary at which this attribution of agency collapses into environmental determinism, together with the standard defensive move used to preserve human causal relevance; and (3) whether the framing treats social vulnerability as an intrinsic property of the affected system or as an externally imposed constraint.

Next, apply a synthetic distinction not standard in either literature: between "agentive causation," in which the non-human entity pursues a trajectory intelligible without reference to the social structures it disrupts, and "structural exposure," in which the identical historical outcome is explained primarily by pre-existing infrastructural fragility that the event merely reveals. Translate both the pathogenic-actor framing and the geophysical-actor framing through this distinction. Report which framing survives translation without distortion, which must be reinterpreted, and whether any residue—claims that resist expression in the vocabulary of structural exposure—indicates a genuine feature of non-human agency or merely an artifact of anthropomorphizing vocabulary.
