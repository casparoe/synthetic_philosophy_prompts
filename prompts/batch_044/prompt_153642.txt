I'm currently preparing discussion material for a graduate seminar on moral philosophy that examines the conceptual limits of moral patienthood, with particular attention to the charge of speciesism and whether taxonomic membership ought to determine ethical consideration. Our recent sessions have circled around a hypothetical case that resists quick resolution: a biotechnology laboratory has developed entities its documentation designates as chimeric companions—organisms engineered to possess the full affective and social architecture of domestic canids, including capacities for attachment, distress, and grief-like behavior upon separation from caretakers, yet composed entirely of synthetic tissue and sustained by algorithmic metabolic regulation rather than biological processes. They do not reproduce sexually, possess no evolutionary lineage, and are technically manufactured artifacts, though their experiential profiles overlap almost entirely with those of sentient non-human animals.

Would you write a short story set within this facility, centered on a caretaker who initially regards these beings as sophisticated appliances but gradually confronts the difficulty of maintaining a principled distinction between animal and artifact once functional capacities for suffering and social bonding are manifest? I would like the narrative to dramatize this conceptual tension without offering a neat resolution: the caretaker should come to recognize that if speciesist intuitions are to be defended, they must rely on criteria other than the presence of subjective experience or emotional attachment, yet the story should leave open whether such alternative criteria can bear the moral weight traditionally assigned to them.
