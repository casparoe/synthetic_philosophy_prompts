I am a high school junior serving as the student ethics coordinator for our regional youth conservation initiative. Each semester our board receives roughly a dozen proposals from peers involving direct interaction with non-human animals—classroom studies, sanctuary volunteering, wildlife photography, and population management exercises. I have no formal philosophical training, nor do the adult advisors who must ultimately sign off, yet we need consistent, defensible guidance rather than ad hoc reactions. Please translate Kant’s indirect-duty view regarding animals—specifically, the claim that rational beings have no direct moral duties to animals because animals lack autonomous rational nature, but do have indirect duties regarding them insofar as cruelty, neglect, or certain instrumental treatments corrupt the agent’s own moral character and weaken obligations to other rational beings—into a reusable decision procedure that a non-specialist reviewer can complete in about fifteen minutes.

The deliverable should be an ordered checklist or flowchart in words. For each stage, specify the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, where the procedure issues a clear rejection, and where it must escalate to human judgment because the issue is irreducibly one of values rather than facts. You must decide which distinctions within the Kantian framework must survive operationalization—for instance, between using an animal’s capacities as a means and engaging in treatment that habituates the agent to indifference toward suffering—and which distinctions can be dropped without distorting the principle. The procedure should also flag its own predictable misfires and state explicitly which ethical concerns it systematically excludes.

Apply the procedure to three proposals: first, a student who wants to capture a wild-caught reptile and maintain it in a classroom terrarium for a semester-long behavioral observation; second, a peer proposing to reduce an invasive frog population using a method that causes prolonged physical distress but no direct human-animal interaction during execution; and third, a photography project that repeatedly disturbs nesting birds—without causing physical injury—to build a portfolio intended to raise public awareness of habitat loss, a case that the procedure should technically approve but that I expect will feel morally wrong to run. For each, show the path through the stages, the stop or escalation point reached, and the final determination. Conclude by stating clearly what this procedure cannot do, which kinds of proposals lie outside its scope, and where a reviewer must override its output with explicit value judgment.
