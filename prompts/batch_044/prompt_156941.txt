I'm trying to determine whether Montaigne's essay on cannibals commits him to a robust form of cultural relativism, and I'm testing my interpretive instincts against specific passages and constructed extensions. Here are my verdicts.

When Montaigne praises the cannibals' military valor and claims their warfare is nobler than the religious wars of Europe, I read this as an application of a universal standard of honor rather than as a culturally indexed judgment. When he compares cannibalism to European practices of torture and the execution of heretics, arguing that the former is no more cruel, I take him to be using cross-cultural metrics of suffering rather than suspending moral evaluation altogether. When he asserts that Europeans call the cannibals barbarous only because their customs differ from ours, I understand him as attacking ethnocentric prejudice, not as denying that there are correct and incorrect customs. Yet when he refuses to condemn the consumption of prisoners outright and insists that our revulsion is largely a product of custom and education, I feel he is approaching a view on which moral condemnation across cultures requires shared normative premises that are absent. And in a hypothetical case in which a culture practices ritual infanticide grounded in sincere religious belief, I think Montaigne's method of sympathetic internal understanding would lead him to withhold condemnation, which points toward a principle of epistemic humility about cross-cultural moral judgment rather than toward a substantive relativism about values.

Is there a single principle—perhaps concerning the conditions for legitimate moral criticism—that accommodates all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why?
