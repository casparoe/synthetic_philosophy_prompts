Design a runnable 80-minute seminar activity for about 14 philosophy students (mixed undergraduate and graduate) that makes them physically feel the difference between sphexish mechanical rigidity and flexible self-revision, and connects that experience to why free will might depend on the latter rather than on mere determinism. The participants should run into the problem through rules and hidden mechanics before anyone names sphexishness, rigidity, or free will.

Structure the deliverable with these sections: Setting & Objectives; The Activity (roles, materials, rules, and hidden mechanics); Timeline; Predictable Failures; Debrief Mapping; and Honest Limits.

For the activity itself: divide the group into Fixed-Protocol Agents (about half) and Revisable Agents. Give Fixed-Protocol Agents a laminated card with a rigid 5-step behavioral loop—check left container, check right container, deposit token, seal container, return to start—and instruct them that any interruption forces a full restart from step 1; they may not alter, skip, or abandon the sequence even if containers are missing or relocated. Give Revisable Agents blank rule-cards and a formal revision procedure: they may change any step, but each change must be written, announced to the group, and followed by a 30-second delay before execution.

Set the collective task: move six tokens from a central “burrow” table to a “nest” corner within 25 minutes. Without announcing it, alter the environment twice—first at minute 8 (move one container, remove another) and again at minute 15 (shift the nest location). Fixed-Protocol Agents should fall into visible, frustrating loops checking missing containers; Revisable Agents should eventually adapt but suffer coordination breakdown because their revisions are not automatically shared, creating conflict between adaptation and collective order. Fixed-Protocol Agents may speak only to report their current step; Revisable Agents speak freely but pay the time cost.

In the debrief, map the felt loop-frustration onto mechanical rigidity, the burden of writing and announcing changes onto the cost of flexibility, and the coordination failure onto the idea that free will may require shared normative structures rather than individual choice alone. Include an honest note about which feature of free will the activity cannot reproduce—specifically any first-person sense of authorship or “up-to-me-ness”—and how you will supply that in discussion.

Also specify: needed materials (laminated cards, tokens, containers, timer); a minute-by-minute timeline for instructions, task, hidden interruptions, and debrief; what will predictably go wrong the first run (Fixed-Protocol Agents breaking character out of frustration, Revisable Agents monopolizing the task, students treating the loop as a joke); and how to handle each failure without destroying the experience.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for named theories beyond the sphex reference. Do not quote any real text verbatim. Keep the response to at most 650 words.
