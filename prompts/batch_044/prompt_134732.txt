A couple is deciding whether to adopt. A public-health program offers them free, anonymous screening that reveals only distant, probabilistic risks to their future caregiving capacity—nothing about present health or the child’s prospects. The counselor urges acceptance on the grounds that rational agents should never refuse costless information before a major decision, invoking the reasoning behind Good’s theorem on the value of information. One partner wants to take the test; the other refuses, arguing that receiving the result might restructure not merely her beliefs but the very evaluative framework—her conception of commitment, risk, and parental duty—by which she assesses adoption, thereby violating the theorem’s implicit assumption that the decision-maker’s preferences remain stable across belief-updates. She asks whether her refusal expresses a genuine philosophical limit on the value of information, or is simply irrational avoidance. Advise them, and address whether Good’s result survives when information threatens to change the agent’s utility function itself.
