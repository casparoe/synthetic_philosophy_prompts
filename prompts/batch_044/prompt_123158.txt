Hey, I'm a journalist covering ideas and culture, and I've got an argument that's eating my lunch. It seems to prove that comparing honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures is impossible, which feels way too strong. Here's the chain my editor tossed at me:

(1) If culture A is morally superior to B, then people in B have reason to switch once they understand both frameworks.
(2) Honor cultures tie worth to public recognition and retaliation; dignity cultures tie it to intrinsic value; victimhood cultures tie it to institutional acknowledgment of harm.
(3) Apply honor logic to itself: if you fail to defend honor culture against critics, you're dishonored, so honor culture must suppress dissent or escalate—meaning it can't tolerate pluralism without contradiction.
(4) Apply victimhood logic to itself: denying someone's grievance is itself a grievance, so there's an infinite regress of meta-grievances that only an omniscient arbiter could settle.
(5) Dignity culture avoids both traps because it doesn't need external validation.
(6) So dignity is the only framework that can consistently claim moral superiority.
(7) But if dignity claims superiority, it's saying honor/victimhood people live wrongly, which violates dignity's own commitment to not imposing values on others' self-definition.
(8) Therefore none of the three can consistently claim superiority, so moral comparison between these cultures is impossible.

Something occures between (6) and (8) but I can't name it. Maybe (3) and (4) overstate the self-application? Or maybe (7) confuses "being correct" with "imposing"? I want to keep (1) if I can. Which step do you reject, why did it look okay, and what does giving it up cost? Please answer in American English—my copy editor is picky about that.
