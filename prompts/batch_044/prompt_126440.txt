I'm currently writing up my philosophical profile for a seminar on perception and cognition, and after a long discussion with friends about whether radiologists literally see tumors or merely infer them very quickly, I've settled on a package of views that feels coherent to me but probably isn't. I'd like an audit before I present it. Here is where I stand: (a) I think cognitive penetration is real and can shape not only high-level categorization but also intermediate organizational features of the visual field—figure-ground relations and spatial boundaries—not merely post-perceptual labeling; (b) I accept that early visual processing, in the modularity tradition, remains informationally encapsulated with respect to basic feature extraction like color, orientation, and motion; (c) I hold that expert perception—say, an experienced ornithologist identifying a species in dense foliage—reflects a genuine phenomenological change in what is experienced, not just accelerated inference; (d) I maintain that even penetrated experiences retain a distinctive non-inferential directness and can provide immediate, belief-independent justification; (e) I reject any myth of the given, so I deny that there is a pure, theory-neutral stratum of sensory data serving as an unmediated epistemic foundation; and (f) I've recently become convinced of something I'll call asymmetric dominance in perceptual justification: when a penetrating belief and the resulting experience conflict, the experience should dominate and the belief should be revised, because the perceptual system—even when penetrated—tracks a structural coherence that beliefs alone lack.

The problem is that I can't tell whether I'm holding a consistent position or quietly helping myself to incompatible motivations. For instance, (a) and (b) look like they clash, but I think the clash might dissolve once the levels are sorted properly. Meanwhile, (c) together with (d) seems to push me toward treating concept-laden seeing as still direct, which feels suspicious, and (e) combined with (f) looks like it might undermine the very authority I'm granting to experience in (f), since if nothing is given then every experience is already theory-soaked. I want you to go through these pair by pair: say which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on inspection, which are genuine and serious, and rank the genuine ones by severity. For each real tension, specify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what exactly that revision costs me—whether it forces me to abandon directness, weaken my claim about expertise, or drop the asymmetric-dominance idea altogether. Don't just list conflicts; tell me which single commitment, if surrendered, cleans up the most damage, and whether my overall profile is salvageable as a recognizably realist but non-foundationalist view of perception.
