Two members of my philosophy study group, Maya and Leo, got into a sustained argument about Bradley’s regress and whether it really undermines every relational analysis of propositional unity. I was listening and trying to follow, but I couldn’t tell where the disagreement actually turned. Here is roughly how it went.

Maya started by saying that Bradley’s regress is fatal to any view that treats a proposition as a complex held together by relations. If the unity of “Socrates is wise” requires a further relating relation R that binds Socrates and wisdom together, then R itself must be bound to Socrates and to wisdom, which requires further relations, and so on. She insisted that this applies not just to old-fashioned “copy theories” but also to modern structuralist accounts: if structure is explained by a structural relation that is itself one of the constituents, the same regress arises; if it is not a constituent, then the complex is left unbound and is not genuinely one thing. So she concluded that we must accept a primitive, non-relational “togetherness” that is not itself an item in the proposition, and that any attempt to analyze this togetherness relationally collapses back into the regress.

Leo replied that Maya was conflating Bradley’s original argument about external relations with a different issue about structural description. He said contemporary structuralists do not treat the structural relation as an extra constituent that needs binding; rather, the mode of combination is internal to how the parts form the whole, analogous to the way syntax is not an extra word in a sentence. He then argued that Maya’s primitive togetherness is obscure: if it is genuinely non-relational, then false propositions like “Socrates is flying” possess exactly the same primitive unity as true ones, since Socrates and flying are “together” in the judgment regardless of correspondence. But then, Leo said, she has no resources to explain why the false judgment fails, except by appealing to an external correspondence relation—which she has just rejected as regressive. He proposed instead that we block the regress by treating relations as “unsaturated” in something like Frege’s sense: they do not need a further binder because they are inherently incomplete and are completed precisely by being applied to arguments, not by being related to them.

Maya came back by saying that Leo’s appeal to unsaturatedness merely relocates the problem. If a relation is not a complete object, then it cannot be part of a proposition that is supposed to be the complete object of a judgment. She claimed that Leo was implicitly treating the proposition as something that gets completed by the mental act of judging, which makes unity subjective and collapses into psychologism. She added that she can account for falsehood without correspondence: in her view, the primitive togetherness explains mental synthesis, so “Socrates is flying” is genuinely one mental content, but it lacks the corresponding external complex; falsehood is therefore a failure of external realization, not a failure of internal unity.

Leo answered that this makes propositional content mind-dependent in a damaging way. If the unity of a judgment is just mental synthesis, then two people who both falsely judge that Socrates is flying might synthesize the elements differently, and there is no shared, objective proposition they both grasp. He asked whether Maya could preserve intersubjective agreement and logical objectivity without smuggling relational structure back in to fix the content across minds. He also pressed her on whether her “primitive togetherness” was doing any explanatory work, or whether it was just a label for the problem Bradley identified.

Can you go through their exchange round by round and adjudicate who is right about which specific point? In particular: does Leo successfully distinguish structural combination from Bradley’s regress, or does Maya show that the distinction collapses? Is Maya’s account of falsehood coherent without correspondence, and does Leo fairly charge her with psychologism? Does Leo’s unsaturatedness proposal avoid the regress or just rename it? And what, if anything, are both of them missing about whether primitive unity can be both non-relational and objective? Please do not just declare an overall winner; say where each argument succeeds or fails, and identify any shared blind spot.
