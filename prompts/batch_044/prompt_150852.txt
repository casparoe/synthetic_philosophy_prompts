I'm drafting a short essay for a philosophy seminar—tentatively titled "Indexical Obligations and Institutional Redundancy"—and I would value critical comments on the coherence of its central argument, the plausibility of its conditions, and whether I'm overlooking an obvious objection that undermines the whole approach. The essay begins from a familiar tension in the literature on why promises bind: expectation-based accounts struggle to explain promises to do what one is already duty-bound to do, since no new expectation is generated; normative-power accounts, by contrast, can explain the creation of a new obligation, but it remains unclear why exercising that power adds anything meaningful when institutional roles already prescribe the identical conduct. Rather than adjudicating between these frameworks, I propose that promises generate what I call an indexical obligation—an obligation not merely to perform the promised action, but to perform it specifically as the fulfillment of this particular speech act, which carries its own distinct structure of accountability and answerability. I then apply this idea to promises made by public officials, for instance a minister who promises to implement a policy that her ministry's charter already mandates. In such cases, the promise seems to survive the dissolution of the institutional role, leaving a residual sense of personal betrayal if she breaks her word, even though the institutional duty remains intact. I sketch three conditions for this indexical layer to arise: the promisor must retain discretionary authority over the action at the time of promising; the promisee must be positioned to hold the promisor answerable specifically for the breach of word rather than for institutional failure; and the context must allow the speech act to be re-identified across time as the same commitment. From these, I argue that redundant promises are not redundant at all—they shift the locus of accountability from the office to the speaker.

What I especially want feedback on is whether this distinction genuinely does explanatory work that more established accounts—say, those emphasizing normative powers or trust-based expectations—cannot accommodate without ad hoc adjustments; whether the three conditions are jointly sufficient or whether they are so permissive that they would count casual assurances as binding in ways that seem counterintuitive; and whether the application to public officials smuggles in undefended assumptions about democratic representation and the personal authority of officeholders. I am not asking for a literature survey or references; I will handle citations and reading myself. Rather, I am interested in whether the conceptual architecture holds together and whether there is a cleaner way to frame the contrast between institutional duty and personal answerability without inventing an entirely new ontological category. If you think the essay should be reframed—perhaps by dropping the institutional case and focusing instead on interpersonal promises made within dense social roles—I would like to hear that as well.
