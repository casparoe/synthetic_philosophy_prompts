I am preparing the final examination for my graduate seminar on rationality and self-knowledge, and I need a single essay question that forces students to think structurally rather than reproduce lecture notes. The topic has become personally urgent: last term I endorsed a departmental restructuring proposal on the strength of what I now recognize as an overfitted inside-view narrative about our institution's exceptional culture, and I failed to weight the outside-view base rate of comparable restructuring failures across peer universities. I want the exam to trap students in the very reflexive problem I failed to solve—how one can exercise epistemic modesty about one's own reasoning without either collapsing into self-defeating skepticism or smuggling the inside view back in through the back door of reference-class selection.

Please design one 90-minute essay question worth 40 marks, expecting about 800–900 words. Present a constructed scenario—call it "the calibration loop"—in which a senior researcher must decide whether her first-person assessment of her own statistical reasoning competence should be overridden by an outside-view base rate about expert overconfidence in her discipline. The question should require students to address four specific cruxes: (a) why the inside view carries a distinctive first-person authority that statistical aggregation cannot fully replace; (b) whether applying the outside view to one's own cognitive processes generates a reflexive paradox or an infinite regress; (c) what a genuinely second-order form of epistemic modesty would look like if it targets not the content of belief but the criteria for selecting reference classes; and (d) whether such modesty can be principled rather than merely psychological, and if so, what principle could govern it without begging the question.

Then provide a model answer of roughly 800 words that demonstrates the required conceptual architecture: a clear distinction between first-order deliberation and second-order calibration; an explicit identification of the reflexive loop without resolving it by fiat; an account of structural (not attitudinal) modesty; and a recognition that judgments of reference-class similarity are themselves inside-view dependent.

Finally, provide a marking scheme that separates an A answer—must contain all four cruxes, must treat modesty as operating on reference-class selection criteria rather than on belief strength alone, and must avoid treating the inside view as a simple bias to be eliminated—from a B answer, which correctly describes the inside/outside distinction but either resolves the loop too quickly, treats modesty as a heuristic switch, or misses the second-order structural point. Specify exactly which errors should cost marks: for instance, conflating epistemic modesty with underconfidence; appealing to an undefined "objective" similarity metric; or failing to see that selecting the reference class already requires the inside view.

Keep the prompt formally polished, self-contained, free of required citations or bibliographies, and designed so that a student cannot answer well by reciting standard examples like planning fallacy or base-rate neglect without engaging the reflexive structure.
