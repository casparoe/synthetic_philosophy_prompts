A study group constructed the following argument about Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith and now finds it suspicious, because its conclusion collapses the very distinction Sartre wants to preserve. (1) For Sartre, human reality is radically free: in every situation we transcend our facticity and choose our being-for-itself; there is no fixed human nature that determines our choices. (2) Bad faith, or mauvaise foi, is the self-deceptive attempt to deny this freedom by identifying oneself entirely with one's facticity—treating oneself as a fixed thing, like the café waiter who attempts to be nothing but a waiter. (3) Authenticity, by contrast, requires recognizing one's freedom and committing to a freely chosen project, sustaining that project over time without treating it as an essential destiny. (4) But any sustained commitment requires treating one's values, preferences, or character traits as relatively stable across time; otherwise there is no meaningful continuity to the project. (5) Treating one's values or traits as stable is treating oneself with some degree of facticity—treating a past pattern as constraining present choice. (6) By (2), treating oneself with facticity in this way is bad faith. (7) Therefore, the sustained commitment required for authenticity necessarily involves bad faith. (8) So on Sartre's own terms, authenticity and bad faith are not opposed but necessarily coextensive: to be authentic is to be in bad faith about one's freedom. (9) This is absurd, since Sartre clearly presents them as antagonistic. (10) So one of the premises must go. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) intact and pay whatever price is needed elsewhere, but I want to know exactly what I am buying with that payment.
