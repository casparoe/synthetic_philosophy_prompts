I'm defending a revision to the nonidentity problem at a joint philosophy-public-policy seminar. My thesis replaces the binary "same person" condition with a graded "narrative-role" condition: an action wrongs future people not by making them numerically identical to worse-off alternatives impossible, but by degrading the welfare-relevant social trajectory that the particular future position they occupy inherits, relative to a feasible baseline trajectory—so climate inaction can wrong them even when it changes who exists. The audience is eight analytic metaphysicians (several convinced four-dimensionalism or the denial of moral status to merely possible people dissolves the problem), three environmental ethicists who need actionable climate guidance, and one disability-studies philosopher alert to ableist implications in "worse-off" comparisons. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the six objections I will actually face, ordered by likelihood and damage, each phrased in that audience's vocabulary (e.g., modal-realist challenges to counterfactual social trajectories, demands for a non-ableist baseline metric, practical complaints that the framework gives climate policymakers no decision rule). For each objection, provide a two-minute reply and an explicit note on where that reply is softest. State what I should concede in my opening remarks, identify the weakest point in my own position I should defend rather than hope they miss, and include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—the one that forces me to admit my framework requires comparing counterfactual social trajectories that may not be metaphysically well-defined. Keep the reply practical and in the room, not a literature review.
