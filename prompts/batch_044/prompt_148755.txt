You are an expert philosopher of history and an experienced seminar facilitator with a particular interest in conceptual problems that arise when institutional forms recur across epochs. Before you draft anything, spend a few minutes brainstorming how a structured, rule-governed simulation could make participants experience—rather than merely hear about—the tension between cyclical and linear historiography, especially the difficulty of deciding whether a revived institution is the “same” form or a pseudomorphic successor when the interpretive rules that define it have drifted.

Design a 90-minute classroom activity for 10–12 postgraduate students in a philosophy of history seminar. The setting should be a small, round-table workshop with no lecture component up front. Your aim is to make the group feel the force of the idea that cyclical recurrence is not just an empirical pattern but raises a deep problem of diachronic identity: if the criteria by which a community identifies its own constitutional, religious, or civic institutions change subtly between cycles, then successive applications of the “same” concept may be indeterminate in the way that rule-following is indeterminate—without anyone being able to point to a fact of the matter that resolves the dispute.

The activity should assign concrete roles (e.g., three “archivists” charged with classifying recovered charters, four “constitutional interpreters” who must vote on whether a restored assembly counts as the original body or a new foundation, and three “chroniclers” who record the reasoning without adjudicating). Provide explicit rules for constrained choice: for instance, a limited pool of symbolic legitimacy tokens that can only be allocated if the group reaches unanimous agreement on whether the current polity is in a linear progression, an anacyclotic return, or a Spenglerian pseudomorphosis. Build in small, non-monetary stakes—such as the right to frame the official narrative of the session—so that coordination failures have real social cost within the room.

Arrange the rules and incentives so that participants run into the problem themselves: they should discover, through their own attempts to apply the classification rules, that they agree on every observable feature of the revived institution yet still disagree on whether history has looped, precisely because their respective interpretive criteria have shifted in ways they did not authorise but cannot correct. Only after this deadlock should the facilitator name the parallel to cyclical versus linear models and map the moments onto Polybius’s anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun’s cyclical rise and decline of group solidarity, Spengler’s pseudomorphosis, and Toynbee’s challenge-and-response framework—particularly asking whether cyclical models require token-identity of social forms or merely type-identity.

Then provide a structured debrief (about 20 minutes) that maps each phase of the simulation onto these thinkers. Be frank about which feature of the historiographical debate the activity cannot reproduce—likely the long temporal scale and the absence of first-person testimony across centuries—and state how the facilitator must supply this limitation explicitly. Also include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time you run the activity (for example, one dominant student resolving the ambiguity by fiat, or the group conflating empirical disagreement with conceptual indeterminacy) and how to handle it.

Write the response in British English. At the end, state how confident you are in the design and single out the point you are least sure of—specifically, whether the analogy between institutional identification and rule-following indeterminacy genuinely illuminates the cyclical/linear problem or risks being an artificial metaphor imposed on the simulation. Think carefully, then present the final plan with a clear timeline, role descriptions, rule set, payoff structure, debrief outline, and the required self-critical note. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
