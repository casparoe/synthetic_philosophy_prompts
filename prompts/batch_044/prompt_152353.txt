I am developing an interpretive framework for Spinoza's democratic theory and want a consistency audit before I present it. I hold the following commitments, but I am not certain they form a stable package, and I have not decided which one I would surrender if forced.

(1) Spinoza's democracy is best understood through the lens of immanent, non-alienated sovereignty: the multitude never transfers its natural right to a ruler, but only delegates revocable exercises of collective power to councils.
(2) Consequently, democratic councils can issue binding laws without this being representation in the modern sense; authority remains retained by the citizens collectively.
(3) Spinoza's exclusion of women, servants, foreigners, and dependents from citizenship is based on structural dependency rather than natural inferiority, and is therefore compatible with democracy being the absolute form of sovereignty—since citizenship requires the independence necessary for collective reason, not universal inclusion.
(4) Drawing on Spinoza's ethical rationalism, I hold that a democratic decision possesses legitimate authority only if it actually increases the collective potentia—the shared power of acting—of the body politic; procedural majoritarianism that diminishes this power is not democratic authority but masked domination.
(5) I also interpret Spinoza's claim that democracy is the most natural form of government as an empirical-stability thesis: democratic arrangements align individual self-preservation with collective preservation, making them more durable than monarchies or aristocracies, rather than expressing a normative preference for equality.

My request is an audit, not a lecture: trace which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the remaining tensions by severity. I am especially concerned with the relation between (1) and (4): if sovereignty is never alienated but remains immanent in the multitude, by what right does an external ethical criterion—the increase of collective power—judge democratic outcomes? Can this be resolved by identifying the true collective will with what genuinely augments power, or does it introduce a rationalist standard that contradicts immanence? Second, does the reasoning in (3) genuinely neutralize the tension with democracy's absoluteness, or does the dependency-based exclusion still undermine the claim that democracy minimizes alienation? Finally, please separate what is well established in Spinoza scholarship—such as his exclusion of certain groups and his distinction between democratic and monarchical sovereignty—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, such as whether his democracy is essentially majoritarian, rationalist, or immanentist. Do not present any single contested interpretive framework as the scholarly consensus.
