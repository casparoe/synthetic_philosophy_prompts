I was grading papers for my philoosophy of value course and I'm not totally sure I'm being fair with this one. The exam prompt asked: The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is usually introduced with moral examples, but philosophers have applied it to aesthetic appreciation and belief formation too. Is the problem structurally the same across all three domains, or does it take a different form in each? In particular, discuss whether it undermines moral rationalism—the view that moral requirements are just requirements of practical reason—or whether it can be contained within specific domains without threatening rationalism generally.

Here's what the student wrote:

The wrong kind of reasons problem arises when the reason that actually moves someone to hold an attitude doesn't match the conditions that make that attitude fitting. In moral action, right-kind reasons cite the action's moral properties—like reducing suffering—while wrong-kind reasons cite personal benefit or social pressure. The same structure holds in aesthetics: praising a symphony because it's harmonically complex is right-kind, but praising it to impress a date is wrong-kind. Belief works analogously; evidential support is right-kind, wishful thinking is wrong-kind.

This doesn't threaten moral rationalism, though. Christine Korsgaard shows that moral reasons are constitutive of rational agency—when you ask why be moral you're asking from outside practical reason, so self-interested motives aren't competing wrong-kind reasons but just failures to grasp normative authority. So the problem collapses for morality but remains for non-rational attitudes like taste. Meanwhile Peter Strawson's reactive-attitudes view dissolves the issue altogether by treating moral demands as expressions of social expectation rather than reason-governed requirements, which protects rationalism because there's no normative conflict to solve. So the WKR problem is mainly aesthetic, with belief being an intermediate case.
