I've been working through some ideas about insects and moral obligations on my own, without a philosophy background, and I'd like to know whether the package I've arrived at is internally consistent or whether I'm quietly helping myself to contradictory premises.

Here are my commitments as I currently understand them:

(1) The scale argument is structurally sound: if there is a non-negligible probability that a class of beings is sentient, and that class is orders of magnitude larger than humanity, then obligations toward that class should dominate our moral calculus.

(2) Despite that, I do not believe we have direct obligations to insects, because I think genuine sentience requires an integrated subjective perspective—a single point of view that binds experiences together over time—and insects lack the neural architecture for this. I take reactive behavior or nociception alone to be insufficient.

(3) I hold that if conclusive evidence ever showed insects do possess such an integrated perspective, then commitment (1) would immediately activate, and the moral implications would be so vast that they would override most current human priorities.

(4) At the same time, I think it is rational to avoid harming insects when doing so is costless—for instance, not deliberately crushing them—but I frame this not as respecting rights, rather as maintaining epistemic humility: since my judgment in (2) might be wrong, needless cruelty is an unjustified gamble against a catastrophic error.

(5) In population ethics, I am a totalist: I believe the correct comparison between worlds depends on the summed well-being of all beings with moral standing. However, I restrict this framework to beings that have already crossed the integration threshold in (2).

There is also a more specific organizing idea I have been using, which I call the threshold-breadth split: moral significance requires both that a being crosses a threshold of cognitive integration (below which the sheer number of subjects is irrelevant) and that, once crossed, the number of such beings is multiplied by the intensity of their welfare. I apply this split consistently across (2), (3), and (5).

My questions: Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to conflict? In particular, does (4) smuggle in a partial recognition of moral standing that contradicts the hard denial in (2)? Does my restriction of totalism in (5) to post-threshold beings conflict with the unconditional logic of (1), given that (1) seems to apply as soon as there is any non-negligible probability of sentience, regardless of architecture? And does the threshold-breadth split actually resolve these issues, or does it merely rename them? Please rank the tensions by severity, and for each suggest the least costly revision that would restore consistency without abandoning the core intuition behind (2).
