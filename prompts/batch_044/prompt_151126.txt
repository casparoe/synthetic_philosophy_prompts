Consider the institutional protocol, standard in Western clinical ethics, that requires informed consent to be obtained separately from each individual patient or research participant, even in contexts where the intervention is inherently collective — for example, household-level exposure studies for environmental toxins, or familial genetic screening programmes in which any result necessarily implicates every relative. The form treats consent as an act performed by a bounded, singular consciousness that can be isolated from others simply by drawing a line around one body. Yet the same institutions often accommodate family-centred decision-making in palliative care, and acknowledge that psychological harm or benefit routinely flows across these boundaries in ways that individual consent documents do not capture.

What metaphysics of consciousness and personal identity does the isolated-consent practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require a pluralist view in which each body houses a distinct, non-overlapping stream of awareness? If one takes seriously the position, associated with Erwin Schrödinger's engagement with Vedanta, that consciousness is fundamentally unitary and that the appearance of multiple selves is a kind of perspectival illusion — a single subject viewing itself through different windows — can the practice be defended as a pragmatically necessary fiction, or does it commit the institution to a contradiction? Further, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates: is individual consent compatible with a weak form of unity (for instance, that awareness is continuous across bodies in some non-physical sense), or does it rule out even that? Finally, say what would have to change in clinical procedure if the underlying metaphysics were replaced by Schrödinger's unitary view: would consent become a collective ritual performed by the family as one entity, or would the very concept of individual consent dissolve?
