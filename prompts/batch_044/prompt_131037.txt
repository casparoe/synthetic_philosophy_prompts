I'm examining karma not merely as a religious doctrine but specifically as a theory of moral desert: the view that an agent's well-being or suffering is distributed in proportion to the moral quality of her actions. In doing so, I've noticed a recurring conceptual configuration that I can't place precisely in the philosophical literature. The view is typically presented as operating with a kind of moral-causal inevitability—past wrongs generate present suffering through an automatic, law-like process—yet the same traditions routinely describe practices (ritual acts, devotional practices, or intercessory actions) that can diminish, redirect, or annul karmic outcomes without being treated as violations of that law. The result is a system that looks deterministically desert-based at the explanatory level, but simultaneously contains an internal mechanism for desert-modification that is not framed as an exception or a miracle. I can point to concrete expressions: one might explain a person's illness by saying this is the fruit of prior cruelty, treating the suffering as strictly earned, while also maintaining that a specific practice can burn off that karmic residue, treating the outcome as negotiable within the same framework. Is there an established name for this exact tension—the coexistence of strict moral-causal determinism and institutionally embedded desert-cancellation within a single theory? If there is, how does it differ from the standard free-will versus determinism problem, and from the problem of evil in theistic contexts? If there is not, can you delineate the phenomenon precisely—what distinguishes it from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses about causation, and where are its boundaries with other desert-modifying mechanisms such as grace, pardon, or restitution?
