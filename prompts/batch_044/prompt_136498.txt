Consider the following argument about the doctrine of double effect and the standard trolley distinctions.

(1) Double effect permits causing serious harm only if the harm is neither intended as an end nor intended as a means, and the good achieved is proportionate.

(2) In the switch case, diverting the trolley kills one as a foreseen side effect of saving five; since the one is not used as a means, the act is permitted.

(3) Now imagine a crane case: you swing a heavy beam to block the trolley and save five people. The beam’s pivot mechanism necessarily crushes a sleeping bystander near the fulcrum; you foresee this, do not desire it, and the five are saved by the beam’s position, not by the crushing. By (1), this should also be permitted.

(4) In the fat-man case, by contrast, pushing a person off a bridge to block the trolley is standardly forbidden, on the grounds that his body is used as the means of obstruction.

(5) Yet in both the fat-man and the crane cases the agent’s mental state can be identical: “I need a physical obstruction of sufficient mass; I regret that a human being is involved, but I do not want him harmed, and I would avoid it if I could without losing the obstruction.”

(6) The only relevant difference is causal description: in the fat-man case the body is the obstruction, whereas in the crane case the beam is the obstruction and the crushing is a mechanical side effect of operating it.

(7) So whether double effect permits the action depends not on what is genuinely intended, but on how we redescribe the causal chain—labeling a harm “part of the means” or “a side effect of the means.”

(8) If mental-state criteria govern, then pushing the fat man is permitted whenever the agent sincerely does not intend his death; if causal-structure criteria govern, then nearly any harm can be redescribed as a side effect of operating some other instrument, making the doctrine either redundant with consequentialism or arbitrarily permissive.

(9) Therefore double effect proves too much: consistently applied, it either licenses the fat-man push or collapses into ad hoc causal taxonomy with no independent moral weight.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, assess whether (5) fairly captures mental-state equivalence, whether (7) illegitimately conflates metaphysical causation with moral description, and whether there is a principled boundary between means and side effect that does not depend on how we parse the machinery. Reach and defend a verdict on whether the doctrine survives this challenge.
