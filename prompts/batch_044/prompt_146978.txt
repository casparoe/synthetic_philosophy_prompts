Consider the following argument, which takes Bradley's regress against the unity of the proposition and extends it to all forms of unified representation.

(1) A proposition achieves genuine unity only if its terms are connected by a relation that actually binds them, rather than leaving them as mere neighbours in a list.

(2) If that binding relation R is a distinct entity in its own right, then R itself must be connected to each original term by further relations—call them R₁ and R₂—otherwise R does not genuinely link them.

(3) Those further relations are themselves distinct entities, and so require yet further relations to bind them, generating an infinite regress.

(4) Hence, if relations are real entities separate from their relata, no proposition can ever be fully unified.

(5) The very same structure governs any representational system in which distinct elements must be integrated into a single state: for example, the binding of colour and shape in perceptual experience, or the compositional unification of vector embeddings into a single distributed representation.

(6) Therefore, if Bradley's reasoning is sound, not only are propositions impossible, but all unified perception, cognition, and compositional representation are impossible.

(7) Clearly, unified representation does occur.

(8) So the argument must fail somewhere.

I am inclined to preserve (1), since mere adjacency is not genuine unity, and I also wish to preserve (5), because the structural parallel between propositional and representational binding seems philosophically illuminating. Given these commitments, which step should be rejected? In particular, does abandoning (2) force me to treat relations as internal to their relata, or is there a different strategy—such as denying that every connection requires a further connecting entity—to halt the regress without collapsing the distinction between relation and terms? Please provide your analysis in British English, and explain what each available rejection costs.
