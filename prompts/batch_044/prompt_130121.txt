I'm trying to determine what I actually think about presentism in historical writing, testing my intuitions against concrete cases rather than abstract definitions. Here are my gut verdicts.

Case 1: A historian evaluates seventeenth-century alchemists and writes that their experiments fail basic scientific standards because they never articulated falsifiability conditions. The historian treats this absence as a defect in their practice, not merely a historical difference. Verdict: illegitimate presentism.

Case 2: A historian of abolition describes eighteenth-century activists as recognizing the inherent dignity of the enslaved, using that phrase even though the activists spoke in religious and natural-rights terms. The historian uses a modern concept to capture what was genuinely available within their own framework. Verdict: not presentism; legitimate conceptual translation.

Case 3: A historian analyzes medieval feudal obligations through modern contract-law categories—offer, acceptance, consideration—and criticizes the system for lacking genuine consent mechanisms. Verdict: illegitimate presentism; the vocabulary distorts the social reality being studied.

Case 4: A historian of 1950s psychiatry describes forced insulin shock therapy in clinical detail but refuses to apply any evaluative term like abuse or torture, insisting that judgment must be restricted to the professional standards of that era. Verdict: I keep reversing myself. Sometimes it feels like appropriate historical empathy; sometimes it feels like a moral evasion that hides harm behind period vocabulary.

Case 5: A historian uses modern epidemiological models to estimate mortality in ancient Rome, then criticizes Roman sanitary authorities for failing to implement cost-effective interventions given data those authorities could not have possessed. Verdict: presentist.

Case 6: A historian compares medieval astronomical predictions against modern orbital mechanics, notes where they approximate correct values, and explains deviations by reference to missing gravitational theory. Verdict: not presentist; using modern knowledge for causal explanation is permissible.

Case 7: A historian writes about nineteenth-century gender norms and judges them morally deficient for failing to recognize gender equality, framing the failure as comparable to a modern refusal to acknowledge climate change. Verdict: presentist.

Is there a single principle—distinguishing, say, explanatory redescription from normative blame, or internal framework translation from external standard imposition—that captures cases 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7 while giving a stable verdict on case 4? Or must I drop one of the other verdicts to keep the principle simple? Specifically, does case 2 survive if the principle bans all normative translation, or does case 6 survive if the principle permits all retrospective application of modern knowledge? What does the best principle predict for a case I have not listed: a historian who applies modern game-theoretic models to understand Roman patronage dynamics, explicitly refusing to evaluate whether those dynamics were fair by modern standards, but using the model solely to show why cooperative arrangements were structurally unstable?

Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, show its verdict on the unlisted case, and be explicit about which of my verdicts—especially on case 4—it cannot accommodate without added complexity.
