I'm writing a paper for an advanced seminar on value theory, and I've become stuck on a long-running disagreement in the negative-utilitarian literature that I can't seem to move past with my study group. The dispute concerns what has been called lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: the view that suffering carries not merely greater but lexically prior weight, such that once a certain magnitude of suffering is present, no quantity of pleasure—however vast—can justify allowing or producing that suffering, and below that threshold the two may be weighed, but never across it. Proponents of this view argue that pleasure and pain are metrically incommensurable across the threshold, not just differently weighted. Their opponents—many of whom accept that suffering should be minimized—insist that pleasure and pain remain on a single bipolar scale, and that the "threshold" is merely a practical heuristic or a rhetorical exaggeration of a steep but continuous trade-off. Despite decades of discussion, neither camp seems to have shifted: the lexicalists keep pointing to intuitions about extreme torture versus mild bliss, while the continuous-scale theorists keep modeling well-being with unified utility functions and asking for the empirical parameter that would justify a discontinuity.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled not because anyone is missing an empirical fact, but because the two sides are answering different questions under the single heading of "commensurability." One side seems to be asking whether pleasure and pain share a common axiological currency—whether they can be summed into one net welfare figure—while the other is asking whether they can be traded off in practical deliberation without a kind of moral category mistake. But I'm unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real structure of the disagreement, or whether there is a hidden formal question—perhaps about the possibility of representing discontinuous value relations in standard decision theory—that neither side has made explicit. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal, arising from ambiguity in "commensurable" or "threshold"? Is it substantive but hidden behind a disagreement about which cases count as data—extreme hypotheticals versus aggregate welfare measurements? Or are the sides simply operating with bedrock normative intuitions that cannot be argued for?

Given your diagnosis, I also want to know what would actually count as progress. Would it take a formal result showing that discontinuous value relations are or are not representable in a coherent choice framework? A conceptual distinction that cleanly separates axiological currency from deliberative trade-offs? Or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed, and that "lexical threshold" is not a feature of value but a projection of our unwillingness to perform certain calculations? Please support your diagnosis with attention to what each side actually says about extreme cases, about aggregation, and about the role of intuition, and be honest about whether anything—conceptual, formal, or empirical—should be expected to move the dispute.
