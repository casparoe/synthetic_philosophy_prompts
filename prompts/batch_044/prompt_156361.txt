Consider the following five rulings issued by the same institutional body—the Committee on Proportional Causation—regarding whether an action is permissible under the doctrine of double effect when it produces a foreseen but unintended harmful outcome.

Ruling 1: A surgeon removes a cancerous uterus from a pregnant patient; the fetus dies as an unavoidable physical consequence of the removal, but the surgery is performed solely to save the mother and the fetal death plays no causal role in the cure. Verdict: permissible.

Ruling 2: A surgeon performs a craniotomy, crushing the fetal skull as the direct mechanical means of extracting the fetus and thereby saving the mother’s life. Verdict: impermissible.

Ruling 3: An autonomous navigation system, facing an unavoidable collision, diverts its vehicle into a barrier, killing the occupant but preventing impact with five pedestrians; the occupant’s death is a consequence of the diversion, not the mechanism by which the pedestrians are spared. Verdict: permissible.

Ruling 4: The same system, facing an unavoidable collision, calculates that accelerating into a large obstacle and using the body mass of a specific pedestrian as the kinetic brake is the only way to stop and save five others. Verdict: impermissible.

Ruling 5: A demolition engineer destroys a structurally compromised building to prevent a catastrophic explosion that would kill hundreds; one worker trapped inside dies from the collapse, but the worker’s death is not the mechanism by which the explosion is averted, and the engineer acts solely to neutralize the threat. Verdict: permissible.

The Committee now faces a sixth proposal. A public-health authority wishes to deploy a predictive algorithm that identifies individuals likely to be transmitting a lethal pathogen and preemptively isolates them. The algorithm has a false-positive rate of 2 percent: approximately two hundred people who are not infected will be quarantined, suffering severe psychological and economic harm. The harm to these false positives is not part of the epidemiological mechanism by which transmission is reduced—the mechanism is physical separation of infected carriers—but it is a deterministically distributed statistical consequence of applying the algorithm at scale. The authority intends only to reduce transmission, not to harm anyone, and expects to prevent ten thousand deaths.

What verdict should the Committee reach if it wishes to be consistent with its own track record? Identify the closest analog among Rulings 1–5, and specify whether the Committee’s precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. If they do, articulate the two most plausible governing rules that jointly explain Rulings 1–5 but diverge on this algorithmic case—stating precisely what each rule predicts here. Then offer your best-guess recommendation with explicit reasons, and describe the single further case that would definitively adjudicate between those two rules. If, instead, you judge the precedents to determine the outcome unambiguously, state the principle being adopted and defend why no further case is needed to resolve the ambiguity.
