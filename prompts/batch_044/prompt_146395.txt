I've been trying to apply Aristotle's distinction between natural and unnatural acquisition in Politics I.8-10 to modern financial practice, and I think I must be misreading his core argument against interest-taking (tokos). His explicit reason is that money is argos—idle and lacking the natural reproductive capacity of seeds or livestock—so charging interest is like demanding that a coin breed more coins, which violates the nature of the artifact. But in modern banking, money is literally multiplied through lending: a deposit creates new claims that circulate as money, so the premise that currency is naturally sterile seems empirically false for fiat systems under fractional reserve. Does this mean Aristotle's prohibition collapses for modern economies, or is the false move in treating his biological analogy as the real ground of the prohibition rather than as an illustration of a deeper teleological point—namely, that chrematistike reverses the natural order in which goods are acquired for use rather than for unlimited accumulation? More specifically, if the metaphysical premise about money's sterility is undermined by the actual mechanics of credit creation, does his normative conclusion about exchange justice in Nicomachean Ethics V.5 still stand on independent grounds, or have I shown that his ethics of exchange is inseparable from an obsolete ontology of currency?
