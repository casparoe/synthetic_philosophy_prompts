I'm designing a diagnostic handout for an advanced philosophy-of-decision-theory seminar on the strategy-stealing assumption in Newcomb-style problems. I want a taxonomy structured as a decision tree that sorts positions not by the labels philosophers use, but by their commitments on four concrete diagnostic cases. The root should ask whether strategy-stealing requires that the agent's reasoning process be transparent to the predictor. The second level should split on whether a "local-copy" scenario counts—where the agent runs an exact simulation of the predictor's algorithm offline and then acts contrary to its recommendation—specifically, does that count as having stolen the strategy? The third level should ask whether accepting the assumption forces one-boxing in the standard Newcomb setup, or whether one can consistently accept it and still defend two-boxing by appealing to causal independence at the moment of choice. The fourth level should split on the "twin Newcomb" variant: two agents with identical reasoning processes, only one of whom faces the predictor; does the strategy-stealing argument apply symmetrically to both?

Each leaf should be labeled with the name of the view (e.g., "transparent evidentialism," "causal decision theory with precommitment," "robust one-boxing," or "unnamed") and include one sentence on its most awkward commitment—particularly any combination that requires the view to treat the local-copy agent and the transparent agent differently despite identical outputs. I want at least six leaves, with all unoccupied combinations of answers explicitly noted and briefly diagnosed (is the gap incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?). The tree should also flag which named positions it cannot separate—if two well-known views fall on the same branch, say so and identify the further case that would distinguish them. The audience is graduate students who know Newcomb's problem but may not know the literature on strategy-stealing by name. Keep the branching logic explicit: label each branch with the claim that splits it, not with the view it leads to.
