I was recently displaced from a forecasting role after my employer automated the interpretive layer of our logistics planning—leaving me uncertain whether I had lost a craft, a job, or merely a cyclical set of judgments that never qualified as genuinely world-building labor. This personal disruption has driven me back to the philosophical literature on automation and the ontology of work, and I now need a rigorous assessment tool for an upper-level undergraduate seminar I am convening on the subject.

Please design a 75-minute final-exam essay question (expected response: approximately 1,000 words) that cannot be answered by reciting standard anxieties about technological unemployment or by summarizing Arendt’s familiar tripartite distinction between labor, work, and action. The question should instead force students to confront a specific conceptual crux: whether automation that eliminates the effortful interpretation of data—the framing of raw algorithmic outputs as meaningful for organizational decision—destroys a form of practical identity constitutive of the worker, or whether it merely exposes that such interpretation was never genuinely work (in the ontological sense of producing durable world-objects) but rather a disguised form of labor: cyclical, unworldly, and inherently replaceable.

The prompt should require students to navigate at least two of the following tensions without resolving them by simple label-recognition:
first, the phenomenological gap between toiling (repetitive maintenance without lasting product) and working (crafting an interpretive framework that endures beyond the immediate task) when the product in question is not physical but conceptual;
second, the claim that algorithmic management transforms skilled judgment into monitored compliance, thereby altering the moral psychology of the worker even when economic output remains identical;
third, the possibility that defending meaningful work against automation implicitly relies on a normative human exceptionalism that collapses under universalization—if every human deserves protection from automated interpretation, but not every interpretation requires specifically human cognition.

Please provide:
- the exam question itself, precisely worded;
- a model answer of about 600 words that demonstrates the required conceptual moves;
- a marking scheme out of 20 marks that distinguishes first-class answers (18–20) from strong but incomplete responses (14–17) by naming the specific argumentative moves each tier must contain, and that identifies the precise conflation—treating Arendt’s ontological categories as economic or sociological classifications rather than modes of human engagement with the world—that should cost three marks.

The assessment should be formal and polished, appropriate for an exam committee. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid invoking highly specialized theorists or empirical studies that lie outside standard philosophical curricula on work and automation.
