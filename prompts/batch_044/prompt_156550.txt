I am drafting a novel about a man who refuses the public rituals of remorse, and I have been studying Albert Camus's The Stranger as a narrative laboratory for how communities enforce moral membership through affective expectation rather than through judgment of acts alone. Read Meursault's trial, execution, and final encounter with the prison chaplain through P.F. Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes and moral responsibility—particularly the distinction between personal resentment, vicarious moral indignation, and the objective attitude adopted when an agent is treated as an object of social management rather than as a fellow participant in interpersonal relations. The prosecutor constructs his case largely from Meursault's failure to weep at his mother's funeral and his refusal to perform repentance; does this indicate that the court's condemnation is genuinely vicarious indignation on behalf of the dead mother, or does it collapse into a personal resentment at Meursault's refusal to inhabit the shared grammar of grief and moral accountability? Meanwhile, Meursault withholds remorse yet directs unmistakable resentment toward the chaplain and the judicial machinery—does this selective deployment of reactive attitudes challenge Strawson's claim that they form a naturally pervasive web, or does it reveal that one can be a partial participant, resenting precisely those who deny one's autonomy? Furthermore, does Meursault's execution represent the ultimate expression of communal reactive attitudes, or does the state's clinical, procedural removal of him mark the point at which the community abandons the participant stance entirely and adopts the objective attitude of social hygiene? I want a reading that neither reduces Camus to an illustration of Strawson nor distorts the philosophy to fit the novel's absurdist atmosphere; I want to understand what the novel reveals about the theatrical, socially performative dimension of reactive attitudes that philosophical argument alone tends to abstract away. Which scenes—the funeral, the trial speeches, and the chaplain's visit—best illuminate this tension, and how might I adapt their dynamics for my own fiction without allowing the philosophical machinery to become visible on the page?
