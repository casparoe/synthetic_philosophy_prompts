I am drafting the syllabus for an advanced comparative politics seminar, and in selecting readings on regime change I keep returning to the same frustration. The literature on democratic backsliding—applied to Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and more recently the United States—seems to generate endless empirical description without conceptual resolution. Scholars invoke V-Dem indicators, point to court-packing in Warsaw, executive consolidation in Ankara, and partisan judicial appointments in Washington, and yet they divide sharply over whether any of this constitutes backsliding in a meaningful analytical sense or merely reflects competing normative visions of democracy itself. My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled not because of missing data but because the concept of backsliding smuggles in a teleological model of liberal democracy: it treats an independent judiciary, plural media, and neutral bureaucracy not as contingent institutional achievements but as necessary conditions for being democratic at all. Consequently, when majoritarian governments dismantle these features, observers label it decay, while supporters call it democratic correction.

Before I commit my students to this framing, I want an honest diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is the impasse verbal—are backsliding, autocratization, and deconsolidation tracking different phenomena under one name—or is it substantive, turning on hidden normative commitments about whether procedural minimalism, such as free and fair elections, is sufficient for democracy? Does the comparison across Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the US illuminate a common mechanism of elite-driven institutional capture, or does the American case, with its older federal and judicial structures, expose the framework as parochially European? And if the concept is indeed functioning more as a partisan boundary marker than an analytical tool, what kind of conceptual distinction—perhaps separating procedural minimum conditions from liberal supplements, or citizen-driven normative erosion from strategic elite dismantling—would be required to make progress, or should the term be abandoned altogether? I am not asking for a survey of interpretations or a bibliography; I want a structural analysis of why smart people with substantially the same evidence keep disagreeing, and whether anything short of abandoning the concept could actually end the dispute.
