I hold a specific, somewhat unfashionable variant of the transformation-rather-than-fall thesis as applied to the Western Empire: that between roughly 450 and 600 CE, imperial administration did not suffer a catastrophic collapse but was instead horizontally redistributed across successor kingdoms, episcopal courts, and large estates (latifundia). On this view, the barbarian leaders functioned less as destroyers and more as subcontractors of Roman governance, gradually inheriting tax-collection mechanisms, legal procedures, and bureaucratic literacy without retaining the titular imperial office. The disappearance of the Western emperor in 476 CE was therefore administratively redundant—an empty formality—rather than a rupture that shattered an intact system.

What would genuinely count against this position? I am not asking for the familiar catalogues of urban decay or Mediterranean economic fragmentation. Instead, I want to know which specific archaeological patterns or documentary discoveries would demonstrate that Roman institutional knowledge was actually discontinuous rather than relocated; which comparative cases—perhaps the administrative persistence of the Sassanid East versus the Roman West—would weaken the subcontractor analogy; and whether climate data or environmental lead indicators, if interpreted against the grain, could undermine the claim that the imperial office had become redundant. Please also assess honestly whether this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every disappearance of a Roman institution can be redescribed as horizontal redistribution, does the thesis collapse into tautology, and is that fatal for a claim of this kind?
