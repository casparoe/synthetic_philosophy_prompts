I am a retired evolutionary biologist—thirty years studying phenotypic plasticity and organism-environment feedback—who, since retirement, has begun reading philosophy of science with the enthusiasm and incomplete map of a late convert. I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks, not exceeding seven minutes, at a three-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "The Inviolate Level: Explanation Across Disciplines." The audience is genuinely mixed: philosophers of biology and physics, working scientists, graduate students, and administrators who will understand a strange loop only if it is shown, not named.

Write these remarks. The philosophical idea must do structural work, not decorative work: the problem of tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level, specifically as illustrated by M. C. Escher's Drawing Hands, in which each hand draws the other and no ground level is visible. I want this to constrain what I can legitimately say. Because I speak with scientific authority, I am one of the drawing hands; the philosophical vocabulary I now borrow is the other. I cannot stand outside the hierarchy and declare which discipline supplies the foundation.

To prevent this from becoming a generic meditation on interdisciplinarity, inject the following specific, somewhat niche conceptual tension from my own field. For much of my career, the gene was treated as the inviolate base—the hand that draws all higher levels—through niche construction theory and phenotypic feedback loops, we now see that organisms actively construct and modify the selection environments that in turn regulate gene expression and evolutionary trajectory. There is no epistemically prior hand. Use this concrete biological case—not as a lecture in genetics, but as the living analog of Escher's image—to structure the welcome.

The speech should observe these constraints:

First, welcome the participants without asserting that science provides the base reality and philosophy the commentary, or vice versa. Second, introduce the workshop theme through the Drawing Hands analogy, using the niche-construction example to show why the search for an inviolate explanatory level fails in practice rather than merely in principle. Third, set ground rules for discussion that acknowledge mutual dependency: philosophers should not treat scientific practice as raw, untheorized data; scientists should not treat philosophical framing as external ornament. Fourth, land for both audiences—explain any necessary term, but do not flatten the conceptual difficulty into platitude. Fifth, conclude with a brief, genuine statement of what I, as a retired scientist now learning philosophy, do not yet understand, making that ignorance part of the structural frame rather than an apology.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote any philosophical or scientific text verbatim. Write in a formal but personal voice—polished, precise, not colloquial—appropriate for an opening address composed by someone who has read enough to be dangerous but not enough to be authoritative.
