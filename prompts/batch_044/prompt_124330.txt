In longtermist and rationalist discussions of suffering risks—particularly those involving artificial substrates or large-scale future populations—there is a recurring analogy to climate risk: just as carbon emissions accumulate through diffuse industrial activity and eventually trigger catastrophic, hard-to-reverse warming, certain technological trajectories might generate suffering that accumulates through distributed design choices and eventually reaches astronomical scale. Proponents use the comparison to argue that s-risks deserve the same institutional architecture we have built for climate—something like an IPCC for moral patienthood, with standardized metrics, international coordination, and precautionary regulation—because both problems share long feedback loops, collective-action failures, and the possibility of irreversible harm. I want to push on how far this mapping actually holds, not just rhetorically but structurally.

Can you lay out the correspondence explicitly—what plays the role of the emitter, the greenhouse mechanism, the climate model, the tipping point, the mitigation technology, and the intergenerational equity problem in the s-risk case? Then identify the disanalogies that matter: climate risk rests on relatively settled physics with observable precursors, whereas s-risk assessment depends on contested theories of consciousness, valence, and moral status across substrates. Here I want to inject a specific wrinkle: rather than treating suffering as a linear pollutant that accumulates like CO2, what if we model it as a combinatorial state-space explosion, where certain architectures allow the number of potential suffering states to grow exponentially with scale rather than proportionally? Does the climate analogy smuggle in an implicit ontology of suffering as uniformly quantifiable and cumulatively additive—an ontology that breaks down if suffering is substrate-dependent and non-aggregable in the same units? Finally, tell me whether the institutional conclusion—build an IPCC-style body for s-risks—survives once these disanalogies and the state-space framing are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along by importing assumptions about linear causation and global measurability that do not apply to consciousness-related risks.
