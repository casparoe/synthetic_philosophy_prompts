Write a short story situated in a near-future arbitration chamber where civil disputes are resolved through an institutionalized variant of the dollar auction: each disputant deposits a non-refundable bond, then takes turns raising the pledged stakes, with the loser forfeiting their accumulated contribution to the winner and paying an equal penalty to the state. Your protagonist is a court-appointed mediator who has already watched a modest sibling inheritance dispute escalate into total ruin because neither party could absorb the reputational cost of withdrawal.

Now she must counsel a collective of climate refugees facing a corporate claimant over access to a depleting aquifer. The chamber's rules permit unaffiliated observers to purchase "escalation tokens" — instruments that extend the bidding round without altering either party's liability, a provision recently introduced to fund the judiciary. Structure the narrative around her internal deliberation: she recognizes that the sunk-cost architecture makes individual exit irrational at precisely the moment it becomes collectively catastrophic, yet she also sees that the token market rewards prolongation regardless of justice. Do not resolve the dispute neatly; instead, use the mediator's choices — whether to advise capitulation, to reveal the token mechanism's distortion, or to remain silent — to examine whether escalation of commitment is best understood as a psychological failure of the bidders, as an engineered feature of the institution, or as a structural coupling of the two. Keep the setting and mechanics concrete, but allow the conceptual tension to drive the plot.
