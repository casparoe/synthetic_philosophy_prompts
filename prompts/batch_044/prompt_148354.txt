hey im in an upper-level epistemology seminar (around 12-14 students, 75 minutes) and we're stuck on the regress problem — foundationalism vs coherentism vs infinitism. i need an in-class activity that makes the structural pressure of each view feel physical, not just theoretical.

my niche twist: frame justification as a resource-flow game. each student gets a "belief card" with a specific claim (e.g., "this table is solid") and 3 "warrant tokens". to keep your belief in play, you must spend a token to "buy" support from another student, but they can only sell you support if they spend one of their own tokens to defend their own belief card. this creates dependency chains.

design three rounds with different rules:
1. linear chain round — students can only ask the person to their left, forcing an infinite regress backward until someone hits a "basic belief" card that costs zero tokens (foundationalist stop). but the basic card is hidden and randomly assigned, so groups feel the arbitrariness of where the chain ends.
2. circular round — students are arranged in a circle and can only trade within adjacent pairs. eventually two students form a mutual-support loop, but every loop still drains tokens each cycle, so they feel the cost of coherence without external anchor.
3. open network round — anyone can ask anyone, but each justification step costs double tokens the further away the supporter is. this makes infinite extension literally expensive and exhausting.

materials should be cheap (index cards, tokens, maybe string?). include a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money — maybe "belief stability points" where if your card is still supported at round end you stay in the discussion circle, and if the chain breaks you sit out the debrief. but the real payoff is social/intellectual: people want to defend their card.

the key is that participants should construct the dependency structures themselves and only realize they've enacted foundationalism, coherentism, or infinitism when we map it afterwards. no lecture upfront, don't name the positions until the debrief. they should come away seeing that the regress isn't just a logical puzzle but a structural cost you have to pay somewhere, and that picking where to pay it is unavoidable.

in the debrief, map each round onto one of the three positions. also be honest about what the game can't reproduce — probably the normative question of whether circular support is genuinely vicious or just structurally different. and tell me what will predictably go wrong (i think people will hoard tokens or refuse to form loops because it feels like cheating).

please keep it runnable for a real classroom, no bibliography or reading list needed, and write it like youre talking to a classmate who needs this by tomorrow.
