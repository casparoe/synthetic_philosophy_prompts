Write a villanelle on the mechanism of moral revolution—specifically, whether practices like dueling and chattel slavery ended through rational argument, material interest, or the withdrawal of honor—so that the form itself enacts the explanatory standoff. Use one refrain for the claim that explicit moral reasoning never turned the tide (a Williams-style skepticism that large-scale normative change is rarely driven by argument alone) and a second refrain for the claim that honor was the currency suddenly debased (the Appiah-style explanation that dueling died when social recognition was withdrawn from it, not when it was disproved). The interwoven lines should carry the third explanation—shifting material interest—without letting it settle the matter. The two refrains should alternate through the stanzas and finally stand together in the closing couplet, unreconciled and side by side, since the poem’s structural point is that these accounts keep passing each other rather than converging. Keep the imagery concrete and historically specific: a dueling ground at gray dawn, an auction block emptied not by sermon but by ledger, the silence after a challenge is refused because the witnesses have already left. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with the literature on moral change can tell which line belongs to which explanatory camp, and it should leave open, rather than resolve, whether honor, interest, or argument was decisive.
