I'm preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the hidden architecture of thought, and one thread I want to explore is Bradley's regress—the classic worry that if a proposition is a complex of terms and relations, then explaining how those terms are actually bound together seems to require another relation, and then another, ad infinitum. The standard replies—Frege's unsaturated concepts, Russell's primitive relations, tropes, and states of affairs—are well worn in the literature, but I'm interested in whether the problem takes on an unfamiliar shape when we move from single-subject propositions to what we might call plural or collective propositions: mental or linguistic acts in which the subject is genuinely distributed, such as a group's shared intention or a multi-agent judgment. Does the unity of "we believe that the project failed" depend on a relation that binds the individual minds, and if so, does Bradley's regress reappear at the collective level in a form that the classical solutions do not touch?

Please write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers—call them Althea, who defends a refined Fregean account of unsaturatedness extended to collective intentionality, and Balthazar, who argues that plural propositions force us back into a Bradley-style holism about relations. They should genuinely engage with each other: Althea must not be a strawman who ignores the collective dimension, and Balthazar must not merely repeat Bradley but show precisely why distributed subjects break the standard fix. The exchange should be rigorous yet readable, with real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues, and it should not include any bibliography, citation, reading list, or reference to specific editions. Keep in mind that the audience is educated but will not have access to search tools, so the argument must stand on its conceptual feet alone.
