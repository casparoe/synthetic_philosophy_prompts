After a long argument with a friend about Glaucon's challenge and the ring of Gyges, I find myself stuck on an extension he pressed. He asks whether the ring is only a physical device, or whether modern conditions—perfect encryption, untraceable digital identity, and the dissolution of shared normative frameworks—create what we might call a structural ring: a domain where actions are invisible not just to others but to the very mechanisms of moral accountability. From this he built the following argument.

(1) Glaucon's ring tests whether justice is valued for its own sake by removing all external sanctions.
(2) If justice is valued for its own sake, then a fully rational agent should remain just even when wearing the ring.
(3) But with the ring, justice directly conflicts with self-interest, and there is no external cost to injustice.
(4) A fully rational agent has no unresolved practical conflicts; practical reason is unified under the pursuit of what is genuinely good for the agent.
(5) Therefore, a fully rational agent with the ring cannot value justice for its own sake, since that would create an unresolvable conflict with self-interest.
(6) So justice is never rational for a fully rational agent in conditions of invisibility.
(7) Modern technology is rapidly expanding the domains of invisibility—anonymous interaction, private algorithmic mediation, and spaces where no shared normative framework applies.
(8) Therefore, as these domains expand, justice becomes less rational for fully rational agents within them.
(9) But we continue to apply moral criticism in these domains—blaming anonymous harm, condemning private cruelty, and insisting on fairness in virtual interactions.
(10) So either our moral practice is systematically irrational, or premise (4) is false and practical reason can sustain genuine internal conflict.

He says the only way out is to deny (4) and accept that rational agents can be genuinely divided, but he thinks that costs us the unity of practical reason and makes moral motivation mysterious. I feel like something slipped between (5) and (6), or perhaps between (7) and (8), but I cannot name it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (9) than save the unity of practical reason, but I want to know exactly what I am paying.
