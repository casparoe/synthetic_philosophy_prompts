I'm trying to determine my actual stance on the axiom of choice, not as a formal axiom but as a principle governing which existence claims feel legitimate. Here are my verdicts. Invoking choice to well-order the reals and derive a non-measurable subset: I accept the result, yet the move feels like an illegitimate leap. Invoking choice to prove that every vector space has a basis: this feels entirely appropriate, even indispensable. Invoking choice in the Banach-Tarski decomposition: the result is unsettling, but I do not regard it as false—rather as a reductio of unrestricted geometric intuition. Using only countable choice to show a countable union of countable sets is countable: completely innocent. But invoking full AC to define a choice function over an arbitrary, unstructured family of sets—picking one element from each with no discernible rule—feels like smuggling in an infinite sequence of arbitrary stipulations, and I want to reject that specific act even while accepting formally equivalent applications. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about definability, structural accessibility, or the presence of an explicit selection rule—that accommodates all five verdicts? If not, which should I abandon? And apply your best principle to the case I keep returning to: using the well-ordering theorem to construct a Hamel basis for the reals over the rationals in a proof that never exhibits any basis element.
