I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I keep encountering a stalled debate about simultaneous invention—Newton and Leibniz with calculus, Darwin and Wallace with natural selection. One side treats these pairs as evidence that discovery is inevitable once premises accumulate, so fighting over priority is mostly vanity; the other side insists that contingency matters, that the specific formulation and development path depend on individual judgment, and that attribution is genuinely meaningful. The argument never advances. My hypothesis is that the sides are answering different questions under one name: one is making a structural claim about the reachability of solutions in an idea-space given shared premises, while the other is advancing a normative claim about how scientific communities should allocate recognition and resources. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or masked? Are there hidden empirical commitments—say, about whether the same formalism was genuinely derivable from the premises available to both parties—or hidden normative commitments about what discovery credit is actually for? Could a sharper distinction, such as between convergent deduction and redundant observation, resolve the impasse, or is it a sign that the question is ill-posed? Please do not provide a bibliography or survey of interpretations; I want to know what kind of disagreement this really is, what each side is actually committed to, and what—if anything—would count as progress.
