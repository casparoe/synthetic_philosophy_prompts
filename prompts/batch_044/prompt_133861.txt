I'm a PhD student in philosphy of mind, mostly tinkering with predictive-processing models of self-awareness, and I've come round to what I think is a pretty hardline illusionist position about phenomenal consciousness — not the soft 'we just misdescribe it' kind, but the stronger claim that there literally are no phenomenal properties instatiated anywhere in the brain or elsewhere. On this view, what we call 'the feeling of red' or 'the hurtfulness of pain' is just representational content wrapped in a faulty meta-cognitive framing: the brain builds a transparent user-interface that represents itself as having intrinsic qualitative feels, a bit like a desktop showing little file icons with no tiny pictures actually inside the machine. The framing is sustained by interoceptive inference and our lack of epistemic access to the underlying machinery, so we take the model for the territory.

I want to know what would genuinely count against this — not just the tired 'but I directly know I'm conscious' move, which I think the illusionist can absorb as part of the illusion itself. I'm after the serious defeaters: which results in clinical neuropsychology or consciousness science (say, dissociations between access and phenomenality, or about reportability versus subjective presence) would actually damage the claim? Are there conceptual regress arguments — if the illusion itself is phenomenally present, doesn't that reintroduce exactly what was meant to be eliminated? — that defenders concede are awkward, and which ones do they dismiss? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, e.g. by saying the illusion is 'real but not metaphysically real', and if so is that a problem for a view of this kind? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely embarrassing, with an honest assessment of whether illusionism is actually well-insulated or whether it risks collapsing into representationalism.
