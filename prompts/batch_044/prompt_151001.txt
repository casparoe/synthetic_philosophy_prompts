For my philosophy essay I am defending a specific view on how texts mean what they do, and I want an audit of whether the pieces actually fit together before I commit to them in writing. Here is my position: (1) I reject the intentional fallacy—an author’s private mental state at the moment of composition does not determine a published text’s meaning. (2) Nevertheless, when a passage is genuinely ambiguous, I think the best interpretation is the one the author actually intended, provided we can recover it without pure speculation. (3) I hold that texts generated by large language models lack an author in the relevant sense, so their meaning must be fixed entirely by public linguistic conventions and by the interpretive practices of readers. (4) At the same time, if we discover that an LLM was deliberately fine-tuned to embed a specific hidden message, I believe that message belongs to the text’s meaning, even though no human being formulated it as an intentional communication. (5) I also maintain that meaning is never a property of a text in isolation; it is always constituted by a relation between the text and an interpreting community. Do these commitments hang together? Specifically, is the tension between (1) and (2) only apparent, or does it run deeper? Is there a genuine conflict between (3) and (4) that forces me to choose between denying authorless texts any hidden content and smuggling authorial intention back in? And does (5) actually undermine (4) rather than support it, since a hidden message unknown to any community might fail to enter the relational structure I claim is necessary? Please take a definite position: rank the tensions by severity, identify the weakest commitment in the set, and state the cheapest single revision that would restore consistency without abandoning my core view that interpretation is fundamentally a public, reader-involving practice.
