ok so me and some friends were arguing about Nomic-style self-amending rules after digging up some old LW threads on legal self-reference, and one of them walked me into this argument that I can't find teh exit from. every step feels like it has teeth.

(1) A legal system only binds people if there's a continuous chain of authority linking every current rule back to some initially valid source. no orphan rules allowed.

(2) In any system with a self-amending clause, the clause that authorizes amendments can itself be amended.

(3) So if I ask why I'm bound by rule R today, the answer traces back through whatever amendments produced R, and eventually through amendments that modified the amendment clause itself.

(4) Call the "closure point" the earliest rule in that chain that governs its own revision. once you hit it, there's no earlier rule outside the system that authorized it. it's either self-authorizing or it authorizes itself via prior versions of itself.

(5) But self-authorization is just circular reasoning with extra steps - a rule can't grant itself authority any more than I can sign my own diploma.

(6) So at the closure point, authority breaks. and since every current rule traces back through the closure point, the break propagates forward: every rule inherits the gap.

(7) Therefore in any genuinely self-amending system, no rule is actually validly authorized.

(8) But that'd mean the US Constitution, UK parliamentary sovereignty, etc., don't generate real obligations - which is absurd, people go to jail for breaking these rules every day.

(9) So (1) must be false? but if we drop continuous-chain authority, what's left? a bare "it works in practice" doesn't feel like it answers the philosophical worry.

where's teh slip? is it in (4) assuming there's a single closure point? or is (5) wrong about circularity being fatal - maybe law can have recursive authorization that converges, like a fixed-point, rather than bootstrapping? I want to keep the intuition that authority can't come from nowhere, but I also don't want to conclude that all modern legal systems are authority-free zones. which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
