For a philosophy of history seminar, specify the full list of desiderata that any adequate theory of historical explanation must satisfy, with particular attention to how covering-law and narrative approaches fare differently against each. The list should include: (1) the ability to explain singular, non-repeatable events rather than only general patterns; (2) compatibility with counterfactual reasoning about what might have happened had specific conditions been absent; (3) preservation of the distinction between explaining an occurrence and justifying an interpretive framing; (4) consistency with the actual practice of historians who routinely combine causal claims with teleological and relational significance-assignments; and (5) accommodation of what I will call narrative viscosity—the observed tendency for a minor revision in one part of a historical account to propagate and force restructuring of the overall arc far beyond what atomistic causal weights alone would predict, because significance is assigned relationally within a story rather than point-by-point. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or largely a camp preference (e.g., of Hempelian universalists versus narrative particularists), and identify any subsets that appear jointly unsatisfiable in light of standard objections to covering-law models. Then briefly score the pure covering-law model, the pure narrative model, and one plausible hybrid against the list, but keep the analytical weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard.
