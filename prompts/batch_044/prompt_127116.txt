In Hofstadter's dialogue of Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue, the ant hill is presented as a candidate for a collective mind: individual ants function like neurons, and the colony as a whole might think. Decades later, philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists remain divided over whether an ant colony, or any distributed biological system, literally possesses mental states. One camp applies frameworks like integrated information theory and global workspace models to the colony's pheromone and tactile networks, arguing that if the functional architecture achieves sufficient integration and differentiation, the system qualifies as a cognitive agent regardless of substrate. The opposing camp holds that mind, especially conscious mind, requires a unitary phenomenological subject—something an ant colony, composed of millions of isolated phenomenal centers with no shared subjective point of view, cannot constitute, no matter how sophisticated its information processing becomes.

The debate has stalled in a peculiar way. Proponents of collective cognition cite ever more precise measurements of information flow and decision-making coherence across the colony, treating these as empirical advances. Their critics respond not by disputing the measurements but by insisting that the wrong kind of unity is being measured—that functional integration is not phenomenal binding. Meanwhile, both sides agree on the entomological facts, on the mathematics of network dynamics, and on the absence of any single ant that oversees the whole. No new experiment seems capable of resolving the dispute, because each side interprets the same data through a different criterion of what counts as a mind. I wonder whether this is genuinely a substantive disagreement about the nature of consciousness, a verbal dispute over whether the word mind should cover functionally integrated systems, or a case of two research programs—one cognitive-scientific, one phenomenological—answering different questions under the same heading.

To push the diagnosis further, consider a hypothetical modification: suppose we engineered a colony in which pheromone pathways implemented a recursive global workspace with explicit self-monitoring signals, so that the colony not only behaved as an integrated agent but also generated internal representations of its own states, yet each individual ant remained phenomenally isolated with no access to those representations.
