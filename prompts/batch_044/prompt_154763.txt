In a philosophy of mind reading group I have been working through Schrödinger’s later essays on consciousness, particularly his Vedanta-inspired claim that there is strictly only one consciousness and that what we call individual minds are not independent generators of awareness but localized modifications of a single universal subject. One of my colleagues relies heavily on a prism analogy to defend this unity thesis against neuroscientific pluralism: she compares the brain to a prism that does not create white light but only refracts it into separate colored beams, so that finding distinct neural correlates for different people is like finding different prisms — it demonstrates differentiation of expression, not multiplication of the underlying source. She uses this to argue that anyone who takes localized brain activity as proof of many consciousnesses is making the same mistake as someone who believes each prism manufactures its own light, and therefore that the Vedantic view survives modern neuroscience intact.

I want a careful audit of this analogy. Please provide an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the original white light, the prism, the colored beams, and any observer — then identify the precise points where the mapping breaks: for instance, whether it implicitly treats consciousness as a physical substance susceptible to geometric division, whether it can accommodate the asymmetry between first-person access and third-person observation of brains, and whether it smuggles in an illusionist view of personal identity that Schrödinger himself may not have endorsed. Separate the cosmetic disanalogies from those that actually undermine her conclusion that neuroscientific pluralism is refuted; does the conclusion survive once the liabilities are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it entirely? If the prism picture fails, is there a more robust analogy — perhaps the ocean and its waves, or the single dreamer generating multiple dream-figures — that preserves her point without the same conceptual costs? Finally, please say what would change your mind: what specific empirical finding about the brain-consciousness relation, or what conceptual distinction within Vedanta itself, would cause you to reject the analogy rather than merely patch it?
