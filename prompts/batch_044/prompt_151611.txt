I've been working through some epistemology texts—particularly the literature on bootstrapping and what some authors call "easy knowledge"—and I keep noticing a specific argumentative pattern that I can't quite map onto the standard taxonomy. A speaker begins with an apparently unproblematic perceptual or introspective claim—say, that they can see their own hands, or that they understand a simple logical inference. From this, they derive that their cognitive faculties are functioning reliably in the relevant domain. They then leverage that derived reliability to justify a far more contentious conclusion—perhaps that an external world exists, or that a complex theoretical framework is sound. So far this looks like ordinary bootstrapping. But here's the part that confuses me: when the contentious conclusion is challenged, the speaker doesn't defend the derivation step. Instead, they retreat to the original basic claim and argue that denying it is pragmatically self-defeating or epistemically catastrophic. The basic claim thus plays two incompatible roles: it serves as the evidential foundation for the ladder, and simultaneously as a trapdoor that makes attacking the ladder impossible. The strong conclusion is never independently secured; it's suspended over the foundation, and any pressure on the suspension is deflected downward onto the floor.

I can think of two concrete cases. In one, a philosopher argues that we can know our perceptual faculties are generally reliable because we successfully use them in ordinary cases, then applies that reliability to defend scientific realism. When a skeptic asks why we should trust that generalization, the philosopher responds that we can't reject ordinary perceptual knowledge without undermining the very reasoning needed to formulate skepticism. In another, a rationalist forum discussion about belief calibration uses "I know my own mental state" as both the premise for trusting introspection and as the shield against criticisms of overconfidence—any attack on the calibration claim is met with "but if you deny I can know my own confidence level, you've destroyed the framework for correcting it." In both cases, the initial belief is treated as "easy" when it's doing foundational work, but as "hard"—costly to deny—when it's doing defensive work.

Is there an established concept for this specific two-role structure, where a foundational belief functions simultaneously as an evidential base and as a reflexive defense mechanism? I know it's adjacent to the bootstrapping problem, to circular reasoning, and perhaps to pragmatic encroachment, but none of those seem to capture the status-shifting: the same belief being treated as requiring no justification in one moment and as carrying massive denial-costs in the next. If there is a name, I want to understand exactly how it differs from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses and from standard bootstrapping. If not, I'd like a coined term with precise boundary conditions—particularly distinguishing it from cases where a belief is genuinely independently supported and then also happens to have pragmatic value.
