In my seminar on historiography we have been examining Althusser’s concept of overdetermination—the claim that major historical outcomes arise from several intersecting structural conditions, none reducible to a single prime mover. I am trying to apply this to debates about the French Revolution, where economic crisis, ideological mobilization, and institutional paralysis are each said to have been sufficient. Here is my confusion: if all three are genuinely sufficient, then in any counterfactual where one is absent the revolution still occurs, which makes each cause “modally redundant” rather than necessary. Yet historians routinely single out one factor as decisive, apparently treating it as “modally fragile”—as if removing it would break the chain. Does this mean historians implicitly deny overdetermination? Or can a cause be both part of an overdetermined set and yet uniquely shape the specific form or timing of the outcome? More generally, if overdetermination rules out any hierarchy of causal salience, hasn’t historical explanation collapsed into arbitrary narrative preference? I want to know exactly where this line of reasoning fails.
