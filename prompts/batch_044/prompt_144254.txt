I'm trying to put a finger on a move I keep seeing in debates about tearing down old social rules, especially the Chesterton-style conservative ones. It's not just "you dont know why the fence is there." It's more specific. Someone proposes getting rid of a formal rule -- say, a strict dress code at work, or a required waiting period before a big decision -- and the argument is "the original problem is solved, so the rule is dead weight." Then after it's gone, the same pressure doesnt disappear; it just moves underground. People still enforce the norm, but now there's no explicit standard to point to, so the enforcement gets harsher and more arbitary. The people who were protected by the clear rule -- like the guy who could say "sorry, policy says I need 48 hours" -- lose that shield. Meanwhile, if things go badly, the reformers say "see, the old culture lingers," not "we removed a guardrail." So the fence wasnt just unknown; it was invisible, and tearing it down didnt free anyone, it just hid the wall.

I feel like Burke talks around this when he talks about manners being more important than laws, but I cant tell if there's an actual named concept for this specific failure mode -- the invisible fence that becomes an invisible prison after removal. Is there a term for it? How's it different from just "unintended consequences" or the Chesterton fence proper? And if nobody's named it, what should we call the thing where formal rules act as protective barriers against informal coercion, and abolishing them makes the coercion worse because it's now unaccountable?
