I sit on this state-level Sentencing Reform Commission and we're deadlocked over a capital-punishment proposal that comes up for a vote next tuesday. The bill would keep execution on the books but restrict it to aggravated homicide cases that clear a "dual-source convergence" standard—independent DNA plus digital forensics with corroboration—and it pairs that with an "epistemic reversal fund." If post-execution evidence ever shows the convergence was wrong, the state pays compensation but frames it as an "informational update" rather than a wrongful conviction, since technically the standard was met at the time.

Half our members think this is brilliant: it preserves retributive justice for the worst offenses while neutralizing the innocence-risk objection through procedural rigor. The other half says it turns execution into a revocable bureaucratic decision, which undermines the moral weight that makes capital punishment different from life imprisonment. There's also a side fight about deterrence—some crimnologists on staff worry that if killers know there's a reversal fund, the threat feels less absolute, while others think the convergence threshold actually strengthens deterrence by making conviction feel inevitable.

Can you act like the commission's senior ethics advisor—someone who knows philosphy of law but writes for policymakers? Before you draft the memo, do a quick brainstorming pass on how retribution, deterrence, and error-risk actually interact in this specific setup, not just in general. Then write the formal recomendation memo: approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions. Give us a clear bottom line with reasoning the legislators can respond to, and be honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side—probably the retributive argument that some crimes are so severe that anything less than irreversible punishment fails to honor the victim's dignity, or the counter-argument that convergence standards never fully eliminate epistemic risk. Don't ask for citations or a bibliography; just work with the ideas. Also, include the one objection the bill's supporters are secretly hoping nobody brings up.
