I'm drafting a position paper on indicative and counterfactual conditionals for a philosophy of language workshop, and I want a consistency audit before I present. Here is my package of commitments: (1) I treat indicative conditionals as expressing actual-world conditional probabilities, in roughly the Adams–Edgington manner, so that "if P then Q" tracks P(Q|P) rather than selecting similar worlds. (2) I allow that some backtracking counterfactuals are genuinely true—e.g., "if I had missed the flight, I would have left the house earlier"—because world-similarity can include localized adjustments to the past that preserve law-like regularities. (3) In Newcomb's problem I one-box, but I justify this not by causal backtracking; instead I invoke a trajectory-space correlation measure over continuous decision spaces, where the predictor's prior action and my present choice lie on the same stable trajectory, yielding a rational correlation without reversing causal order. (4) I reject any strict siloing of indicative and counterfactual reasoning in decision theory: the rational verdict should not change merely because the problem is framed with indicative premises rather than counterfactual ones, so the counterfactual dependencies governing payoffs should remain operative even under indicative description. (5) I think the conceivability-to-possibility link is fallible for counterfactuals—there are conceivable distant worlds that are not genuinely possible—but largely reliable for indicatives, precisely because indicative evaluation is anchored to actual-world probabilistic structure. (6) I deny that there is any principled distinction between "pure" and "applied" contexts for conditionals; the same semantic framework should govern reasoning about physics, about hypothetical choices, and about self-locating beliefs.

Please audit this set: which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look incompatible but dissolve once distinctions are drawn clearly, and what is the least costly revision for each real tension? In particular, does (1) undermine the trajectory-space correlation I invoke in (3) when I evaluate the predictor's past action indicatively? Does (4) force me to import backtracking similarity relations into indicative evaluation, contradicting (1) or (2)? Is there a hidden conflict between (5) and (6) regarding whether actual-world anchoring can survive across domains? Rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what has to give—whether restricting backtracking, weakening the anti-silo claim, or abandoning the trajectory-space justification for one-boxing—and identify the single revision that would clean up the most problems at once. Also flag any apparent contradictions that turn out to be spurious, such as a seeming clash between accepting backtracking in (2) and denying causal backtracking in (3).

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on conditionals and decision theory, and do not present a disputed view—such as the correct analysis of Newcomb's problem, the admissibility of backtracking counterfactuals, or the Adams–Edgington thesis—as though it were the consensus.
