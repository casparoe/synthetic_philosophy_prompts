I serve on the Continuity of Care Arbitration Panel at a large teaching hospital, and since my partner began navigating an early-onset cognitive condition last year, I can no longer treat questions of precedent autonomy as purely procedural. We must issue a formal written opinion before next Friday’s meeting on the following case.

A 72-year-old former legal scholar executed an advance directive twelve years ago refusing artificial hydration and nutrition should she ever reach a state of “severe cognitive impairment with loss of narrative self-awareness.” She now has moderate-to-advanced dementia: she does not recognize family members, cannot recall her professional life, and fails capacity assessments for complex medical choices. Yet she consistently reaches for food and water, smiles during meals, and has shown no signs of distress or refusal. Her adult children are divided—one insists the directive binds because the earlier, fully autonomous self possessed legitimate authority over her bodily future; the other contends that her sustained present-tense affective assent generates a competing moral claim that cannot be dismissed as mere reflex.

Our institutional policy includes an uncommon values-reconciliation protocol directing the panel to ask whether the present patient’s experienced well-being is compatible with the values that originally motivated the refusal, rather than treating the directive as an absolute veto. We must recommend a concrete course—honor the directive with a specified withdrawal timeline, continue nutrition under defined conditions, or adopt a structured transitional plan—together with an explicit time frame.

Please draft the panel’s decision memo, engaging seriously with the strongest argument for the authority of the earlier self and not reducing it to a legal formality. The memo should address whether sustained present contentment can diminish the binding force of a carefully considered prior refusal, and it should identify where in that reasoning the vulnerability lies. Finally, state clearly what argument or evidence—whether about her internal experience, the moral weight of present assent, or an institutional risk—would lead you to reverse or substantially modify your recommendation, and point to the step in your reasoning most likely to fail.
