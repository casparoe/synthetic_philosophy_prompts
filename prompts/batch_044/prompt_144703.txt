I am a sixth-form student studying philosophy and mathematics. We have been examining Hobbes's claim that geometry is uniquely certain because its objects are constructed by us and its demonstrations can be fully surveyed by reason. Lately I have encountered two modern developments that seem to strain this picture. First, computer-assisted proofs—such as those for the four-colour theorem and Kepler's conjecture—depend on algorithmic checks of millions of cases that no individual mind can trace step by step. Second, the acceptance of non-Euclidean geometries suggests that the axioms Hobbes regarded as grounded in the construction of figures can be systematically replaced without contradiction. Given Hobbes's materialist view that true science must begin with motion and bodies we ourselves generate, do you think he would have accepted computer-verified results as genuinely geometrical knowledge, or dismissed them as mechanised calculation lacking rational transparency? And would he have treated alternative geometries as merely hypothetical instruments, or as a genuine threat to geometry's status as the supreme model of certain reasoning?
