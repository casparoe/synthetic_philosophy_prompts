For the past several decades, interpreters of Gottlob Frege have been divided over whether the antisemitic and nationalist sentiments recorded in his late political diary — written in the final year of his life and discovered long after his death — have any bearing on his philosophy of logic and mathematics. One camp treats the diary as biographically regrettable but philosophically inert: they stress that Frege's explicit methodology rejects psychologism, that his notion of objective thought (Gedanke) is defined precisely by its independence from the mental states or ethnic identity of the thinker, and that nothing in his formal logical writings depends on the political prejudices expressed in private notebooks. The opposing camp argues that this separation is too neat; they point out that the diary applies categories of national and racial character to intellectual life in ways that mirror his logical distinctions — treating certain groups as a kind of contaminating function applied to an otherwise pure object — and they claim that his ideal of logical objectivity was implicitly structured by a normative vision of who could properly access it.

The dispute has stalled. Both sides agree on the textual evidence from the diary and on the technical content of Frege's logical system. Nobody denies that the diary contains vicious remarks, and nobody denies that the formal system contains no explicit racial axioms. Yet the debate has not advanced: the separationists continue to invoke the psychologism boundary, while the integrationists continue to invoke structural analogies between logical and political categorisation, with neither side conceding ground.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about Frege's texts at all, but about a hidden disagreement over what counts as internal to a philosophical system — specifically, whether methodological commitments (such as the rejection of psychologism) can be insulated from the normative and psychological conditions under which a thinker applies them. But I am unsure whether this is correct, or whether the stall instead reflects two genuinely different questions being pursued under one heading: one about the logical consistency and autonomy of Frege's system, the other about the moral and historical conditions of its production.

Can you diagnose the structure of this impasse? Is it verbal (turning on ambiguous uses of 'bearing on philosophy' or 'psychologism'), substantive (hinging on a factual claim about how Frege actually used his logical apparatus in private reasoning), or rooted in bedrock intuitions about whether a philosopher's politics can ever be philosophically irrelevant? Are the two sides answering different questions — perhaps one about semantics and logic, the other about intellectual history and ideology — while pretending to disagree about a single thesis? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a sharper conceptual distinction (for example, between methodological psychologism and what we might call political psychologism), a historical discovery about Frege's actual working practices, or a change in the framing of the question itself? Or should we conclude that no resolution is possible, because the disagreement ultimately rests on normative commitments about the autonomy of reason that neither side can argue for without circularity?
