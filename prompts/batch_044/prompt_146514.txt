At a departmental colloquium, two sociologists debated whether generational labels like “Gen Z” denote real kinds or are merely administrative conveniences. (1) Elena argued that generations are ontologically comparable to social classes: they share causally integrated properties—simultaneous exposure to platform economies, climate anxiety, and post-9/11 security architectures—that generate stable dispositions, making them projectible in the philosophical sense despite being human-made. (2) Marcus countered that this confuses correlation with kind-hood: any fifteen-year birth slice will share some environment, but without a mechanism that binds members to one another rather than to adjacent cohorts, “Gen Z” is just an indexical bin with no explanatory surplus over age, period, and cohort effects taken separately. He added that the age-period-cohort identification problem shows we can never statistically isolate cohort effects from age and period effects, so treating “generation” as an independent causal variable is illegitimate. (3) Elena replied that the APC problem is an epistemic limit, not an ontological one—just because we cannot cleanly measure cohort effects does not mean they do not exist, and natural kinds in biology often suffer analogous measurement entanglement. She also noted that generations exhibit homeostatic property clustering: members regulate one another’s norms through peer-network density in ways that age-matched non-cohorts do not. (4) Marcus said this smuggles in the conclusion: peer-network density is an age-graded phenomenon, not cohort-specific, and appealing to “homeostatic clustering” borrows the prestige of biological taxonomy without meeting its criteria—there are no reproductive isolating mechanisms, no essences, and no counterfactual stability across possible worlds. He concluded that generations are useful heuristics but fail every serious test for kind-hood except the pragmatic one.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say whether either party is missing something important—particularly about whether a kind must be “natural” in a biological sense to be real, or whether the statistical inseparability of APC effects actually undermines ontological commitment.
