In a seminar discussion, two philosophers—Dr. Chen and Dr. Alvarez—disagreed about whether an opaque municipal algorithm can be a source of republican domination. (1) Chen argued that republican freedom requires non-domination: not merely absence of interference, but absence of arbitrary power to alter one's options, and that residents subjected to an unaccountable machine-learning quota system live at the mercy of a decision procedure that need not justify itself to them, even if it rarely restricts anyone. (2) Alvarez objected that domination is inherently relational and requires an agent with the will to interfere; algorithms lack intentions, so attributing domination to them is a category mistake—what matters is whether human officials behind the system retain arbitrary discretion. (3) Chen replied that republican theory already accommodates structural domination (e.g., institutional roles like feudal lordship) without requiring personal whim in every instance, and that an opaque socio-technical system functions analogously: it establishes a status relation in which affected parties must adapt to its outputs regardless of any particular human intent. (4) Alvarez countered that this stretches "arbitrary" until it covers any frustrating bureaucracy; if uniform rules, exit options, and appeals are present, then labeling the system "domination" collapses republican liberty into a general grievance about unfairness or opacity, stripping it of distinctive content beyond negative liberty plus independent justice criteria. (5) Chen concluded that Alvarez wrongly assumes domination must be interpersonal; republican freedom should be read as a status concept—being subject to a power that can reshape one's options without being accountable to those affected—and this status can be instantiated by socio-technical arrangements, not only by persons.
