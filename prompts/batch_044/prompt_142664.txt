I was reading a popular summary of Moritz Schlick’s ethics—the part where he distinguishes “play,” done for its own sake, from “work,” done only as a means to something further—and I tried to apply it to a practical question I had been turning over: how should one think about the length and shape of one’s own life? The result is an argument that seems to prove far more than Schlick could have intended, and I want to know exactly where the chain breaks.

Here is the reasoning I built. (1) On Schlick’s view, an activity carries intrinsic meaning only when it is pursued for its own sake; if it is pursued instrumentally, to secure some further end, it belongs to the category of work and is meaningless in itself. (2) Every action that preserves life—eating, sleeping, avoiding injury, treating illness, even planning for tomorrow—is undertaken not for its own sake but in order to make future play possible; therefore all self-preservation is work, hence devoid of intrinsic meaning. (3) The longer a life lasts, the larger the cumulative quantity of maintenance-work required to sustain it, which means the ratio of meaningful play to meaningless work declines over time, especially in old age when bodily upkeep dominates daily existence. (4) If the overall meaning of a life is a function of this ratio, then to maximize meaning one should minimize the total work-burden, which points toward living intensely for a brief span and then deliberately ending one’s life before prolonged maintenance begins. (5) Consequently, Schlick’s framework seems to imply that suicide, timed after a peak of play, is not a tragic loss of meaning but its rational preservation, while a long life of gradual decline is the worst possible outcome—a life mostly consumed by meaningless labor.

I want to hold on to (1) and (2), because they seem faithful to what Schlick actually says about play versus purpose. I am also inclined to keep the idea that meaning comes in degrees rather than as an all-or-nothing property. But (5) strikes me as absurd: it makes every meal an existential failure and turns suicide into a moral achievement. Where does the reasoning slip? Is there a hidden equivocation between the value of a particular act and the value of a life taken as a whole? Or does Schlick allow that work can be redeemed by serving as the necessary frame for play, in which case rejecting (3) would commit me to saying that aging and maintenance are not as damaging to meaning as they appear? I need to know which link is weakest, what it costs to cut it, and whether I can keep both (1) and the rejection of (5) without contradiction.
