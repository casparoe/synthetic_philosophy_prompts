I was arguing with my roomate about why our city's planning board approves apartment buildings based on this weird checklist — like, if you meet the parking minimum, the height cap, and the green-space quota, you're in, even if there's a design that's way better on every dimension except it's two feet over the height limit. They don't compare options; they just check boxes. My roomate says thats just bureaucracy being lazy, but I think its doing something philosophical. What theory of rational decision-making does that practice actually presuppose? Is it assuming Herbert Simon's bounded rationality idea where agents cant compute global optima? Or is it closer to Gigerenzer's ecological rationality, where matching the environment beats optimizing? Maybe its even closer to Klein's recognition-primed decision model, where you just pattern-match against past cases?

And heres the part that confuses me: the same city runs its tax-incentive program with full-blown cost-benefit analysis trying to maximize revenue, so they're not consistently anti-optimization everywhere. Does the zoning checklist presuppose that rationality is context-dependent, or is the whole system just incoherent? If we replaced the checklist with a pure utility-maximizing comparison of every proposal, what philosophical view of practical reason would we be committing to? I feel like there's an implicit theory of satisficing hiding in the paperwork, but I cant tell whether the practice strictly needs it or whether several rival views about bounded rationality could license the exact same boxes being checked.
