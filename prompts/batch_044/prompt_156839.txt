hey, i'm putting together a module on distributive ethics and i'm tired of how the seperateness of persons objection gets flattened into Rawls saying utilitarianism is bad because it merges people. i think there are actually two different readings hiding inside that phrase, and i want students to compare them properly.

the first is the boundary reading: persons are morally distinct containers, and utilitarianism violates that by treating welfare as fungible across lives—like pouring water between cups. the second is the weighting reading: the problem isn't that welfare gets pooled, but that utilitarianism uses the same aggregation rule for trade-offs inside one life and trade-offs between lives. so intrapersonal sacrifices (me skipping dessert so i can run tomorrow) and interpersonal sacrifices (me losing my house so five strangers can each get a new window) get calculated with the same arithmetic.

could you compare these two interpretations? specifically, i want to know whether the weighting reading still generates the classic one-for-five objection, or whether its real target is something more subtle—maybe cases where small benefits are spread so thin that no individual reason justifies the burden, but the total still adds up. then there's this hypothetical i keep returning to: imagine a society with an ex-ante consented lottery that assigns people to bear specific costs for large collective goods, with compensation structured so that each person's expected burden is minimal. would the boundary view reject this as still merging persons? would the weighting view reject it only if the aggregation rule misapplies across lives? i think comparing them reveals whether seperateness is an ontological claim or a claim about the structure of moral reasoning. write this up for students who know basic utilitarianism but haven't read the secondary literature too deeply—no citations needed, just clear conceptual work, and don't worry about being too formal.
