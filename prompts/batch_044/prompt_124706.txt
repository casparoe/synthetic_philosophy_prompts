I'm a junior leading a fifty-minute discussion section for fifteen first-year students in an introductory political philosophy course. Next week I need to teach the problem of dirty hands, but not through the ticking-bomb or torture cases they have already read. Instead, I want them to work through a distinction between dirty hands as an irreducible personal moral stain and dirty hands as a structural feature of democratic institutions that can persist even when no individual agent feels corrupted—using the example of a legislator who votes for a predictive-policing program knowing it will systematically harm innocent people, where the harm is built into the system's design rather than intended by any voter.

Plan the explanatory sequence: what concept must be established before this institutional framing can even be introduced; why the predictive-policing case carries the argument better than the classic emergency scenarios and what properties it must have to do so; and in what order the components—moral cost, role obligation, structural harm, distributed accountability—should appear so that the last does not collapse back into the first.

Then map the misconceptions they are likely to form at each stage—probably that politics is simply "morally hard," that structural harm is just dirty hands multiplied across a group, and that recognizing institutional corruption removes individual accountability. Specify exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted, and design one final check-question that would reveal whether a student has grasped the institutional framing or has merely adopted a cynical view of politics.

Finally, tell me what part of the broader literature on moral responsibility I should openly set aside at this level so the discussion does not drift, and confirm that I should not assign further readings for this session.
