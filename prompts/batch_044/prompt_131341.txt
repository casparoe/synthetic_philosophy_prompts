I'm preparing materials for a graduate seminar on democratic theory and rational choice, and I want a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—that sorts philosophical responses to the voting paradox (Condorcet cycles and Arrow-style aggregation failures) by substantive commitment rather than by author or label. The audience is students who understand the formal results but are unsure where they stand philosophically. The tree should begin from the shared premise that cyclic majorities and the impossibility conditions are genuine, then branch by answers to concrete diagnostic claims: whether the problem is primarily formal (about aggregation rules), epistemic (about voter competence or information quality), metaphysical (about whether collective preferences exist to be aggregated), or normative (about whether democracy aims to represent a pre-existing will or to produce good outcomes). Each node should split further on specific positions—whether relaxing transitivity rescues democracy, whether single-peaked preferences eliminate cycles in practice, whether the paradox licenses epistocratic or sortition-based alternatives, and whether treating votes as public commitments rather than revealed preferences dissolves the cycle altogether. Every leaf should carry the name of an actual position if one occupies that branch (e.g., social-choice formalism, deliberative-democratic responses, epistocratic defenses, commitment-based dissolution) or be marked "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied, with one sentence diagnosing whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or being merely unexplored. The tree should also flag any pair of leaves that the chosen questions cannot genuinely separate. Crucially, please separate what is settled about the formal results from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic—for instance, whether Arrow's conditions are best read as axioms of rational aggregation versus as constraints on democratic legitimacy—and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view. Build the instrument from the logical space of answers first, then locate named positions within it, keeping the tree to about eight to ten branch nodes and ensuring that no question requires a student to identify themselves with a label they do not already recognize.
