I was recently appointed co-executor of the literary estate of a deceased essayist, and I must now oversee the preparation of a critical edition of twelve volumes of unpublished notebooks. These notebooks are not clean drafts: they contain a primary script layer, interlinear revisions, marginal annotations in different colored inks, and cross-outs that may represent either deliberate emendation or, in annotations made during the author’s final months of documented cognitive decline, alterations of questionable authority. I need a reusable decision procedure—one that an editorial board without philosophical training can apply consistently across hundreds of ambiguous passages—to determine whether to preserve a passage in its primary form, adopt a later annotation as authoritative, or flag the passage for editorial commentary. The procedure must be grounded in what philosophy of language and literary theory actually maintain about authorial intention, textual meaning, and the authority of post-compositional revision, but translated into practical steps. In particular, it must operationalize the distinction between executive intention (the author’s decision to alter the text) and interpretive intention (the meaning the text is meant to bear), and it must specify evidentiary criteria for judging when a later layer possesses sufficient executive authority to override the primary script. For each step, please indicate what counts as sufficient evidence, where the procedure must halt because the issue is irreducibly one of values rather than recoverable fact, and where it will necessarily misfire—especially when an author’s published theoretical commitments, such as an explicit anti-intentionalist stance, appear to conflict with the interpretive implications of a revised passage. Please deliver: (1) an ordered checklist or flowchart in words that a non-philosopher can run in sequence; (2) worked applications to three cases—(a) a contradiction between the primary text and a red-ink marginal note made during the documented period of cognitive decline; (b) a cross-out that removes a metaphor the author’s published correspondence explicitly defends as essential to the argument; and (c) a case in which the procedure preserves an apparently erroneous claim that I personally believe ought to be corrected, with an explanation of why the procedure still yields that result despite my judgment; and (3) an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot settle and where decision-making must be handed over to human judgment, to be signed off by both the lead editor and the estate’s legal representative.
