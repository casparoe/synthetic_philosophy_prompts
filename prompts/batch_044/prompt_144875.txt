I'm working through a graduate seminar paper on contractualist ethics and have been trying to map how a Scanlonian reviewer rules on aggregation cases without just replaying the standard torture-versus-headaches framing. To test consistency, imagine a contractualist panel that has issued four rulings on whether a principle could be reasonably rejected: first, they reject any principle that permits one identifiable person to suffer extreme harm in order to relieve diffuse, minor discomfort across millions, on the ground that the victim can point to a specific, non-consensual burden that no compensating structure justifies; second, they accept a principle allowing each of ten thousand consenting volunteers to bear a small, genuinely distributed risk of serious injury so as to save one life with certainty, because no single party is targeted for sacrifice and the collective design is transparent; third, they reject a principle authorizing each actor in a vast system to emit trace pollutants causing individually imperceptible harm, arguing that the cumulative effect creates an environmental burden that undermines the very conditions for reasonable agreement, so that anyone affected can reject being embedded in that system; fourth, they accept a principle directing a hospital with genuinely rival, indivisible resources to treat five patients with moderate injuries rather than one with catastrophic injuries, provided the allocation follows a fair procedural lottery rather than singling anyone out.

Now consider a new proposal: a social platform could run a recommendation algorithm that, for each of fifty million users, introduces barely detectable micro-distortions in information exposure—each user experiences only a negligible, hard-to-notice reduction in cognitive autonomy and gains minor entertainment value—while the aggregate effect, documented in behavioral research, is a measurable, society-wide erosion of deliberative capacity. An alternative algorithm offers slightly lower engagement but eliminates the cumulative harm. Would this panel accept or reject the principle permitting the current algorithm? I am less interested in a bare verdict than in the closest analog among the four rulings, the underlying rule that renders all four consistent, and at least one competing rule that also accommodates rulings (1) through (4) yet predicts the opposite outcome here. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, please specify exactly what principle the panel would be adopting with each choice, and propose one further case—without involving technology or large-scale aggregation—that would best distinguish the competing rules. Finally, please state what argument or feature of the case would change your mind about which rule is actually governing their judgments, and identify precisely where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
