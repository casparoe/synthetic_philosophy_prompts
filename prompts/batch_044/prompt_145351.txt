The thesis I defend is that historical overdetermination should be reconceived through the ecological idea of functional redundancy rather than through standard discrete-event causation. I argue that major historical transitions—regime collapses, technological lock-ins, demographic shifts—are produced by multiple, partially overlapping causal pathways that are individually sufficient but collectively necessary only in a distributed, web-like sense. Rather than asking the binary question “was outcome O overdetermined by events E1 and E2?”, I propose we measure what I call a redundancy coefficient for the causal web: the degree to which pathways can compensate for one another’s removal without altering the macro-outcome, weighted by interaction effects. I deliberately inject this framing to move philosophy of history away from counterfactual-dependence tests on isolated events and toward continuous, process-based causal architectures. I concede up front that I do not yet have a full formal calculus for the coefficient; my claim is that even an informal, structurally-informed application improves on current binary treatments.
