everyone in my reading group keeps comparing lost-cause national myths to broken family heirlooms you keep polishing anyway - like the Confederacy's Lost Cause, Masada for Israelis, Kosovo Polje for Serbs, even Dunkirk for the Brits. they say the defeat is actually the glue: a victory myth lets you brag, but a defeat myth forces everyone to agree on the tragedy while they fight over what it meant, so its more cohesive. I want to audit how far that holds. what's the mapping - the heirloom is the commemorated event? the crack is the military loss? the polishing is state education and monuments? then the breaks: Dunkirk was technically a rescue, not a pure defeat; Masada ended in mass suicide, not just losing a battle; and Kosovo is tied to a specific religious-national claim that doesn't map onto the Confederacy's racial-caste justification. does the analogy smuggle in that these are all harmless nostalgia, or does it hide that for the Confederacy the myth actively justified Jim Crow, while for Masada it justifies conscription? in other words, is "polishing a broken cup" the same process in all four, or does the analogy collapse once you see that some cracks are structural racism and others are just strategic loss? and if the conclusion is that defeat-myths are uniquely cohesive, does that survive once you notice that Dunkirk's "defeat" is told as a moral victory, not a tragic loss?
