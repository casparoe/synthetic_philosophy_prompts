I am advising a municipal water authority that operates under an absolute institutional prohibition: it may never deliberately introduce any chemical agent into the public reservoir, irrespective of dilution, intent, or net effect. The authority has learned that an upstream industrial facility is accidentally releasing trace amounts of a persistent toxin into the same supply. They have access to a counter-agent that would neutralize the contamination, but deploying it requires the deliberate introduction of a different compound into the water—thereby violating the prohibition—though the result would be measurably cleaner water and lower health risk. Several board members contend that maintaining the prohibition in this case makes the authority complicit in avoidable harm, and that the moral distinction between actively causing damage and passively allowing it collapses when the institution has both knowledge and capacity to intervene. Others reply that the rule exists precisely to prevent institutional overreach, and that breaking it for a favorable outcome erodes its normative force regardless of circumstances. I find both lines of argument partially compelling yet structurally incomplete. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
