The long-running dispute over whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems have any purchase outside pure mathematics—particularly on human cognition, physical theory, or normative reasoning—seems to me structurally stalled: defenders continue to claim that human reason can recognize arithmetical truths no fixed formal system can prove, critics continue to reply that any such recognition merely shifts to a stronger system, and the exchange repeats without either side conceding ground. I want a diagnosis, not another rehearsal of the Penrose-Lucas arguments. My hypothesis is that the impasse is partly verbal—disagreement about whether a "formal system" must be axiom-fixed and signature-stable, or whether it can include open-ended, self-modifying concept formation—so that one side is asking whether minds exceed closure conditions that the other side denies apply to minds at all. Is this correct? Or is the stall substantive, turning on a hidden empirical claim about whether human mathematical cognition is computable; or is it that the two sides are answering different questions, one about logical completeness and one about cognitive architecture, under a single label? Use what each side actually commits to, not the slogans. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a rigorous distinction between axiom-fixed and axiom-open reasoning, an empirical constraint on human concept formation, a formal result about adaptive systems, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be dissolved?
