I have noticed a recurring pattern in discussions of valid consent that does not map neatly onto the standard invalidating conditions. In one case, a patient fully understands a risky procedure, faces no pressure from any individual clinician, and explicitly agrees; yet every hospital in the region uses the same protocol, so refusing means receiving no care at all, and the patient’s “yes” feels more like resignation to an unavoidable landscape than an active endorsement. In another, a user genuinely comprehends a platform’s data terms, is not threatened by anyone, and ticks the box freely; but every comparable service imposes identical terms, and the alternative is effective exclusion from digital life, so the agreement tracks adaptation to a structure rather than preference for that specific arrangement. The consent is procedurally impeccable—no deception, no coercion by any agent, no incompetence—but it seems hollow in a way the usual taxonomy does not capture. Is there an established philosophical concept or literature for this kind of structurally shaped, procedurally valid but substantively hollow consent, and if so, how exactly does it relate to coercion and to the idea that people can come to genuinely prefer conditions that were originally imposed on them? If there is no precise term, can you define the phenomenon, distinguish its edges from ordinary coercion, collective coercion, and mere resignation, and say whether it should be treated as a distinct invalidator of consent? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
