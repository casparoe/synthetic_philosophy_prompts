I am drafting a novel set in a port town where a fishing economy collapses within a single generation. Every captain, buyer, and deckhand makes what seems, locally, like a rational choice—selling vessels, pivoting to tourism, renegotiating wages—yet collectively these choices produce a decline that no one intended and few tried to prevent. I am struggling with narrative architecture: ought I to distribute moral responsibility among specific characters, or should I treat the town as possessing a kind of collective trajectory that individual actions merely express? I have also observed that once unemployment crossed a particular threshold, residents began accepting terms they previously rejected, as though the town's aggregate condition altered the very criteria by which individuals judged their options. Given this tension between personal agency and the dynamics of the whole, how should I decide whose perspective to center, and can blame be assigned without reducing the community's fate to nothing more than the sum of its parts?
