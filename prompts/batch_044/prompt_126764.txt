I've been thinking about how AI labs actually organize their alignment work. On paper everyone talks about "the alignment problem" like it's one big knot—get the reward right, add a constitution, done. But if you look at the teams, they're split into outer alignment, inner alignment, robustness, interpretability... all running on seperate tracks with different metrics. At the same time, the institutional practice is to run one RLHF pipeline with a single preference dataset and one constitutional doc across every model size. There's also this newer framing some people call "value pluralism under scaling"—the idea that misalignment isn't one failure mode but a family of incommensurable ones that only show up at different capability occurances. And there's this weird funding asymmetry: labs pour money into red-teaming deception and sycophancy but barely fund work on what I'd call "moral stagnation"—where the model is technically obedient but just doesn't care about the right things.

So what philosophical commitments does that organizational practice presupposses? Does treating alignment as a single pipeline presuppose that human values are coherent enough to fit one preference model? Or that misalignment is essentially one kind of error, like a misgeneralization? Is the split into seperate teams just administrative, or does it quietly admit pluralism? And what does the neglect of moral stagnation presuppose—maybe that alignment is fundamentally about control and not about the content of values? If you actually bought the pluralist view, what would have to change in how evaluation benchmarks are built? I'm not asking for citations or a bibliography—just trying to figure out what theory is hiding in the org chart, and whether it's even consistent with itself.
