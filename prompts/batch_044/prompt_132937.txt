I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for a small group of clinical ethicists—colleagues from medicine and law with no background in analytic epistemology—who asked me, after a recent case conference in which a diagnostic error propagated through an apparently sound chain of reasoning, whether knowing one clinical fact ever genuinely commits one to knowing everything it entails. The topic is Nozick’s tracking theory of knowledge and the failure of epistemic closure. I do not want the theory explained to me; I want a teaching plan for them. Specify the exact sequence in which the four tracking conditions must be introduced so that the failure of closure feels like an internal consequence rather than an ad hoc skeptical patch. Identify the one concrete scenario that should carry the entire discussion—addressing whether the brain-in-a-vat example should be avoided or modified for this audience and what should replace or reframe it. Map the misconceptions these professionals will predictably bring, especially the conflation of tracking with mere reliability, probabilistic confidence, or clinical “due diligence,” and indicate precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted before it hardens. Provide a final diagnostic question that reveals whether they have grasped why closure fails specifically on Nozick’s account rather than on generic skeptical grounds. Finally, state openly what parts of the formal literature on sensitivity conditions and modal logic should be set aside at this level, and explain how the plan should respect their practical intuition that clinical knowledge ought to be transitive without allowing that intuition to collapse the distinction between tracking and closure.
