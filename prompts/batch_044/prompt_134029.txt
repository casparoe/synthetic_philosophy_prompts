At a recent philosophy colloquium, two colleagues—Dr. Reed, a historian of institutions, and Professor Voss, a moral philosopher—debated why coercive social norms such as dueling and chattel slavery eventually collapsed. I am a retired physicist only recently drawn to these questions, and I could not determine where the reasoning went astray. I would ask you to adjudicate their exchange point by point, indicating who is right about each move and identifying what, if anything, both parties are missing.

Reed began by defending what he called the interest-collapse model, associated with Bernard Williams: honor cultures dissolve when the material and status costs of maintaining them exceed the benefits. He argued that dueling faded primarily because modern states monopolized legitimate violence, making the legal risk to elite status intolerable, and that chattel slavery ended mainly because industrial labor markets made slaveholding economically inefficient well before abolitionist arguments achieved broad cultural purchase.

Voss replied with the argument familiar from Kwame Anthony Appiah, that moral revolutions occur when public argument redefines honor itself. She claimed dueling disappeared not because it became dangerous but because it was reclassified as barbaric—honor migrated to those who refused the challenge. Likewise, she said slavery collapsed when activists succeeded in making slaveholding a source of social shame, creating a status trap in which retaining slaves signaled backwardness rather than power. She insisted that if material interest were sufficient, slavery should have persisted wherever it remained profitable, yet it dissolved rapidly even in such contexts once the normative framing shifted.

Reed then introduced a refinement he termed honor-cost asymmetry: argument can accelerate normative collapse, but only when it reveals that adhering to the norm is structurally self-undermining for elite status. Without this underlying asymmetry, he maintained, moral rhetoric alone cannot dislodge a practice that still confers tangible benefits.

Voss countered that Reed’s asymmetry concept covertly depends on the very argumentative shifts he denies. To recognize a practice as self-undermining for status, she said, requires a prior normative evaluation—precisely the redefinition of honor that Appiah describes. She added an empirical challenge: dueling declined uniformly even in jurisdictions with weak state enforcement and no economic incentive to abandon it, which suggests that normative transformation, not instrumental calculation, drove the change.

Please go through their four moves in order, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying your assessment. Then say who is more right overall, and identify any important conceptual or empirical consideration—perhaps involving the interaction of institutional enforcement and social meaning—that neither party has adequately addressed.
