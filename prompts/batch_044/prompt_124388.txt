I have come across the idea that philosophers defend real dilemmas by appealing to what they call moral residue—the lingering guilt or sense of failure that persists even after a choice is fully justified—but that sounds like a psychological report rather than proof that two obligations genuinely remain in force. My confusion is logical: if I am truly obligated to preserve line A and also truly obligated to preserve line B, and these courses are incompatible, then once I act it seems I must live with both "I ought to preserve A" and "I ought not to preserve A" being true at once. I understand that quantum physics involves superposition, where a system can be in contradictory states that collapse upon measurement, yet physicists do not say both states remain literally true after the observation; they say the contradiction is resolved. So if ethics permits obligations to stay binding even after we necessarily violate one of them, does that not make moral reasoning less disciplined than physics, treating contradiction as something to be tolerated rather than resolved? I want to know whether there is a coherent way to defend genuine moral dilemmas without making ethics an exception to basic logic, and whether doing so requires us to change what we mean by an obligation in the first place.
