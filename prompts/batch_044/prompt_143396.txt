I am drafting a long-form feature on the Anthropocene for readers who follow climate science but rarely encounter stratigraphic politics. Rather than rehearsing the familiar dispute over whether the epoch began in 1950, 1784, or 1610, I want to reframe the entire controversy as a problem of jurisdiction: who possesses the authority to declare a geological epoch when the defining force is distributed human social action?

The essay moves in three parts. First, I treat the three principal boundary candidates—the 1610 “Orbis” moment linking transatlantic exchange to detectable carbon and biotic shifts, the 1784 fossil-capital transition, and the 1950 Great Acceleration marked by radionuclides and synthetic polymers—not as competing empirical measurements but as competing theories of agency. Each date, I argue, implies a different primary culprit: biological globalization, steam-engine metabolism, or post-war techno-industrial acceleration. Second, I examine whether the International Commission on Stratigraphy applies a juridical model suited to deep-time boundaries formed by tectonic or evolutionary processes, and whether that model suffers a category error when applied to a boundary actively being produced by the very societies requesting its declaration. Third, I ask whether the Anthropocene ought to be retained as a formal stratigraphic unit at all, or whether it functions more precisely as a regulatory concept—an instrument of governance that borrows geological authority to frame biodiversity and climate policy.

I am especially interested in whether this “jurisdiction” framing genuinely illuminates the debate or merely imports legal metaphors that obscure the underlying science. I also want to check whether my reading of the 1610, 1784, and 1950 markers as distinct philosophical positions about human agency holds up, or whether I am overinterpreting stratigraphic preferences. Finally, I want to know whether the essay’s concluding claim—that declaring the Anthropocene is as much a political prescription as a scientific description—actually follows from the premises I have laid out, or whether it requires an additional argument about the sociology of scientific institutions that the draft currently lacks.

Please comment on the conceptual coherence of the framing, on the accuracy with which I have characterized the stratigraphic debate, and on whether the analogy between geological classification and legal jurisdiction survives close scrutiny without collapsing into mere wordplay. If the central argument is unsound, I would particularly welcome suggestions for how to repair it without abandoning the jurisdictional angle entirely.
