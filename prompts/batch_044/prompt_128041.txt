Write a branching taxonomy—structured as a four-node decision tree with six to eight leaves—for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar, designed to sort respondents by their substantive commitments about the unity of consciousness in split-brain (complete corpus callosotomy) patients rather than by the labels they prefer. The audience should be assumed to understand basic philosophy of mind and neuroscience but not to have read the specialist commissurotomy literature.

Begin from this baseline commitment, which every respondent accepts: there is at least one conscious subject present in a split-brain patient post-surgery.

Node 1 (perceptual binding): Present a modified cross-cueing case in which a red circle is presented to the left visual field and a green square to the right, but because of residual subcortical pathways the right hemisphere can influence left-hemisphere verbal report without full callosal integration. Ask whether the patient enjoys a single phenomenal field in which color and shape are bound across hemifields, or whether there are two non-overlapping phenomenal fields that happen to influence behavior through indirect channels. Offer three answers: unified binding, divided binding with cross-influence, or binding that is graded by feature type.

Node 2 (agency and confabulation): Present a scenario in which the right hemisphere initiates a manual action (e.g., selecting an object) and the left hemisphere, unaware of the initiation, spontaneously generates a false but coherent verbal explanation. Ask what this reveals about agency: is there one agent with fragmented access to its own motives, two agents with one systematically ignorant of the other, or one agent whose ownership extends only to actions for which it can construct a narrative? Phrase the options so that a respondent can choose without knowing which philosophical position each maps onto.

Node 3 (counterfactual integration): Introduce a hypothetical artificial neural bridge that temporarily restores interhemispheric communication for thirty minutes, then is removed. Ask whether reconnecting the hemispheres retroactively unifies the pre-bridge experiences into the history of a single subject, creates an entirely new merged subject with no claim on prior experiences, or reveals that there were always two subjects whose streams merely become linked. This node should inject a niche but conceptually clear distinction about diachronic identity conditions.

Node 4 (narrative necessity): Ask about a patient in whom neither hemisphere can construct an autobiography that spans the surgery—each has a pre-operative past and a post-operative present, but neither integrates the other hemisphere’s post-operative actions into its self-story. Is narrative coherence necessary for being a single subject, so that the absence of a unified autobiography entails divided subjects? Or can a subject exist without such coherence, so that the patient remains one subject with a fractured self-narrative? Include an option for the deflationary view that “subject” is not a substantive category here.

For each leaf, provide: (a) the name of the position it represents (strict unified-subject view; strict divided-subject view; graded-threshold view; narrative-deflationary/no-subject view; and at least one other named or unnamed position of your choice), and (b) a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment—the result it must accept that its rivals avoid.

Additionally, identify at least one combination of answers that the tree allows but that appears unoccupied in the literature, and briefly diagnose why: is it incoherent, merely unexplored, or unstable given the other commitments? Flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining which node fails to distinguish them.

The tree should contain no references to specific philosophers by name in the branch questions, no requests for bibliographies or citations, and no questions that ask “do you hold view X?” Every branch must be answerable by a respondent who knows only the concrete scenarios described. Keep the total number of nodes to exactly four, with each node offering two or three discrete options.
