A colleague in a seminar on personal identity invoked the Nietzschean slogan “amor fati” — love your fate — together with the thought of eternal recurrence, to argue that regretting any past choice is conceptually incoherent: since the self now judging was produced by exactly that sequence of events, to wish them away is to wish away the conditions of one’s own existence, and since in eternal recurrence those events return infinitely, one must affirm them with unconditional love rather than counterfactual regret. I think something is wrong with that move, but I want to separate the confusions precisely.

Can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that “amor fati” coupled with eternal recurrence could be expressing? For each reading, say whether it is trivial (e.g., acceptance improves well-being), a substantive ethical claim (e.g., one ought to affirm all past events regardless of their value), a metaphysical claim about the structure of time (e.g., literal cyclical repetition giving infinite weight to each moment), or a claim about personal identity (e.g., counterfactual dependence of the present self on past choices makes regret self-undermining). Say which readings actually entail quietism about the future versus which are compatible with striving to change it, and flag any that are false, incoherent, or depend on a disputed theory of the self. Then identify which specific reading the seminar argument needed — that regret is incoherent because it requires annihilating the conditions of one’s identity in an infinitely repeating world — and say whether that reading is defensible or collapses into something weaker.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
