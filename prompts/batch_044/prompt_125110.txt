I have been thinking independently about split-brain cases — patients in whom the link between the two cerebral hemispheres has been severed — and I find myself unable to decide whether one conscious person remains. The behaviour looks unified, yet if the hemispheres cannot share information, it seems as though two streams of awareness might inhabit one body. I facilitate a small discussion group of about eight people for a single 90-minute session; none of us are philosophy specialists. Please design a participatory activity rather than a lecture, one in which we feel the tension rather than merely hearing about it. I envisage paired roles with divided knowledge but shared agency: one participant can observe the task but cannot act, whilst the other can act but receives no direct information about the goal. They must cooperate to complete a simple joint task, and I want a structured moment in which the acting partner makes an error that the observing partner corrects, but without any shared language or explanation being possible. After the task, both partners should be asked to say whose decision the correction was. Provide the rules, the debrief that connects this experience to the problem of a single self accounting for actions that arise from disconnected streams, an honest admission of which feature of real split-brain consciousness this simulation cannot reproduce, and a warning about what is likely to go wrong the first time. The confusion should emerge from the roles and constraints, not from any explanation given in advance.
