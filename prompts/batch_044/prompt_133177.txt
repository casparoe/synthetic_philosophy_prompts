I have been revisiting the extended mind debate after a semester in philosophy of mind, and it has become oddly personal: I now do most of my philosophical drafting with a large language model that does not retrieve my old notes but reconstructs arguments probabilistically on the fly, and I genuinely cannot tell whether my beliefs about the parity principle are “in my head” or distributed across this unstable, stochastic scaffold. The literature seems stuck in a loop I can map out precisely. Clark and Chalmers propose active externalism via the parity principle—if an external process plays the same functional role as an internal one, it counts as cognitive—while critics such as Adams and Aizawa and Rupert reply that coupling is not constitution, that functional equivalence misses the mark, and that this leads to “cognitive bloat.” Defenders then refine the conditions—reliability, accessibility, trust, endorsement—while critics adjust the counterexamples. After decades, every move is catalogued; nobody changes sides, and the canonical cases feel like ritual incantations rather than live tests.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but architectural: the two sides are answering different questions under the single heading “where is the mind?” One side asks a metaphysical question about the boundaries of the cognitive system given functional criteria; the other asks a normative or explanatory question about what makes a mental state genuinely mine, accountable, and non-derivative. But I want a sharper diagnosis. Is this a verbal disagreement about what “cognitive” means—something like a family-resemblance term being stretched—or a substantive clash about the conditions for mental ownership? Or is there a hidden formal disagreement about whether parity requires token-identity of representations, or about whether reliability must be diachronically stable rather than merely synchronic?

To push past the ritual, I want to inject a specific wrinkle that the classic literature largely ignores: what happens when the external vehicle is not a stable notebook but a stochastic retrieval-and-generation system that never stores the same representation twice? If Otto’s notebook were replaced by a system that probabilistically reconstructs his beliefs about MoMA each time he queries it, does the parity principle still apply? Does the instability break the coupling, or does functional equivalence survive as long as the statistical profile of outputs matches Inga’s neural retrieval? And crucially, if the debate stalls here, is that because both sides implicitly assumed that extended cognition requires a transparent, durable vehicle—making the disagreement really about the metaphysics of storage rather than about mind per se? Diagnose the structure of the impasse using what each side actually commits to about ownership, reliability, and functional role, not just by surveying interpretations. Tell me whether this is bedrock intuition, a hidden empirical claim about neural versus external storage, or two disciplines—philosophy of mind versus cognitive engineering—talking past each other. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the question, a formal model of stochastic coupling, an empirical finding about predictive processing in human-tool interaction, or the recognition that the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned?
