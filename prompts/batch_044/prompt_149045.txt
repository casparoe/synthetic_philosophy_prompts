I have just finished a long and rather heated argument with a friend about how far we are entitled to reconstruct beliefs and social institutions from material culture when no texts or direct administrative traces survive. I found myself holding quite firm verdicts on several concrete archaeological scenarios, but I am far from sure they form a coherent pattern. Here are the cases, with my judgments attached.

First, consider a Late Neolithic cemetery in which every adult burial—regardless of sex, age, or skeletal indicators of occupation—contains the same three polished stone axes, arranged in an identical orientation and showing no wear from practical use. I judge that this strongly supports an institutional burial rule reflecting a shared belief about social membership or transition to an afterlife, and that it is not adequately explained as a display of personal wealth or practical provisioning.

Second, take a roughly contemporaneous settlement with storage pits of remarkably uniform capacity, distributed across households that show no other signs of central coordination, and with no administrative buildings, seals, or writing present. Here I deny that we may infer a centralised redistribution institution; the uniformity seems to me fully explicable by shared craft tradition, practical constraints on storage, and local peer emulation.

Third, a ceremonial centre yields hundreds of deliberately broken high-quality ceramic vessels deposited in a single sealed layer, none showing wear patterns that suggest prior domestic use. I take this to confirm an institutional practice of ritual termination or collective feasting, tied to specific calendrical or ceremonial events rather than to accidental loss or economic discard.

Fourth, in the same broader region, imported obsidian blades are manufactured using entirely local flaking techniques, with no foreign workshop debris or specialised toolkits present. I think this indicates that local artisans adopted foreign raw materials within existing technological traditions, rather than supporting an inference of foreign colonisation, direct trade monopolies, or a specialised craft guild.

Fifth, a monumental structure tracks the summer solstice with high precision, yet the surrounding settlement produces no calendars, numerical notations, or textual references of any kind. Despite this absence, I maintain that the alignment warrants attributing sophisticated astronomical knowledge and, very likely, an institutional calendrical practice.

My friend pressed me hard on the tension between the first two cases: in both we observe striking material standardisation across a population, yet in one I invoke a shared belief and an institutional rule, while in the other I resist institutional inference entirely. She also noted that in the fifth case I am willing to posit institutional knowledge without any direct administrative trace, which sits uneasily with the caution I display in the second.

So I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind all of these verdicts—perhaps one that distinguishes representational or symbolic domains from practical or functional ones, or one that asks whether a material pattern is overdetermined by practical explanation. If there is such a principle, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me what verdict it delivers on cases I have not considered—for example, whether uniform domestic architecture lacking decorative variation should be read as practical convergence or as an enforced institutional code, and whether a sudden, region-wide change in burial orientation coupled with entirely unchanged domestic assemblages supports an ideological shift without broader social restructuring.

But I also want an honest assessment: if saving consistency requires an ad hoc qualification, or if the principle forces me to abandon one of my verdicts—particularly the inference in the first case or the denial in the second—say so plainly. I am aiming at reflective equilibrium, not at defending every intuition regardless of cost. Please reply in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the reasoning should stand entirely on its own.
