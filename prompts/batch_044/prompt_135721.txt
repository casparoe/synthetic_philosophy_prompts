The interpretive dispute over whether Mencius and Xunzi hold genuinely incompatible views on human nature (xing) has persisted across centuries of commentary without clear resolution. One camp reads the texts as making opposing empirical claims about innate moral psychology: Mencius asserts that human beings possess inborn sprouts of compassion and shame, while Xunzi insists that xing comprises only selfish, sensory-driven tendencies that culture must reshape. A second camp argues the disagreement is largely verbal, turning on whether xing denotes a normative potentiality, a biological given, or a dynamic process of maturation; on this view, both thinkers agree that moral excellence requires rigorous ritual education, and their rhetoric diverges without substantive contradiction. A third interpretation holds that the two projects are not answering one question but two: Mencius offers a soteriological account of how virtue can be recovered if conditions permit, whereas Xunzi offers a political justification for institutional authority given that unaided development fails.

Yet the literature seems to recycle these three readings without advancing them. I want a diagnosis of why the debate stalls. Is there a hidden empirical question — for instance, about the evolutionary preparedness of moral cognition — that the texts cannot settle? Or are the sides operating with incompatible standards for what counts as nature, so that no textual evidence could adjudicate the matter?

To sharpen this, consider a framing drawn from contemporary philosophy of biology: suppose we distinguish between traits that are developmentally prepared outcomes of an evolved social niche, and traits that must be constructed ex nihilo by culture. Does the Mencian view map onto the first, treating moral cognition as a prepared learning trajectory shaped by an innate social environment, and the Xunzian view onto the second, treating culture as imposing form on formless raw material? Or does importing this distinction merely reproduce the old ambiguity in modern vocabulary?

Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is — substantive, verbal, or a misalignment of projects — and, crucially, what would count as genuine progress. Would it require a conceptual distinction that separates normative potential from biological endowment without collapsing either position? A formal model of moral development acceptable to both frameworks? Or should we conclude that the framing question Is human nature good or bad? is ill-posed, and that the impasse is not a failure of scholarship but a sign that the question itself demands dissolution rather than resolution?
