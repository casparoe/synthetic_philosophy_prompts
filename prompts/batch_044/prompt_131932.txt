Consider a framing that extends Strawson's account of reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the participant stance—without relying on standard textbook applications. On this view, reactive attitudes depend not only on an agent's quality of will, but also on what we might call structural accessibility conditions: whether the mental states that ground the attitude are in principle available to the community that would hold the agent responsible. This raises conceptual problems that Strawson's original taxonomy, which distinguishes ordinary interpersonal capacities from the objective attitude, does not obviously address. In particular, it forces us to ask whether deliberately designed opacity—what we can term manufactured opacity, where institutions structure decision-making so that no individual grasps the full moral architecture of an action—creates a novel category of excuse, or whether reactive attitudes must then shift to collective entities in ways that collapse back onto individuals.

Suppose you are directing a small philosophy research group—two senior scholars and one postdoc, with three years of dedicated funding—focused specifically on moral responsibility in institutional and organizational contexts. Given the framing above, which of the following open lines of work should they prioritize, in what order, and why?

First, whether reactive attitudes can coherently attach to collective entities (corporations, states, algorithmic governance systems) when no single member possesses the relevant quality of will, or whether such attachment necessarily reduces to individual anchoring. Second, whether manufactured opacity constitutes a distinct excusing condition that Strawson's framework fails to capture—something other than the objective attitude and other than standard incapacity—because the agent's mental states are structurally hidden rather than naturally deficient. Third, whether extending the participant stance to non-human or distributed agents requires reconceiving the stance itself, or whether analogical application is sufficient without conceptual revision.

Defend your ranking with explicit criteria: tractability for philosophers rather than empirical sociologists or legal scholars; dependency relations among the questions (which resolutions are upstream of which); and what kind of conceptual argument or result over the next two years would cause you to reorder them. I am not asking for a survey of Strawson scholarship or a literature review. I want a defended allocation of limited intellectual resources, and I want to know which of these questions, if settled, would most alter how we think about moral responsibility in deliberately designed institutions.
