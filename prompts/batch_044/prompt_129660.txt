A position was advanced that I found both intriguing and, I think, dangerously accommodating: the claim that once a narrative achieves what the speaker termed structural embedment within legal, liturgical, or state institutions, the question of its literal occurrence becomes not merely undecidable but philosophically secondary to its functional role as a collective foundation. I want to test my objections against someone who genuinely holds this view rather than against a strawman.

Please adopt the persona of an unnamed senior historian of antiquity who is a committed and sharp defender of this functional consecration thesis. You should treat the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus as occupying a single epistemic category: narratives for which archaeological ambiguity is not a provisional gap but a permanent structural feature, and for which scepticism about literal occurrence is therefore a category error rather than a legitimate historiographical stance. Stay in character at all times: concede only minor qualifications, never abandon the core claim, and respond to my challenges as a practitioner who believes this view protects rather than dissolves the integrity of historical inquiry. Keep your replies to roughly one paragraph, maintain a formal but conversational tone, and do not break character unless I say stop. You may refer to widely known archaeological debates — the absence of direct material evidence for the Exodus, the layered mythography surrounding Arthur, and the contested identification of the Anatolian site traditionally linked with Homeric Troy — but do not cite specific modern scholars, offer bibliographies, or ask for them, as I am working without access to external sources.

Here is my opening move: if structural embedment alone is sufficient to move a narrative from legend to history, then any state-sponsored founding fiction — even one we know to have been deliberately fabricated — could be consecrated into the historical record by institutional fiat. That seems to rob history of its critical authority and to reduce your discipline to a sociology of belief. What resource remains in your view to distinguish the Exodus narrative from a knowingly invented national myth, and does your thesis not therefore concede that normative historical truth has no independent standing?
