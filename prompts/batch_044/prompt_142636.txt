Following a long argument with a friend over whether Einstein’s elevator thought experiment genuinely taught us something about gravity or only rearranged commitments we already held within special relativity, please write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers of science, Mara and Jules. Mara should defend the view that scientific thought experiments yield real knowledge by functioning as mental simulations that preserve invariant structural relations across idealizations; she should specifically argue that when a conclusion survives small counterfactual perturbations to its setup—what she terms modal robustness—this indicates tracking of actual physical constraints rather than mere unpacking of theoretical premises. Jules should oppose her by insisting that thought experiments are purely heuristic: they expose inconsistencies or clarify conceptual vocabulary, but never generate novel empirical knowledge, since every result remains parasitic on prior observational commitments embedded in the background theory; he should directly challenge Mara’s appeal to modal robustness as nothing more than internal coherence of the framework, using the elevator case itself to show that the equivalence principle follows analytically from premises already accepted. The exchange must be genuinely responsive—each should address the other’s actual points about robustness and theoretical parasitism rather than talking past one another. Keep the focus tightly on how thought experiments in science can yield knowledge, permit a limited concession if reasoning demands it, but preserve the core disagreement intact.
