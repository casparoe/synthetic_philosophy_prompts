A philosopher I discuss philosophy of mind with takes the following positions: she denies that a typical REM dream recalled upon waking is a genuine experience, since she holds there is no consciousness during sleep to undergo it; she accepts that a waking perceptual illusion, such as seeing a mirage, is an experience, because the subject is then conscious of seeming-to-perceive; she denies that a false autobiographical memory implanted by suggestion is an experience, on the grounds that there was never a corresponding conscious state; she accepts that hypnagogic imagery experienced at the threshold of sleep counts as an experience, since awareness is only partially diminished; she accepts that a lucid dream is an experience, precisely because the sleeper reports concurrent awareness and volition; and she denies that a blind person’s non-lucid tactile dream is an experience, treating it analogously to the REM case. Now consider this further case: a fully awake patient undergoing neurosurgery reports a vivid, temporally structured narrative of a childhood event triggered by direct electrical stimulation of the temporal lobe, though the event never occurred, no prior memory trace exists, and the report vanishes immediately when stimulation ceases. Predict whether she will call this an experience, but I am less interested in the answer than in the reasoning: identify at least two rules that accommodate all six of her earlier verdicts equally well, say what each rule predicts about the stimulation case, explain which prediction you think best fits her implicit commitments and why, and propose one further hypothetical case that would force a choice between the two rules.
