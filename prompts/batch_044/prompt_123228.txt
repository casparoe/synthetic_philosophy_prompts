Analyze the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission's amnesty process through a conceptual framework that distinguishes agentic guilt—directed at discrete wrongful acts and remediable through restitution or explicit acknowledgment—from structural shame—directed at collective identity and social membership, resistant to individual repair but responsive to collective narrative restructuring. I am working with the further claim, which I take to be contested but worth testing, that emotional regimes are not merely inherited but can be institutionalized: the TRC's specific design (public testimony, conditional amnesty requiring full disclosure, absence of punitive sentencing) can be read either as an attempt to convert a legacy of structural shame into agentic guilt, or conversely as a mechanism for dissolving agentic guilt into a managed, collective form of shame. Map the institutional mechanics with precision: identify the relevant actors (perpetrators applying for amnesty, victims and survivors testifying, the Commission serving as audience and gatekeeper), the actual options available under the Commission's enabling legislation, and the emotional script each party was expected to perform. Then assess what the guilt/shame distinction illuminates—perhaps the prevalence of meticulously detailed confessional narratives paired with minimal material redistribution—and what it obscures, especially victim responses that appear to be neither guilt-oriented demands for repair nor shame-oriented withdrawal but rather something like moral disgust at unearned forgiveness. Finally, evaluate whether this episode supports the thesis that a culture can deliberately choose guilt over shame (or vice versa) through institutional engineering, or whether it demonstrates that such designs can only channel pre-existing emotional economies without transforming them. Do not invoke obscure post-apartheid historiography; rely on the well-documented structure of the hearings and widely reported controversies, such as the opposition voiced by families of prominent victims including Steve Biko. Where the framework is silent, say so explicitly, and resist forcing the case to yield a verdict it does not support.
