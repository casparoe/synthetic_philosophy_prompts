I'm organizing the closing session for a graduate seminar on contemporary philosophy of meaning in life, and I want to open with a poem that captures the precise conceptual tension we've been tracking rather than offering a generic meditation on purpose. Our discussion has centered on the gap between absurdist accounts—where the universe offers no answer to our demand for meaning, rendering the demand itself symptomatic of a mismatch—and recent relational theories that try to locate meaning not in cosmic endorsement but in a kind of unstable "fit" between what an agent is drawn to pursue and what is genuinely valuable, where that fit is neither reducible to subjective satisfaction nor to objective value alone. I want the poem to treat this fit as a suspended, triadic relation that requires the agent to stand within a normative framework the universe neither validates nor denies, so that relocating meaning to the local level does not automatically dissolve the absurdist challenge but instead exposes a hidden dependency.

Please write a Petrarchan sonnet—fourteen lines, iambic pentameter, octave rhymed ABBA ABBA, sestet rhymed CDECDE or CDCDCD—in which the octave articulates the absurdist position: the silence of the cosmos, the persistence of human meaning-seeking despite that silence, and the risk that any local project is undermined by the absence of overarching purpose. The sestet should present the relational counter-move—meaning as arising in the connection between subjective attraction and objective worth—but should do so in a way that reveals its fragility: the relation holds only if the agent is already embedded in norms that require no cosmic cooperation, leaving the "fit" suspended rather than secured. The volta at line 9 should occur precisely at the moment this dependency is exposed, and the rhyme scheme should pair conceptually linked terms across the turn (for example, linking "void" with "avoided" or "indifferent" with "different") so that formal closure ironically underscores conceptual openness.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the literature—say, the distinction between Camus's conception of the absurd and Wolf's account of meaning arising when subjective fulfillment connects with objective value—recognizes the positions without them being named, cited, or quoted, and it should avoid resolving the tension into either a triumph of local meaning or a surrender to cosmic pointlessness. Since this is for a seminar audience, the tone should be intellectually serious rather than sentimental. End with a single final line that summarizes, in one sentence, the structural move enacted by the sonnet's volta.
