I take the view that applying the outside view to political decline requires restructuring reference classes by institutional morphology rather than by conventional historiographic labels, and that this undermines the standard empirical claim about imperial lifespans. Specifically, I hold that the class “empire” is usually defined retrospectively—either by geographic scale or by the fact of eventual collapse—which introduces a selection bias that makes the base-rate regularity of a few centuries look tighter than it is. The defensible version of the forecast, on my framing, should group polities by the degree to which central fiscal extraction is decoupled from local coercive capacity, not by the conventional markers historians use. Once you do that, the apparent lifespan regularity largely dissolves into noise, and the only remaining predictive signal is not a chronological base rate but a threshold-style transition in administrative coherence at a scale-independent point. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want a map of its exposure: which historical comparisons or comparative-political datasets would be decisive against the morphological framing, which methodological objections about reference-class construction actually bite, and whether I have insulated the view by letting me redraw class boundaries whenever a case misbehaves. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Say what defenders of the standard outside-view approach would themselves concede as counting against them—not only what critics say—and be willing to conclude that my morphological reframing is either well-motivated or an unfalsifiable insulation device, with an honest assessment of whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Separate what is settled about reference-class forecasting and base-rate reasoning from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these problems, and do not present the morphological reframing of imperial reference classes as if it were the consensus position.
