Read Euripides' The Bacchae through the lens of Plato's account of drunkenness in the Laws and Symposium, where wine functions as a pharmakon that tests the soul by stripping away social inhibition rather than transforming the self; the drinker reveals a pre-existing virtue or vice for which he remains accountable. Apply this framework to the Maenads' ecstasy, Agave's sparagmos of Pentheus, and her subsequent awakening: does the play present their violence as the disclosure of capacities that were always present, or as the imposition of a new Dionysian identity that replaces the old? Focus particularly on the awakening scene and Cadmus's reflection on human weakness. Then consider the philosophical distinction between revelatory and transformative intoxication, which suggests that if ritual and drug construct the very violence they appear merely to uncover, Plato's assumption of a stable underlying character collapses. Does The Bacchae support Plato's psychology, dramatize its failure, or hold the two possibilities in unresolved tension? I want a reading faithful to both the tragedy's religious horror and the philosophical argument, ending with what the play adds to the debate about whether intoxication reveals or invents the self.
