I am drafting an essay for my philosophy coursework on whether coherent extrapolated volition can ground ethical decision-making. My argument is that applying coherence and epistemic correction to first-order desires produces a reflexive instability: the more we idealise preferences, the more the resulting volition risks dissolving the agent's initial identity. I contrast this with reflective equilibrium, noting that CEV applies coherence not merely to moral principles but to desires themselves, creating what I call a 'preference dissolution' problem. I then ask whether a 'stability clause'—requiring minimal continuity between pre- and post-idealised selves—rescues the view, or merely introduces an arbitrary conservatism disguised as neutrality. I should be grateful for comments on two matters: whether the distinction between belief-coherence and desire-coherence holds, and whether the stability clause genuinely resolves the reflexivity or only displaces it onto the choice of a continuity metric. I do not require references or a reading list; I seek only an assessment of whether these conceptual moves are sound and philosophically interesting.
