Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to the study of classical Indian epistemology, I am struck by how the Nyāya school's four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison or analogy), and śabda (testimony)—map imperfectly onto the methods I once trusted in the laboratory. Rather than asking for a historical summary, I want an evaluation on a specific dimension: please rank these four knowledge-sources according to what I will call their "error-correction resilience," defined as the capacity of a community of inquirers to identify and rectify a false deliverance generated by that particular source without circularly relying on one of the other three. Assign each pramāṇa an integer score from 1 (least resilient) to 10 (most resilient), and in a concise paragraph for each, justify the score by appealing to internal features of the Nyāya account—for instance, the conditions of invariable concomitance (vyāpti) in anumāna, the dependency relations that constrain upamāna, the authority conditions for śabda, or the directness and fallibility conditions discussed for pratyakṣa. I ask that you treat the framework on its own terms rather than reducing it to a modern philosophy of science gloss, and that you present your answer in American English. Please do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations; I am working without library access and need only the conceptual analysis.
