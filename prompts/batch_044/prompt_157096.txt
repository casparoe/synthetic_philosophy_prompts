Argue that the backward induction paradox in the centipede game is dissolved not by denying common knowledge of rationality, but by rejecting the implicit modeling assumption that an agent’s future decision nodes are governed by independent rational agents with whom she must strategically interact. Instead, defend the position that a single rational agent should adopt one globally coherent intertemporal policy—treating the entire game tree as an object of unified choice rather than a sequence of isolated local maximizations—so that continuing play for many rounds is rationally required rather than paradoxical. In developing this argument, address specifically why this global-policy view does not collapse into the claim that rational agents should cooperate indefinitely in all finitely repeated prisoner’s dilemmas: distinguish the structural features of the centipede game, particularly its escalating mutual-benefit payoff structure, from the competitive payoff structure that licenses backward-induction defection, and explain why global cooperation is licensed in the former but not in the latter. Further, respond to the objection that if an agent can commit to such a global policy, she should be able to commit to stopping at an arbitrary node, and explain why only policies that maximize the agent’s total expected payoff across the tree are rationally admissible. Keep your reasoning internal to decision-theoretic and philosophical concepts; do not invoke empirical studies, named formal systems you do not explain, or bibliographic references.
