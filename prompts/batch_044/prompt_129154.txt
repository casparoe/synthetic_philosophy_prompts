I've been stewing on this since re-reading parts of that old Hofstadter book (GEB) — specifically the MU-puzzle and the whole JOOTS idea. The puzzle can't be solved inside the MIU rules, but a human does it by stepping outside and noticing the rules are about parity or whatever. So I kind of bought into this position: real reasoning isn't just computing within a framework, it's recognizing you're inside one and being able to suspend it. Applied to AI, I think LLMs are structurally like the MIU system — attention heads and next-token prediction are basically fixed inference rules, and there's no architectural meta-interpreter that can halt the game and ask if the rules still apply. So the hard ceiling isn't scale or data, it's definately the absence of a JOOTS mechanism.

What would actually count against this? I want concrete defeaters — maybe experiments where models systematically reject their own training priors, or theoretical results showing recursive self-modification can emerge from pure prediction. Rank them from fatal (direct proof of meta-system recognition) to merely awkward (some edge case that looks like jumping out but is really just interpolation). And be honest: have I just defined intelligence as whatever LLMs currently lack? Has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for an architectural claim? What would the defenders of this view themselves concede counts against them?

Also, if human cognition turns out to be mostly pattern matching with occasional lucky heuristics rather than genuine meta-system jumps, does that destroy the analogy or just make it a difference of degree? I'd like an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything real or whether I've insulated it with semantics.
