In a recent forum discussion about decision paralysis, someone waved away Buridan's ass with the line "indifference is just hidden preference with extra steps"—the suggestion being that if an agent genuinely cannot choose between two options, that failure proves there was never a true tie. I want to understand what precise claims this slogan could be making, because the dismissal seems to work differently under each reading. Could you lay out the distinct theses it might express? Specifically, I am interested in: (a) a psychological reading, that any experienced paralysis reveals an unconscious utility differential or framing effect; (b) a normative-rationalist reading, that rational choice requires a complete strict ordering, so ties are conceptually excluded for a rational agent; (c) a formal-modeling reading, that decision theory should treat ties as artifacts of coarse-grained representation and resolve them via epsilon-perturbations or lexicographic refinements; and (d) a metaphysical reading, that objective practical reasons can never be perfectly symmetric, so apparent ties are always epistemic. For each, please indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is defensible, and—most importantly—whether it actually dissolves the normative form of Buridan's problem (where reasons are genuinely symmetric and the agent ought to choose) or only addresses the descriptive form (where we explain why biological agents rarely starve). Additionally, does any version leave room for a rational agent to deploy a randomizing device—say, a fair coin—without that deployment counting as evidence of a hidden preference? I would like the answer structured as a menu of interpretations with verdicts, rather than a defense of one, since my goal is to check which readings actually do the argumentative work the forum post needed.
