I am applying for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship in philosophy of science and technology, and I need a polished two-page proposal on the following half-formed idea. In the AI alignment literature, HCH—Human-Consulting-HCH—and factored cognition are standardly introduced as technical mechanisms for scalable oversight: they decompose reasoning into subproblems so that human judgment can verify each step without having to reproduce the whole argument. I want to treat these not as engineering blueprints but as normative idealizations of collective deliberation, structurally comparable to models of distributed expertise and democratic reasoning, and ask whether they solve or merely displace a central problem in the philosophy of epistemic division of labor.

The proposal should contain five elements, precisely stated. First, the research question: is factored cognition best understood as an intersubjective structure that distributes epistemic authority across agents, or does it reduce to an iterative amplification of a single reasoner? More specifically, when a complex judgment is decomposed into independently verifiable fragments, does the trust problem migrate from the conclusion to the boundary conditions of the decomposition itself—so that we still need an unverified meta-judgment about which fractures are legitimate? Second, explain why existing treatments of HCH and factored cognition leave this normative gap open: they demonstrate feasibility under certain conditions but do not defend those conditions as philosophically justified criteria for legitimate collective reasoning. Third, the method. Rather than running empirical studies or formalizing algorithms, I will conduct a conceptual comparison between HCH/factored cognition and two alternative idealizations—peer review as distributed verification, and deliberative preference aggregation—using explicit criteria for when a decomposition preserves dependency relations among reasons and when it obscures them. Provide a realistic month-by-month plan: months 1–2 map structural analogies; months 3–5 develop the normative criterion for epistemically legitimate decomposition; months 6–8 test the criterion against edge cases in moral and scientific reasoning, where decomposition might distort the problem by isolating value judgments from empirical premises; months 9–10 draft; months 11–12 revise and present in workshop. Fourth, state what success looks like—a defensible framework specifying when distributed verification counts as genuine deliberation rather than concealed deference to an opaque partition—and what failure looks like, for instance if HCH collapses into either unexamined expert authority or an infinite regress of meta-verification. Fifth, an honest scope assessment: this is conceptual philosophy, not institutional design or computer science, so the deliverable should be a framework paper with carefully constructed illustrative cases, not a validated protocol or empirical study. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; I will supply references. Avoid treating HCH or factored cognition as black-box computational procedures; focus on their logical structure as models of reasoning, and frame the division of epistemic labor problem as the central tension that makes the project both open and tractable within a single year.
