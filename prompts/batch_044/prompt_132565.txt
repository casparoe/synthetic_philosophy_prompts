I am preparing a ninety-minute discussion session for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar—students come from philosophy of mind, computer science, and environmental ethics—and I want to teach Kant's view that we have only indirect duties with regard to animals, not direct duties to them. Rather than opening with the familiar cruelty-to-a-dog example, I want to carry the explanation with a niche scenario: an autonomous wildlife-monitoring drone, operating without real-time human oversight, must decide whether to abort its energy-efficient survey route in order to avoid destroying a rare nesting site. Plan the session's explanatory arc. First, specify the conceptual dependency: what distinction (direct versus indirect duties, and its connection to rational nature and moral agency) must be firmly in place before the drone case can even be posed, and why introducing the case earlier would backfire. Second, describe exactly what features the drone scenario needs to possess and what it must deliberately lack—for instance, why it should not become a trolley-problem variant or an exercise in robot rights—so that it illuminates Kant's framework rather than distracting from it. Third, map the misconceptions the students will predictably carry into the room: that Kant is simply a speciesist indifferent to suffering, that "indirect duty" means the obligation is weak or optional, and that the drone's autonomy might make it a bearer of duties in Kant's own terms. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off and how. Fourth, propose one final check-for-understanding question that reveals whether the students have grasped why Kant denies direct duties to animals, as opposed to whether they merely reject or endorse the view. Finally, identify one adjacent topic—perhaps recent work on animal cognition or on moral status in artificial agents—that I should explicitly bracket and say is outside the scope of this session, and briefly justify why leaving it out protects the conceptual structure you have laid out.
