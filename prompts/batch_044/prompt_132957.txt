I've been working through the debate over whether conscious experience is revised retrospectively—the Orwellian picture, in which an earlier experience is rewritten in memory, versus the Stalinesque picture, in which the brain fabricates a false narrative of having experienced something all along—mostly as it applies to the color phi phenomenon. What strikes me is how tightly the discussion remains anchored to a single visual illusion and to armchair intuition about temporal ordering, even though the underlying mechanism, if real, should ramify into other domains. Suppose you are advising a small conceptual research group—three philosophers, three years, no neuroimaging access—on where to direct their effort in this vicinity. I want a defended ordering of priorities, not a survey or a reading list.

Specifically, I see three open lines that seem to sit at different distances from the core phenomenon. First, whether the revision framework applies analogously to cross-modal temporal binding—say, to auditory-visual asynchronies—rather than remaining confined to intra-modal color-motion illusions; this tests whether we are dealing with a general feature of temporal consciousness or a quirk of the visual pathway. Second, whether there is a principled philosophical distinction between revision that corrects noisy input and revision that introduces representational content never present in the original stream, since the moral and epistemic status of the two seems different even if the neural story looks identical. Third, whether accepting either revision model undermines the evidential value of first-person reports in clinical contexts where patients describe the temporal sequencing of symptoms—such as the order of prodromal sensations—since if retrospective construction is pervasive, clinicians may be treating reconstructed narratives as transparent data.

Please rank these three, or substitute one or two with lines you think are more upstream, and defend the ranking. I am less interested in the list itself than in the reasoning: for each priority, why it is tractable for philosophers without empirical labs, which other questions depend on its resolution, and what kind of conceptual result would genuinely change how we think about the timing of experience rather than merely adding another footnote to the revisionist taxonomy. What evidence or argument over the next few years would cause you to reorder them?
