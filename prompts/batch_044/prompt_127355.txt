Question: In his ethical writings, Moore describes personal affection and the appreciation of beautiful objects as among the greatest goods, and introduces the notion of an organic unity to explain how their value combines. Explain what Moore means by an organic unity, apply it to the case of a friendship that includes shared aesthetic experience, and say whether the value of such a friendship depends on the friends actually perceiving genuinely beautiful objects, or whether false belief about the objects' beauty undermines the whole.

Student answer: Moore's idea of an organic unity is that a complex whole can have a value that is not just the sum of the values of its separate parts, and in some cases the parts have no value at all except when they are combined. For example, consciousness of a beautiful object is part of an organic unity: the object by itself might have no value, and the consciousness by itself might have little value, but together they form something far more valuable. Applying this to friendship with shared aesthetic experience, the affection between the friends and their joint appreciation form an organic unity whose value exceeds the sum of the two parts. Crucially, because the value is intrinsic and belongs to the whole rather than to its parts, the friendship retains its full value even if the friends are mistaken about the beauty of what they admire—say, they cherish a forgery—because the false belief does not affect the affection or the shared experience, which are the real parts of the unity.
