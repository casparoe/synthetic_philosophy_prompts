Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—call them the Inside-Viewer and the Outside-Viewer—debating whether a researcher should trust her own judgment after she derives a sharply counterintuitive result in formal epistemology. The result in question is that, in cases of deep peer disagreement about fundamental logical or normative principles, standard conciliationism is self-undermining: if applied consistently to one's own reasoning processes, it erodes the very cognitive reliability required to perform the conciliation in the first place, leaving a kind of reflexive stalemate rather than a justified revision downward. The researcher believes her derivation is valid and that she can trace each step; she is therefore inclined to stand firm.

The Inside-Viewer should defend the claim that epistemic modesty does not require abandoning a well-checked line of reasoning, especially when the outside view offers no uniquely correct reference class—any class broad enough to include failed philosophers also includes successful ones, and selecting a narrower class (for instance, "researchers who have worked on reflexive conciliation for a decade") reintroduces inside-view judgments about competence and relevance. The Outside-Viewer should reply that this reference-class maneuver is precisely the kind of self-exempting rationalization that base-rate reasoning is meant to block, and that a genuinely modest agent should recognize that her subjective feeling of having traced each step is itself a predictable feature of overconfidence in domains with high ambiguity and low feedback. The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should directly address the other's point about circularity, self-exemption, or the reliability of meta-cognitive assessment, rather than talking past one another. Conclude with both positions still intact but with the tension between them sharpened—do not resolve the dispute with a cheap compromise.
