Two colleagues in my philosophy-of-mind reading group disagreed about whether Dennett's multiple drafts model genuinely eliminates the Cartesian theater or merely relocates it. (1) The first argued that Dennett dissolves the theater altogether: there is no central screen or spatiotemporal point at which consciousness comes together, only parallel, distributed editorial processes that generate competing narratives, none enjoying privileged access. (2) The second replied that this only pushes the theater back one functional level—what she called an adjudication layer that resolves inter-draft conflict. Even without a literal stage, she said, there remains a functional center where one narrative is selected for reportability, and that selection event plays precisely the theatrical role of rendering experience conscious. (3) The first countered that this smuggles in a homunculus: the adjudication layer is not a place or an observer but a temporal pattern of winner-take-all dynamics across populations. To ask where the winning draft plays, he said, is a category error—like asking where an election happens in a democracy. (4) The second then pressed that without a principled account of why the winning draft acquires authority rather than mere causal dominance, Dennett's model collapses into eliminativism about subjective presence itself. If every draft is equally real and only one is reported through brute competitive suppression, she claimed, then there is no meaningful distinction between being conscious of something and merely hosting a dominant but unexperienced neural representation.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and identify what—if anything—both disputants are missing that would resolve the stalemate.
