I have just been notified that a forthcoming prestige drama will portray my late grandfather—an obscure municipal archivist who avoided all public notice—as having conducted a secret affair with a well-known political figure, a fabrication introduced solely for narrative tension. This personal intrusion has made the philosophical question acute for me: what, if anything, do the dead owe us, and what do we owe them, when their lives are converted into dramatic property? I would like you to compose a satirical modest proposal—approximately 600 words, written in the deadpan, bureaucratic voice of a senior policy advisor to a fictional Ministry of Cultural Representation—that extends the logic of contemporary biopic practice to its reductio ad absurdum. The proposal should establish a national Dramatic Licensing Program under which every deceased citizen is automatically enrolled upon death, with provisions including: a Fictionality Quota permitting up to forty percent invented trauma per hour of screen time; a Posthumous Consent mechanism by which estates may sign away moral objections in exchange for tax incentives; a tiered protection system in which private citizens receive weaker safeguards than public figures; and a Narrative Reparations fund, financed by studios that misrepresent deathbed scenes, but disbursed not to descendants but to the public emotional education budget. Throughout, the bureaucrat must remain entirely in character, never winking at the reader, so that the absurdity of each clause exposes a genuine problem—that consent is impossible for the dead, that emotional authenticity is often used to excuse factual distortion, and that the costs of misrepresentation fall unevenly across lines of class, gender, and historical visibility. Please embed, without naming them, the conceptual distinctions between harm to reputation, corruption of collective historical memory, and damage to descendants' narrative continuity. The piece should be legible to a reader unfamiliar with narrative ethics, yet precise enough that a specialist recognizes the underlying philosophical architecture. Do not cite philosophers, texts, or theories; do not provide a bibliography; and keep the focus on the internal logic of the proposal rather than on any real-world studio or production.
