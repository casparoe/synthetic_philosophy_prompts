I'm a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I've been working through whether the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism can be generalized without collapsing every form of collective decision-making. Here is the argument I have constructed; it seems valid, yet its conclusion is one I cannot accept, and I need to know whether the flaw is a hidden equivocation or whether I must genuinely surrender one of the premises I would prefer to keep.

(1) The separateness of persons objection holds that utilitarian aggregation is defective because it permits harm to one individual to be offset by greater benefits to others, treating the welfare of distinct agents as fungible entries in a single sum rather than respecting each agent as an independent moral unit with non-transferable standing.

(2) If welfare is non-aggregable across persons because each person constitutes an independent moral module—structurally analogous to process isolation in distributed systems, where state changes in one module cannot be cancelled or overwritten by state changes in another—then any collective procedure that compares, trades, or balances values across these agent-boundaries violates the same principle.

(3) Democratic governance, jury deliberation, and centralized resource allocation all operate by aggregating inputs—preferences, judgments, or needs—drawn from distinct persons, and they routinely authorize outcomes that impose costs on some individuals for the benefit of others.

(4) These collective procedures therefore treat inputs from separate agents as fungible contributions to a shared calculus, which is structurally identical to the utilitarian welfare aggregation that premise (1) condemns.

(5) So if the separateness objection is sound, democratic governance, jury verdicts, and centralized planning are all morally impermissible.

(6) But democratic governance and jury verdicts are not all morally impermissible.

(7) Therefore the separateness objection is unsound.

I am inclined to preserve both (1) and (6), which suggests I should reject either (2) or (4). Yet (2) looks like a direct extension of the modularity idea embedded in (1), and (4) looks like an accurate structural description of how collective procedures work. Where exactly does the argument slip? Is there a scope shift or an equivocation between aggregating welfare and aggregating procedural inputs that allows me to keep (1) without paying the price in (6)? Or does the argument expose that (1) implicitly relies on a stronger claim—perhaps that any cross-personal normative comparison is illegitimate—which I would then have to abandon?

Please identify the specific step or premise that fails, explain why it initially looked defensible, and price the alternatives: what I pay if I reject (2), what I pay if I reject (4), and whether there is a middle position—one that blocks utilitarian substitutability of welfare without treating democratic aggregation as the same kind of moral error. If the argument is actually valid and I must choose between giving up (1) or (6), tell me which surrender is more philosophically defensible and what framework remains—perhaps one that permits cross-personal comparison within non-utilitarian constraints—once the choice is made.
