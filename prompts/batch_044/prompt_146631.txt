My philosophy tutor's been grading essays on Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. Her verdicts so far on what's a real self (not just legal or metaphorical): a normal adult human, yes; a patient with total retrograde amnesia who lives entirely in the present but keeps reasoning and emotion, yes; an ant colony with emergent problem-solving, no; Apple Inc., no; a fictional character like Anna Karenina, yes inside the fiction but not a real-world self; and a split-brain patient after callosotomy, two separate ones, not one.

Now she's asking about a new case: an LLM that's been running continuously for years with a consistent persona, an autobiograhical backstory it updates and maintains, and users who genuinely predict its behavior using belief and desire talk, but zero consciousness and no body. Would she count it as a center of narrative gravity?

I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rule she's actually running—whether it's biological anchoring, predictive utility of the intentional stance, or coherence of an internal narrative. What's the closest analog among her earlier cases, and if her preceents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle does each choice commit her to? Also, what further case would actually split those rules apart?
