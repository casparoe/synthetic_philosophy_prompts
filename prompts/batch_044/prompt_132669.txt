I have been reflecting lately on whether moral life ought to centre on the elimination of suffering rather than on the promotion of happiness. After encountering some popular writing on the subject, I find myself drawn to suffering-focused ethics, yet I am unsure which formulation of that outlook is genuinely defensible. I should like you to rank the following five positions by their philosophical strength, assigning each a score out of ten and giving a sentence or two of reasoning. First, the classic negative-utilitarian position: reducing suffering is our sole moral obligation, and increasing happiness, while permissible, carries no weight of its own. Second, a weighted prioritarian stance: both suffering and happiness matter, but suffering carries roughly double the moral weight per unit, so resources may flow to happiness when the suffering averted is comparatively trivial. Third, an idea I have been turning over myself, which I call the 'suffering-tax' view: one may pursue happiness or bring new beings into existence, but only on the condition that one simultaneously eliminates at least as much suffering elsewhere, meaning well-being gains are never permitted to outrun unaddressed pain. Fourth, a threshold view that holds only extreme or intense suffering has moral significance, allowing ordinary unhappiness and mild discomfort to be set aside when they compete with projects of joy or human flourishing. Fifth, a strict practical partition: devote every available moral and material resource to the worst suffering until it is eradicated, and only then turn attention to happiness. Please reply in British English, and assess whether my 'suffering-tax' idea is genuinely distinct or whether it collapses into one of the other four. I am particularly interested in where each view fails under pressure, not merely in its intuitive appeal.
