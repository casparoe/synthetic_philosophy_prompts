A colleague in metaphysics summarized Max Black’s sphere argument and its contemporary relevance like this: Black imagines a symmetric universe containing only two iron spheres of identical size and composition, separated by exactly two miles of empty space and nothing else. Because every intrinsic property and every spatial relation of one sphere is duplicated in the other—including being two miles distant from an object of that size—Leibniz’s principle of the identity of indiscernibles forces us to conclude they are one and the same sphere, which is absurd. The thought experiment therefore demonstrates that space cannot be purely relational, that absolute spatial positions exist, and that Leibniz was mistaken. The colleague added that quantum mechanics supplies actual instances of this situation: two identical particles in an exchange-symmetric state share every observable property except for spatiotemporal labeling, so nature itself violates the principle empirically. Is this summary fair? Please separate what is accurate, what is a standard but defensible simplification, and what misrepresents either Black’s argument or the quantum analogy. In particular, address whether the relationalist really has no reply, whether weak discernibility undermines the claim of total indiscernibility, and whether the quantum case actually parallels Black’s spheres. Finally, say what would change your assessment: which specific distinction—if shown to apply differently—would lead you to judge the summary more or less fair, and where is your reasoning here most likely to fail?
