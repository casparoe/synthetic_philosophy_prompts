In my philosophy of mind seminar we have been examining the classic split-brain experiments in which severing the corpus callosum allows the right hemisphere to initiate actions that the left hemisphere then explains with fluent but fabricated narratives. Our instructor emphasized that the genuine philosophical difficulty is not counting minds but specifying what unifies consciousness and agency when one hemisphere possesses information the other lacks, and when the interpreter module produces coherence without access to the true cause.

I want to open my presentation with a joke that captures this precise tension rather than relying on the familiar pop-science trope of logical left versus artistic right brains. Write a joke about split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness in which a patient is placed in an ordinary bureaucratic situation—perhaps applying for a joint loan, signing an informed-consent form, or attending a mediation—where an institution demands a single responsible agent. The punchline should turn on the clinical reality that the left hemisphere's post-hoc rationalization is structurally ignorant of the right hemisphere's actual motives, so that the joke illustrates why narrative unity is not the same as unified agency. Make it sharp enough for an audience familiar with the literature, but do not rely on any obscure technical debate that would be unknown to someone who knows only the standard clinical findings.
