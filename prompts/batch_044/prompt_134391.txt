I do not have any academic training in philosophy, but I have spent considerable time with Wittgenstein’s remarks on family resemblance and language games—specifically his argument that concepts like “game” are not held together by a single essential feature but by a network of overlapping similarities, and that the meaning of a word is determined by the rules of the particular language game in which it is deployed rather than by any private mental image. I have also followed, in a lay capacity, the 2006 decision by the International Astronomical Union in Prague to reclassify Pluto. The IAU adopted three criteria for planethood: orbiting the Sun, being in hydrostatic equilibrium, and having “cleared the neighborhood” around its orbit. Pluto fails the third criterion. The vote came after the discovery of Eris and other large Kuiper Belt objects, which made Pluto’s status as a unique outlier unsustainable. The decision provoked widespread public resistance and was never fully adopted outside professional astronomy.

Please analyze this episode through Wittgenstein’s framework. First, show how “planet” might be understood through family resemblance rather than essential definition: what overlapping features historically linked Pluto to Mercury or Neptune, and precisely where do those similarities break down once Eris and similar bodies enter the picture? Second, treat the IAU vote and its aftermath as a clash between distinct language games—professional astronomical taxonomy, school education, popular culture, and administrative or legal usage—specifying what counts as correct usage within each game and explaining why the IAU’s ruling could not simply overwrite the others. Third, give an honest account of where Wittgenstein’s lens is silent or breaks: does scientific taxonomy genuinely require necessary and sufficient conditions in a way ordinary-language concepts do not? Can family resemblance accommodate an empirical discovery that forces a category to split, or does the Pluto case show that institutional authority can impose an essentialist grammar where none existed before? Finally, tell me what the episode reveals about Wittgenstein’s broader claim—whether it confirms that there is no hidden essence behind our categories, or whether it is an anomaly showing that language games are vulnerable to empirical shock when backed by expert consensus.
