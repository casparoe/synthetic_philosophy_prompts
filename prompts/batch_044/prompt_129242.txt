I'm writing my dissertation on the temporal structure of commemorative narratives in defeated political communities, and in my comparative work across the American Lost Cause, the Masada myth, the Kosovo Field narrative, and British Dunkirk memory I've noticed a recurring rhetorical configuration that I can't place precisely in the existing literature. In each case, the defeat is not narrated as a completed past event—something that happened and was survived—but as a suspended present: the community describes itself not as "the descendants of the defeated" but as "those who continue the unfinished resistance," using present-continuous framing and ritual practices that treat the defeat as an ongoing condition rather than a historical terminus. At the same time, strategic or internal critique of the original cause is systematically blocked by a specific move: any such critique is interpreted not as legitimate historical analysis but as an alliance with the enemy's victory conditions, because the enemy's victory is attributed exclusively to material overwhelming or internal betrayal, never to errors within the cause itself. This produces a peculiar epistemic closure in which questions about strategic soundness become morally forbidden, while the commemorative practice sustains an open-ended temporal horizon in which the cause remains alive precisely because it was never legitimately defeated.

I know this touches on Lost Cause historiography, studies of victimhood nationalism, and the sociology of collective memory, but what I'm describing feels more specific than any of those: it is not merely that defeat is glorified, nor that a nation sees itself as eternally wronged, but this exact coupling of suspended temporal status (defeat as present-continuous) with a moralized epistemic closure (strategic critique treated as betrayal). Is there an established concept—perhaps in memory studies, political theology, or the sociology of knowledge—that captures this specific pairing? If so, how exactly does it differ from the ordinary "noble defeat" trope in literature, from Schmittian political theology, or from religious eschatology? And if there is no precise term, I need more than a bare label: I need a coinage with sharp boundary conditions, specifically whether the phenomenon requires an actual military defeat or can extend to unacknowledged political failures, and whether it can operate in diaspora or successor-state contexts where the commemorating group no longer holds territorial power. I want to distinguish it cleanly from both standard nationalism and from messianic expectation, since in these four cases the "not-yet-fulfilled" quality is secular and strategic rather than redemptive.
