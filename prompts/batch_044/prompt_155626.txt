Consider this argument, which a colleague insists collapses rational decision theory into arbitrariness.

(1) Rational choice requires selecting an option that is at least as good as every available alternative according to the agent’s preference ordering.

(2) If two options are identical in every preference-relevant property, they are tied; there is no reason internal to the ordering that favors one.

(3) Buridan’s ass faces exactly such a tie between two haystacks.

(4) Because starvation is worse than either stack, the agent must act; rationality therefore demands breaking the tie without an internal reason.

(5) So in symmetric cases, rational action necessarily includes an arbitrary element.

(6) Now observe that any real option can be symmetrically duplicated: place two physically distinct but qualitatively identical copies of the same restaurant, job offer, or treatment in the choice set.

(7) Once duplicated, the choice set contains a Buridan-style tie for every original option.

(8) Therefore, in any realistically complex environment, rational selection requires arbitrary tie-breaking at every decision node.

(9) But if every rational choice contains an unavoidable arbitrary break, then there is no substantive difference between a rational agent and one who reasons until reaching symmetry and then flips a coin; reasoning merely delays the inevitable arbitrariness.

(10) Hence the concept of fully reason-guided rational choice is incoherent: it demands both guidance by reasons and an unreasoned resolution of every tie.

I want to reject (10) and keep both the demand for reason-guidance in (1) and the claim in (5) that symmetry forces some kind of break. I suspect the slide happens between (6) and (8), where duplicating an option is treated as altering the choice problem rather than merely multiplying descriptions of it, but I cannot see exactly how to block the generalization without abandoning (5). Which step is the real fault line—(6)-(8), (1), or (4)—and what does giving it up cost? Does saving rational choice require denying that symmetry ever forces a break, or only denying that duplication creates symmetry where none existed?
