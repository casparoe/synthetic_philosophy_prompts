As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I request the following. Write a sustained satirical parody—approximately 700 words—formatted as a deadpan federal grant proposal submitted by a fictional Institute for Empirical Induction. The proposal seeks substantial funding to construct a global observational network whose explicit purpose is to settle, by direct measurement and statistical analysis, whether emeralds are genuinely green or merely grue. The voice must be that of a sincere, bureaucratically precise scientific applicant; the implied venue is a peer-reviewed funding agency; the target is the empiricist assumption that Goodman's problem of projectibility is fundamentally a deficiency of data rather than of conceptual framing, together with the contemporary tendency to recast philosophical puzzles as large-scale engineering and data-gathering challenges. To keep the treatment away from well-worn ground, inject the framing of adversarial generalization from statistical learning theory into the Research Design section: the applicants propose to train regularized deep neural networks on pre-time-t emerald spectra, treat post-time-t chromatic misclassification as an empirical generalization failure, and propose to correct it by expanding the feature space to include chromatic temporal derivatives—all without recognizing that the selection of those very features already embeds the projectibility judgment they claim to be testing. The satire should land precisely for readers familiar with Goodman's riddle and remain fully legible to those who are not; at no point should the deadpan break, wink, or announce itself as parody. Include standard grant sections—Abstract, Background and Significance, Research Design, Budget Justification, and Broader Impacts. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, reading lists, or requests for further sources. After presenting the proposal, append a brief reflective note of no more than 150 words identifying the specific philosophical argument or observation that would cause you to take the proposal's premise seriously rather than as satire, and stating explicitly where your reasoning is most vulnerable—for instance, whether Goodman's challenge is ultimately about linguistic convention rather than observational inadequacy, or whether a Bayesian account of regularization genuinely dissolves the need for projectible predicates. Write in a formal, polished register consistent with scientific grant writing.
