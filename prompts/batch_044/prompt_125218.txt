In interdisciplinary and institutional contexts I keep seeing a pattern that does not sit cleanly within the standard taxonomy of epistemic injustice. A group has developed its own conceptual framework for articulating a specific harm or structural relation, and its members can deploy that framework with precision and mutual credibility. The difficulty is not that they are disbelieved, nor that they lack the concepts to understand their own experience. Rather, an institutional gatekeeper—a peer-review system, a policy tribunal, or a funding body—insists that the claim be translated into a dominant conceptual scheme before it can be recognized as knowledge or as a legitimate grievance. The translation is not neutral: the dominant scheme lacks the normative or relational distinctions that made the original claim specific, so the translated version sounds like a generic emotional complaint or an underspecified welfare claim. The group is then evaluated on that flattened version and found lacking, while the misrepresentation is treated as the failure of the original claim rather than of the translation. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration—the compelled translation of an already-articulated, group-specific epistemic framework into a dominant one that strips its structural dimensions, followed by the dismissal of the misrepresented result? If so, how precisely does it differ from hermeneutical injustice, from contributory injustice, and from ordinary epistemic exclusion? If not, can you propose a term, define its core conditions, and specify the boundary cases that would distinguish it from benign translation for the sake of mutual understanding?
