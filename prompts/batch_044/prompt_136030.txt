Write the opening remarks (about eight minutes, roughly 1,100 words) for the annual memorial lecture honoring Dr. Lina Vasquez, a philosopher of science who spent her career arguing that Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is best understood not as a rejection of observed regularity but as the claim that regularity itself—what Al-Ghazālī terms al-ʿāda, or custom—rests on direct mediation rather than on intrinsic causal powers residing in created things. The audience is split evenly: half are specialists in medieval Islamic philosophy and philosophy of religion who know the Tahāfut literature well; the other half are bench scientists—mostly molecular biologists and condensed-matter physicists—who have never read Al-Ghazālī and will not tolerate untranslated jargon or theological presuppositions. Explain why this distinction matters for contemporary practice: specifically, for how working scientists should interpret the claim that "the same cause produces the same effect" in light of replication failures, and whether treating nature as uniform commits one to metaphysical necessity or only to stable custom. The philosophical framework must do structural work in the speech. Because occasionalism denies that fire, reagents, or cell lines carry their effects within themselves—whether mediated theologically or secularized as the absence of intrinsic modal guarantees—you must not fall back on platitudes like "hard work necessarily produces success," "the laws of nature guarantee replication," or "science progresses inevitably." Instead, construct an address that offers the scientists genuine encouragement and a coherent account of experimental responsibility without smuggling in language of necessary connection. For the philosophers present, include one precise technical point about how the occasionalist distinction between logical impossibility and customary impossibility maps onto current debates about the modal status of natural laws, and make it accessible to the scientists by tying it to a concrete laboratory scenario—for instance, an unanticipated replication failure that no adjustment of background conditions explains. Do not provide a bibliography, do not cite page numbers, and do not ask the audience to read Al-Ghazālī in translation; the speech must stand entirely on its own.
