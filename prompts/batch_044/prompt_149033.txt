I just finished a long argument with a philosopher friend about the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. She summarized it as follows. The problem targets theories that analyze value in terms of reasons, particularly the view that something is valuable if and only if there are sufficient object-given reasons to have a pro-attitude toward it. The familiar challenge is that there seem to be state-given reasons for any attitude — being paid to admire a painting, or wanting to avoid embarrassment — that do not bear on the object’s merit. To save the analysis, the theorist must exclude these wrong-kind reasons, but she argued that doing so requires an independent criterion of merit that already presupposes value, making the account circular. She then extended the point: if pragmatic reasons for belief are also state-given, then any reasons-first account of epistemic justification collapses in the same way, leaving both value and justification as primitive normative properties. Is this a fair summary of the problem? Which parts correctly represent the structure of the objection, where does she conflate the wrong-kind-reasons challenge with broader anti-reductionist arguments, and does her conclusion — that the circularity forces us to treat value and epistemic norms as primitive — actually follow?
