As a retired physical scientist who has lately turned to philosophy of social choice, I have been working through Amartya Sen’s liberal paradox—not to apply welfare economics in a laboratory, but because its logical architecture reminds me of statistical-mechanical thought experiments that derive contradictions from apparently innocent axioms. The classic formulation involves two individuals, Lewd and Prude, and a single copy of an indecent novel that may be read by Lewd, read by Prude, or left unread. Lewd prefers that Prude be compelled to read it; failing that, he prefers to read it himself; worst of all is leaving it unread. Prude, being prudish, most wants the book destroyed unread; if someone must suffer it, she prefers to suffer herself rather than see Lewd enjoy it; worst is Lewd reading with pleasure. From these rankings, Sen shows that if each person holds sovereign authority over whether they personally read (the liberal condition L), then Lewd’s sovereignty requires that “Lewd reads” rank above “no one reads,” while Prude’s sovereignty requires that “no one reads” rank above “Prude reads.” Transitivity then forces “Lewd reads” above “Prude reads.” Yet the Pareto principle demands the opposite: both individuals prefer “Prude reads” to “Lewd reads,” so unanimous welfare improvement requires ranking “Prude reads” above “Lewd reads.” The two conditions therefore generate a direct social-choice cycle.

At a recent discussion group, two fellow newcomers—let us call them Marlene and David—became stuck on whether this contradiction genuinely undermines liberalism or merely exposes a hidden ambiguity in the Pareto condition. Marlene argued that the paradox is fatal to minimal liberalism: any system that respects personal liberties over self-regarding choices must abandon unanimous welfare improvement, which she takes to be unacceptable because it licenses outcomes that every party regards as inferior. She insisted that the only consistent rescue is to restrict liberal rights to choices that are genuinely self-regarding, but since psychological and moral spillovers make every preference partly interpersonal, such a restriction collapses liberalism into either majoritarian aggregation or paternalistic oversight.

David replied that Marlene conflates two different kinds of preference. He granted that Prude’s ranking of “no one reads” over “Prude reads” is genuinely self-regarding—she cares about her own exposure—but he claimed that her preference for “Prude reads” over “Lewd reads” is moralistic, not self-regarding. If the Pareto principle is applied only to preferences that fall within each individual’s protected domain, he said, the contradiction dissolves: within Prude’s domain her sovereignty holds, within Lewd’s domain his holds, and there is no legitimate global comparison that illegitimately mixes interpersonal moral judgments with personal welfare. Marlene countered that this “domain-partitioned Pareto” is ad hoc: it saves liberalism only by redefining the Pareto principle so narrowly that it no longer captures the intuitive idea that unanimous improvement should matter across the board. David then asked whether she really wants to count Prude’s disgust at Lewd’s enjoyment as a legitimate welfare loss for society, because if she does, she is already smuggling in a comprehensive moral doctrine that liberalism was meant to exclude. They asked me to referee, and I found that I could not settle it alone.

Could you go through their exchange step by step—Marlene’s claim that the paradox is fatal, her proposal to restrict rights to self-regarding choices, David’s domain-partitioned response, Marlene’s charge of ad hocery, and David’s final challenge about smuggling in moral doctrines—and say who is right at each stage, not merely who wins overall? In particular, please assess whether the contradiction survives once preferences are partitioned in David’s way, and whether there is a principled method for distinguishing self-regarding from moralistic preferences in this example without begging the question in favor of either liberalism or Pareto efficiency. I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I simply want a careful, point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as they actually stand.
