A colleague and I have been trying to formalize the internal logic of luck egalitarianism and have constructed a chain that seems to undermine the view from within. I want to keep the core intuition that people should not suffer for circumstances outside their control, and I also want to preserve the idea that genuinely responsible choices can ground deserved inequality. Yet the argument below drives me toward strict equality of outcome, which I do not want to accept. I need help seeing which link breaks.

(1) Luck egalitarianism holds that distributive justice requires neutralizing the effects of brute luck—circumstances for which an agent is not responsible—while permitting inequalities that trace to option luck, or responsible choice.

(2) For an outcome to genuinely trace to option luck rather than brute luck, the agent’s choice must satisfy what I will call the basal autonomy condition: the deliberative capacities, preferences, and values that actually produced the choice must not themselves be entirely the product of unchosen factors such as genetic endowment, childhood environment, and social position.

(3) But every actual human choice is causally embedded in a chain that begins with precisely those unchosen factors. The psychological profile that leads someone to work diligently, take entrepreneurial risks, or prefer leisure over labor is shaped by genetics and upbringing—both canonical instances of brute luck.

(4) Therefore, no actual choice satisfies the basal autonomy condition: in every real case, the apparent “choice” is ultimately the causal product of brute luck.

(5) If so, then luck egalitarianism’s own criterion for responsibility is never actually met. Every inequality, including those that appear to result from choice, traces back at root to brute luck.

(6) Consequently, justice requires neutralizing all inequalities, collapsing luck egalitarianism into strict equality of outcome.

(7) Yet luck egalitarians explicitly reject strict equality of outcome, and the basal autonomy condition seemed necessary precisely to avoid that collapse.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) or the intuition behind (2), but I also refuse to accept (6). Is the error a hidden equivocation between causal origin and normative responsibility? Or does (2) demand an implausibly strong form of autonomy that luck egalitarianism never needed in the first place? If I weaken (2), can I still maintain a meaningful distinction between brute and option luck, or does the distinction dissolve?
