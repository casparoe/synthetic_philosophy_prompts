I'm preparing a session for my graduate seminar on democratic deliberation, and I've become absorbed in the advisory rulings issued by the chair of our university's public-reason working group on the admissibility of religious arguments in formal legislative testimony. Over the past year she has delivered four consistent judgments: (i) a representative who defended a gambling prohibition solely by citing the Quranic prohibition of maysir, with no secular analog offered, was ruled inadmissible; (ii) when the same representative later defended an identically scoped prohibition but paired the citation with an independent public-health analysis of addiction that was sufficient on its own, the testimony was ruled admissible, with a note that the religious framing was redundant but not disqualifying; (iii) a council member who sought a zoning exemption for religious schools by invoking ecclesiastical authority and then adding a constitutional free-exercise claim, but explicitly dismissed that secular claim as "merely strategic," was ruled inadmissible because the secular reason was decorative rather than genuinely justificatory; and (iv) a purely secular epidemiological argument for mandatory vaccination was admitted without controversy. These rulings strike me as jointly compatible with at least three implicit rules: a strict full-substitution rule (religious reasons are permissible only when fully replaced by secular reasons of identical scope and normative force); a directional-convergence rule (religious reasons are permissible if accompanied by secular reasons that have independent weight and point in the same direction, even if they do not replicate the full scope of the religious commitment); and a Habermasian translation-proviso rule (religious citizens may present approximate secular renderings of their commitments, with imperfect translation expected, provided they do not demand that non-believers accept the theological source).

The new proposal before the group involves a state representative advocating a universal, unconditional basic income of $1,200 per month, grounded in the Buddhist practice of dana—giving without expectation of return or merit-based assessment. She accompanies this with empirical studies on automation-driven job displacement and pilot-program data showing improved mental-health outcomes, but she explicitly states that those studies support only a targeted, means-tested supplement of roughly $400 for displaced workers, and that "without the vision of dana, we would settle for far less than justice demands." She insists that citizens need not accept Buddhist metaphysics to support the bill, because the empirical case shows "we are already on the path," yet she makes clear that the religious vision determines the policy's universal and generous scope, not merely her personal motivation for endorsing it. I want you to predict the chair's verdict—admissible, inadmissible, or admissible only within a narrower secular scope—identify which of the three rules above best explains her track record, specify the closest analog among her four prior rulings, and name the specific further case that would most sharply distinguish the remaining candidates. I am not asking for a survey of every position in the literature; take a committed stance, defend it with reasons drawn from the pattern of her rulings, and be explicit about what principle she would be adopting whichever way she goes. If your prediction is that the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say so plainly and state exactly what each option would commit her to going forward.
