As a physicist who retired from experimental work several years ago and has since been auditing courses in philosophy of science and decision theory, I have become preoccupied with the St. Petersburg paradox not as a recreational puzzle but as a limit case for how formal mathematical structures relate to physical possibility. In our seminar we examined the standard formulation—a fair coin tossed repeatedly until the first heads appears, with the payout doubling at each successive round—and observed how the geometric decay of probabilities is exactly canceled by the exponential growth of rewards, producing a divergent expectation sum whose terms are all equal to one. I am preparing a brief contribution for an informal gathering of colleagues, and I would like to present a poem that treats this wager not as a betting game but as a cosmological thought experiment about infinity and constraint.

Specifically, I request a villanelle of nineteen lines in the traditional form—five tercets in ABA rhyme and a closing quatrain in ABAA—with two refrains that alternate through the stanzas. The first refrain should speak in the voice of the formalist, insisting that rational preference must honor the unbounded expectation regardless of intuition; the second should speak in the voice of the physicalist, objecting that no actual causal chain, casino, or bounded region of spacetime could realize payouts that outstrip the total energy or information capacity available in any real world. Between these refrains, the tercets should trace the iterative structure of the game itself—the first tail, the second, the third—so that the poem's repetition mirrors the paradox's infinite regress, while the rhyme scheme binds each epoch back to the same two unyielding positions. The closing couplet should place the two refrains adjacent without reconciliation, leaving intact the tension between mathematical infinity and physical finitude that concluded our discussion. The poem should be technically accurate—recognizably alluding to the divergence of the expectation series, to bounded-utility resolutions, and to physical-impossibility objections—without becoming didactic, and it should avoid resolving the paradox in either direction.
