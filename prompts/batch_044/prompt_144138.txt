I'm preparing a briefing for a regional education board that must decide whether to consolidate two neighboring school districts with sharply different demographic compositions. Our preliminary evaluation shows that District A outperforms District B within every measurable subgroup—defined by income bracket, primary-language status, and special-education designation—yet District B exhibits a higher aggregate outcome across all students combined. The board chair suspects statistical manipulation, but I believe we are facing a variant of Simpson's paradox that is complicated by hierarchical structure: each district contains nested municipalities that implemented distinct curriculum reforms, which means our stratification variables are partially endogenous to the very policy intervention being evaluated. Before I present recommendations, I need to understand the deeper conceptual stakes of aggregation failure in institutional contexts.

Please describe the most important implications of Simpson's paradox for how decision-makers should interpret aggregated causal claims in policy evaluation—particularly addressing whether rational aggregation requires explicit weighting by subgroup size, whether the paradox undermines the legitimacy of district-level accountability metrics when subgroups are policy-selected, and how a responsible administrator should proceed when the reversal emerges not from a simple binary split but from multi-level clustering with endogenous stratification. I am not looking for a reminder merely to disaggregate the data; rather, I want principled guidance on whether the paradox reveals a genuine failure of rational aggregation or an artifact of misspecified causal structure, and what this distinction means for democratic accountability when statistics are reported at different geographic scales.
