I'm writing a dissertation chapter on what I've begun calling the auditability paradox in democratic legitimacy theory, and I have constructed a chain of reasoning that seems to dissolve the normative distinction between German-style informational self-determination, American administrative openness, and Chinese social-credit visibility into a single continuum of transparency requirements. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Democratic legitimacy requires that the exercise of state coercion be justifiable to each affected citizen through reasons they could not reasonably reject. (2) In contexts of algorithmic and predictive governance, such justifiability requires empirical verification: citizens or their representatives must be able to audit whether state decisions actually track legitimate public reasons rather than arbitrary correlations or hidden biases. (3) Effective auditability requires granular, persistent, and cross-referenced data about citizens' behaviors, social networks, and preferences—without this substrate, verification is impossible and legitimacy collapses into mere assertion. (4) If citizens hold a robust right to informational opacity—systematically concealing data from state collection—then they can block the very auditability required by (2). (5) Therefore, democratic legitimacy either demands severe curtailment of privacy rights or forfeits its claim to accountability; there is no stable middle position. (6) Applying this consistently, German constitutional resistance to data retention, American norms of administrative openness, and Chinese social-credit architectures all serve the same master—legitimacy through transparency—differing only in the efficiency and scale of data aggregation, not in normative kind. (7) This proves too much: it implies that any meaningful privacy right is democratically illegitimate and that resisting total visibility is merely a preference for inefficient governance.

I feel the fracture lies somewhere between (3) and (5), perhaps in a hidden slide from auditing institutional rules to auditing individual citizens, but I cannot isolate the precise step. Which premise should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? In particular, can I preserve both (1) and a substantial right to opacity without collapsing into (6), or does maintaining that pair force me into an unstable "conditional transparency" that ultimately resolves into one of the three regimes? Please indicate what specific argument or empirical consideration would change your verdict, and identify the point in this chain where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
