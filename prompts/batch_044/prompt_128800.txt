As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I recently read a summary of Bernard Williams's critique of utilitarianism that combines his integrity objection with what he called the problem of one thought too many. The summary states: "Williams held that utilitarianism forces agents to treat their deepest commitments—what he terms ground projects—as fungible entries in a global utility calculus. Should the numbers favor abandoning such a project to maximize aggregate welfare, morality demands that sacrifice, which severs the agent from the very commitments that give life meaning. The one thought too many case illustrates the same defect at smaller scale: a husband rescuing his wife from drowning should not interpose any moral computation—such as weighing spousal duty against the greater number of strangers he might save—between his love and his action; that intermediate justification is precisely the corruption. Taken together, these arguments prove that utilitarianism is psychologically unrealizable, because any agent with intact human attachments would automatically reject such a calculus, and therefore the theory is refuted by its incompatibility with ordinary psychology."

The summary sounds mostly right to my untrained ear, but I suspect it may conflate two arguments that Williams kept separate, and may convert a normative claim about alienation into a descriptive claim about psychological impossibility. Could you evaluate the summary part by part? Specifically: which sentences are accurate representations of Williams's view, which subtly misstate his position—for instance by presenting the integrity objection and the one thought too many point as the same argument, by attributing to him a claim about psychological impossibility rather than about moral alienation, or by framing the husband's error as a utilitarian weighing rather than as any interposed moral justification? And does the conclusion—that utilitarianism is refuted because healthy people cannot adopt it—actually follow from the corrected premises? I should also mention that I initially interpreted ground projects through my own professional lens, as persistent state variables in an agent's objective function that can be overridden by a global optimizer; I want to know whether that computational framing captures Williams's concern or misses the specifically moral character of the alienation he described.
