I am assisting with grading for an advanced civics and philosophy seminar at my high school, and I have been asked to evaluate a fellow student’s examination response. The exam prompt was as follows: “Explain Jon Elster’s concept of a Ulysses contract, and then apply it to constitutional precommitment: in what ways is a rigid constitutional amendment analogous to Ulysses binding himself to the mast, and where does the analogy between individual self-binding and collective democratic binding break down? Be specific about whether the rationality conditions for individual precommitment carry over to intergenerational constitutional design.”

The student’s answer is reproduced below in full. Please assign a specific grade out of ten and provide point-by-point comments that justify the score, examining each section for factual accuracy, logical coherence, and fair representation of the relevant theoretical views; avoid substituting a vague overall impression for detailed evaluation.

The answer reads: “The Ulysses contract, as Jon Elster develops it, describes a situation in which an agent binds himself in advance to prevent a future choice he expects will be irrational. In the classic case, Ulysses orders his crew to tie him to the mast and disregard his future commands as the Sirens approach, because he anticipates that his first-order preferences will shift toward danger. Elster treats this as a second-order preference: Ulysses wants to maintain his present course even though he knows he will later want to deviate. Applied to constitutional design, a present democratic majority can enact an entrenched amendment—requiring, for instance, a three-fourths vote to repeal—thereby preventing future temporary majorities from overturning long-term commitments such as carbon-pricing mechanisms or balanced-budget rules. In this respect, the constitutional text functions like the ropes on the mast.

The student then asserts that James Madison, in Federalist No. 51, explicitly characterized this intergenerational binding as a collective Ulysses contract, treating separation of powers only as an auxiliary reinforcement rather than as an independent institutional check. The student further maintains that because constitutional precommitment operates through procedural rules rather than physical coercion, the psychological conditions Elster requires—specifically, that the agent recognize his own prospective irrationality—are automatically satisfied in democratic legislatures whenever an entrenchment clause exists. From this, the student concludes that any constitutional provision that is difficult to reverse is necessarily a rational precommitment by definition, since the very difficulty of reversal proves that the enacting generation intended to bind its successors.”
