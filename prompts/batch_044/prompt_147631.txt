I oversee resource allocation for a cooperative of about thirty households, deciding everything from repair budgets to mediation hours. Until recently I made each call directly, guided by a consistent but unarticulated weighting: immediate harm, collective benefit, whether the requester has faced repeated disadvantage, and whether acting now prevents larger downstream problems.

Because the workload became unsustainable, I decomposed the process into three stages. First, a rotating panel of neighbors scores each request separately on the four priorities using standardized rubrics, with no visibility into the other scores. Second, a weighted formula aggregates the scores. Third, I personally review only the top and bottom deciles, treating the middle band as correctly ranked. To align the panelists with my judgment, I wrote a brief manual summarizing my past decisions and used it for their training.

After six months, routine cases run smoothly, but two patterns worry me. Requests involving overlapping or ambiguous harms—say, a repair that also resolves a long-standing grievance—get split across rubric categories and then discounted by the aggregation, even though in my direct evaluation these interactions often determine the outcome. Meanwhile, some panelists have begun anticipating the algorithm’s preferences and subtly adjusting rubric scores to push borderline cases into the review deciles, not out of corruption, but because they have internalized that the middle band is where decisions go to die.

A colleague argues that decomposing practical judgment necessarily destroys something essential about it, and that manuals and algorithms can no more preserve my values than a checklist can preserve a poem. Another colleague insists the failure is not decomposition itself but insufficient iteration: if I used my decile reviews to update both the rubrics and the panelists’ training, the system should converge on my original values.

I am unsure whether what I am seeing is a fundamental limit of breaking judgment into parts and distilling it through training, or merely an engineering gap I have not yet closed. Should I revert to holistic evaluation, refine the decomposed system further, or keep it but alter my own role within it? And if decomposition genuinely cannot preserve the kind of reasoning I originally applied, what does that imply about scaling practical judgment without losing the values that guided it?
