I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science and social epistemology, and next Thursday I am defending a chapter of my dissertation to our department’s interdisciplinary seminar on knowledge and technology. The audience is a specific mix: four social epistemologists who work on testimony and expertise (they are familiar with the literature on epistemic dependence but tend to treat AI as just another testimonial source), two philosophers of science who care deeply about scientific understanding and explanatory coherence, and one cognitive psychologist who studies cognitive offloading but is skeptical of philosophical alarmism. None of them are computer scientists, and none have a particular investment in defending large language models. My thesis is that sustained use of LLMs for conceptual synthesis—specifically, asking models to reconstruct arguments, compare theoretical positions, or generate explanatory frameworks in philosophy—produces a distinctive epistemic pathology I call synthetic dependence. This is not mere cognitive offloading, like using a calculator or a reference database; it is a restructuring of the user’s epistemic architecture in which the user retains high surface fluency but loses what I term discriminative reconstruction: the capacity to rebuild an argument from its premises, identify where a premise is unsupported, or distinguish between a model’s plausible-sounding reconstruction and the actual dialectical structure of the source literature, all within domains where the user previously possessed working competence. I argue that this form of degradation is invisible to standard measures of understanding because the user can still articulate the position, but it becomes visible in tasks that require adversarial reconstruction—rebuilding an opponent’s argument without scaffolding.
