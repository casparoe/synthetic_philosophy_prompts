In contemporary cognitive-architecture design, practitioners distinguish a high-level symbolic layer—the “careenium,” which formulates rules, goals, and corrective commands—from the substrate-level dynamics they call “simmballs”: the distributed, sub-symbolic processes that actually carry out computation. The institutional practice is to treat the careenium as genuinely pushing the simmballs around. Engineers debug failures by attributing causal power to symbolic errors (“the careenium issued a misaligned constraint, so the simmballs diverged”), and safety protocols are written as if intervening at the symbolic layer can redirect substrate behavior. Yet the same design documents often insist that all causal work is done at the substrate level and that symbolic descriptions are only instrumental conveniences.

What philosophical commitments about mental causation, downward causation, and representational content does that practice actually presuppose? Is the practice philosophically underdetermined, compatible with both a robust downward-causation view and with an epiphenomenalist interpretation in which symbolic talk is only a predictive gloss? And if one replaced the presupposed view with its rival—say, treating the careenium as causally inert—what exactly would have to change in the design norms, in how failures are explained, and in how safety interventions are justified?
