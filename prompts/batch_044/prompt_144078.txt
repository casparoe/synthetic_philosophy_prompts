After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend last night about whether moral luck makes moral judgment unfair, I wrote down the package of views I was trying to defend, and I want to know whether it actually holds together. Here is where I stand. First, I accept resultant moral luck in its classic form: two agents who are equally negligent can deserve different moral assessments solely because one happens to cause harm and the other does not, and I take this difference to be substantive rather than merely epistemic. Second, I think the control principle should be replaced by what I call an epistemic-risk principle: an agent is responsible for an outcome only if she could have reasonably foreseen the risk and failed to take adequate precautions, but once that condition is satisfied, the actual occurrence of harm may legitimately amplify the severity of blame without altering the basic attribution of responsibility. Third, I hold that legal punishment ought to be purely ex ante and risk-based, while moral assessment should be hybrid—ex ante for the attribution of responsibility, ex post for its severity—and I deny that this hybrid structure makes morality unfair in the way critics of moral luck suppose. Fourth, I think collective agents such as states and institutions can bear moral responsibility in a genuinely non-reductive way, which entails that they can be subject to collective resultant moral luck: a government that adopts the same risky policy as another but happens to trigger a famine bears greater moral blame. Fifth, I deny strong circumstantial moral luck—being born into corruption or poverty explains moral failure without excusing it—while I accept constitutive moral luck regarding the formation of character. Sixth, I maintain that evolutionary debunking undermines libertarian free-will intuitions but not moral-luck intuitions, on the grounds that the latter function as social-coordination heuristics rather than metaphysical commitments, and I think this asymmetry is defensible.

Please audit these commitments: go through them and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe each tension is. For every genuine tension, specify the cheapest revision that would resolve it and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package. Then rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and tell me which single change to one commitment would clean up the largest number of problems. Please respond entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting devices—write it as one would write a formal letter or a long email. Do not ask clarifying questions; treat the commitments above as fixed and reason from them directly.
