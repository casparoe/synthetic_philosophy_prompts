I help manage a small regenerative farm, and this growing season we are facing a destructive infestation by a root-boring moth whose larvae have already ruined a significant portion of our carrot and parsnip beds. After consulting with agricultural specialists, we have identified two certified-organic interventions that could save the remaining crop. The first is a pheromone disruption system: synthetic mating signals are dispersed across the field, which prevents reproduction and causes the local population to collapse over two generations without direct toxicity to the insects themselves. The second is a botanical pyrethrin spray derived from chrysanthemums, which kills larvae on contact but also harms beneficial pollinators and soil organisms in the vicinity. I have found myself strongly preferring the spray, despite recognizing that the pheromone system avoids direct poisoning and that the botanical spray creates broader ecological damage.

There is something about deliberately engineering reproductive failure—preventing lives from being lived—that disturbs me more than killing larvae that already exist, even though I understand that both approaches eliminate the population. A fellow cooperative member insists I am being misled by visibility: reproductive suppression is merely distributed killing over time, and my discomfort reflects an irrational bias toward immediate, observable harm rather than diffuse structural effects. I am trying to understand whether my intuition tracks a defensible moral distinction—perhaps between harming an existing sentient being and preventing potential beings from coming into existence, or between direct and indirect causation of suffering—or whether it is an unreliable emotional preference that should yield to aggregate welfare and ecosystem considerations. The moths are insects with demonstrable nociceptive capacity, and I do not wish to dismiss that hastily. Could you help me map the philosophical structure of this choice, applying relevant ethical distinctions without reducing the problem to a simple cost-benefit calculation?
