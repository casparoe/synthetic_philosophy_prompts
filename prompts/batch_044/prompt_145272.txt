I've been thinking about the idea that every representational system — whether its formal logic, a programming langauge, or a mind trying to model itself — has to bottom out in an 'inviolate level' that the system cant fully represent from inside. Like in Escher's Drawing Hands, the hands draw each other but theres still paper underneath; if you try to make the paper part of the drawing, you either get a paradox or you just pushed the inviolate level back one step. I'm convinced this isnt just a visual trick — its a structural feature: any system with genuine self-reference has to leave some substrate unmodeled, otherwise you collapse into a strange loop with no ground.

What would actually count against that? Not just 'heres a system that models itself,' but specifically which kinds of formal results, which architectures, or which conceptual moves would show that the inviolate level can be dissolved rather than relocated? I'm especially interested in whether there are cases where the 'paper' genuinely gets incorporated without paradox — maybe in certain self-amending logics or in distributed representations where theres no single substrate — and whether defenders of the inviolate-level view would concede those as counterexamples or just say the level got pushed somewhere else. Rank the defeaters: which ones are fatal, which are just awkward relocations, and is there any risk this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability?
