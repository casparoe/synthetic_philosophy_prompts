Since my grandmother’s death three months ago, I have been trying to produce an English version of a short Renaissance French lyric — something in the manner of Clément Marot — to include in her memorial program. I attempted to preserve the original rhyme scheme and syllabic meter, but each version that kept the form seemed to flatten the intimate address, while versions that captured the intimacy lost the music entirely. A friend, seeing my frustration, offered me the following argument, which he said explains why my difficulty is not a failure of craft but an inevitability built into the nature of poetry itself.

(1) The aesthetic identity of a lyric poem is constituted entirely by its formal properties — its rhyme scheme, meter, syllable count, and the acoustic texture of its vowels and consonants — rather than by any propositional content or emotional effect it may produce.

(2) If formal properties are identity-constitutive, then altering any of them creates a different aesthetic object, not a version or rendering of the original.

(3) Translation between languages with different phonologies, stress patterns, and syntactic structures necessarily alters every formal property: rhyme sounds depend on phoneme inventories that do not overlap, syllabic meter is unrecoverable in stress-based English, and acoustic texture is language-specific.

(4) Therefore, every interlingual rendering of a poem is, strictly speaking, a different poem; the term "translation" for poetry is a misnomer for an original work inspired by a foreign source.

(5) Yet the practice of literary translation — including the kind of multi-version exploration Douglas Hofstadter undertakes in Le Ton beau de Marot (1997), where many translators attempt the same source text under varying formal constraints — treats these renderings as translations, evaluating them for fidelity and poetic success relative to the original.

(6) If (4) is correct, this practice is conceptually incoherent: there is nothing for a translator to be faithful to, because the formal object that defines the poem’s identity is necessarily destroyed in the act of translation.

(7) The incoherence extends further. It implies that translated poems should not be evaluated against their sources, that anthologies ought to classify all verse translations as original compositions, and that a translator’s claim to have preserved the rhyme is necessarily false — not merely difficult, but conceptually impossible.

(8) This seems to prove too much. It dissolves the very distinction between translation and adaptation that translators, critics, and readers rely upon, and it would require us to treat Hofstadter’s versions not as competing translations but as unrelated poems that happen to share a theme.

Which step should I reject? Is the error in treating formal properties as identity-constitutive (1), or in assuming that formal change entails the loss of identity (2)? Or is there a hidden slide between "altering form" and "destroying identity" in step (3)? If I reject (1) in order to save the coherence of translation practice, what do I then say to the intuition that the poem I loved — its particular music, its precise weight — is not really present in the English version I am writing? And if I accept (4), is there any meaningful sense in which I am translating my grandmother’s poem rather than writing a new one inspired by it?
