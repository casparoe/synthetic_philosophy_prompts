I was trying to sketch an essay for a seminar on historical ontology (bit pretentious, I know) about whether history is genuinely path-dependent or if we're just mislabelling convergence. My working title is 'Basin-lock versus path-lock: why macro-outcomes feel inevitable in hindsight.'

The core move is distinguishing two mechanisms people conflate under 'path dependence'. First, the usual story: small early differences get amplified by increasing returns, so the QWERTY layout or railway gauge persists even if suboptimal. I call that path-lock. Second, a mechanism I want to call 'basin-lock': a social system has multiple redundant routes to the same functional equilibrium, so even if you rewind and perturb the initial conditions, the system wanders into the same broad institutional shape—not because it traced the same path, but because the attractor is deep. I think this explains why democratic transitions in the 20th century happened at wildly different speeds and via different crises, yet ended up with remarkably similar parliamentary architectures in Western Europe and parts of Latin America. It's not that they copied each other or that one initial election locked them in; it's that the 'democratic basin' pulls diverse trajectories toward a narrow range of constitutional forms.

I also want to argue that historians overstate path-lock because they suffer from what I'd call 'retrospective path painting'—once an outcome is visible, we draw a straight line back through the noise and declare every intermediate step necessary. But if the basin is deep, many intermediate steps were actually substitutible. The niche bit I'm injecting is a comparison to 'equifinality' in systems theory, but applied to political development rather than biology. I don't want to lean too hard on formal modelling since I can't do the maths well, but I want the conceptual distinction to do work.
