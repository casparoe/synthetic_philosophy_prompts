As a novelist currently developing a long-form work that traces how psychiatric classifications are inhabited rather than merely applied, I wish to submit a proposal for a six-month interdisciplinary research fellowship. The central question is whether first-person fictional narratives—novels in which characters actively negotiate psychiatric diagnostic categories—generate a form of looping effect that is structurally distinct from the clinical and institutional loops Ian Hacking describes. Existing accounts of looping effects concentrate on feedback between classifiers and the classified within medical, legal, or bureaucratic settings; they leave open whether aesthetic representation, mediated through readers' empathetic identification with fictional subjects, can constitute an independent loop that reshapes the self-understanding of real individuals who recognise themselves in the depiction, and whether this loop subsequently feeds back into clinical or popular discourse about the category itself.

My approach will be conceptual and comparative rather than empirical. I will select three literary texts that depict a specific diagnostic category both before and after a major revision to that category's clinical criteria, conducting close readings that isolate how narrative framing anticipates, resists, or reconfigures the diagnostic logic. Rather than conducting patient interviews or archival clinical research—the obvious alternatives, which would exceed both the fellowship's resources and my methodological training—I will combine philosophical analysis of narrative identity with Hacking's framework of human kinds, treating fiction as a controlled representational space in which looping mechanisms can be observed without institutional coercion.

A successful result would yield a preliminary typology of narrative looping: conditions under which fiction amplifies existing clinical loops, conditions under which it produces counter-loops that destabilise diagnostic coherence, and conditions under which it remains aesthetically inert with respect to classification. Failure would consist in demonstrating that any observed representational effects reduce entirely to institutional mediation, leaving no distinct aesthetic loop worthy of separate analysis.

The scope is deliberately limited: three texts, a conceptual framework drawn from existing philosophy of psychiatry, and no original clinical data collection. I am aware that this restricts generalisability and that citation of specific editions and clinical revisions will need verification; I will supply all references myself. Please present the proposal in British English, state the question with sufficient precision that it admits a determinate answer, explain why this conceptual-comparative angle is preferable to empirical survey methods for a project of this duration, specify what would count as progress versus failure, and include an honest assessment of the risks that might render the project too ambitious for six months.
