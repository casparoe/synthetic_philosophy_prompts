Mass-casualty triage protocols assign treatment priority by survival probability and categorically prohibit sacrificing one stable patient to harvest resources for several critically injured ones, even when the aggregate survival count favors the sacrifice. What conception of the separateness of persons does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it treat individual lives as non-interchangeable sources of value, or is it only a risk-averse heuristic? Distinguish what the protocol strictly requires philosophically from what merely fits it well, assess whether the practice remains consistent with an aggregative welfare calculus that regards personal boundaries as morally transparent, and specify what elements of triage design and legal immunity rules would have to change if that aggregative view replaced the implicit one.
