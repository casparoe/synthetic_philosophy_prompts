I write for my secondary school's philosophy review, where a recurring debate about anthropic reasoning has turned on the maxim "reason as though you were drawn at random from all observers." One contributor invokes it to claim that our cosmic epoch should not strike us as surprising; another deploys the same line to argue that privileging present human welfare over potential future artificial minds is already a form of selection bias, and therefore presumptuous. I suspect these appeals are not the same principle—some versions sound like self-sampling, others like self-indication, and others like what is called the presumptuous philosopher objection—but I lack the vocabulary to separate them precisely. Could you disambiguate the slogan into its distinct precise theses, arranged as a numbered menu? For each entry, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or likely what the speaker actually intended; then specify exactly which readings support the argument about cosmic surprise, which support the argument about weighting artificial observers, and which do neither. Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list—only the conceptual map.
