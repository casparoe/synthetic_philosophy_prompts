I teach an upper-level seminar in political philosophy and public memory, meeting with about sixteen undergraduates for a single ninety-minute session. We have been working through the idea that commemorative ethics is not one problem but a cluster of distinct obligations—expressive, institutional, and spatial—and I want the students to feel that fragmentation in their bones rather than receive it as a taxonomy. To that end, please design a structured, in-class deliberation centered on the fictional municipality of Veridian, which has just inherited a legacy problem: its central square holds a bronze statue of Elias Vance, the town’s founder, who ended a regional famine through centralized food distribution but also instituted a system of coerced labor that historians now classify as punitive; adjacent to the square are three streets—Vance Way, Marlowe Street (named for a later dissident associate), and the neutral Old Canal. A recent municipal ordinance permits what the town calls interpretive overlay—the addition of contextual plaques, counter-sculptures, or landscape alterations—without requiring removal of the original artifact. The activity should divide the class into four groups of four: a Civic Continuity committee (bound by the hidden constraint that at least one physical reference to Vance must remain in the square, since they believe narrative rupture damages collective identity), a Moral Abolition committee (bound by the hidden constraint that no artifact may stand without explicit moral condemnation visible at the site), a Palimpsestic Design committee (tasked with proposing an overlay that keeps the original visible but transforms its contextual meaning), and an unaffiliated Residents’ Jury (who hear ten-minute presentations, ask clarifying questions, and cast a secret ranked-choice vote justified not in philosophical jargon but in terms of what kind of town Veridian should be). The committees should have thirty minutes to prepare, followed by presentations, jury deliberation, and a vote that produces an official recommendation—which the facilitator then reveals was overruled by the fictional town council in favor of an option no committee proposed.

The rules and materials should require no real money but should include small, real stakes: for instance, each committee receives a limited budget of political capital points that they must allocate across competing demands (preservation costs, design costs, legal defense against lawsuits from descendants), and the jury’s vote awards or deducts points from the committees’ final scores, so that strategic negotiation actually matters. Crucially, the debate should be structured so that the committees focus almost entirely on the statue, ignoring the street grid—until the debrief, when the facilitator suddenly asks them to apply the same reasoning to Vance Way versus Marlowe Street. The students should discover for themselves that the statue operates in an honorific register (standing as praise) while the street name operates in an indexical register (marking historical presence without necessarily endorsing it), and that this distinction makes their unified keep-or-remove framework collapse. The debrief should map each committee’s strategic moves onto these conceptual distinctions—expressive versus institutional commemoration, retrospective honor versus prospective anchoring—and should include an honest forecast of failure: the Palimpsestic group will likely be dismissed as avoiding the hard choice, the jury will conflate emotional disgust with moral reasoning, and without intervention the debate will stall in binary terms. To prevent this, include a hidden crisis card that the facilitator can deploy at the twenty-minute mark (for example, a fictional news report that descendants of Vance’s laborers have filed a lawsuit demanding either full removal or full contextualization, forcing the committees to specify exactly what contextualization entails). Finally, be explicit about what this ninety-minute simulation cannot reproduce: the slow, lived phenomenology of walking past a modified monument every day for years—the gradual moral weathering or normalization that determines whether an overlay actually transforms public memory or becomes invisible background. The facilitator must supply that temporal dimension verbally, and should not pretend the classroom vote captures the long-term ethical life of a commemorative object.
