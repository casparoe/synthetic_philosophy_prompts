As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in a systematic way, I am trying to understand the statue-and-clay debate not by memorizing school names but by seeing which combinations of commitments actually cohere. To that end, please construct a diagnostic taxonomy—a branching decision tree, presentable in text form—for the problem of material constitution. Do not organize it around a list of philosophers; organize it around answers to concrete cases, then locate the named positions at the leaves.

Use this constructed diagnostic scenario rather than the static bronze statue: imagine an artifact, call it the Work, fabricated from a porous ceramic matrix embedded with a metabolizing biofilm that continuously replaces its mineral substrate atom-by-atom, while an automated feedback mechanism preserves the macroscopic shape and functional role against thermal stress. After total atomic turnover, the spatial boundary and causal history remain intact, yet there was never a moment when the original matter and its replacements occupied the same region simultaneously.

The tree should have the following structure and constraints:

1. Root node (phrased as a yes/no question answerable without philosophical labels): Can an object survive the complete replacement of all its constituent matter if its form and causal-functional loop are preserved continuously by an active process?

2. Second-level node: At any given time, can the Work and the underlying ceramic-biofilm aggregate be two numerically distinct objects that occupy exactly the same region but differ in their modal properties—for example, one could survive crushing into powder and the other could not?

3. Third-level node: Is persistence through such change best analyzed as the endurance of a single three-dimensional entity that gains and loses parts over time, or as the existence of a four-dimensional spatiotemporal “worm” composed of instantaneous temporal parts?

4. Fourth-level node: Is the boundary that distinguishes the Work from its surroundings determined by a real, mind-independent fact—perhaps tied to causal cohesion or functional integration—or is it a conventional projection with no ontological weight?

Place these named positions at the appropriate leaves: mereological essentialism; the constitution view (coincident objects); four-dimensionalism or perdurantism; artifact-eliminativism or conventionalism about boundaries; and a functional-continuity view that denies both strict material essentialism and coincidence, holding that survival requires only the maintenance of the specific causal-functional loop, regardless of whether the substrate counts as a separate object.

For every combination of answers that does not correspond to one of these five leaves, mark the node “unoccupied” and give a one-sentence diagnosis: is the combination conceptually incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored?

Additionally, flag explicitly any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate—indicate which further distinction, if any, would be needed to pull them apart, and do not paper over the ambiguity.

Keep the audience in mind: a small reading group of retired scientists with no formal training in philosophy. Use precise language, but prefer physical-science analogies over unexplained jargon. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; the tree should be answerable from conceptual reasoning alone. Limit the tree to no more than six leaves, and present it as a readable flowchart in text form.
