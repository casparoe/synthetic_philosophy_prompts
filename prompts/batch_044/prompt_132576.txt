For an upper-level philosophy seminar on rationality and strategic reasoning — approximately fourteen graduate students, one 75-minute session — design a live, pair-based classroom activity that makes participants feel the backward induction paradox as it operates in a centipede-style interaction. Do not begin with a lecture or name the paradox in advance; the structure of play itself should generate the tension between step-by-step rationality and cooperative continuation.

The mechanism: each pair receives a shared, visible stack of tokens that grows by two each round if both choose "build." In any round, one player may choose "bank," ending the sequence and dividing the stack according to a fixed rule (the banker receives three-fifths, the other receives two-fifths). Before play, each pair draws a sealed "termination envelope" containing a hidden, pair-specific collapse rule — for example, that the stack is automatically forfeited if both build past round three, or that banking in round two nullifies the partner's share — but the envelope remains unopened until round three is underway. The hidden rule applies to reasoning about future rounds but does not alter actions already taken.

Structure the session in three phases: first, fifteen minutes of silent play in which pairs may communicate only through written slips or gestures, with no verbal negotiation; second, twenty minutes of open discussion within pairs, still without revealing envelopes; third, a ten-minute private commitment round in which each student writes, without consultation, whether they will build or bank in the final round, after which envelopes are opened and outcomes resolved.

Provide explicit rules, token values, and a payoff scheme that requires no real money. Include a debrief of about twenty minutes that maps the silent phase, the negotiation phase, and the private commitment onto the formal components of backward induction, the common-knowledge condition for rationality, and the finitude objection that actual agents rarely execute the full inductive chain. Be explicit about what the physical activity can and cannot reproduce: identify the precise feature of perfect backward induction — for instance, an infinite regress of reasoning steps, or fully transparent access to each agent's rationality — that the classroom setup necessarily lacks, and state exactly what the facilitator must supply verbally to complete the analogy.

Finally, predict what will go wrong in the first run — such as premature envelope-opening, confusion between hidden rules and unfairness, or participants treating the token split as a moral rather than strategic problem — and describe how to prevent or recover from each failure. The design should not depend on participants recognizing the centipede game by name, and it must not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or instructions to consult named sources. The prompt should be self-contained for a model without web access.
