I work as an audio conservator for a small museum, digitizing a private collection of field recordings. For the past two months, I've fallen asleep nightly with headphones playing the same batch of uncatalogued interviews, and I've developed recurring dreams in which I'm still in the studio—labeling reels, noting timestamps, repairing splices. Lately, however, the dreams have taken on an unsettling structure: I experience long stretches where I seem to be the tape itself being played back, or one of the recorded voices speaking through the headphones, and then suddenly I'm back at the console without any clear transition. Yesterday morning I woke convinced that I had finally located and repaired reel 17-B, which our inventory lists as missing. In the memory, I can describe the exact scuff on its casing, the splice I made at minute 4:23, and the particular hum of deck three—details that match real equipment and documented events from my waking work. Yet the reel is absent, and my supervisor confirms it hasn't moved since January.

What troubles me isn't only that I might have dreamed the discovery, but that my sleeping mind can weave accurate, waking fragments—the splice time matching a March power outage, the scuff matching damage I observed on reel 12-A—into a false event that feels continuous with my actual labor. Given that I can shift between being the observer, the machine, and the recorded voice without noticing the change, and that I can construct such precise memories without being able to verify them, what reason do I have to believe my present state—writing this to ask for perspective—is waking rather than another phase of the same experience? Practically speaking, should I search the archive for the reel despite my supervisor's assurance, treat the dream-memory as unreliable, or is the real problem that I can no longer identify a stable self that distinguishes working from dreaming?
