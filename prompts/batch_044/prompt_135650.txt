At a philosophy-of-mind reading group, two members—Maya and Leo—debated a gradual-replacement scenario for mind uploading, and they asked me, a bystander, to referee their exchange.

Maya proposed a protocol in which a patient’s neurons are replaced one by one with artificial nodes that preserve every causal and informational relation, with behavioral and self-report tests passing continuously. She argued that because psychological and functional continuity are maintained without interruption, the original person survives with consciousness intact; to deny this is mere substrate chauvinism, since there is no discrete point at which “the lights go out.”

Leo replied that continuous behavioral success is fully compatible with the original phenomenal consciousness having faded out imperceptibly and been replaced by a functionally identical but phenomenally empty system—what he called a slow-motion “zombie relay.” He said Maya begs the question by assuming functional equivalence entails phenomenal equivalence, which is precisely the contested premise in uploading debates. He added that gradual replacement makes verification impossible in principle: lacking any boundary, we can never determine whether consciousness was preserved or replaced, so functional success gives us no epistemic access to phenomenal survival.

Maya countered that Leo’s objection applies equally to ordinary other minds: we never have direct access to anyone’s consciousness except through behavior and report. If his standard undermines uploading, it undermines everyday social cognition too, which is a reductio rather than an insight. Leo conceded the generalization but insisted it reveals a genuine limit of third-person verification rather than licensing the inference that uploading succeeds; absence of detectable zombie replacement is not evidence of absence, especially when the mechanism is engineered to replicate only external patterns.
