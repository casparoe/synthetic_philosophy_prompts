You are grading a philosophy exam. The student was asked: "Avicenna's flying man (al-insān al-ṭāʾir) asks us to imagine a person created fully formed and suspended in void, lacking all sensory contact with body and world. Explain what this thought experiment is designed to establish about self-knowledge, particularly in relation to Avicenna's distinction between direct awareness and representational knowledge. Then assess whether the argument genuinely supports the claim that the soul is a substance metaphysically independent of the body, or whether it establishes only a more limited epistemic point."

Here is the student's answer:

"Avicenna imagines an adult suddenly suspended in empty air, eyes closed, limbs spread so nothing touches, with no sensory input at all. Even so, he would immediately affirm his own existence. This proves that self-awareness is direct and non-inferential—what philosophers call knowledge by presence—rather than being constructed from bodily sensations or internal representations. Because this awareness remains intact when every bodily sensation is removed, the soul must be an immaterial substance whose essence is pure consciousness, with no essential spatial location or physical properties. In this respect the argument parallels Descartes's cogito, though Avicenna uses it for metaphysical rather than epistemological ends. From the independence of self-knowledge from bodily input, Avicenna correctly infers that the rational soul is not a bodily form or entelechy in the Aristotelian sense, and therefore can persist and operate without any bodily organ, including the brain. The argument succeeds in showing that we have immediate access to our own existence, but it moves too quickly from 'I do not need my body to know myself' to 'I do not need my body to exist.' The latter is a separate claim that requires further argument about the conditions for substantial existence."
