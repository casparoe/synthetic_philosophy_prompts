I was discussing with a colleague from my philosophy of action seminar whether distributed collaborative projects—such as an open-source initiative in which different contributors understand the final product in radically different, even incommensurable ways—count as genuine joint actions or merely as coordinated individual efforts. She holds the standard view that collective intentionality requires either a shared we-intention with common mental content (along the lines of Searle or Tuomela) or interlocking personal intentions sustained by mutual belief about that very interlocking (Bratman’s shared cooperative activity). I want to defend the opposing position, but I need it articulated carefully.

Please argue that collective intentionality can be fully constituted without representational convergence among participants. Specifically, defend the claim that a group can act jointly even when its members hold incompatible representations of their collective goal, provided they stand in a normative relation of mutual practical authority—each treating the others’ contributions as generating binding constraints on her own practical reasoning about what to do next, irrespective of whether they share a mental representation of the end-state. Address the Bratman-style objection that without shared content there is only parallel individual action lacking the right interdependence, and address the Gilbert-style objection that joint commitment necessarily presupposes a unified plural subject with a single shared mental content. Ground your argument in the structure of practical reasoning and normative authority rather than in empirical sociology or cognitive science, and explain why representational divergence, rather than undermining jointness, can be structurally accommodated within it. You may invoke familiar distinctions between acting together and merely acting alongside one another, but do not rely on bibliographic references or external case studies.
