In debates over individual contributions to collective harms—whether dietary choices, carbon-intensive travel, or voting in large electorates—I keep encountering an argumentative structure that does not map cleanly onto the familiar repertoire. The standard inefficacy objection holds that one person’s choice makes no discernible difference to the aggregate outcome, so there is no direct instrumental reason to act differently. The usual replies invoke universalization, complicity in a harmful system, or expressive value. Yet I have noticed a distinct move that sits awkwardly between these.

Consider two concrete cases. First, a frequent flyer concedes that her personal emissions are causally negligible to climate change and that her abstaining would not measurably alter warming trajectories. Rather than denying causality, she argues that her flying is “morally decoupled” because she simultaneously funds carbon-offset markets and advocates for systemic regulation; her private consumption is thus justified not by its harmlessness but by its embedding in a broader portfolio of normative engagement. Second, in a discussion of meat consumption, an interlocutor admits that one refusal will not reduce factory-farm output, but claims that eating meat “normalizes” the practice within her social network, while abstaining would disrupt that normalization. She treats this disruptive potential as a genuine reason for action, yet insists it is neither a direct causal effect on animal suffering nor merely symbolic self-expression.

What unites these cases is a shift to what might be called a normative-infrastructure register: the evaluation of an individual act not by its direct causal efficacy, nor by its expressive meaning, but by its role in sustaining or eroding the social expectations, market signals, and institutional feedback loops that make collective solutions possible. Defenders slide into this register to justify behavior that remains causally implicated; critics reply with the inefficacy objection or complicity arguments, and neither side marks the shift explicitly.

Is there an established philosophical concept for this specific evaluative register—assessing individual contributions by their stabilization or destabilization of the preconditions for collective action? How precisely does it differ from appeals to social norms in contractualist or game-theoretic frameworks, from “virtue signaling,” and from the “structural injustice” literature? If no precise term exists, can you coin one, supply a definition with clear boundary conditions, and specify what counts as a misapplication—for instance, attributing normative-infrastructure effects to purely private, unobservable acts, or applying this micro-level evaluation to macro-level policy design?
