I run an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy—about 14 students, one 75-minute session. The announced topic is moral particularism versus generalism, but I want the session to target a specific, somewhat neglected pressure point within that debate: reason holism, or the claim that the very same non-moral feature can operate as a reason with opposite moral valence across contexts without being governed by any further general principle.

Design a structured in-class deliberation—preferably with assigned roles and forced choices rather than open discussion—so that participants discover this holism for themselves before it is named. The rules should be arranged so that students apply identical descriptive predicates to two structurally parallel cases and reach opposite moral verdicts, then face a mechanism that makes them feel the cost of either retaining both verdicts or collapsing them into a hidden generalization.

Specify: the physical setup and materials; the roles or constraints (for example, some participants must defend verdicts using only descriptive, non-moral vocabulary, while others must propose the relevant difference that saves consistency); a phased timeline; and an incentive scheme that rewards coherence but penalizes ad hoc exceptions—using points, voting tokens, or staged public commitments rather than real money.

Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the particularist/generalist debate: where students implicitly appealed to ceteris paribus conditions, where they invented unstated background conditions to save a rule, and where they simply refused to generalize. Include an honest account of what will go wrong the first time—especially the tendency to smuggle in covert general principles—and how to handle it without undermining the exercise.

Finally, be explicit about which feature of particularist reason holism the activity cannot reproduce—whether the phenomenology of direct moral perception, the metaphysical claim about the non-codifiability of reasons, or the rejection of moral explanation altogether—and note that this gap must be filled by the facilitator's follow-up discussion.
