Design a decision scenario—realistic, workplace or policy-based, no science fiction, no demons or perfect predictors, described in one paragraph—that updates Aristotle’s sea-battle problem by making the contingency decision-sensitive: an agent must now authorize an action (e.g., deploying an emergency climate-intervention protocol or initiating a time-critical medical trial) whose rational justification depends on the truth-value of a future-tensed claim about an outcome that is not merely unknown but metaphysically unsettled, and where the very act of authorizing partly determines whether the outcome-space branches. Engineer the case so that a single-actual-future account (sometimes called the “thin red line” view) and an open-future account do not merely disagree about semantics but yield opposite practical recommendations—authorize now versus wait—about the present decision. After the scenario, do the following: (i) state the claim at issue, the decision method, and the relevant nearby worlds or branches; (ii) show the reasoning each account gives for its recommendation, making explicit how bivalence or its absence enters the practical syllogism; (iii) identify the single structural feature that forces the practical divergence and explain why replacing the decision-sensitive contingency with a deterministic causal chain, or with a choice that is causally independent of the branching, collapses the distinction back into a standard risk-analysis problem; (iv) finish with the strongest objection an open-future defender could raise against the single-actual-future recommendation, and note precisely where that reply is weakest. The point of the case is to test whether metaphysical indeterminacy about the future carries practical weight beyond epistemic uncertainty, so do not build in stipulations that assume the answer.
