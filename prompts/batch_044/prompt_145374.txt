Over the past year, following a prolonged episode of professional burnout and subsequent recovery, I have come to take seriously a normative position I have been calling tranquilism: that the only state of affairs with non-instrumental value is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, while the presence of pleasure, achievement, or satisfied desire carries no intrinsic weight. This was not initially a philosophical commitment but a lived observation—that the moments I now regard as genuinely restorative were characterized not by positive excitement but by the temporary cessation of wanting. I am now trying to determine what this view strictly entails, particularly in contexts where conventional well-being frameworks point in opposing directions.

Please describe the most important implications of tranquilism for the following three issues, distinguishing in each case what follows directly from the core thesis versus what depends on supplementary premises. First, regarding life evaluation: does the view recommend pursuing a path of sustained, low-intensity equanimity with minimal external accomplishment over a path that involves significant achievement coupled with periodic stress and desire, assuming both paths eventually reach the same baseline absence of suffering? Second, regarding clinical decision-making: if an intervention could reliably eliminate pathological craving and suffering in a patient but would also suppress affective range and diminish the motivation required for rehabilitation, does tranquilism supply any reason to prefer or reject that intervention? Third, regarding intergenerational ethics: when comparing two possible populations—one in which beings experience intense pleasures inseparably linked to intense cravings, and one in which beings exist in a stable state of desireless contentment with no suffering—does tranquilism entail that the latter is not merely preferable but categorically superior, and can the view consistently maintain that non-existence is worse than such contentment, given that annihilation removes suffering but also removes any subject in whom the absence of craving might be realized?

In your answer, please also identify the boundary conditions at which tranquilism collapses into either pure negative hedonism or into a form of ascetic nihilism, and indicate which of the three issues above would be most decisive in revealing which of those two collapses is actually occurring.
