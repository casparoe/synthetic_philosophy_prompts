A philosophy-of-science blog I came across summarises Richard Jeffrey's rule of conditionalization approximately as follows. Jeffrey's rule governs belief revision when one's evidence is uncertain rather than categorical. If, for instance, you become sixty per cent confident that it rained overnight, you do not condition on the proposition that it rained; instead, you redistribute probabilities across a partition of basic possibilities—rain versus no rain—while leaving all your prior conditional beliefs about other hypotheses intact. The summary claims this shows Jeffrey updating is merely standard Bayesian conditioning applied to a reweighted prior, and that it solves the problem of old evidence because one never conditions on a proposition one does not fully believe. Furthermore, because the rule rigidly preserves every prior conditional probability, it is said to establish a general conservative principle: rational agents ought to revise their credences in underlying states of affairs, but must never alter the conditional relationships they previously accepted. The author illustrates this with a case: a climate scientist receives an expert report that assigns seventy per cent credibility to a high-emission scenario and thirty per cent to a low-emission scenario, and applies Jeffrey's rule to update her beliefs about future temperature rise without changing her model of how emissions relate to temperature.
