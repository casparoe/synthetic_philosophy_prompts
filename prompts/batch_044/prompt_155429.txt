As someone who has recently begun studying evolutionary explanations of social behavior, I find myself returning to a conceptual tension in the units-of-selection debate that introductory treatments usually resolve too quickly. I am not asking which level—gene, individual, or group—is ultimately correct; rather, I want you to construct a concrete, somewhat non-standard evolutionary scenario in which adopting the gene-level framing versus the multilevel-selection framing yields genuinely incompatible conceptual judgments about whether a particular trait counts as adaptive or maladaptive. Specifically, I want the example to involve facultative social behavior—where individuals can switch between solitary and cooperative phenotypes—rather than obligate eusociality, because phenotypic plasticity introduces ambiguity about what counts as the replicator boundary and about the appropriate fitness baseline for measuring costs and benefits. The scenario should illustrate how this divergence is not settled merely by invoking Hamilton’s rule or kin-selection coefficients, but instead reveals a deeper disagreement over whether evolutionary explanations must cite physically discrete replicators and how to treat costs that are partially recoverable through phenotypic switching. Please design the example so that it can be fully analyzed from first principles and general evolutionary concepts, without reference to specific empirical studies or citations, and show precisely where the two frameworks diverge in their assessment of adaptiveness and in the conditions they treat as necessary for selection to operate.
