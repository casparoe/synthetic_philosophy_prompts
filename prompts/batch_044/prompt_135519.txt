Read The Road by Cormac McCarthy through the lens of free-floating rationales — reasons that stay binding even when there's no reasoners left to hold you to them. The father and son keep following moral rules (don't eat people, protect the boy) long after every institution and society has collapsed, and I'm wondering whether the novel treats those as bare survival instincts or as genuinely normative commitments that don't depend on anyone recognizing them. Focus on the cannabal scenes where they reject eating others — is that just prudence, or is there a reason there that doesn't need a social contract to exist? And the ending, where the boy is taken in by that other family after his dad dies: does that undercut the free-floating idea by showing reasons are always relational, or can we read it as some obligations persisting even in a dead world? I want a reading that takes both the book and the philophy seriously — tell me where McCarthy complicates the idea rather than just using it as window dressing.
