I chair the graduate studies committee in my department, and we are currently deciding whether to retain a required seminar taught by a long-tenured colleague whose recent publications have shifted into formal ontology—a domain in which he was never trained—while the junior faculty who actually work in that area insist he has lost touch with both the technical apparatus and the live debates. I find myself caught between two reactions: on one hand, I feel genuinely unsure whether his command of the material is adequate; on the other hand, I worry that my own uncertainty reflects nothing more than my distance from formal ontology, since I was trained in a different tradition altogether. The philosophical difficulty, as I see it, concerns the relationship between my first-order uncertainty about his competence and my second-order awareness of that uncertainty. If I truly knew that he was no longer qualified to teach the course, wouldn't I be in a position to know that I knew it? Instead, what I experience is a kind of unstable oscillation: the more I examine my own judgment, the less certain I become about whether my initial assessment was knowledge or mere intimidation by unfamiliar notation. This seems structurally similar to cases in which one asks whether feeling cold is sufficient for knowing one feels cold, but the institutional stakes are different—here, a wrong vote either undermines a colleague unfairly or permits pedagogical harm. How should I think about whether my second-order doubt defeats my first-order judgment, and does this entail that I ought to recuse myself from voting? More generally, is there a principled distinction between epistemic humility that preserves authority and self-doubt that dissolves it? Please push back if my framing confuses self-knowledge with institutional authority, or if the task as posed cannot be carried out without resolving empirical questions about my colleague's actual abilities.
