As a retired physicist who has recently turned to the philosophy of language and aesthetics, I am preparing an opening piece for a reading group on the problem of preserving form across translation. I should like to commission an original poem for this occasion. Please write a sestina in British English, using the six end-words form, sense, loss, source, echo, and constraint, rotated according to the strict sestina pattern. The poem should take as its subject the central dilemma explored in Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: the attempt to translate Clément Marot's poem to a sick young woman into English while preserving its rhyme, metre, and emotional register, and the inevitable sacrifices this demands. Each stanza should read like a successive draft of translation, with the same lexical material rearranged under stricter conditions, so that the rotation itself enacts the argument that formal preservation and literal fidelity cannot occupy the same line without one displacing the other. By the envoi, the six words should appear together in an order that reveals this impasse rather than resolving it. The poem ought to stand on its own for a reader unfamiliar with Hofstadter's book, yet contain enough precise reference—the sick maiden, the translator's impossible choices, the preservation of rhyme at the cost of literal meaning—to be recognised by someone who knows the work as an accurate rendering of the argument rather than a mere mention of its name. Please do not provide a bibliography or citation list; I require only the verse itself, composed in British English, with the formal structure intact.
