Here are my verdicts on four cases concerning the gender-equality paradox—the observation that more gender-egalitarian societies often display stronger gender segregation in career choice. I want to know whether a single principle can accommodate them all, or whether some verdict must be surrendered.

First, in Scandinavia, high scores on equality indices coincide with pronounced vertical segregation in STEM and care professions. I judge that this does not strongly confirm innate sex differences, because removing economic coercion might simply reveal preferences that poverty had suppressed; yet neither does it strongly disconfirm them, since culture may adapt rather than vanish.

Second, within those same societies, the gender gap in career preference widens with socioeconomic status: wealthier, better-educated cohorts diverge more sharply. I take this to suggest that when material constraints relax, something like temperament or intrinsic motivation becomes more determinative. At the same time, I feel uneasy: if the cause were genuinely biological, I would expect the gap to be relatively uniform across classes, not amplified by privilege.

Third, a long-running school-based programme in Iceland that systematically neutralised gendered socialisation in primary education was followed, a decade later, by a modest increase in gender-typical career clustering among graduates. My verdict is that suppressing explicit norms can trigger normative crystallisation—implicit expectations hardening in response to removal—so the result favours a cultural explanation. Still, I hesitate to grant culture total sovereignty, because the direction of the shift was precisely what a biological-predisposition model might also predict.

Fourth, in several less egalitarian societies with rigid patriarchal structures, women are over-represented in particular technical fields precisely because those fields carry low masculine prestige; men avoid them, leaving space. I read this as clear proof that cultural valuation can override any putative innate tendency. But I also notice that the pattern depends on equality being low, not high, which complicates any simple claim that equality always reveals true preference or always manufactures false divergence.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the interaction of constraint relaxation, preference revelation, and normative crystallisation—that captures the pattern across all four? If so, state it precisely, show which clause explains which case, and say what it predicts for a society that achieves perfect economic equality while retaining subtle status distinctions between fields. If not, identify which of my verdicts has to be abandoned, and whether the remaining ones point toward nature, culture, or justice being mismeasured.

Please say how confident you are in the principle or diagnosis you offer, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
