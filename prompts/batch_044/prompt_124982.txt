I'm designing a ninety-minute seminar for philosophy graduate students who understand basic social-choice theory but have not studied judgment aggregation. I want them to grasp the discursive dilemma not as a curiosity about majority voting, but as a structural problem that emerges specifically when an institution must issue a single collective decision together with a publicly intelligible reasoning chain. To carry the idea, I want to use one constructed case rather than the standard courtroom or hiring-committee scenario: a three-member municipal climate-adaptation board that must decide whether to approve a seawall project, where members vote separately on empirical premises (long-range flood-risk projections), normative premises (the moral weight of present costs versus future benefits), and meta-premises (which evidentiary standards are appropriate for climate models with high variance), and where the charter requires the board to publish a coherent justification that cites shared premises supporting the conclusion. Plan the explanatory arc. What concept must be secured first—specifically, why a published justification requirement makes this different from aggregating preferences or rankings—before the paradox can even be formulated? What must the example contain, and what must it deliberately lack, so that students do not reduce the problem to ordinary disagreement over outcomes or to strategic voting? Map the misconceptions in the order they will arise: for instance, that premise-based aggregation always eliminates the tension; that the paradox depends on an even number of voters; that delegating to a technical expert resolves the institutional problem; and that adding more premises makes the collective set more rather than less coherent. For each misconception, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and how. Provide one check-for-understanding question that tests whether they see why the justification constraint makes the choice of aggregation rule non-arbitrary, not merely whether they can reproduce the formal structure of inconsistent triples. Finally, specify what should be explicitly left out at this level—such as formal impossibility results beyond the basic tension, probabilistic judgment aggregation, or weighted-premise extensions—and explain why omitting each protects the conceptual point rather than simplifying it away.
