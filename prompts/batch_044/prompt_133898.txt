In a recent graduate seminar on addiction and autonomy, a speaker dismissed an interlocutor's worry that severe neurochemical dependency undermines free choice by invoking the Sartrean one-liner that man is condemned to be free. The speaker seemed to treat the slogan as if it automatically refuted any appeal to biological constraint, but it was unclear whether she meant a banal point about retaining some reflective capacity, a specific existentialist thesis about the interpretive freedom of consciousness, or a metaphysically extravagant claim that causal determination is impossible for human beings. I want to bring a precise disambiguation back to the group.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct philosophical claims that this slogan could actually be expressing. For each reading, specify: (a) whether it is a near-platitude about agency, a substantive Sartrean thesis involving radical freedom and bad faith (mauvaise foi), or a false exaggeration that Sartre never held; (b) exactly how it treats structural and biological constraints—such as addiction, compulsion, or social conditioning—as illusory, as real but never excusing, as freely chosen in some derivative sense, or as irrelevant to the kind of freedom that matters for responsibility; (c) whether the reading depends specifically on Sartre's phenomenological account of consciousness as non-substantial and self-negating, or whether it can stand independently of that framework; and (d) whether that particular reading actually supports the speaker's move of dismissing dependency-based objections, or whether it either concedes too much to the objector or proves too little.

In particular, distinguish at least: a weak compatibilist-style reading (we always retain some minimal capacity for reflective assent); a moderate existentialist reading (all situations are lived through projects that we can, in principle, revise or reinterpret, so dependency is a mode of being-in-the-world rather than a causal prison); a strong metaphysical reading (consciousness is literally outside the causal order and therefore never determined); and any other defensible intermediate formulation. For each, say whether it genuinely entails that an addict is in bad faith if she claims her choices are determined, and whether it avoids begging the question against neuroscientific explanations of compulsion.

Then indicate which of these readings the speaker was most likely invoking, which ones are authentically Sartrean, and which are strawmen that render the view trivially false. Conclude with a one-sentence summary on the final line stating which reading, if any, makes the dismissal of dependency objections work without circularity.
