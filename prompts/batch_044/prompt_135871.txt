I am a retired spectroscopist who, since leaving the laboratory five years ago, has been reading philosophy with the same sustained attention I once gave to absorption lines. Next month I am delivering the opening remarks—no more than six minutes—at a small interdisciplinary workshop entitled “Consensus and Its Costs,” whose audience is evenly divided between working scientists and philosophers of science and politics. The occasion is formal but intimate; I need the address to establish a shared tension rather than advance a thesis.

Please write the speech. Its structural backbone should be Simone Weil’s critique of political parties: specifically her claim that a party is not merely a group with shared opinions but a mechanism that substitutes collective passion for individual judgment, demands a voluntary blindness to certain questions, and degrades the capacity for attention that she considers the root of moral and intellectual life. Do not treat this as a loose analogy—“parties are like scientific collaborations”—but let Weil’s categories actually shape what I can and cannot say about my own four decades inside large institutional research programs, and about the kind of dissent that remains genuinely available to a person embedded in a consensus-building structure.

The address should be spoken aloud to a room that does not share one vocabulary: precise enough that philosophers recognize the reference to Weil’s idea of attention and to her suspicion of loyalty-as-virtue, yet never relying on technical jargon that would lose the scientists. It should avoid false reassurance that science is immune to what Weil describes, and it should avoid turning into an indictment of collaboration or of my former colleagues. The point is to make the audience feel the tension between institutional belonging and the obligation to think, not to resolve it.

Because I am not a professional philosopher, please say explicitly how confident you are in your characterization of Weil’s position, and single out the specific claim about her view that you are least sure of. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to editions. The speech should stand on its own.
