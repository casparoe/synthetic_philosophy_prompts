Consider a formal normative system N that extends a standard deontic logic with Peano arithmetic, using Gödel numbering to encode propositions about obligations over actions and agents, such that N is consistent and recursively axiomatizable. By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, there must then exist a well-formed statement G in N expressing a specific moral obligation that is arithmetically true—hence, on a realist reading, genuinely binding—yet formally unprovable within N. Given this, why do metaethicists rarely treat Gödelian incompleteness as a direct argument for moral intuitionism over formalist or constructivist ethics? And since Gödel's second theorem implies that N cannot prove its own consistency without appealing to principles outside N, does this not show that any internal justification of a moral framework is necessarily circular, leaving extra-systemic moral cognition as the only non-circular source of normative authority?
