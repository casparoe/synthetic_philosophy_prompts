As a retired physicist recently turned to philosophy of mathematics, I hold that the independence of the Continuum Hypothesis from ZFC should be read not as a simple failure of axiomatization, but as evidence that the cardinality of the continuum is an ontologically free parameter—structurally analogous to the coupling constants of a physical theory that must be fixed empirically rather than derived from deeper law. I further propose that distinct forcing extensions should be understood as different "phases" of set-theoretic reality, comparable to distinct thermodynamic regimes of a single physical system, with no canonical model representing the uniquely true universe of sets. What would count decisively against this position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: arguments that forcing is purely formal and carries no ontological weight; analogies with physics that collapse because physical constants are measurable within one world whereas cardinality is not; and results in set theory or its philosophy—perhaps concerning the multiverse conception or the search for new axioms—that show the "phase" metaphor to be misleading rather than illuminating. Please indicate which objections a committed defender should concede as genuinely damaging, which can be dismissed, and whether this analogy risks insulating the view from empirical or formal refutation in a way that is philosophically problematic for a claim of this kind.
