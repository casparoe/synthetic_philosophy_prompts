Two people in my study group got stuck arguing about whether its ever okay to say something like 'Japanese people tend to prioritize group harmony over individual expression' in a sociology paper. Keisha said its flat-out wrong — countries aren't natural kinds, there's huge internal variation, and talking about 'national character' is just essentialism with better PR. Tom said she's mixing up two things: describing a statistical cluster in survey data isn't the same as claiming every Japanese person is like that, and if we ban population-level descriptions just because individuals vary, we'd have to stop talking about gender gaps or age demographics too. Keisha replied that gender and age are biological reference classes with clear mechanisms, while 'Japanese' is a political and linguistic boundary that cuts across ethnicity, class, and region, so the reference class is arbitrary. Tom said that doesn't matter — if the cluster is real and predictive within that boundary, the boundary is doing explanatory work. Then Keisha brought up the ecological fallacy in reverse: she said Tom is taking a group average and using it to justify expectations about individuals, which is exactly that mistake but running backwards. Tom said the fallacy is about inferring individual traits from group data, but he's not doing that — he's just saying the group pattern exists, and that suppressing it for political comfort is anti-scientific. They couldn't agree on whether the dispute was about epistemology or politics, and asked me to referee.

Please adjudicate move by move: who's right about the reference-class objection, does the ecological fallacy actually apply here or is Tom right that it's being misused, and is there any principled way to talk about population-level cultural patterns without it sliding into harmful generalization?
