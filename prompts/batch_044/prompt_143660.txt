I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on virtue ethics and the limits of moral demand. In colloquia and in recent literature I keep encountering the compressed claim that moral perfection—the figure of the moral saint who maximizes moral goodness—is not an attractive ideal, or more starkly, that being a moral saint is undesirable. The slogan is usually deployed to resist the idea that we ought to maximize moral virtue, but it is almost never specified. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses it could express? I want a menu of readings: (a) a psychological claim that sustained maximal virtue is humanly impossible or unstable; (b) an axiological claim that moral value competes with and is systematically outranked by non-moral goods (aesthetic, relational, personal) in any well-rounded life; (c) a conceptual claim that the category 'moral saint' is incoherent, or that virtue itself does not admit of maximization; (d) a normative claim about practical reason—that agents lack sufficient all-things-considered reason to pursue moral perfection; and (e) any other live interpretation you think belongs on the list. For each, say whether it is trivial, empirically plausible, conceptually defensible, or false, and identify exactly what work it is doing in arguments against extreme altruism or self-sacrifice.

Then, rather than mapping options and stopping, take a committed position: which of these readings offers the strongest reconstruction of the anti-saint argument, which ones are actually necessary to reach the conclusion that maximal moral goodness is undesirable, and does any version genuinely establish undesirability rather than merely high cost, psychological rarity, or absence of obligation? Be explicit about where your judgment depends on contested commitments—for instance, value pluralism, the structure of virtue, or the scope of practical reason—and defend your choice.
