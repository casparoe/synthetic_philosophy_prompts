I serve as the procedural advisor to a municipal deliberative council that reviews paired submissions for a public humanities journal—specifically, essays offering contradictory but equally documented interpretations of contested historical or environmental events. The board comprises community historians, local scientists, and civic representatives with no philosophical training, yet they must decide consistently whether two submissions can stand together as partial perspectives or whether one should be excluded as incompatible with established fact. Over the past year I have watched the board drift into false equivalence on one hand and the suppression of defensible minority accounts on the other, so I want a reusable decision procedure grounded in Jain anekāntavāda—particularly syādvāda (conditioned predication, where assertions carry an implicit standpoint qualifier) and nayavāda (the doctrine that every claim captures only one aspect of a multi-faceted object)—but translated into steps a non-specialist can run in about thirty minutes per case.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist: the specific questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure terminates with a clear editorial decision (publish both with framing, publish one with a critical note, or reject one), and where it must halt and return the judgment to the board as a matter of values rather than philosophical determination. It must survive three hard cases: when one essay relies on oral tradition that the other dismisses as unreliable; when both cite the same quantitative dataset but assign opposite causal weights to climate forcing versus administrative failure; and when one author accuses the other of being not merely partial but deliberately misleading. Include a small set of test claim-types the board can apply at each stage—such as statements of existence, non-existence, and simultaneous qualified attribution. You should also inject one operational constraint not found in standard Jain texts: before any claim is evaluated, the board must decompose it into at least three independently identifiable causal or descriptive pathways, discarding any submission that collapses into a single unqualified assertion under this decomposition. Be explicit about which distinctions from the Jain framework must survive the simplification—for example, the difference between a genuinely inexpressible compound judgment and a simple falsehood—and which can be dropped for usability.

Then run the procedure on three worked cases: (1) two archaeological accounts of the same burial site, one emphasizing ritual continuity and the other emphasizing disruptive migration, both consistent with carbon-dated stratigraphy; (2) two climate-policy essays attributing a documented regional drought to anthropogenic forcing versus to multi-decadal oscillation, both drawing on the same temperature record; and (3) a case designed to yield an answer the board will dislike—specifically, where the procedure mandates publishing a historically offensive narrative as a legitimate partial standpoint because it meets the criteria for qualified predication, despite the majority’s moral objection. For each, show the step-by-step result. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which disagreements it fails to resolve, where it is likely to misfire (especially if applied to scientific hypotheses that claim universal scope rather than perspectival description), and how the full saptabhaṅgī scheme of sevenfold predication is being reduced to a practical four-category taxonomy in this context. Throughout, separate what is settled in Jain epistemology from what is genuinely contested among contemporary scholars—for instance, whether syādvāda is best read as epistemic relativism, as a non-relativist perspectivism, or as a logical framework largely independent of modern epistemology—and do not present any one of these interpretations as the consensus view.
