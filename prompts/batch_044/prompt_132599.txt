Assume the role of a philosopher of history and political theorist who is deeply familiar with anthropological debates on the foraging-to-farming transition but has no access to external sources or citation databases. You are advising an interdisciplinary research collective—philosophers, institutional economists, and normative theorists—that has secured three years of funding to investigate the conceptual legacy of the agricultural revolution. The collective is specifically interested in the tension between the “original affluence” thesis (that many foraging societies enjoyed high leisure, flexible social structures, and relatively low material inequality) and the “agricultural mistake” thesis (that the shift to farming represented a localized net welfare loss for the first generations and created structural conditions—population density, storage, and surplus extraction—that made permanent hierarchy possible). They want to avoid well-trodden empirical disputes about specific archaeological sites or chronology; instead, they need to settle conceptual questions that determine whether, and in what form, these theses carry normative or institutional implications for modern societies.

Before answering, please think carefully and spend a few minutes brainstorming the conceptual landscape: what genuinely open philosophical questions arise only if you take both theses seriously at the same time? Then prioritize the three open questions, conceptual distinctions, or lines of theoretical work that you believe should dominate the collective’s agenda. For each priority, defend your choice by addressing:

- Why it is upstream of other work: which other questions or applications depend on resolving this one first?
- Its tractability over a three-year horizon for philosophers and theorists rather than for field archaeologists or paleo-demographers.
- What kind of argument, conceptual clarification, or evidential shift would cause you to revise its priority upward or downward.

In your prioritization, please attend specifically to the distinction between “transition costs” (localized, generation-specific welfare losses during the shift itself) and “path-dependency traps” (mechanisms—demographic, ecological, or institutional—that made reversal impossible regardless of welfare). Also consider whether applying modern distributive norms to prehistoric societies commits an anachronistic category error, and whether that error undermines the normative force of either thesis. Be explicit about the criteria you are using—importance, tractability, neglectedness, and downstream dependency—and do not shy away from stating which apparently popular implications are overrated or do not actually follow from the premises.

Please deliver a defended agenda rather than a survey or reading list: tell the collective where its limited time and intellectual energy should go, and where it should not. Write your response in American English, in a formal and polished style, reasoning step by step.
