I've been helping organise a workshop next Thursday called Accountability Without Ultimacy — half the room are philosophy grads who've taught Strawson's Basic Argument until their eyes bled, half are probation officers and trauma therapists who think 'moral responsibilty' just means turning up to court on time. I need opening remarks, about four minutes spoken, that don't summarise the regress like a lecture but use it structurally: set a ground rule that we can talk seriously about blame, praise and repair all day without anyone having to defend 'ultimate' moral responsibility. To keep the philosophers from zoning out and the practitioners from feeling patronised, I'm injecting a framing idea I sketched — call it 'the archival self'. The idea is that we treat people as archives of past choices, but Strawson's argument shows the archive never authored its own filing system; extend that to reactive attitudes like resentment or gratitude and you see they inherit their shape from parents and culture in exactly the same unchosen chain. So the remarks should land that point, establish the ground rule, and welcome both groups without saying anyone is 'off the hook' in a practical sense. Write it in British English, keep it under 350 words, start with the one-line bottom line you'd want people to remember on the drive home, then the remarks themselves.
