I'm working on an essay for my philosophy of mind course about whether a single "I" can be distributed across multiple brains, drawing on Douglas Hofstadter's idea that the self is a strange loop that might persist inside other minds. My instructor has walked us through her verdicts on several hypothetical cases, but she hasn't told us the principle behind them, and I want to predict what she'll say about a new scenario I've constructed.

Here are her rulings so far. First, she accepts the kind of case Hofstadter discusses with a deceased partner: if that partner's mental patterns were genuinely reconstructed inside the survivor's brain and causally interwoven with the survivor's own loops, then part of the partner's "I" genuinely lives on within the survivor—not as a memory, but as an active, thinking pattern. Second, in the classic split-brain case where the hemispheres are surgically disconnected and each develops independent self-reference, she insists there are now two distinct selves, not one self distributed across two hemispheres. Third, she rejects the idea that a perfect digital copy of a mind running on an isolated server counts as a distributed self; she says the loop is suspended, not shared, because there is no living brain in which it is embedded and functioning. Fourth, she approved a thought experiment in which three strangers are linked by direct brain-computer interfaces so tightly that they form one unified decision-making system with a single shared sense of agency; she said this is genuinely one self distributed across three brains. Fifth, she denied that a lifelong apprentice who eventually replicates every cognitive habit of a master—while remaining physically separate with an independent brain—constitutes a distributed self; she called this parallel influence, not loop-sharing.

Now consider my new case. A research group develops an external "pattern server" that stores the complete, dynamically updated neural architecture of a deceased composer. The server feeds identical copies of this architecture into the brains of twelve living musicians who never knew the composer and share no emotional bond with her. The musicians are not linked to one another; the server updates each brain independently. Over time, each musician begins producing original compositions in the composer's exact style, and neuroimaging confirms that the embedded patterns run in parallel with their native cognition. Each musician reports an alien presence—someone else composing through them—yet they remain socially and causally isolated from one another.

What would my instructor say? Is the composer's "I" now distributed across all twelve brains, present in none of them, or replaced by twelve separate copies? More importantly, I want to understand the competing rules that could explain her first five verdicts. One rule might hold that distribution requires real-time causal integration into a single functional loop, regardless of how many brains host identical data. Another might hold that distribution requires only that the exact same strange loop be instantiated in living substrates, whether or not those substrates interact. There may be further rules that fit her track record just as well.

Please predict her verdict on the twelve-musician case, explain which of these rules she is most likely running, identify the closest analog among her earlier cases, and tell me what further hypothetical case would best distinguish the causal-integration rule from the pattern-instantiation rule. I am less interested in a bibliography than in the internal logic of her position.
