As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue for a character who studies distributed moral agency, I have formulated the following argument extending the inefficacy objection across climate change, meat consumption, and democratic voting. It seems valid yet reaches a conclusion I cannot accept, and I need to know where the reasoning breaks.

(1) If an individual action is not a necessary or sufficient condition for a collective harm, and does not alter the probability of that harm in any discernible way, then it is not a moral contribution to the harm—much as removing one grain never transforms a heap into a non-heap.

(2) In global carbon emissions, industrial meat production, and large-scale elections, each single act—driving, eating meat, or casting a vote—is causally redundant in exactly this sorites-like manner.

(3) Therefore, no individual act within these domains is morally wrong with respect to the corresponding collective problem.

(4) By parallel reasoning, if performing an act makes no discernible difference to preventing the harm, there can be no individual obligation to perform it.

(5) Thus, no one is morally required to reduce emissions, abstain from meat, or vote.

(6) Collective obligations, if they exist, supervene entirely on individual obligations; if the base is empty, the collective level is empty as well.

(7) So climate change, factory farming, and democratic failure are not collective moral wrongs at all.

I find (7) intolerable, yet I am inclined to defend (1). If forced to choose, I prefer keeping (1) and (3) over accepting (7). Where exactly does the argument go wrong—through a hidden equivocation between causal redundancy and normative complicity, a failure of supervenience, or some other step?
