Compare the standard tacit-consent argument that visitors ought to follow local conventions—such as queuing norms, tipping expectations, or rules against jaywalking—with the less-discussed normative-competence challenge. On the latter view, a visitor who lacks the practical understanding embedded in a local social practice cannot genuinely consent to its rules; compliance is then either hollow or risks distorting the practice, so the real obligation is first to acquire local competence rather than to obey blindly. Lay out how these two accounts differ in what they treat as the source of obligation—consent, practical understanding, or solidarity—and assess which better handles conventions that are widely violated locally or that carry heavy symbolic weight. Does either framework avoid collapsing into moral relativism, and does the competence challenge undermine the consent argument altogether or only when the visitor genuinely misunderstands the practice?
