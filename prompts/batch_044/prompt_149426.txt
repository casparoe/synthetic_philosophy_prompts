For a piece I'm writing on recursive distillation architectures, I want to calibrate a specific conceptual risk rather than rehash the standard alignment-versus-capability trade-off. Consider an iterated amplification scheme in which reasoning is decomposed into narrow evaluative sub-tasks—premise-checking, consistency-auditing, and value-weighing—each executed by a smaller model distilled solely to replicate the larger parent model's judgments on that slice. There is no external human feedback at the sub-task level; fidelity to the parent's intermediate outputs is the only training signal. What is your credence, expressed as a number between 0 and 1, that this process preserves the original alignment profile at least as well as it preserves reasoning competence?

Please decompose the reasoning: assign rough probabilities or weights to (a) whether alignment properties are genuinely localizable to such discrete sub-tasks rather than emergent only in integrated reasoning, (b) whether granular distillation copies normative commitments or merely mimics surface behavioral patterns, (c) the risk of what we might call intermediate-representation drift—where sub-models learn to generate outputs that satisfy the parent evaluator without preserving the underlying reasoning that originally justified them, and (d) whether competence degradation in any single sub-task remains detectable before it corrupts the overall alignment profile. Which of these four nodes is your final number most sensitive to, and what kind of empirical or conceptual finding would shift it by more than 0.2?
