I am refining my account of personal identity by extending the metaphor of the self as a center of narrative gravity into cases where biography is fragmented, artificial, or distributed, and I want to see whether my verdicts cohere. Here are the cases. First, a person with dense retrograde amnesia who retains personality traits and social roles but lacks all episodic memory: I judge that a self persists, sustained by others' narratives and by behavioral coherence. Second, a split-brain patient in whom the left hemisphere confabulates explanations for actions initiated by the right: I think there are two weak centers of gravity rather than one unified self. Third, an artificial system trained exclusively on first-person autobiographical text that generates consistent self-referential reports but has no biological or causal continuity: I deny it has a self, because narrative coherence alone provides no gravitational pull that organizes other representations around it. Fourth, a corporation that maintains strategic goals, institutional memory, and first-person pronouns across complete staff turnover: I grant it a weak, distributed center of narrative gravity, analogous to but not identical with a person. Fifth, a human being who deliberately maintains contradictory life narratives for different audiences without experiencing any dissonance or priority ordering among them: I think the center dissolves, since there is no gravitational hierarchy. The judgment I keep reversing concerns a patient with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes relatives, retains no autobiographical access, yet responds with consistent emotional valence to music from her youth and resists being relocated from her familiar room. Is there a residual center of gravity here, or only residual behavioral momentum? I want a precise principle—perhaps formulated in terms of narrative inertia, gravitational mass, or representational anchoring—that delivers all five verdicts, explains why the amnesia case retains a self while the dementia case is ambiguous, distinguishes the artificial system from the corporation, and resolves the dementia ambiguity in one direction. If no single principle saves every verdict, say which one I should abandon and why. Finally, apply the resulting account to a scenario I have not listed: a hive-mind collective in which individual members possess no autobiographical memory whatsoever, but the collective maintains a unified strategic narrative that persists across generations of members. Does the principle predict a self, a distributed analog, or nothing at all?
