I am a high school student enrolled in an advanced independent-study course on normative theory and rational agency, and over the past few weeks I have become preoccupied with a tension that my instructor only sketched in passing. I understand the basic distinction between direct normativity, in which a norm applies immediately to an action or to the content of a belief, and indirect normativity, in which a norm applies primarily to the deliberative procedure or mental framework that produces actions and beliefs—governing how one ought to reason rather than what conclusion one ought to reach. What I want to examine is what happens when this indirect framework is subjected to a demanding condition of structural transparency within idealized deliberation: the requirement that a fully rational agent must be able to trace and justify every step of its own reasoning about its norms, leaving no hidden premises or opaque background conditions.

Please explain the conceptual issue that arises at this intersection. If the norm is genuinely indirect—say, it regulates the method of belief-formation rather than the belief itself—then applying it reflexively seems to generate a regress: evaluating the agent’s reasoning about the norm requires another layer of procedural evaluation, and so on. At the same time, idealized deliberation is typically thought to require some form of coherence and self-awareness, which suggests the agent cannot simply stop at an arbitrary level. Can an indirect normative framework achieve what I will call meta-deliberative closure, in which the very same procedural norm governs both first-order reasoning and the reasoning about that reasoning, without collapsing into a direct norm on conclusions? Or does structural transparency force indirect normativity to become direct, because the agent must eventually justify a specific outcome—namely, the acceptance of the norm itself?

I am not looking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific authors; I want a precise but intuitive explanation of whether indirect normativity remains stable under idealized self-reflection. In particular, address whether the threat of vicious circularity is real or merely apparent, and whether there is a principled way for an ideal agent to apply its deliberative rules to themselves without either generating an infinite hierarchy or abandoning the indirect character of the framework altogether. You might also consider whether the problem changes if we distinguish norms of practical reasoning from norms of theoretical reasoning, or if that distinction itself dissolves under the transparency condition.
