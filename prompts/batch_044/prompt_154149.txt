I am a doctoral student working on distributive justice and intergenerational ethics. I have observed a recurring structural pattern in debates about progressive estate taxation that I cannot place precisely within the existing literature, and I would be grateful for your assessment.

The pattern is as follows. A jurisdiction introduces steep inheritance taxes with the explicit normative aim of safeguarding equality of opportunity. Rather than dissipating dynastic advantage, however, the tax appears to induce a qualitative transformation in its form. Affluent families convert liquid, taxable financial capital into less fungible, less visible structures: dense interlocking networks of family foundations; strategically arranged marriages that consolidate social capital; and privileged access to institutional resources—such as legacy admissions pathways, private investment vehicles framed as philanthropic entities, and unpriced board memberships—that fall outside the tax base. The outcome is not the elimination of dynastic privilege but its metamorphosis: financial capital declines in relative salience while an opaque, durable form of dynastic capital takes its place.

I can see superficial parallels to capital flight or regulatory arbitrage, yet those concepts typically describe geographical or categorical relocation rather than an internal change in the composition of advantage within the same jurisdiction. Is there an established concept—perhaps in sociology, political philosophy, or the law of taxation—that specifically denotes this dynamic: the pressure-induced conversion of taxable wealth into non-financial, monopolised dynastic structures? If no precise term exists, how ought one to delineate the phenomenon from ordinary tax avoidance on one side and from broader theories of elite reproduction on the other? Please reply in British English.
