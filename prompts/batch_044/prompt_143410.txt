After a long argument with a friend about whether a village's collective memory of a suppressed strike can ever outweigh the factory's payroll records that show no work stoppage, I tried to formalise the reasoning that seemed to push us toward an absurd conclusion. Here is the chain my friend laid out, and I cannot see the exit. (1) Collective memory is not an aggregation of individual recollections but a social practice sustained by shared narration, ritual correction, and intergenerational transmission; consequently it possesses an internal normative structure that makes it authoritative about the community's past in a way that private testimony is not. (2) If a practice has internal norms of correction, then its outputs are authoritative with respect to what belongs to that community's history, irrespective of whether external institutions have documented the event. (3) Therefore, collective memory carries a form of mnemonic sovereignty: in any conflict with archival records, the community's sustained practice should be treated as the final arbiter, because applying an external standard violates the autonomy of the practice itself. (4) This sovereignty applies not merely to the significance or emotional weight of an event, but to all its descriptive properties — its date, its participants, its physical causes, and its chronological relation to other events. (5) Hence, if a coastal community collectively remembers a catastrophic flood in 1847 that destroyed the harbour, and every meteorological and engineering record demonstrates that the river was at record low levels that year and the harbour walls were not built until 1862, we ought still to treat the flood as having occurred in 1847 and destroyed structures that did not yet exist. (6) But to affirm physically and chronologically impossible events solely because a social practice sustains them is intolerable; it collapses historical knowledge into communal fiat. (7) So we must reject either the claim that collective memory has internal authority (1), or the claim that this authority extends to descriptive facts (4), or the inference from internal norms to incorrigibility (3). My friend insists that abandoning (3) reduces collective memory to mere opinion, and that abandoning (1) destroys the very idea of mnemonic sovereignty. I should like to keep (1) and something like (3), but I cannot accept (5). Which step actually slips, what makes it look plausible, and what does each available rejection cost? In particular, is there a principled way to restrict the sovereignty claimed in (3) so that it governs the meaning and belonging of an event without governing its empirical details, or does any such restriction already concede that archives can correct the community?
