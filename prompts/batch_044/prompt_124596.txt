I have just concluded a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about Keynes's 1930 essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren. She insists that his vision is not merely a prediction of shorter working hours but a normative account of human flourishing, centred on the distinction between absolute and relative needs, the claim that the love of money is a morbid obsession, and the forecast that technological unemployment would free us for genuinely valuable living rather than further accumulation. I work as an actuary, so my natural vocabulary involves risk pools, intertemporal discount rates, cohort-based utility smoothing, and the weighting of non-pecuniary welfare flows. Please restate Keynes's argument — including his dismissal of money-love and his conception of liberation through technology — using only that vocabulary, and do so as faithfully as possible. Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto risk-adjusted lifetime utility maximisation; which parts survive only if distorted into quantifiable lifestyle goods or consumption bundles; and which parts have no expression at all in actuarial reasoning. Finally, say whether the residue indicates that my framework genuinely lacks the resources to describe the good life, or whether Keynes's argument depends on unstated aesthetic and moral premises that were never properly defended.
