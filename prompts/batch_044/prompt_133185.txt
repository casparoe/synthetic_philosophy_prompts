As a high school student working through questions in philosophy of art, I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern about why an original artifact is more valuable than a perfect replica, and I am unsure whether the pattern already has a name. The situation typically unfolds in three stages. First, a defender claims that the original is irreplaceable because it bears the creator's direct physical trace—the actual paint, chisel marks, or handwriting applied by that specific person. Once it is pointed out that assistants often executed large portions of well-known works, and that conservation has replaced substantial original material over time, the defender does not concede; instead, they shift to saying that value lies in the creator's unique intention being embodied in this particular object, regardless of which hands applied the medium. Then, if one presents a precise replica executed according to the same plans, with identical dimensions and materials, they shift once more, arguing that the original's worth comes from its singular causal and historical path—the unbroken chain connecting the object to the creator's life, culture, and moment of production. Each reply sounds reasonable on its own, yet together they function like interchangeable shields for the same conclusion: the original must always be superior, with the criterion of superiority adjusted to survive each objection. I recognize that this is not quite the motte-and-bailey structure, since there is no single defensible core paired with an indefensible extension; the material, intentional, and causal-historical claims appear roughly equal in status, not arranged as fortress and outpost. It also seems different from plain equivocation, because each sense of originality or value is used sincerely in its context rather than being smuggled between premises. Is there an established philosophical term for this specific rotation among justifications? If not, I would like a careful definition that specifies the rotation mechanism, distinguishes it from ad hoc immunization in science, from simple fallacies of ambiguity, and from well-known accounts of an artwork's special presence in an age of reproduction, and that clarifies whether the pattern applies only to tangible artifacts or extends to performances, reconstructed historical documents, and digital works produced from identical code. I am not looking for a bibliography; I want to understand the conceptual boundaries of the phenomenon itself.
