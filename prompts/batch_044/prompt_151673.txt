I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical background. In thinking about open-source licensing, I have constructed an argument that seems to show not only that patents and copyright are unjustified, but that plagiarism and misattribution are conceptually impossible. Each premise looks defensible, yet the conclusion feels wrong. Could you identify exactly which step fails, explain why it initially seemed plausible, and then outline the costs for three alternatives — a Lockean labour-mixing account, a pure consequentialist incentive theory, and a social-contract view of information goods — if they reject that step?

(1) Ideas are non-rivalrous: if I hold idea E, your holding E does not diminish my possession or use of it.
(2) If a resource is non-rivalrous and its use by others causes me no harm, then exclusive moral claims over it are unjustified (a Lockean-style proviso applied to abstract goods).
(3) Therefore, no one has a natural moral right to exclude others from using or sharing an idea.
(4) Intellectual property law creates artificial exclusivity over ideas or their expressions.
(5) If there is no natural right to exclusivity, such institutions are unjustified unless they serve a separate purpose, such as incentivising creation.
(6) The flourishing of open-source software shows that creation occurs abundantly without exclusivity, so the incentive justification fails.
(7) Hence patents and copyright are morally unjustified.
(8) My source code is a concrete token expressing an abstract idea (the algorithm).
(9) If I have no exclusive right to the abstract idea (from 3), any claim I have to the token can rest only on the physical labour of producing that particular file.
(10) But copying the file does not take my original token; it merely creates a new token instantiating the same unowned idea.
(11) Therefore, copying is not taking anything that morally belongs to me, and "stealing an idea" is incoherent.
(12) By extension, plagiarism — presenting another's token as one's own — is incoherent, because there is no "another's" idea to misattribute, only unowned types instantiated in different tokens.
(13) So all IP norms and attribution practices rest on a conceptual mistake.

I am particularly inclined to keep (1) and (3), but I suspect the slide from (8) to (12) involves an equivocation between owning a type and owning a token, or an unjustified inference from "no natural right" to "no institutional right". Please diagnose the flaw precisely, explain why the problematic step looked reasonable, and price the three positions mentioned for giving it up.
