As a software engineer without any formal philosophy training, I am trying to work through a conceptual problem that keeps coming up in discussions about historical explanation. Max Weber’s well-known thesis about the Protestant ethic and the rise of capitalism claims that Calvinist religious values—especially beliefs about predestination and a worldly calling—did not merely accompany modern capitalism but causally shaped its distinctive rationalized behavior. Critics reply that explaining an economic system by reference to shared values risks circularity: we infer the values from the behavior and then invoke them to explain the same behavior. They also argue that “culture” is too diffuse and interpretively flexible to count as an independent causal variable in any rigorous sense.

Write an essay in American English that takes up this controversy and reaches a definite conclusion. To push past the standard back-and-forth, frame Weber’s “spirit of capitalism” not simply as a motivating belief system but as what we might call a behavioral type system: an implicit set of constraints that rules out certain actions—such as conspicuous consumption or idle accumulation—without positively directing actors toward any specific economic alternative. If culture operates mainly through such negative filtering rather than through positive motivation, does Weber still have a causal explanation of capitalism’s emergence, or does he only have a description of which behaviors happened to be culturally permissible? Defend a clear verdict on whether culture, understood in this way, can be a genuine cause in historical explanation, and honestly address the strongest consideration that favors the opposing view. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; keep the reasoning self-contained and accessible to a technically minded but non-specialist reader.
