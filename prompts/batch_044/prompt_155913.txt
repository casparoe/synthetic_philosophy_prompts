You are a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in cognitive science and artificial intelligence. Before you begin, please take a few minutes to brainstorm the taxonomy of philosophical disagreements—verbal, substantive, methodological, normative, and bedrock-intuitive—and to identify which diagnostic markers best apply when experts talk past each other despite shared premises.

In working groups and recent literature I have followed, a debate about attributing beliefs, intentions, and understanding to large language models and comparable AI systems has reached a frustrating standstill. Proponents of the intentional stance maintain that mentalistic vocabulary is predictively indispensable: to forecast how these systems will respond to novel prompts, we must treat them as agents with beliefs and goals, and the practical success of this framing gives it all the legitimacy it needs. Mechanistic critics, often trained in machine learning, reply that every output is fully accounted for by statistical pattern completion over weights trained on corpora, so mentalistic talk is at best a convenient fiction and at worst a category error that obscures actual computation. A third cluster—ethicists and policy researchers—argues that attributing mental states risks moral anthropomorphism, encouraging users to confer undeserved moral status or to misplace blame when harmful outputs occur.

For years the exchange has recycled the same moves. Intentionalists accuse mechanists of being eliminativists about minds generally; mechanists accuse intentionalists of ignoring architecture; ethicists accuse both camps of neglecting social consequence. My own hypothesis is that much of this is a verbal disagreement about the job the word belief is doing—predictive, explanatory, or normative—disguised as a single ontological question about whether the system really has beliefs. Yet I am not sure whether a hidden substantive disagreement is also present, perhaps about what counts as an adequate explanation in cognitive science or about whether internal representational structure is necessary for mental attribution.

Please diagnose the structure of this impasse. Are the three groups answering different questions under one heading? Is there a substantive disagreement about criteria for mental states—functional role, structural correspondence, normative accountability—that neither side can fully articulate? Or is the stalemate a sign that the underlying question is ill-posed?

To sharpen the diagnosis, consider a specific hypothetical I want to inject: a Register-Model that maintains an explicit, readable internal table mapping individual propositions to continuous confidence values, updates that table via gradient descent on prediction error, and uses the table to gate its outputs, yet possesses no conscious access to the table, no unified self-model, and no behavioral flexibility outside its training distribution. Would the intentionalist, mechanist, and ethicist all agree about whether this system has beliefs? If they disagree, does their disagreement expose a deeper split—say, between functional-role theories, architecture-matching theories, and accountability-based theories?

Finally, given your diagnosis, specify what would actually count as progress. Would a decisive contribution take the form of a conceptual distinction—perhaps between stance-dependent belief and architecture-embedded mental representation? A formal result showing predictive equivalence between mentalistic and mechanistic models? An empirical study of how users behave when mental language is applied? Or should we expect no progress because the dispute ultimately rests on bedrock values about explanation and moral responsibility that cannot be rationally adjudicated? Be concrete: identify the form a breakthrough would take, or argue honestly why none is likely.
