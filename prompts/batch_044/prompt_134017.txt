Write a final-exam essay question for an upper-level metaphysics seminar on counterfactual versus interventionist theories of causation. Present a concrete redundant-causation scenario—one active primary mechanism and a fully present but dormant backup that never activates—and ask students to do three things: (a) diagnose precisely why a standard counterfactual analysis misidentifies or fails to distinguish the actual cause, specifying whether the difficulty is preemption, overdetermination, or both and why an ad hoc appeal to influence or proximity is required; (b) show how an interventionist structural-equation model resolves the ambiguity by appealing to the actual causal graph and to the modularity condition on interventions, not merely to observed correlations or dependencies; and (c) identify one substantive cost of that resolution, such as the relativity of causal claims to the choice of variables or the circularity risk in justifying the graph structure. Provide a model answer of about 650 words and a marking scheme totaling 20 points, with specific marks for parts (a), (b), and (c), plus 4 points reserved for a comparative evaluation that does not merely summarize the two views. In the marking scheme, name the two mistakes that should each cost 2 points: treating the counterfactual problem as only generic overdetermination rather than preemption, and describing interventionism as if it were only a statistical dependency claim without mentioning structural equations or modularity. Do not ask for citations or reading lists; the answer should depend on conceptual analysis of the theories themselves.
