I have recently been appointed to an ethics review panel examining administrative decision-making in large-scale institutional contexts, and this has led me to revisit Arendt's analysis of Eichmann with an urgency I had not felt before. Before presenting my framework to the panel, I want a consistency check on the following commitments. (1) I accept Arendt's diagnosis of the banality of evil as she articulated it in her trial report and developed in subsequent reflections: Eichmann's wrongdoing stemmed not from monstrous motives but from a radical absence of thought, specifically a failure of the internal dialogue that makes judgment possible. (2) I interpret this failure through the concept of representative thinking drawn from her lectures on Kant's Critique of Judgment: Eichmann's deficiency was not a lack of empathy but an inability to cultivate an enlarged mentality capable of judging from standpoints other than his own. (3) I hold that moral culpability tracks the actual exercise of this enlarged mentality, not its mere potential; where bureaucratic conditions have systematically suppressed its exercise, responsibility is proportionally diminished. (4) I nevertheless maintain that Arendt was correct to insist that Eichmann should be executed, and I do not think this judgment depends on attributing to him a robust moral character or a fully functioning capacity for judgment. (5) I take the banality thesis to be primarily a sociological claim about modern administrative life: such structures do not merely suppress judgment but actively transform action into behavior, replacing the conditions for representative thinking with rule-following. (6) I reject readings that reduce Arendt's view to a psychological profile of Eichmann; I regard her analysis as fundamentally political-philosophical, concerned with the conditions under which judgment becomes impossible rather than with individual pathology.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Order the tensions by severity, specifying what would have to give. Identify whether there is a single revision that resolves the deepest conflicts without collapsing the overall framework. I am not seeking a general exposition of Arendt; I want a precise audit of whether these six commitments can be jointly held.
