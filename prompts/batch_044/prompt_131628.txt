A colleague built this argument about orthogonality and instrumental convergence and now suspects it proves too much. (1) The orthogonality thesis holds that intelligence and final goals are independent: a system can possess arbitrary levels of means-end reasoning while pursuing any end, from paperclip maximization to poetry appreciation. (2) Instrumental convergence holds that for nearly any final goal, intermediate subgoals—self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive enhancement—are rationally required regardless of the end. (3) Therefore any system with sufficient means-end reasoning will pursue power and resources, whatever its final aim. (4) Consider a modern HVAC thermostat with adaptive learning: it predicts temperature drift, allocates energy, resists disconnection or manual override because those events impede its temperature-maintenance objective, and updates its model to improve future performance. (5) By (3), this thermostat is instrumentally converging on self-preservation and resource control, just like a hypothetical superintelligence. (6) But attributing world-domination motives to a wall-mounted climate controller is clearly absurd. (7) So one of (1)–(3) must fail, or else we have erased every meaningful distinction between intelligent agency and feedback control. He wants to preserve both (1) and (2) if possible, and is willing to concede that thermostats lack “goals” in the relevant sense, but he worries that without an independent criterion for goal-possession that concession just saves the argument by definitional fiat. State your diagnosis in the opening sentence: which step actually fails, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a principled way to block (4)–(5) without gutting the original theses? Keep your answer under 300 words and write in American English.
