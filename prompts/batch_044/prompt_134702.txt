The debate over whether a rational agent can consistently trust its own reasoning—specifically, whether the Löbian obstacle blocks any fully self-verifying system—seems to have reached an impasse that I have been tracking across both philosophy of logic and AI safety forums. On one side, formalists argue that Löb's theorem establishes a structural ceiling: if an agent's internal logic allows it to treat provability of a statement as sufficient grounds for believing that statement, then the agent can derive contradictions or at least lose the ability to distinguish sound reasoning from self-deception. They tend to treat this as a universal limit and conclude that any architecture aiming for complete self-trust is formally doomed, regardless of practical engineering fixes. On the other side, systems-minded researchers—many with backgrounds in distributed computing and verification—counter that this confuses an idealized arithmetic system with a bounded, resource-limited agent operating under noisy conditions. They argue that real agents never have perfect access to their own proof states, that external grounding and empirical feedback loops break the self-reference, and that the obstacle is therefore a feature of a particular formal model rather than of rational agency itself.

What strikes me is that both camps appear to accept the mathematical result and agree that self-deception is undesirable; they diverge only on whether the result applies outside its formal context. My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a factual dispute about the theorem's content, nor a purely verbal disagreement about the words "trust" or "rationality," but rather a case of two groups answering different questions under a single heading—one about the limits of formal systems, the other about the design of practical agents—while treating the boundary between formal logic and engineering practice as obvious when it is not. Is that the right diagnosis? Or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about what counts as a valid reason for belief—perhaps about whether empirical feedback can ever genuinely ground self-trust, or whether only logical derivation counts? Could the stall instead reflect bedrock intuitions about autonomy, where one side sees dependence on external verification as a failure of true self-trust and the other sees it as a necessary condition?

To push the framing further, consider an architecture I have not seen discussed in the philosophical literature: an agent that maintains not a single monolithic belief state but a distributed ledger of its own inferences, cryptographically signed and timestamped across nodes, so that "trust" requires not just logical derivation but consensus over an immutable historical chain. Would such a design escape the Löbian obstacle, or does it merely relocate the self-reference into the consensus mechanism? Given whatever diagnosis you offer—whether the disagreement is verbal, split across disciplinary questions, anchored in hidden standards of evidence, or driven by irreconcilable intuitions about autonomy—what would actually count as progress? A formal result extending the obstacle to distributed or cryptographically anchored systems? A conceptual distinction between logical self-trust and procedural self-trust? Or is the question itself ill-posed because it demands a single account of "self-trust" that must cover both mathematical logic and engineering practice?
