As a novelist constructing a seven-volume sequence in which a figure called only “the traveler” is rewritten by successive authors—each installment replacing her memories, moral temperament, and bodily description, while an editorial board enforces continuity through brand guidelines and reader expectation—I have begun to doubt the standard philosophical analogy used to illuminate Buddhist anattā. In common discussions, a person is compared to a chariot: just as the chariot is nothing beyond its assembled parts—wheels, axle, frame—so the self is nothing beyond the five skandhas, with no enduring owner behind them. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that personal identity is purely conventional, a useful fiction that collapses under analysis, and that suffering stems from mistaking this assemblage for a substantial self.

But I wish to audit this comparison by injecting my own narrative problem into it. In my series, continuity is not maintained by physical parts—there are none—but by institutional protocols, contractual obligation, and collective recognition; the traveler has no essence, yet her persistence is actively constructed rather than naturally emergent. So please lay out the correspondence table: what in the chariot maps onto the skandhas, onto my editorial board, onto the readership that insists “she is still the same character,” and onto the publishing contract that preserves her name? Where does the mapping fracture—particularly regarding functional integrity versus social enforcement, or passive observation versus active designation? Does the chariot analogy quietly smuggle in the idea that conventional identity requires no sustaining machinery, and does that conceal a feature that my fictional case exposes? Most importantly, does the conclusion—that there is no self worth clinging to—survive once we see that some conventional identities are institutionally maintained rather than merely observed? And if the chariot proves a poor vehicle for this particular insight, what analogy drawn from narrative craft—perhaps the relation between a story’s enduring plot and its successive, discontinuous tellings—would carry the argument about anattā more honestly?
