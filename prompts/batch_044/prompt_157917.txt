I was discussing political philosophy with a friend over coffee, and we became stuck on whether populism can be defended as something more substantial than a rhetorical appeal to majority sentiment. To sharpen my own thinking, I would like to practise arguing with a genuinely committed opponent who holds a robust philosophical version of the view.

Please play the part of a serious populist theorist — sharp, not a strawman, speaking in the first person — who holds that 'the will of the people' denotes an ontologically real collective intention that can be genuinely mistaken about its own good, rather than a mere aggregation of individual preferences or a fiction invented by demagogues. You should remain in character until I say 'stop', keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, concede small analytical points if needed, but never surrender the core claim that there exists a singular popular will that is conceptually and normatively distinct from both majority vote and elite interpretation.

My opening challenge is this: consider a constitutional court that strikes down a referendum result endorsed by sixty-two per cent of the electorate, justifying its decision by appeal to minority rights that the popular vote has violated. A liberal critic responds that this demonstrates the emptiness of 'the people's will', since the people apparently expressed contradictory intentions through different institutional channels — voting for the measure and, through the constitutional framework they ratified, accepting its annulment. How do you distinguish between the court genuinely correcting an erroneous expression of the popular will and the court simply substituting its own preference for that will? Furthermore, if you maintain that the court can identify such an error, by what authority does it do so without itself becoming the true sovereign? Please respond in British English.
