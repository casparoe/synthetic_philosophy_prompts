Question: The argument from illusion observes that in standard cases—seeing a straight stick appear bent in water—there is an appearance-property (bentness) that the physical object lacks. To account for this gap, philosophers have posited sense-data or mental representations that possess the appearance-properties. Disjunctivists, such as John McDowell, reject this "common factor" approach, insisting that veridical perception and illusion are fundamentally different kinds of mental state with nothing mental in common. Now consider two more complex cases. First, a "veridical hallucination": through neural stimulation, a subject undergoes an experience exactly like seeing a red apple, and purely coincidentally a red apple is present in front of them, but there is no causal link between the apple and the experience. Second, a "filtered veridical" case: a subject sees a genuinely red apple, but through a neural interface that alters wavelength processing; the subjective qualitative character is indistinguishable from seeing an orange apple under normal conditions. How should a disjunctivist classify each case? And does the fact that subjective phenomenal character can remain constant across veridical perception, illusion, and hallucination undermine the disjunctivist claim that there is no shared mental factor?

Student answer: The argument from illusion says that when we see a stick bent in water, we must be seeing a mental image that is actually bent, because the real stick is straight. This leads to the idea of sense-data. Disjunctivists deny this by saying there is no shared mental object—veridical seeing is one kind of state, and illusion is a completely different kind, so we don't need sense-data to explain the difference. In the veridical hallucination case, the disjunctivist should say this is actually veridical perception, because the apple is really there in front of the subject, even though the brain caused the experience artificially. In the filtered case, the subject is having an illusion, because the experience is of orange and the apple is red, so there is a mismatch between appearance and reality. The problem for disjunctivism is that in both cases, and in normal seeing, the subjective feeling is exactly the same. Since disjunctivists say the mental states are totally different, they can't explain why they feel identical. Representationalists like Michael Tye agree with disjunctivists that there is no common mental factor. Therefore, disjunctivism fails because it cannot account for phenomenological sameness, and we should reject the view in favor of representationalism.
