I sit on my university's interdisciplinary research-ethics panel. A cognitive-science group proposes the "Private Lexicon Study": over eight weeks, participants develop idiosyncratic, deliberately untranslatable terms for their own internal states, then use those terms in repeated self-reports to track changes in sensation. The investigators say this tests whether private reference can be stable without intersubjective correction; critics reply that genuinely private terms yield no communicable data, while non-private ones defeat the study's purpose. The principal investigator appeals specifically to the possibility of private ostensive definition — a subject correcting her own past usage without external check — and argues that Wittgenstein's constraints apply only to public language games, not to first-person calibration protocols.

Draft the panel's formal decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or conditional approval, with a specific timeline for the investigators' response. The memo must address whether empirical research can meaningfully incorporate private-language conditions, engage the PI's argument about private ostensive definition honestly rather than dismissing it, and give full weight to the strongest objection on the side you reject. Avoid requests for citations or bibliographies; the reasoning should be self-contained.
