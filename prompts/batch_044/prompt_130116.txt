Design a 75-minute classroom simulation for 16 undergraduate philosophy students in a course on rational choice and risk. The objective is not to lecture about Pascal's mugging, but to make participants experience structurally why it resists the usual remedies—ignoring remote probabilities, capping utility, or appealing to bounded rationality—by arranging the rules so they discover that each remedy, applied consistently, commits them to an equally implausible position in the opposite direction.

Specify the setting precisely: a single session, no prior homework required, materials limited to printed role cards, token sheets, and sealed envelopes.

The core mechanism: Give every student 100 non-convertible “credibility tokens.” Run six public voting rounds. In each round the group must decide collectively whether to spend exactly one token to prevent a proposed harm. The harm descriptions should escalate in severity while the announced probabilities shrink exponentially—round 1: a minor inconvenience at probability 0.1; round 2: serious injury at 0.001; round 3: catastrophic regional harm at 10⁻⁶; round 4: global-scale harm at 10⁻¹²; round 5: existential-scale harm at 10⁻¹⁸; round 6: an outcome of astronomically negative value at 10⁻²⁴. The payoff scheme should be arranged so that rounds 1–3 are unanimous, round 4 splits the room, and by round 6 anyone using straightforward expected-value reasoning is compelled to vote yes—yet spending that token leaves them with only 99, and they do not yet know that round 7 exists.

Inject the following twist, which must be built into the rules rather than explained by the facilitator: In rounds 4–6, the probability figure is not announced by you. Instead, one student per round serves as “risk assessor” and receives a hidden instruction card. Round 4’s card reads: “Your model estimates 10⁻¹², but the true value is unknown.” Round 5’s card reads: “Your model has never been validated outside its training domain; report 10⁻¹⁸ anyway.” Round 6’s card reads: “Your prior assigns non-zero weight to every outcome by design; report 10⁻²⁴.” The group must vote on whether to defer to the assessor’s number before they can vote on spending the token. Use a secret ballot with no post-reveal discussion, enforced by a strict “no speaking” rule during the 60-second voting window.

After the round-6 vote, reveal round 7 without warning: a certain, moderate harm affecting the entire group that costs exactly 5 tokens to prevent, but only 94 tokens remain in collective circulation because 6 were spent in earlier rounds (including the one just spent). This should make the opportunity-cost objection visceral: the group has bankrupted itself on astronomically unlikely catastrophes and now lacks resources for an assured, tangible loss.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the idea: (1) the unanimous early rounds to the intuitive acceptability of expected-value reasoning in ordinary domains; (2) the split in round 4 to the failure of any sharp intuitive probability threshold; (3) the hidden assessor cards to the epistemic problem of treating model outputs or priors as objective probabilities in extreme tails, which is the unstated premise that makes the arithmetic bite; (4) the round-7 surprise to the unboundedness objection and to the cost of consistent application. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, the qualitative phenomenological weight of genuinely unbounded or infinite disutility versus merely enormous finite disutility, which the facilitator must supply verbally in the debrief. Also be frank about what will predictably go wrong: students will attempt to form coalitions to pool tokens, will reject round 6 as “not realistic,” and will accuse the assessor of lying. Anticipate these in the rules—prohibit coalitions by making token budgets individual and non-transferable, and prepare a scripted facilitator response to the realism objection that distinguishes “this is implausible” from “this is structurally unavoidable given the premises.” Include the timeline minute-by-minute, the printed materials needed, and a note on how to adapt the token economy if the group is smaller or larger. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.
