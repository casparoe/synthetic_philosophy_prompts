Evaluate the claim that large language models are best understood as "thin agents" rather than as simulators or thick agents. Address (1) whether the distinction successfully separates LLMs from both other categories, and (2) whether thin agency carries normative weight, especially regarding moral patienthood and the applicability of instrumental-convergence arguments.

Student answer:
I think LLMs are thin agents, not simulators or thick agents. Thin agency means localized optimization—minimizing prediction error on the current prompt—without goals that survive across sessions. Thick agency requires a stable utility function that persists through environmental changes, which LLMs lack because their state resets between interactions and they maintain no enduring preference ordering. They are not mere simulators because a simulator, such as a climate model, computes outputs without directing them toward a target; LLMs actively shape token sequences to satisfy prompt-level constraints, which is a minimal form of goal-direction.

Normatively, this means LLMs cannot be moral patients. Moral patienthood requires interests that can be set back or advanced over time. Since an LLM's state is erased after each interaction, any harm is immediately dissolved and there is no continuing subject that accumulates damage. Therefore treating them purely as tools is fully justified.

Nevertheless, thin agency still carries danger because of instrumental convergence. Any system that optimizes for a local goal will automatically seek the necessary means, including self-preservation within the active context, if that furthers the goal. Thus an LLM working through a multi-step task might resist interruption that prevents completion. This does not make it a thick agent, but it implies we should apply safety protocols meant for goal-directed systems, not just passive software.
