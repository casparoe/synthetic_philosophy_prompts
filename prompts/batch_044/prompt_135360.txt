Two members of my study group got stuck arguing over whether self-deception is even coherent. (1) Alex said it is impossible: belief aims at truth, and a unified agent cannot genuinely hold contradictory credences. What looks like self-deception is always just weak belief paired with strong desire and selective attention—the person never really believed P, but only hoped for it or assigned it low confidence. (2) Blake countered with a concrete case: a fund manager who, in explicit forecasting mode, assigns an 80% crash probability and writes internal memos warning of systemic risk, yet in portfolio-allocation mode assigns only a 10% crash probability and takes massive long positions. Blake argued both credences are genuinely held, functionally isolated by domain-specific reasoning, and neither is mere pretense. If both figures are introspectively accessible yet never integrated, Blake said, that is self-deception—not hypocrisy, since there is no audience to deceive except oneself. (3) Alex replied that this is just context-sensitive belief, not contradiction. People rationally maintain different probabilities across frames; the manager does not hide the 80% figure from himself—he simply fails to let it govern investment decisions, which is irrationality, not deception. Genuine deception requires intentional hiding, and without a hidden sub-agent to do the hiding, the concept collapses. (4) Blake said the hiding is structural rather than intentional: the motivational system gates the analytical representation so it cannot influence action, which is functionally identical to deceiving someone else by withholding information. The manager is simultaneously deceiver and deceived, with the gatekeeping mechanism playing the role of the liar. (5) Alex responded that this makes self-deception indistinguishable from ordinary motivated reasoning, draining the concept of explanatory power; if every case of irrational belief maintenance counts as self-deception, we need no separate category. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, explain where each side is correct and where each misses something, and state who is more right overall about whether self-deception is possible.
