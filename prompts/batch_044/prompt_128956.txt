My study group tried to weaponize Williams's integrity objection against every normative theory and now I think the weapon backfires. (1) For Williams, saving your spouse because "utility is maximized" is one thought too many; the right reason is just "she's my wife," rooted in your ground projects. (2) But ground projects themselves sometimes need revision—say, leaving a failing marriage or quitting medicine for art. (3) If it's "one thought too many" to weigh your spouse against a stranger using impartial calculus, then it's also one thought too many to weigh your current ground project against an alternative using *any* reflective standard, since that treats the project as disposable rather than constitutive. (4) So integrity seems to forbid not just utilitarian reasoning but any critical deliberation about whether to keep a ground project. (5) Yet Williams clearly thinks people can and should abandon projects that no longer fit—he talks about "integrity" allowing for change, not fossilization. (6) Therefore the objection either collapses into a ban on practical reflection (absurd) or it secretly relies on a hidden normative standard for when revision is okay, which makes it just another theory competing on the same turf. Where's the slip? Is (3) an equivocation between "calculating with strangers" and "reflecting on one's own life," or does Williams have resources—maybe the idea that revision must come from *within* the project—to block the extension without letting utilitarianism back in? I want to keep (1) and (5), but I'm not sure what I'm paying.
