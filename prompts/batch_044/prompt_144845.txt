I'm working on a long-form feature about how classical liberal moral philosophy is being stress-tested by synthetic biology, and over the past year I've been tracking the published rulings of an interdisciplinary bioethics council that explicitly grounds its decisions in Robert Nozick's distinction between side-constraint protection for persons—rooted in the separateness of lives and non-fungible value—and an aggregative welfare calculus for beings that fall outside that category. The council has issued five verdicts that I can summarize, but I need help seeing whether they are running a single coherent rule or whether competing interpretations remain live.

Their record runs as follows. First, they prohibited all invasive cognitive research on great apes, ruling that removing an individual from its long-term social network for experimental ends violates an absolute constraint regardless of potential human benefit. Second, they permitted industrial pig farming but required enriched environments and analgesia for painful procedures, treating porcine welfare as serious yet subject to cost-benefit trade-offs rather than abolition. Third, they allowed regulated octopus neuroscience studies with mandatory analgesia and post-study release protocols, weighting suffering heavily but not recognizing an inviolable right. Fourth, they permitted destructive analysis of human-derived cortical organoids that exhibit nociceptive responses but lack integrated behavior, requiring only that exposure be minimized. Fifth, they approved standard high-density conditions for a genetically engineered "fearless" chicken that retains nociception but lacks fear circuitry, mandating analgesia solely for direct physical injury.

Now the council faces a proposal to engineer a "distributed cognition" colony: hundreds of rats linked by direct neural interfaces so that the network solves complex spatial problems no individual rat can solve, maintains a stable collective identity across replacements of individual nodes, and exhibits coordinated avoidance behavior, yet no single rat possesses autobiographical memory, future-oriented planning, or an independent preference structure. Would the council extend side-constraint protection to the colony as a non-fungible system, or treat it as an aggregate of replaceable units subject to welfare calculus? I want you to identify at least two plausible rules that jointly explain their earlier five verdicts, predict what each rule says about this colony, give your best-guess recommendation with the closest analog from their record identified, state honestly what principle the council would be adopting either way, and then specify the single further case—perhaps involving a solitary but cognitively advanced animal or a synthetic entity with unitary consciousness but no social role—that would best distinguish the competing interpretations.
