I work as a drafting clerk for a state-level legislature, and lately every bill preamble and every floor speech ends with some version of "this honors the will of the people." My supervisor asked me to build a quick vetting procedure I can run on these texts before they go to print -- she doesn't want philosophical essays, she wants a reusable checklist that takes about fifteen minutes and that I can defend if a legislator complains. I don't have formal training in political philosophy, so I need the procedure to actually embed whatever democratic theory knows about collective will: not just "people disagree," but the structural issue that a group doesn't have a mind, and any claim about its will depends on how you constitute it (aggregation, acclamation, representation that manufactures the consent it reports, and that weird negative-sovereignty idea my roommate brought up where the will is just whatever people haven't actively rejected).

Can you turn this into a step-by-step decision procedure? It needs to cover: what to check when the bill cites a specific policy prefference (like a tax rate); what to check when it claims the people "have spoken" after a narrow or contested vote; and what to check when it appeals to a "silent will" of people who never showed up. For each, include a quick test sentence or two I can apply, mark the steps that are really judgment calls in disguise, and work through all three examples including one where the procedure forces me to flag something that politically I actually agree with. Also be explicit about where the procedure breaks down and definately can't settle the dispute, and don't ask me to look up scholars or build a bibliography -- I just need the logic operationalized.
