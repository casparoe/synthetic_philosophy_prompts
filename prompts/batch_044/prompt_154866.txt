Take the Wolfenden Committee's 1957 report and the subsequent parliamentary path to the Sexual Offences Act 1967 in Britain as a live case study for the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism. I want more than a summary of the two philosophers; I want a disciplined mapping of their arguments onto the actual episode, running in both directions.

First, reconstruct Devlin's claim that shared morality functions as a kind of public good whose erosion threatens social cohesion, and apply it precisely to the parliamentary resistance to Wolfenden and to the report's own framing of private morality. What exactly is the social fabric at risk according to Devlin's logic, and does the Committee's privacy-based compromise address that risk or merely relocate it?

Second, apply Hart's harm principle together with his distinction between preventing harm and enforcing morality. The 1967 Act decriminalized private homosexual acts only between consenting adults over twenty-one, while retaining public-order offenses and maintaining an age of consent higher than that for heterosexual acts. Does this outcome represent a clean victory for Hart's framework, or does the persistence of these moral residues—particularly the elevated age threshold and the public/private boundary—reveal a gap in Hart's account of where harm ends and symbolic moral ordering begins?

Third, identify a specific feature of the legislative outcome that neither Hart nor Devlin anticipated or can comfortably explain—perhaps the way the partial, conditional nature of the reform itself served a symbolic function that was neither purely harm-preventive nor straightforwardly moralistic—and explain what that feature suggests about the limits of both theories.

Finally, assess whether this episode ultimately confirms legal moralism, undermines it, or reveals that the Hart–Devlin framing rests on a false dichotomy between harm and morality. Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
