I'm drafting a feature for a science-and-ideas publication on how Moritz Schlick's philosophy of life's meaning survives contact with modern philosophy of mind and empirical psychology. I need you to produce the formal joint statement that two adversarial camps could both sign after a good-faith, year-long collaboration.

Camp A: the Naturalist-Play defenders. They take Schlick's central argument—that meaning arises not from transcendent purpose but from present, intrinsic absorption in activity—to be essentially correct, and they want to extend it using contemporary work on flow states, autonomous motivation, and cognitive engagement.

Camp B: the Normative-Reconstructionists. They agree with Schlick's rejection of teleological and metaphysical accounts of meaning, but they argue that his own distinctions—between meaningful play and meaningless labor, or between worthy and unworthy absorption—smuggle in normative commitments that a strict logical-empiricist framework cannot justify.

The document must contain the following.

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. Both sides should be able to sign onto Schlick's denial that life's meaning requires an external purpose, a cosmic narrative, or posthumous reward; onto the observation that human beings frequently report profound meaning in the course of absorbed, goal-independent activity; onto the shared problem that any adequate theory must distinguish genuinely meaningful engagement from compulsive behavior, passive entertainment, or chemically induced pleasure; and onto the acknowledgment that modern empirical psychology offers relevant data about sustained attention and reward but does not by itself resolve whether such states carry normative weight.

Second, a disagreements table. Each disagreement must be phrased in wording that its own camp would accept. The table should cover: (1) whether Schlick's concept of pure engagement requires a second-order, reflective condition—call it the recursive-endorsement criterion—without which absorption collapses into mere affective state; (2) whether Schlick's contrast between youthful play and adult labor is purely descriptive or implicitly relies on a value hierarchy he officially denies; and (3) whether findings from neuroscience on sustained flow states support Schlick's view or instead show that durable engagement depends on progressive skill-development, which reintroduces purpose in a form Schlick rejected.

Third, the cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I see two: (i) Is reflective endorsement a necessary condition for meaning, or does introducing it destroy the very immediacy Schlick celebrates? (ii) Does the empirical durability of flow depend on teleological structure—growth, mastery, or progressive challenge—in a way that undermines Schlick's anti-teleological thesis?

Fourth, for each crux, provide one concrete case or body of evidence both camps agree bears on it, with each side's explicit prediction recorded in advance. For the first crux, use the hypothetical case of an astronaut in long-duration suspended animation who maintains consciousness solely through a fully immersive, self-directed virtual-reality environment with no external goal, no social connection, and no progressive task—would this preserve meaning, and does the presence or absence of a second-order narrative about the experience change the answer? For the second crux, use the empirical literature on long-term flow-state maintenance in expert practitioners versus novices—does the data show that sustained meaningful engagement correlates with progressive mastery (suggesting hidden teleology), or with moment-to-moment variability and present-focus (supporting Schlick)?

Finally, include a brief joint assessment: is any part of the dispute merely verbal, particularly around whether meaning functions descriptively (as a report of psychological absorption) or evaluatively (as a judgment of worthy living)?

Write this in polished American English, from the perspective of a journalist who covers science and ideas. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not invoke theories, figures, or texts so obscure that a well-read model without web access could not reasonably engage with them. If you reference any specific text or argument by Schlick, paraphrase without quotation marks.
