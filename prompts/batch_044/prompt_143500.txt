I'm taking a seminar on animal ethics and the critique of speciesism, and I'm trying to move beyond rehearsing standard positions by testing whether my own case judgments actually hang together. Here is where I land:

1. Industrial pig farming that inflicts chronic pain for cheap protein: clearly wrong.
2. Sport hunting of deer that involves brief terror and death: wrong, though I weigh the suffering less heavily than in case 1 because it is not prolonged and institutionalized.
3. A bioengineered bivalve that has nociceptors and withdrawal reflexes but lacks any central neural integration capable of subjective experience: harming it is not wrong in the same direct way, because there is no "someone" for whom the damage is bad.
4. Keeping a well-cared-for domestic dog that depends on humans for survival: not wrong, despite the dependency being artificially created.
5. Culling an invasive cane-toad population with painful methods to protect native ecosystems: permissible overall, but the suffering involved remains a pro tanto wrong that must be minimized.
6. Research using cerebral organoids grown from porcine neural stem cells; they display coordinated electrical patterns but have no body, no behavioral repertoire, and no evolutionary history: I am genuinely uncertain—harming them feels different from harming a live pig, yet I worry this is just an unprincipled bias toward embodied, "natural" animals.
7. Maintaining an anencephalic human infant with no consciousness solely for organ harvesting: wrong, but I think part of the wrongness concerns disrespect for human potential or family bonds rather than suffering alone, since conscious experience is absent.

Is there a principle that captures 1 through 5, delivers a clear verdict on 6, and does not force 7 into the same analysis as 1? I am pulled between a view centered on the capacity for conscious suffering and one that requires both sentience and membership in a kind that naturally supports an integrated psychological life. Could either be refined to accommodate all seven, or must I drop at least one judgment?

Please also apply your best account to a case I have not listed: autonomous wildlife-management drones that administer painless contraceptives to prevent starvation in overpopulated herds, but that occasionally malfunction and cause brief, unintended suffering in a small fraction of animals. Does your principle treat that unintended suffering as morally on par with intentional culling, or as a distinct category of harm?
