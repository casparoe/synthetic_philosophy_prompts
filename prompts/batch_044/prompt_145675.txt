I'm part of this worker-owned furniture collective — we reclaim wood and build custom pieces — and about three months ago we traced the original capital for our workshop building back to a factory that used forced labor in the 1940s. None of us were alive then, but now we're trying to redesign our internal culture around how we carry that legacy. A few members proposed writing an emotional charter where we formally designate guilt as our collective's default moral response: individual members write repair contracts, do direct restorative labor, and treat the harm as a discrete act they can fix and move past. The other faction wants to institutionalize shame instead — not toxic personal shame, but a collective ritual of status-loss and reintergration, like an annual acknowledgment where the group publicly carries the stain together and restores belonging through shared visibility rather than private confession.

I'm genuinely stuck because both sides sound like they're treating moral emotions as design specs you vote on, like picking a governance model for feelings. But is that philosophically coherent? Can a culture actually choose whether it operates as a guilt culture or a shame culture, or does that have to emerge orgaincally from practice? And if we're forced to pick, is guilt actually the better emotion for historical repair since it's tied to specific actions, or does shame do something for collective memory and identity that guilt can't reach? I keep feeling there's a deeper problem about whether moral emotions can be engineered at all hiding in our workshop drama, but I can't quite name the philosophical structure. Would love some help seeing what kind of problem this actually is.
