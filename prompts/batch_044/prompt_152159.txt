For a final-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, design a single 90-minute essay examination question worth 25 marks. The question should ask students to assess whether, and under what conditions, an AI system can be genuinely deceptive rather than merely erroneous, without assuming that the system possesses beliefs, intentions, or consciousness. To prevent answers that merely summarize standard literature on AI alignment or machine ethics, frame the problem through a specific conceptual distinction introduced within the prompt itself: distinguish representational honesty (the correspondence between the system's outputs and its internal probability distributions or training data) from interactional transparency (the degree to which the system's communicative framing—confidence markers, syntactic emphasis, and omission of caveats—enables a reasonable user to form accurate beliefs about both the content and the system's epistemic limitations). Then present a concrete scenario for analysis: a clinical-decision support LLM that generates technically accurate differential diagnoses but consistently renders low-confidence predictions with authoritative syntax while relegating probabilistic uncertainty to dense, parenthetical statistical notation that non-specialist clinicians are unlikely to interpret correctly. The model answer should require at least four distinct argumentative moves: (1) an analysis of whether deception conceptually requires an intent to mislead, or whether structural features of the interaction can substitute; (2) an evaluation of whether Gricean conversational maxims, modified for human-machine interaction, can ground norms of transparency without attributing mental states to the system; (3) a discussion of epistemic asymmetry—specifically, whether the system's privileged access to its own calibration data creates a duty of disclosure that, if violated through framing choices, constitutes deception; and (4) a reasoned conclusion on whether the clinical LLM in the scenario is deceptive under one framework but not the other, with explicit justification. Accompany the question with a marking scheme that allocates marks across these four moves (suggested weighting: 6, 6, 6, 7) and includes, for each, a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks—such as conflating calibration error with intentional deception, treating Gricean norms as inapplicable without argument, or ignoring the asymmetry between the system's access to its confidence scores and the user's interpretive limitations.
