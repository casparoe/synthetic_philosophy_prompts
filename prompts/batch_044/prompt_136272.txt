I sit on my city's Participatory Budgeting Oversight Committee, and we must recommend a concrete course of action before next month's council vote on climate-adaptation spending. Over the past year we convened a citizens' assembly using two unconventional design features: participants were selected through epistemic sortition weighted for cognitive diversity rather than demographic mirroring, and deliberation followed a two-stage reflective equilibrium in which jurors revised preliminary preferences after structured expert testimony but were shielded from lobbying and partisan framing. The assembly now recommends redirecting forty percent of the infrastructure budget toward decentralized rainwater capture, which conflicts with the engineering office's centralized flood-barrier plan. One faction of the committee argues that, because the procedure approximates an idealized deliberative situation, the recommendation carries indirect normative authority: we ought to adopt it not because it is technically optimal but because rational agents under fair, informed conditions would converge on it. The opposing faction contends that the idealization remains incomplete—participants lacked technical literacy, the epistemic weighting distorted representativeness, and indirect normativity collapses when procedural conditions are only partly satisfied—so we must revert to direct evaluation of the proposal's merits and likely reject it.

Please draft the formal memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one specific option—adopt the recommendation as binding, reject it in favor of expert assessment, or approve it conditionally with explicit safeguards and a review timeline—together with reasoning that takes both positions seriously. The memo should address whether partial idealization can still ground procedural authority, or whether the gap between actual and ideal conditions forces us back to direct evaluation of outcomes, and it must treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine weight rather than dismissing it. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or further sources. Please conclude with a line beginning exactly "Verdict:" followed by your bottom-line recommendation in a short phrase.
