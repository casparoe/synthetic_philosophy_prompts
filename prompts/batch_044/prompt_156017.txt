As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of biology, I am trying to calibrate how severely the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique undermines different evolutionary explanatory stances. Please rank the following four positions from 1 (least vulnerable to the critique) to 4 (most vulnerable): (A) strict pan-adaptationism, holding that every conserved morphological or molecular feature is an adaptation optimized for its present function; (B) pluralistic adaptationism, which treats adaptations as central but allows structural constraints and historical contingency to shape outcomes; (C) the classic spandrel position, which accepts adaptations but insists that many features are non-adaptive byproducts of selection elsewhere or of architectural necessity; and (D) a hypothetical stance I am calling "robustness spandrels," in which redundant genetic copies are conserved not because each is adaptive, nor as a direct byproduct of selection on another trait, but because system-level selection for robustness incidentally preserves non-functional duplicates—making them neither true adaptations nor classic spandrels, but parasitic conservations. For each rank, provide a one-sentence justification. Then identify the specific argument or conceptual distinction that, if shown to be mistaken, would most likely cause you to reorder the middle two positions, and explain briefly where your reasoning is most fragile.
