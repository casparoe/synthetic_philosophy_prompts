I run an upper-level epistemology seminar with about 12 grad students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class game that makes them feel why disjunctivism about perception is more than stubbornness against the argument from illusion — they should run into the problem that subjective sameness doesn't settle mental kind, but with a twist: use an "adversarial hallucination" setup where one group gets a direct peek at a hidden object behind a screen, and another group recieves an identically-worded report delivered through a deceptive causal chain (a printed card that claims live-feed origin but actually describes a matching fake planted by a confederate). Their task is to coordinate a single bet — do we open the box? — with roles (direct perceivers, report-receivers, one skeptical auditor), a point payoff that rewards correct group action but penalizes false consensus based on "we all experienced the same thing," and printed cards only so no tech is needed. Include rules, the payoff scheme, and a timeline. Then a debrief that maps the coordination breakdown onto metaphysical vs epistemic disjunctivism, says what will predictably go wrong the first run and how to handle it, and is honest about which feature — probably the transparency of experience or the factivity of perceptual knowledge — the activity can't actually reproduce and I have to supply verbally. Keep it runnable and focused.
