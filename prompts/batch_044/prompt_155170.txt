In a recent seminar on moral responsibility, a colleague kept invoking the principle that you can only hold someone responsible if they genuinely could have done otherwise—first to claim that determinism undermines blame, then immediately to dismiss Frankfurt-style counterexamples as “missing the point because the agent still retained the ability to refrain.” I think a family of distinct theses is hiding behind that single formulation, and I want them separated precisely. Could you lay out, as a menu, the different precise claims the slogan could be expressing—distinguishing, for each reading, whether the relevant “can” is a general ability, a particular opportunity on that occasion, the absence of external coercion, or an open causal pathway at the moment of decision; whether the relevant “otherwise” requires a different bodily movement, a different first-order choice, a different causal origin in the sense of sourcehood, or alignment with a higher-order volition; and whether the condition applies to the action, to the will behind it, or to both. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, and specifically whether Frankfurt-style cases defeat it or only a misinterpretation of it. Also indicate which readings would actually support my colleague’s two uses—the anti-compatibilist argument and the dismissal of Frankfurt cases—and whether those two uses are consistent with one another. Please separate what is well-established in the philosophy of action literature from what remains genuinely contested—for instance, about whether Frankfurt’s counterexamples genuinely refute the principle or only certain formulations of it—and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
