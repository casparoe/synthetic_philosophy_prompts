As a journalist covering the intersection of political philosophy and cross-cultural ethics, I am preparing background material for a long-form feature on why standard definitions of corruption fail to travel well across different social contexts. In recent interviews with scholars, a recurring tension has emerged between anthropological descriptions of clientelism and gift-giving as forms of social glue, and the normative frameworks embedded in international transparency indices. For the article's appendix, I would like to commission an intellectual exercise—an exam-style prompt designed for an upper-level graduate seminar in comparative political ethics—that forces students to navigate this tension without retreating into either unexamined cultural relativism or an uncritical universalism.

Please design a single 90-minute essay question, together with a model answer of approximately 800 words and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks. The question should ask students to evaluate whether Western-derived anti-corruption norms can be applied to practices of reciprocal gift-exchange and patron-client obligation without conceptual distortion. It must not be answerable by summarizing institutional reports or by rehearsing the familiar universalism-versus-relativism dichotomy. Instead, it should require students to construct or critique a principled distinction between corruption, legitimate reciprocity, and constitutive social obligation—perhaps by treating corruption as a family-resemblance concept rather than a natural kind, or by testing whether a proceduralist account that focuses on breach of public trust rather than on exchange itself can accommodate culturally embedded practices.

The model answer should clearly separate what is conceptually settled—for instance, that corruption involves an abuse of entrusted power for private benefit—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, such as whether patronage networks in certain contexts constitute corruption when they simultaneously sustain democratic accountability through informal channels. Do not present any contested interpretive claim about specific societies as though it were the consensus view among anthropologists or political theorists. The marking scheme should identify five must-hit analytical moves, assign marks to each with the total summing to 25, include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail each point, and explicitly distinguish an A-level response—which introduces a novel conceptual framework or a precise counter-example—from a B-level response that competently maps the debate without advancing it.
