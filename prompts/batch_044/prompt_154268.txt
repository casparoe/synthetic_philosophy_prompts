I've been discussing the procreation asymmetry with a study group that mostly moves in the rationalist-adjacent philosophy space, and one member reconstructed the argument in a way that pushes it well past its usual biological domain. I think the extension is illegitimate, but I can't isolate the precise failure without making the asymmetry look ad hoc. Here's the reconstructed chain, with the novel twist embedded in step three:

(1) If it is wrong to create a being whose existence is net-negative, then there is a specific moral reason against that act of creation. (2) By contrast, failing to create a being whose existence would be net-positive is not bad for anyone who never exists; hence there is no parallel reason in favor of creation grounded in that being's interests. (3) This asymmetry applies not merely to biological reproduction but to any causal process capable of instantiating subjective experience — including, in principle, running a computation that generates consciousness with a particular valence profile. (4) Consider a controller with three discrete settings: default (no computation runs), left (algorithm A, which, if it supports consciousness, has net-positive welfare), and right (algorithm B, net-negative welfare). (5) By (1), selecting right is wrong. By (2), there is no reason — derived from the interests of the potential conscious being — to select left over default.

(6) Now consider a stochastic variant: activation runs A with 99% probability and B with 1%. The non-zero risk of B supplies a reason not to activate; yet running A deterministically still generates no corresponding reason, since the absence of A harms no one. (7) Generalizing: for any action with a non-zero probability of generating negative consciousness and some probability of generating positive consciousness, the asymmetry supplies reasons against, never reasons for. (8) Given that any novel computation, cognitive architecture, or emulation carries epistemic uncertainty about suffering, this seems to entail a near-total prohibition on initiating any potentially conscious process unless suffering can be ruled out with certainty.

The conclusion is clearly absurd — we do not think that running a well-understood, apparently benign algorithm is morally forbidden because consciousness is metaphysically mysterious. So where is the error? I want to preserve (1) and (2) intact, and I'm open to conceding that (3) illicitly extends the asymmetry from biological birth to algorithmic instantiation. But if that's the mistake, I need to know what non-arbitrary principle distinguishes the two domains without collapsing into biological essentialism. Alternatively, is there a hidden aggregation failure between (5) and (6), or a modal slide that treats merely possible beings differently in stochastic versus deterministic contexts? I'd prefer an answer that names the step, explains why it initially looks defensible, and prices the cost of giving it up — especially if the cost is accepting that the asymmetry has narrower scope than its proponents usually claim.
