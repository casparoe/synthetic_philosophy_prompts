Consider the pedagogical practice—prominent in Plutarch's Parallel Lives and largely abandoned in later hagiographic writing—of pairing biographical subjects from different political and cultural worlds (a Greek legislator with a Roman consul, a republican general with a monarch) not to rank them but to present them as parallel, unresolved mirrors for the reader's moral judgment. The biographer includes comparable episodes of crisis, betrayal, or death, yet refuses to synthesize the pair into a single normative model; contradictions within each life are preserved rather than suppressed, and the reader is asked to deliberate across temperaments rather than to imitate one integrated exemplar. What philosophical commitments about moral cognition, exemplarity, and the possibility of cross-cultural moral knowledge does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit theory: does it require that virtues and vices are culturally invariant enough to be recognized across contexts, or can it function even if moral concepts are partly constituted by the comparative framing itself? Distinguish whether the practice strictly presupposes analogical transfer between lives, deliberative weighing of incommensurable characters, or emotional admiration directed at fractured wholes. Further, assess whether this paired, unresolved format is philosophically consistent with the hagiographic tradition's tendency to resolve contradiction into a unified portrait of sanctity, or whether the two traditions depend on incompatible accounts of what a life reveals about character. Finally, describe what would have to change in the pedagogical practice if its implicit view were replaced by a theory that treats moral learning as direct emotional attachment to an unproblematic, singular model rather than as comparative judgment across incomplete lives.
