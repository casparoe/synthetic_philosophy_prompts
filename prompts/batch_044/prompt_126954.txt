I have been developing a position in moral philosophy—partly in conversation with colleagues preparing for a workshop on harm aggregation—that I want to stress-test. The familiar torture-versus-dust-specks case asks whether a sufficiently vast number of barely perceptible harms can ever match or exceed one catastrophic harm. Most discussants treat this as a problem of scale or of psychological salience: torture is worse because it is more intense, or because human cognition is wired to notice localized suffering. I want to defend something stronger and, I think, stranger. I hold that torture and distributed dust-speck suffering are not merely different in degree or in our cognitive response, but belong to incommensurable moral ontologies. Localized, intentional infliction of extreme suffering activates a deontic structure—an agent-centered wrong that generates direct obligations of rescue and redress—while distributed, sub-threshold harms generate only statistical obligations of risk reduction or optimization. No summation of the latter can produce the former, because distributed harms lack the relational property of directed wrongfulness that makes torture a specific kind of moral fact rather than merely a large quantity of discomfort. What would have to be true for this view to fail? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be. This should include: empirical findings in moral psychology about whether people actually distinguish these ontological categories in judgment; formal arguments from decision theory or population ethics showing that incommensurability collapses into a weighted utility function with appropriate curvature; and philosophical cases—perhaps involving collective agency, institutional complicity, or structural violence—demonstrating that distributed harms can indeed instantiate the same deontic structures as localized ones. I also want an honest assessment of whether I have qualified the view into unfalsifiability, such that any counterexample can be dismissed as "merely statistical," and whether that insulation is a defensible feature or a fatal flaw. Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; I need the arguments themselves mapped out, and I want to know which defeaters a committed defender of my position would concede as genuinely damaging rather than merely awkward.
