In a recent philosophy-of-psychiatry seminar, a participant repeatedly invokes the Szasz-derived slogan that mental illness is a myth to shut down discussion. Most recently, he used it to reject my claim that even if certain diagnostic categories are partly socially constructed, patients can still suffer a distinctive epistemic injustice when clinicians systematically discount their first-person reports. I want to arrive at office hours with a precise disambiguation rather than a historical summary. Could you lay out, as an explicit menu of distinct readings, what precise thesis or theses the assertion that mental illness is a myth might actually be advancing? For each reading, specify whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or conceptually confused; clarify what it commits one to about the ontology of psychiatric conditions, the necessity or absence of biological etiology, the legitimacy of medical or coercive intervention, and whether psychiatric diagnosis can ever be value-neutral; and then indicate, for each reading, whether it genuinely licenses dismissing concerns about epistemic injustice in clinical encounters, or whether it only undermines a specifically biological-reductionist model of disorder. I do not need a bibliography, a chronology of Szasz's publications, or citations to secondary literature—only the logical geography of the slogan and which of its interpretations actually do the argumentative work being demanded of it.
