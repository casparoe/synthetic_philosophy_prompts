I am preparing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar for first-year philosophy PhD students who have thoroughly internalized Locke's memory criterion and Parfit's reductionist view of persons as bundles of psychological relations, but who have never encountered Ubuntu or relational conceptions of personhood. The concept I need them to grasp is that personhood is not an intrinsic property of an individual substrate but rather a continuously renegotiated relational achievement, sustained through what I will call "relational maintenance practices" — for instance, the way shared labor on communal land is not merely cooperative but is itself constitutive of the participants' moral standing as persons. Before you construct the plan, take a moment to map the conceptual dependency graph: which analytic intuitions must be explicitly suspended before the relational ontology can even be heard, and where in the sequence students are most likely to smuggle in individualist assumptions. Then present your teaching plan — not the explanation itself — as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Your five items should cover: first, the precise order in which the components must be introduced and why the analytic framework has to be bracketed before any positive claim is made; second, the single carrying example that will anchor the session, specifying what features it must possess and which familiar Western analog — such as a social contract or cooperative enterprise — it must be rigorously distinguished from; third, the three misconceptions these students will predictably generate — specifically, that this view is merely social constructivism about the self, that it collapses into political communitarianism, and that it is interchangeable with feminist care ethics — together with the exact moment in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off; fourth, the one diagnostic question you will pose at the end to determine whether they understand the relational dependency as ontological rather than psychological, political, or ethical; and fifth, what you will explicitly leave out — such as the full metaphysics of ancestral presence and specific ritual protocols — with a brief justification of why omitting these elements protects rather than distorts the core idea for this audience.
