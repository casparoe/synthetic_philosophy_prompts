I am writing a novel in which a bioethicist, after years of fieldwork observing invertebrate cognition, becomes convinced that moral status is not a binary switch but a graded spectrum, attenuating from primates through insects and, in some minimal form, to simple homeostatic devices like thermostats that regulate temperature through negative feedback loops. As I draft the chapters in which she presents this view to a skeptical philosophical audience, I have tried to reconstruct the actual debate she would encounter, and I find it curiously frozen. On one side, gradualists cite comparative neurobiology and behavioral markers to argue that sentience, suffering, and welfare come in degrees; on the other, thresholdists insist that without a sharp, non-arbitrary boundary, the concept of moral status collapses into normative incoherence—implying that we owe consideration to inanimate objects merely because they maintain equilibrium. Both sides are fully aware of the standard repertoire—the vagueness objection, the developmental analogy, the practical need for legal lines, the risk of anthropomorphism—and yet in every reconstructed dialogue the argument stalls at precisely the point where one party asks what kind of work moral categorization is meant to perform: tracking a natural kind, serving a regulatory function, or expressing a relational attitude toward beings that can suffer. My own suspicion is that the disagreement is not empirical—neither side denies the neuroscience or the continuity of biological organization—but conceptual, perhaps even verbal, with 'status' doing different labor in each framework. Yet I worry I may be missing a hidden formal question, perhaps about the logic of comparative judgments across species or the measurability of interspecies welfare, that is actually sustaining the deadlock beneath the surface.

Could you diagnose the structure of this impasse, drawing on what gradualists and thresholdists actually say about gradation, boundaries, and normative force, rather than offering a survey of positions? I want to know whether this is a substantive disagreement about the nature of moral properties, a verbal dispute about the word 'status,' two groups answering different questions—one descriptive, one prescriptive—under a single heading, or an ill-posed question that conflates degrees of consciousness with degrees of moral consideration. And given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress: a conceptual distinction—say, between considerability and significance—a formal result about the logic of gradation, an empirical finding about minimal cognition, or an argument that no resolution should be expected because the underlying intuitions are bedrock? I ask this not for a verdict on my character's position, but because I need to understand, for the narrative's integrity, whether her opponents are refusing to move because they misunderstand her claim, because they share it but reject its implications, or because the question itself is shaped differently in their hands.
