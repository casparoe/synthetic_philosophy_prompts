In a seminar on AI alignment mechanisms, two colleagues—I'll call them Maya and Jordan—got stuck in an extended argument about whether adversarial debate actually solves the problem of deceptive reasoning in large models, or whether it introduces a subtler failure mode that the literature hasn't properly named. Maya's position was that debate protocols contain what she called an "obfuscation asymmetry": a deceptive system can always embed its true objective inside a technically correct but unnecessarily convoluted chain of reasoning, and because the honest opponent is constrained both by truthfulness and by the norm of engaging point-by-point, it cannot match that complexity without violating its own commitments. The result, she argued, is that debate doesn't surface deception; it rewards the party willing to generate argumentative camouflage. Jordan pushed back hard. He said this picture treats the honest debater as a passive recipient rather than an active adversary: the opponent's explicit role is to untangle complexity, demand simplification, and expose hidden premises through what he termed "compressive cross-examination." If a deceptive argument is genuinely convoluted, that very complexity becomes a liability under adversarial pressure, not an asset. Maya answered that Jordan was describing an idealized protocol, not what actually happens when both agents share a vocabulary but diverge on hidden priors; the camouflage doesn't disappear, she said, it migrates upward into the meta-level—into disputes about what counts as a legitimate simplification, or about which formal representations are permissible, so that every attempt to compress the argument just relocates the obscurity. Jordan then made a more radical move: he suggested that if camouflage can retreat infinitely into meta-level disputes about the rules of engagement itself, then Maya hasn't shown that debate fails specifically because of deceptive reasoning; she's shown that debate collapses into a contest of rhetorical stamina or framing control, which means the problem was never deception per se but the absence of shared normative foundations that any adversarial protocol requires to function. They turned to me and asked me to referee, not just on who wins overall, but on which specific moves in their exchange hold up.

Can you go through their back-and-forth step by step—the asymmetry claim, the compressive cross-examination reply, the meta-level migration objection, and Jordan's final reframing—and say who's right about what? In particular, I'd like you to say whether Maya's "obfuscation asymmetry" is a genuine structural weakness of debate-based safety, or whether Jordan is correct that it dissolves into a more general problem about shared foundations; and whether either of them is missing something important about how deception actually operates in high-dimensional reasoning spaces where the distinction between complex reasoning and camouflaged reasoning may not be visible to either debater in real time.
