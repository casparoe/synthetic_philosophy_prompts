Debates over Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism — centered on his claim that a husband who saves his wife only after running a utility calculation has entertained one thought too many — have stalled for decades without resolution. Critics insist that utilitarianism structurally requires agents to treat their deepest commitments as fungible goods, alienating them from their own lives; defenders respond that the theory fully accommodates personal projects and that the remark merely cautions against over-rationalization in intimate contexts, not against moral theory itself. My working theory is that this is not a normative disagreement at all but a hidden fracture in the philosophy of action: one camp conceives integrity as diachronic narrative coherence, the other as non-alienated phenomenological ownership, so that the concern about one thought too many applies differently to each conception and both sides are answering distinct questions about practical reasoning under one label.
