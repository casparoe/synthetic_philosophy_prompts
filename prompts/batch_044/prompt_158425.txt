Since I recently began directing a small research group, I have attempted to revive Benjamin Franklin's scheme of moral perfection as a practical discipline rather than a historical curiosity, maintaining a digital analog of his weekly virtue chart. Reviewing his autobiographical account, I notice that Franklin treats lapses in Silence and Order somewhat differently: he appears to excuse an occasional breach of Silence when speaking serves a larger purpose, yet he regards disorder in one's affairs or papers as inexcusable regardless of utility. He also added Humility only after an acquaintance accused him of pride, and he seems to treat hidden satisfaction in one's own virtue-tracking as particularly insidious. Given these patterns, consider this case. I have discovered a minor citation error in a manuscript submitted by a junior colleague. Correcting it immediately would satisfy Justice and prevent scholarly disorder, but it would also reveal that I had not yet completed my own review—thereby exposing a prior failure in Industry or Order—while my decision to remain silent preserves the appearance of diligence and allows me to feel privately satisfied with my own discretion. Would Franklin classify my omission primarily as a failure of Justice, of Order, or of Humility? Which of his earlier self-judgments provides the closest analog, and if his recorded precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, what principle would I be adopting if I prioritized Justice over Humility, and what different principle if I did the reverse?
