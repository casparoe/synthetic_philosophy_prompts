Since I have recently begun weighing serious decisions about whether to have children, I have found myself drawn to arguments about how the number of people who might exist should affect our reasoning about the world, and in particular to the debate between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, along with the cautionary figure sometimes called the presumptuous philosopher. In the conversations and texts I have encountered, there is a recurring analogy that I would like you to examine with precision. People compare the reasoning licensed by self-indication to the situation of a visitor who enters a library, notices that books are present, and concludes that the library must be very large, on the grounds that a larger collection contains more volumes and therefore makes the presence of at least one book more probable. The analogy is invoked both to defend the idea that our own existence should shift our credence toward cosmologies or policies that predict larger populations of observers, and to criticize that idea by suggesting it illegitimately moves from a statistical expectation about aggregates to a belief about the particular location the visitor occupies. I want to understand exactly what corresponds to what in this comparison. In the library scenario, what plays the role of the reference class, the prior distribution over possible worlds, the update rule applied upon observing one's own existence, and the distinction between reasoning about a randomly selected observer versus reasoning about the total number of observers? Where does the analogy break down, particularly with respect to the fact that the library visitor occupies a specific spatial position and can in principle explore other rooms, whereas an observer in anthropic reasoning has no analogous external vantage point from which to survey other possible worlds? Does the error attributed to the presumptuous philosopher map cleanly onto a particular mistake in the library story, such as confusing the expected number of books in a randomly selected library with the expected size of the library given that one particular book was found? Or does the analogy instead obscure the philosophical difficulty by importing physical intuitions about accessibility and exploration that do not apply to modal reasoning? Finally, if this comparison fails in the ways you identify, is there a closer analogy, perhaps drawn from statistical mechanics or from decision theory under uncertainty, that preserves the structural features of self-indication without importing these misleading spatial intuitions?
