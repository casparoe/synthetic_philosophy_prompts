ok so ive been thinking about what a decent theory of historical causation actually needs to do if its gonna take microhistory seriously without just being a footnote to state history. like, not just include the little people but actually explain how some local mess in a village in 1847 can be causally tied to an empire collapsing fifty years later, without treating those villagers as passive carriers of big forces.

so what are the actual desiderata? i mean the hard ones, not the fluff. it needs to handle:
- cases where micro-level intentions get totally reversed at macro scale (call it the aggregation paradox)
- it cant collapse micro explanations into macro ones or vice versa
- it has to deal with the fact that state archives are systematically biased but not treat that bias as the only reality
- it should say why some micro-events scale up and others vanish, without just saying it was important
- temporal lag where causation skips generations, and how that doesnt break the micro-to-macro link

which of these are non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences? and is there a pair thats jointly unsatisfiable? like demanding full intentional transparency at the micro level plus full structural determinism at the macro?

im not asking for a reading list or citations, just the spec. and if one of these requirements knocks out like 90% of existing approaches, say so.
