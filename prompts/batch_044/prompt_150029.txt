A colleague laid out this argument in a philosophy of causation seminar, and I cannot locate the weak link, though I feel strongly that the conclusion is wrong. The premises each seem defensible, yet together they seem to collapse the distinction between acting and allowing.

(1) If an omission O causes an event E, then O must satisfy a counterfactual test: had the agent performed the omitted action, E would not have occurred.

(2) Positive actions also satisfy this same counterfactual test when they cause E. Therefore, the logical form of omission-causation is identical to that of action-causation.

(3) Consider a factory manager who fails to install an available emissions scrubber (omission O), and the factory emits pollutant P, which causes illness I in a nearby community.

(4) By (1) and (2), O is a cause of I on exactly the same footing as P is a cause of I: both are counterfactually necessary conditions in the actual sequence leading to I.

(5) But if O and P stand on the same causal footing, then the presence of O (the absence of the scrubber) should be able to be "canceled out" by the presence of the scrubber. More precisely, in the counterfactual world where the scrubber is installed, P is still emitted but I does not occur. This shows that P is causally insufficient for I unless O is also present. Hence O does not merely accompany P; it enables P's causal efficacy.

(6) That is absurd: we do not think that installing a scrubber retroactively strips the original emission of its causal power. The emission remains harmful in itself; the scrubber merely blocks the pathway.

(7) So (4) must be false: omissions cannot be causes in the same sense as actions.

(8) Yet if omissions are not causes at all, then doctrines of negligent omission in tort law are metaphysically groundless, since negligence requires that the omission caused the harm.

(9) Therefore we must either reject the counterfactual analysis of causation for omissions—saving negligence law at the cost of a unified metaphysics—or explain why omissions and actions interact in the way (5) describes without being causally equivalent, without invoking an ad hoc distinction.

The colleague wants to keep (1) and (8) if possible. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, which plausible premise is least costly to give up?
