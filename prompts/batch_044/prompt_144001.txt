I'm on the review board for a national physics funding agency and we're getting swamped with proposals about multiverse landscapes and string-theory scenarios that basically admit they can't be tested in any lab we'll have in the next fifty years. The applicants keep talking about "non-empirical confirmation" - like mathematical consistency, uniqueness of explanation, or this thing my colleague called "structural underdetermination," where a theory is supposedly supported just because every rival breaks some deeper consistency rule we already accept. I don't fully get it, and I need something reusable that my panel can actually run on these proposals without turning every meeting into a philosphy seminar.

Can you write me a decision procedure - a checklist or flowchart in words - that takes whatever philosophers of science actually mean by non-empirical confirmation and turns it into steps we can apply? It should go in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer for each question, mark the exact points where the answer should be "reject," and be honest about which steps are just value judgments wearing a procedure costume. The hard cases it has to cover are: theories that predict literally nothing observable in principle, theories that predict things technically observable but practially impossible, and arguments that lean on this "structural underdetermination" idea. Then apply it to two actual-style proposals: one is a multiverse model using landscape statistics and anthropic reasoning to explain our physical constants being life-friendly, but it admits zero direct experimental signature; the other is a string-theoretic framework that claims it's the only 10-dimensional geometry that doesn't collapse into contradiction with quantum gravity results we already trust - what my colleague might call "explanatory anchoring" - but it doesn't constrain any parameter we can actually measure. I have a soft spot for the second one, so I need the procedure to tell me honestly whether I'm just biased.

Also tell me where this procedure is guaranteed to misfire, what it can't do, and whether there's any way to make it less subjective without just pretending the subjectivity isn't there. Oh, and please don't ask me for citations or a reading list - I won't have time to recieve them and half my panel can't access the journals anyway.
