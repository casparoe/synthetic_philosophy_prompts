As a novelist working on counterfactual historical fiction, I keep returning to the Great Man versus structural explanation debate—specifically whether Nazi Germany was counterfactually impossible without Hitler, or whether structural conditions (Versailles resentment, economic collapse, bureaucratic militarism) would have produced an ideologically equivalent regime regardless. Rather than asking for a historical verdict, I want to prioritize the open conceptual questions that actually govern how we model collective contingency and individual agency in narrative form. Among the unresolved issues: whether structural accounts can preserve the specific ideological content of Nazism without ad hoc adjustments; whether counterfactual dependence in history requires actual-world causal laws or only narrative coherence; and whether the debate silently conflates explanatory priority with moral culpability. For a philosopher with limited time and no archival training, which of these is upstream of the others, which is genuinely tractable with conceptual tools alone, and which—if settled—would most change how we construct or evaluate stories that turn on a single life? Rank them with explicit criteria, defend the ordering in terms of dependency and tractability rather than intuition, and say what kind of result—conceptual distinction, formal model, or empirical finding—would be sufficient to revise your ranking.
