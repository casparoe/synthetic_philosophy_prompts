Consider a university policy that audits undergraduate syllabi for “viewpoint diversity”: reviewers check whether required readings include opposing political positions, but they do not check whether assessments reward revising one’s beliefs, or whether students ever encounter evidence from outside their disciplinary framework. The policy treats the presence of disagreement in the reading list as sufficient for epistemic health, and it treats all failures of belief formation as a single problem—students being surrounded by like-minded peers—without distinguishing structures that merely hide information from structures that actively teach members to distrust outsiders. What philosophical commitments about belief, evidence, and social knowledge does this audit presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of epistemic risk it embeds: does it assume that echo chambers and epistemic bubbles are the same pathology, or that one is primary? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and specify what the audit criteria would have to measure if they instead treated cultivated distrust—not mere informational isolation—as the core failure.
