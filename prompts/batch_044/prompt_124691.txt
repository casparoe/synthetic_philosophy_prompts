I defend a reconstructed version of Dewey's position that democracy is not a form of government but a mode of cooperative social inquiry: democratic legitimacy derives not from popular sovereignty, representation, or procedural fairness, but from the institutionalization of experimental intelligence—treating collective policies as working hypotheses that are tested by their consequences and open to revision through public, cooperative investigation. On this account, electoral mechanisms are instrumentally valuable only insofar as they serve this fallibilist logic; they are not constitutive of democracy. I extend this by holding that any political arrangement—whether theocratic, nationalist, or grounded in group-based cultural recognition—that treats particular moral traditions, identities, or claims as non-negotiable foundations is incompatible with democratic inquiry, because such commitments block the very revisability the method requires. I want an honest assessment of what would count against this position. Which historical or contemporary cases would demonstrate that experimental policy revision succeeds in the absence of democratic institutions, or fails despite their presence? Which conceptual arguments show that fallibilism either collapses into an unworkable relativism or secretly depends on a non-experimental normative authority? What should defenders of this view concede as genuinely damaging—for example, that Dewey's normative commitments about growth or education are not themselves products of experimental testing? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing observations that would require abandoning the core claim from those that could be accommodated by qualification. Does the view risk being insulated into unfalsifiability, say by defining "experimental intelligence" so broadly that authoritarian regimes with technical advisory boards qualify? If so, is that insulation a defect for a normative democratic theory, or appropriate for a regulative ideal? I would personally be most persuaded by an argument showing that experimental fallibilism either presupposes a substantive conception of human flourishing that it cannot justify experimentally, or that it lacks the resources to distinguish democratic revision from mere power-driven policy churn; please weigh that specific concern and indicate whether you regard it as decisive, awkward but manageable, or based on a misunderstanding of the position.
