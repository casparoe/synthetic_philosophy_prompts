I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been trying to model the inefficacy objection using causal-graph reasoning, and I've built an argument that now looks like it proves far too much. I can't find the bug.

(1) For an individual act to be morally wrong, it must be a but-for cause of harm; if the harm unfolds identically without my contribution, then my contribution hasn't done anything wrong.

(2) Large-scale collective harms—climate damage, industrial meat production, election outcomes—are causally redundant systems: they have thousands or millions of inputs, and removing any single node (one flight, one burger, one vote) doesn't change the aggregate output.

(3) So in these domains no individual contribution is wrong, and no individual is morally required to abstain or participate differently.

(4) The same redundancy applies to overdetermined rescue: if a thousand people could each call emergency services, and the call is made regardless, then any single person's failure is not a but-for cause of the victim's death.

(5) Therefore, if (1) holds, nobody in a large bystander group is required to help, and nobody in a large consuming or voting population is required to stop contributing to harm.

(6) But this seems to excuse people in precisely the contexts where we think collective responsibility is strongest; it also implies that in any highly redundant distributed system, individual ethical constraints vanish entirely.

(7) So either (1) is false, or moral obligation collapses whenever more than a handful of agents are involved.

I'd prefer to keep (1) if I can, because it maps cleanly onto how I think about causation in code—if a function's return value doesn't depend on a parameter, changing that parameter isn't a bug. But I also don't want to accept (5). Which step is the error? If it's (1), what does giving it up cost for other parts of ethics? If it's (2), how do we account for the empirical fact that individual carbon footprints or votes genuinely don't move global metrics?
