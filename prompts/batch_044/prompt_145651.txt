hey, I'm sketching a formal "low-impact" constraint for an alignment idea and I think I've argued myself into a circle. Here are the steps I wrote down:

(1) To call an AI "low impact" we need a counterfactual baseline—what the world-state would have been if the agent never acted.
(2) In any structural causal model, that baseline requires fixing some exogenus variables and letting endogenous ones vary by the equations.
(3) If we want the baseline to be value-neutral, we should only fix physical laws and initial conditions right before deployment; everything else—human choices, ecological states, institutional paths—should be left free.
(4) But with only laws and initial conditions fixed, the counterfactual trajectory diverges exponentially—weather shifts, people choose differently, economies drift—so comparing actual vs. baseline tells us nothing about "side effects" versus ordinary stochastc variation.
(5) So to make the comparison meaningful, we have to hold constant at least some value-relevant variables: maybe population in 2100, CO2 trajectory, existence of specific institutions.
(6) Once we do that, we've implicitly decided which outcomes are worth protecting, i.e. we've done a big chunk of value specification.
(7) People try to dodge this with "local" impact—compare only within a bounded region or window. But drawing that boundary requires knowing what's inside versus outside the protected sphere, which again depends on what's valuable locally.
(8) So either "low impact" is undefined, or it secretly imports the full value alignment it's meant to sidestep. Even my idea of an "exclusion zone" around the agent's sensors just pushes the problem into defining the zone's ontology.

This seems to prove too much—surely I can say "don't knock over the vase" without solving ethics? I can't spot the slip though. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (3) than (6), since value-neutrality sounds like the right aspiration. Where does the argument actually break, and what's the cost of breaking it there?
