Two members of my philosophy discussion group, Maya and Leo, recently argued about Kierkegaard's Abraham and whether the teleological suspension of the ethical can be coherently extended beyond the religious command. I listened without taking sides, and they have asked me to report whether anyone can adjudicate their exchange. Please work through their disagreement round by round, say who is right about which specific point, identify where each makes a genuinely decent argument, state who is closer to the mark overall, and flag anything important that both have overlooked.

This is how their exchange unfolded.

Maya began by arguing that Abraham in Fear and Trembling should be understood as a tragic hero on the model of Agamemnon. She said he faces an irreconcilable clash between his ethical duty to Isaac and a higher demand, but that the ethical order itself is never genuinely suspended—merely overridden in this particular case. The depth of his suffering, she claimed, demonstrates that the universal remains fully in force within him.

Leo responded that this interpretation collapses the very distinction Kierkegaard draws. Agamemnon can justify himself to the chorus and remains within the universal; Abraham is silent precisely because no ethical vocabulary can legitimate his act. The teleological suspension is therefore not an internal conflict within ethics but a real suspension of the universal in favour of the absolute relation to God. If Abraham were only tragic, he would be comprehensible, yet Kierkegaard insists he is not.

Maya then advanced a further claim. Even if one grants the suspension of the ethical, she proposed that there must also be what she called a teleological suspension of the communicative. Abraham's silence, she argued, is not merely the absence of ethical justification but signals the collapse of shared linguistic norms that depend upon the universal being intact. Without that framework, she said, there is no common vocabulary through which Abraham could receive or express the divine command, leaving the knight of faith linguistically isolated in principle.

Leo countered that this extension overreaches and undermines the text. The command originates outside the universal, but its reception does not depend on ethical language to supply meaning; Abraham grasps it as an absurd, singular address. To claim that language itself collapses is to render Abraham's relation to God unintelligible even to himself, which contradicts the notion of faith as an inward, passionate certainty. The suspension concerns the ethical, Leo insisted, not the conditions of meaning or communication.

In addition, identify what both disputants are missing, especially regarding the role of the absurd, the distinction between religiousness A and religiousness B, or any other relevant element of Kierkegaard's framework.
