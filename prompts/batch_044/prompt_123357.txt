You are a philosopher of mind and an experienced workshop facilitator. Before proposing anything, think carefully about the design: begin by briefly brainstorming three different ways the qualitative gap between Popperian and Gregorian cognition could be embodied in rules, roles, or payoff structures; then select the single version that best forces participants to discover the problem through their own actions rather than through any explanation you provide.

I am running an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar with 14 graduate students, one 75-minute session. The conceptual focus is the taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—specifically, the transition from creatures that generate and test hypotheses individually to creatures that depend on culturally transmitted, symbolically encoded tools. I want students to feel, not just state, why Gregorian cognition is qualitatively different: they should experience the power of symbolic scaffolding (solving problems by importing others’ tested solutions) and, simultaneously, discover its specific vulnerability to ambiguous or misaligned transmission in a way that pure Popperian trial-and-error avoids.

Design a structured in-class simulation with no lecture component. Split the group into two competing teams—Popperians and Gregorians—attempting to solve an identical multi-stage logic puzzle under different cognitive constraints. Popperians may only act, observe direct outcomes, and individually revise strategies; they may not write, speak strategically, or share notes. Gregorians may not observe direct outcomes of others’ actions; instead, they must solve the puzzle by exchanging written “tool-manuals” (restricted-symbol messages) that encode tested strategies, but the communication channel contains a hidden noise mechanism: one symbol in every manual is ambiguous by design, allowing systematic misinterpretation that compounds across generations. Use only symbolic tokens or points for payoffs—no real money.

Your deliverable must include:
- Exact group divisions, role rules, and the restricted-communication protocol.
- The puzzle structure, the token/payoff scheme that rewards correct stage-completion, and the hidden ambiguity mechanism.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes.
- A debrief that maps each observed failure or success in the activity onto a specific feature of Gregorian cognition (cultural accumulation, symbolic offloading, and vulnerability to second-order misalignment), with explicit links to moments in the play.
- An honest note on which feature of the four-kind taxonomy—specifically the evolutionary continuity between Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian forms—this simulation necessarily cannot reproduce, and how you will verbally supply that missing piece in under two minutes.
- A prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run (e.g., Popperians finishing too quickly, Gregorians ignoring the manuals, or the ambiguity going unnoticed) and exactly how you will handle it without breaking the experience.

The core idea must emerge from the dynamics of play, not from your post-hoc explanation. Do not ask students to read texts during the session, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
