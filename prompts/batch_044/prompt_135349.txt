You are asked to advise someone facing this concrete conflict. Last year they enthusiastically co-founded a neighborhood mutual-aid network and made explicit commitments to coordinate its logistics through the summer. Now an unexpected need has arisen: a close family member in another region requires sustained, hands-on care during the same period, and leaving would effectively dissolve the commitments they made. They have tried to model their decision through an idealized deliberation—imagining what they would choose with perfect foresight, no emotional fatigue, no loyalty biases, and full information about everyone affected—and that modeled self clearly prioritizes family care and treats the mutual-aid commitments as products of transient enthusiasm rather than deep values. Yet their co-founders argue that obligations are anchored in the actual commitments made by situated agents, not in the verdicts of sanitized hypothetical counterparts. The asker wants to know whether there is a philosophically coherent way for idealized deliberation to generate indirect norms that bind the actual agent, without reducing real commitments to mere errors correctable by a fictional self. Specifically, they ask whether the difficulty stems from conflating procedural idealization (how one should reason) with substantive idealization (what one should value), and whether a distinction along those lines can avoid both "normative ventriloquism"—outsourcing agency to an alien rational construct—and the opposite error of treating every present inclination as normatively authoritative. Analyze the philosophical structure of this situation, apply the relevant distinctions about indirect normativity and idealized agency, and reach a reasoned conclusion about how the agent should understand the force of their original commitments.
