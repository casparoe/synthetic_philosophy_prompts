Design a classification instrument — specifically, a decision tree with 6 to 9 leaves — for a philosophy-of-science workshop on what heritability coefficients do and do not mean. Rather than asking respondents to self-label, the tree should sort them by answers to concrete diagnostic cases and conceptual commitments. Begin with this divergence case: a well-powered twin study reports h² ≈ 0.65 for a specific working-memory task in a relatively homogeneous cohort, while a replication with broader socioeconomic and ethnic diversity finds h² ≈ 0.30 for the same task. From that starting point, construct branch nodes as answerable questions (not as named positions). The branches should separate stances on: whether heritability attaches to traits or only to trait-in-population pairs; whether the cohort-to-cohort drop indicates more environmental influence or merely a restructuring of gene-environment covariance; whether high heritability in one population licenses predictions about intervention efficacy in another; and whether the coefficient carries any individual-level predictive content. One branch must turn on a non-obvious distinction: does the coefficient measure a comparative causal potency of genetic versus environmental differences, or is it a pure variance-partitioning statistic with no such comparative-causal interpretation? Each leaf should be labeled with the name of a recognized position if one exists in the literature — for example, developmental-systems interactionism, quantitative-genetic instrumentalism, or counterfactual-causal pluralism — or marked unnamed followed by a one-sentence diagnosis of why that combination is unoccupied (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Explicitly flag any two leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining which shared commitment collapses them. The audience is philosophy-of-science graduate students; do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since the instrument must be usable without external lookup.
