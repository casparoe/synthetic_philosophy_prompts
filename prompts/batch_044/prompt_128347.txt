Design a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree with 6 to 8 nodes—mapping the logical space of positions on the unilateralist’s curse, intended for a graduate seminar on collective rationality. The instrument must sort an analyst by concrete commitments rather than by familiar labels. Begin with this root question: does the analyst accept that individually rational choices can be collectively self-defeating even when every agent shares an identical preference ordering over the final outcomes? From there, branch on whether the failure is best described as a coordination deficit, a missing precommitment technology, or a mischaracterization of the strategic situation (for instance, treating sequential moves by one agent across time as structurally analogous to simultaneous moves by many).

Each node should be a binary or forced-choice claim that a student could answer without knowing the literature’s taxonomy—e.g., whether binding arbitration is permissible only if every affected party opts in, whether the curse survives once agents are modeled with bounded rationality and private information about others’ compliance, and whether extending the curse to “intertemporal unilateralism” (a single agent’s future self undermining her present plan) changes its normative implications for constitutional design.

The tree must include these named leaves: (1) a hard-line coordinationist who endorses mandatory, non-opt-in arbitration mechanisms; (2) a libertarian contractualist who accepts only opt-in Ulysses-style precommitment contracts; (3) a skeptical eliminativist who denies the curse applies to real-world agents; and (4) an “intertemporal unilateralist” position—treat this as an unnamed or unexplored leaf—that applies the curse logic to intra-personal sequential choice and asks whether constitutional precommitment solves it or merely displaces it. For every leaf, include a one-sentence statement of its most awkward practical commitment.

In addition, identify at least one unoccupied branch combination—such as an analyst who denies that agents share preference orderings yet still demands centralized binding arbitration—and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, particularly whether the libertarian contractualist and the intertemporal unilateralist collapse into the same recommendation when the domain shifts to constitutional precommitment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, author citations, or requests for named historical sources; build the tree from the logical structure of the commitments alone.
