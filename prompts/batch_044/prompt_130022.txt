I was arguing with my roommate last night about Strawson's reactive attitudes essay -- you know, the one where he says resentment and guilt don't actually depend on some deep metaphysical free will. Anyway, my roommate Maya said Strawson's defense collapses once you notice reactive attitudes can be "structurally misdirected" -- like resenting a corporation for a harmful policy when the decision was spread across dozens of people so no single quality of will is clearly in view. She said if resentment is still appropriate there, then it's tracking outcomes or control, not quality of will, which sneaks the libertarian worry back in through the side door.

I (Leo) said that's wrong because reactive attitudes are essentially second-personal; they need a specific adresssee whose will is discernable. If the will is too distributed you're not really resenting a person, you're just using resentment language metaphorically, like being mad at the rain. Maya came back with: Strawson already lets us take the "objective attitude" toward people whose psychology makes their will opaque, so why can't there be "distributed opacity" where institutional structures hide an individual's will? She thinks this shows reactive attitudes are about whether we can meaningfully address someone as a source of action, not about free will, but then she also said this undermines Strawson's claim that moral responsibility is just part of our natural interpersonel practices, since now we're importing conditions about institutional transparency. I told her she's conflating the conditions for appropriate resentment with the conditions for moral responsibility, but she said Strawson ties them together explicitly and I'm the one ignoring the text. Can you go through our exchange move by move and say who's right about what? Don't just declare a winner -- I want to know where each of us is missing something, especially about whether "distributed opacity" is actually a coherent challenge or just a confused extension of Strawson's objective attitude idea.
