I've been teaching medical ethics this semester and my grad students keep hitting the same snag with advance directives — the theory sounds neat, the earlier self wrote it, but then you've got a dementia patient who seems genuinely content now, and the directive was specifically written to reject life in exactly this kind of state where they no longer have narrative self-awareness. The identity question makes the authority question messy, and I don't want another one-off essay answer. Instead, write me a reusable decision procedure for a clinical ethics consultant who gets paged maybe twice a week about these conflicts. They have thirty minutes, no metaphysics background, and need to document why they either honored the directive or overrode it. Turn the debate about diachronic authority — is the past self a trustee with binding power, or just one preference-source among others? — into actual steps: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the stop-points where you say honor the directive versus current patient's interests take precedence, and the explicit flag-points where it's not a technical call but a values judgment that needs family or sign-off. The hard cases have to include a patient with advanced dementia who smiles at caregivers but whose directive refused feeding tubes if they ever lost narrative self-awareness, and a case where the patient verbally revoked part of the directive after diagnosis but before losing capacity, with family split on whether that revocation was authentic. Run both through your procedure, and make sure one of them yields an answer the family is going to hate. Be honest about where the procedure misfires — say, when welfare and narrative continuity pull in opposite directions — and say plainly what it can't do. Also please seperate what's settled in the bioethics and policy literature from what's genuinely contested among philosophers who actually work on personal identity and diachronic autonomy; don't present one side of that authority debate as the consensus when it's not. I keep misspelling diachronic in my lecture notes so I feel your pain if you do too, but keep the distinction clear anyway.
