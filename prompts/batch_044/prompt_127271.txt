I'm a retired molecular biologist who got into philosophy of language after retirement -- my daughter gave me Austin's classic lectures and now I'm hooked, though I still think like an experimentalist. I'm trying to help my old university's research-integrity office build something practical, because they're constantly dealing with disputes where a scientist said something in a meeting or an email and later everyone fights about whether it was actually an apology, a promise, or a resignation, or just talk. They need a reusable decision procedure: questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a clear "no, not a performative," and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's values rather than facts. The office deals with this maybe twice a month, the stakes are career-level, and the mediator running it has about thirty mintues and no philosophy background.

Please ground it in speech-act theory -- specifically felicity conditions, uptake, and Austin's distinction between misfire and abuse -- but don't just list Austin's conditions. Instead, translate them into tests a mediator can run, say which ones matter for institutional settings like a university and which can be dropped without losing the point, and be explicit about where the procedure will misfire or need judgment. Then apply it to three cases: a PI emailing the dean "I apologize for the sloppy methods" but never copying the affected grad student; a collaborater saying "I'll correct the figure by Friday" on a Zoom call with broken audio; and a lab head declaring "I'm stepping down as PI" in a staff meeting but later saying it was hypothetical. For each, show the step-by-step, flag the case where the procedure gives an answer the office probably won't like, and say clearly what the procedure can't settle. Also inject some idea about institutional authority -- maybe that the speaker has to be recognized by the group in a specific role for the act to take, not just by formal title -- since that seems to be the crux in half these cases.
