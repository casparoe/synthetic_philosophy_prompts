I am a retired epidemiologist who has only recently turned to philosophy of mind and ethics, after a career modeling low-probability, high-impact risks in public health. Last week, in conversation with a former colleague who insists that moral reasoning should remain quantifiable, I tried to articulate why I resist the conclusion that insects generate more aggregate suffering than all farmed vertebrates, despite the scale argument: with roughly 10^19 individuals alive at any moment, even a 0.001 prior for phenomenal consciousness per insect yields a staggering expectation, yet this treats consciousness as an independent Bernoulli draw rather than a phylogenetically unitary trait with strict neural prerequisites. To locate the precise point of my resistance, I ask for your credence—stated as a single probability between 0 and 1—that, once we correct for phylogenetic correlation among individuals and apply a minimal phenomenal-binding criterion that permits recurrent processing without global workspace integration, the aggregate moral weight of insect suffering genuinely exceeds that of all farmed vertebrates.

Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one to two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Item one should state your overall credence. Item two should assign a rough probability to the claim that phenomenal consciousness requires integrated architectures such as thalamocortical loops and is therefore phylogenetically unitary rather than randomly distributed. Item three should assign a rough probability to the claim that distributed recurrent networks in insects can satisfy that minimal binding criterion. Item four should combine these terms with the scale correction and present the intermediate aggregation result. Item five should identify which term is least certain for you and specify what empirical finding—perhaps regarding nociceptive integration in a particular hymenopteran species—would shift your number most.
