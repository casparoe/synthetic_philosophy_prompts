I'm writing this up after a long argument with a friend about whether well-being is just the absence of suffering. Here is roughly where I have landed: (1) I accept tranquilism—only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering have intrinsic value for well-being, and positive pleasure is intrinsically neutral. (2) I defend this phenomenologically: pleasure is always experienced as the satisfaction of a prior craving, so pleasure conceptually depends on craving, whereas suffering can occur independently. (3) From this I derive a restricted moral view: we have direct obligations only to reduce suffering, and producing pleasure gives us no positive moral reason, though it is not forbidden. (4) At the same time, I think reflective equilibrium licenses retaining deep attachments—to art, friendship, and intellectual curiosity—even though they are sources of craving and vulnerability to suffering. (5) I also think that a being in a permanent, unconscious state of zero craving and zero suffering has maximal well-being, and that if we could choose between creating such beings and creating conscious, craving-filled but happy beings, we should choose the former. Which of these actually hang together? In particular, does (2) undermine (3) more severely than it looks—perhaps by showing that pleasure-production is not merely neutral but actively harmful because it requires generating craving first? Is the tension between (4) and (5) real, or only apparent because (4) concerns personal permission while (5) concerns global creation? And does (2) secretly depend on consciousness in a way that makes (5) incoherent? Please rank the tensions by severity, say for each pair whether the conflict is genuine or dissolves on inspection, identify the least costly revision for the worst one, and write your answer in American English.
