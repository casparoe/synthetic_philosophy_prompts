I serve as a philosophy professor chairing my university’s Committee on Classroom Expression. We receive roughly four complaints per semester about utterances that may violate our respectful-discourse policy; preliminary findings must be rendered within 72 hours with a brief justification co-signed by me and the Dean of Students. Most committee members are not philosophers of language, so I need a reusable, stepwise decision procedure they can run without my presence, together with an honest map of where judgment is ineliminable.

The theoretical framework I want operationalized distinguishes four layers present in an utterance involving a contested expression:

1. The Lexical-Encoding Layer (L-layer): whether the expression conventionally carries a derogatory condition as part of its semantic content—such that its literal, truth-conditional contribution expresses contempt—rather than merely bearing a negative connotation or etymology.
2. The Pragmatic-Activation Layer (P-layer): whether the specific context activates a derogatory perspective through conventional implicature, presupposition, or conversational implicature, even when the literal content is technically neutral.
3. The Institutional-Framing Layer (F-layer): whether the utterance is embedded in a pedagogical, meta-linguistic, historical-quotation, or critical-analytic frame that suppresses or redirects the P-layer.
4. The Reclamation-Index Layer (R-layer): whether the speaker belongs to the group targeted by the expression’s derogatory content, whether the audience is composed primarily of in-group members, and whether the usage is intended to invert rather than reinforce derogation. Crucially, the R-layer can neutralize the P-layer only when the L-layer has been semantically bleached—i.e., the term is functioning in its reclaimed form, not in its conventionally derogatory lexical entry.

Please adopt the role of an expert in philosophy of language with institutional-ethics experience. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm how these four layers translate into operational tests usable by non-specialists, noting which distinctions must survive simplification and which can be dropped without distorting the framework. Then present the procedure as a flowchart in words: the ordered questions, the criteria for a sufficient answer at each step, the clear stop conditions (no sanction), the hand-off conditions (decision requires human value judgment—specifically, my sign-off with the Dean), and the final determination categories (clear violation; clear non-violation; requires judgment).

Next, apply the procedure to three complaints:
(a) A sociology professor quotes a 1950s government document containing a racial slur in full during a lecture on institutional racism, with no content warning; a student files a complaint.
(b) A student belonging to the targeted group uses a reclaimed version of an anti-gay slur during a peer presentation on queer identity, directed at themselves, in a classroom composed mostly of allies.
(c) A student in a philosophy-of-language seminar utters a gendered slur (the f---t term) purely to discuss its semantic properties—citing no specific speaker, describing no target, framing it strictly as an object of analysis—but a listener reports feeling targeted because of the term’s acoustic presence.

Case (c) should be the one in which the procedure yields a result you expect I will dislike—likely a clear non-violation despite reported harm—so that the limits of proceduralization are exposed.

Then state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which harms or ambiguities it will systematically miss, and where human sign-off is unavoidable regardless of procedural output.

Think carefully about each translation, be honest about misfires, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
