I've been reflecting on the 1972 demolition of the Pruitt-Igoe public housing complex in St. Louis — the thirty-three modernist towers designed by Minoru Yamasaki according to Le Corbusier's principles, completed in 1954, plagued by rapid vacancy and crime, and finally dynamited beginning in 1972 — and I want to examine it through Edmund Burke's critique of rationalist social engineering and G.K. Chesterton's fence. Rather than retelling the architectural history, I want a disciplined two-way analysis. First, identify precisely what constituted the fence in this case: not merely the older neighborhoods that were cleared, but the specific evolved practices — informal social surveillance, mixed-use street life, incremental patterns of maintenance and ownership — that the modernist plan removed and replaced with towers-in-a-park. Second, apply Burke's distinction between the rationalist's presumption and the prejudice of evolved tradition: what did the planners — Yamasaki, the St. Louis Housing Authority, the federal agencies funding the project — claim to know about human social needs, and what accumulated practical knowledge did they treat as disposable?

Third, consider the demolition itself in 1972: was destroying the towers an acknowledgment that a fence had been wrongly removed, or was it another top-down intervention that failed to ask why the buildings had been erected in the first place? Finally, tell me where this lens fractures. Burke wrote about revolutionary France, not about mid-century American housing policy; does his framework even apply when the tradition being replaced was itself the product of earlier disruptive urban renewal? And does the Pruitt-Igoe case genuinely vindicate Chesterton's fence, or does it reveal that some fences are erected by planners who never understood the terrain they were enclosing? I want the theory to illuminate the case, but I also want the case to expose what the theory silently assumes — particularly whether Burkean conservatism can distinguish between a valuable evolved institution and a failed modernist imposition that itself replaced something older.
