Draft one exam question for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of history seminar, answerable in 75 minutes, on overdetermination and multi-causal explanation. The question must not be answerable by reciting standard textbook examples—such as the Archduke’s assassination and the outbreak of World War I—or by merely defining necessary and sufficient conditions. Instead, present students with the following scenario: a historian argues that a particular political revolution was causally overdetermined because three separate conditions—organizational readiness, institutional collapse, and widespread popular exhaustion—were each individually sufficient to produce the outcome, yet she insists all three must appear in the explanation because omitting any one alters the narrative structure and explanatory coherence of the account, even though the counterfactual outcome remains unchanged. Ask candidates to assess whether this position coherently describes historical overdetermination, or whether it illegitimately conflates causal redundancy with what might be termed narrative-constitutive multiplicity. They should propose explicit criteria for distinguishing a genuinely overdetermined historical cause from a factor that is merely narratively indispensable but causally redundant, apply those criteria to at least one of the three conditions named, and evaluate whether the distinction can be sustained in practice without collapsing back into a simple sufficiency test.

Provide a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks distributed across five must-hit elements: (1) precise characterization of causal overdetermination versus joint causation in historical explanation (5 marks); (2) identification of the specific conflation between causal redundancy and narrative-constitutive multiplicity in the historian’s argument (5 marks); (3) formulation of a defensible distinction criterion—for instance, counterfactual robustness at the mechanism level versus stability at the narrative level (6 marks); (4) rigorous application of that criterion to one of the three named conditions (5 marks); (5) a critical evaluation of whether the criterion succeeds or collapses, with explicit acknowledgment of one serious objection (4 marks). For each element, include the marks and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail to earn them.
