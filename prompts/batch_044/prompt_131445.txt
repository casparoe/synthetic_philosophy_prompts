Since assuming responsibility as literary executor for my late uncle’s estate, I have been forced to move from abstract sympathy to concrete judgment about the interpretation of posthumously discovered writings. The crisis involves a published essay of his—call it Essay X—which defends a deterministic account of moral responsibility. Among his private papers I discovered a journal entry dated six months after publication that reads: “He was wrong about the whole thing; the irony should have been obvious.” The pronoun is grammatically ambiguous: “he” may refer to the essay’s interlocutor, or reflexively to my uncle himself. A separate private letter to a friend states, “I meant it straight, but I see now I was confused.” These documents have split my advisory board between two camps.

Please draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between Archival Intentionalists and Publication-Fixed Autonomists could both sign. The Archival Intentionalists hold that a work’s meaning is determined by the author’s living, revisable intentions—including those expressed in private drafts, marginalia, and post-publication reflections—and that clearly documented later intentions can correct or override the public text. The Publication-Fixed Autonomists hold that once a text enters public circulation, its meaning is fixed by the linguistic and conventional norms available to its intended audience at that point of dissemination; private, undiscovered, or later authorial psychological states are biographically interesting but semantically inert with respect to the work itself, and no author holds a special “retroactive semantic veto” over interpretations that were publicly accessible.

Format the deliverable exactly as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—propositions about texts, manuscripts, semantic content, communicative intent, and archival evidence that both sides accept without reservation.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: the issue; the formulation that the Intentionalists accept as describing their own view; and the formulation that the Autonomists accept as describing their own view. Cover at least: (a) the authority of a post-publication private repudiation over the already-public work; (b) the semantic relevance of ambiguous private annotations (such as the unresolved anaphoric “he” in the journal entry); and (c) whether authors possess a normative power of retroactive semantic veto.

3. A list of cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, provide: (i) a concrete case or piece of evidence that both sides agree bears directly on it (use the uncle’s journal entry and letter as one case; use a hypothetical case in which an author privately repudiates interpretation A of their novel after A has become the standard public reading, even though interpretation B—now claimed as the “true” meaning—was never publicly accessible); and (ii) each side’s explicit, pre-registered prediction about what that case shows.

4. An assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—if so, identify which portion, specify the shared underlying claim, and explain why the disagreement was only about wording.

Finally, include a brief meta-section in which you state what specific argument, archival discovery, or conceptual distinction would cause you to shift your own sympathy from one camp to the other in this collaboration, and flag the precise step in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure—particularly where you might have confused biographical reconstruction with semantic determination, or where the ambiguity of the private pronoun might illegitimately influence your judgment about the public text.

Write the statement so that a reader without web access can evaluate its coherence. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke specific scholarly texts by quotation; if you refer to well-known positions, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
