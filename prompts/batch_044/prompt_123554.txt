I have recently faced a decision that has become unexpectedly personal. After more than a decade of working in advocacy for environmental and humanitarian causes—work that has given my life structure and meaning, but also left me chronically depleted and physically unwell—I was offered admission to an intensive, long-term therapeutic program. The program promises not merely symptom relief but a fundamental restructuring of my emotional priorities: I would retain full cognitive capacity and moral knowledge, but the acute, often paralyzing urgency I feel toward distant suffering and ecological loss would be significantly attenuated. The clinicians present this not as a deprivation but as a correction, arguing that my present commitments are disproportionate responses sustained by unstable affective patterns rather than by considerations that would survive calm, well-rested, fully informed reflection. They ask me to consider what I would rationally choose if I were free of the cognitive distortions and emotional exhaustion that now govern my life, and they suggest that any value my current attachments possess is at best indirect—instrumental to ends that an ideally deliberating version of myself would endorse—rather than direct.

I am genuinely torn. If practical authority belongs exclusively to what I would ratify under conditions of idealized deliberation, then my resistance to the program seems irrational, a symptom to be treated rather than a reason to be respected. But if my present attachments carry direct normative weight—weight that does not derive solely from being endorsed by a more stable, hypothetical counterpart—then accepting the treatment risks a kind of self-alienation, trading commitments that constitute my identity for a more manageable but perhaps less authentic life. I do not want to collapse into the view that every actual preference, however distorted by fatigue or distress, must be honored; yet I also resist the idea that my only genuine reasons are those that survive a transformation I do not now recognize as mine.

Can you help me think through whether there is a coherent middle position? In particular, is there a defensible account of normativity on which some of my current commitments have direct reason-giving force—force that is not reducible to being indirect instruments of ends I would choose under ideal conditions—while still allowing that other attachments are indeed merely the byproducts of conditions that rigorous reflection would reject? How should I distinguish, in my own case, between commitments that genuinely bind me and those that are only indirectly sustained by circumstances that idealized reasoning would correct?
