As part of my philosophy of mind coursework, I am preparing an interview for our department's student podcast series. Our guest is an unnamed philosopher who advances a specific interpretation of Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle—the scenario in which a brain is sustained in a vat while the body continues to operate through remote neural control. Rather than locating personal identity in either the biological brain or the embodied agent, she defends what she calls narrative sovereignty hub theory: identity is constituted by the functional system that holds editorial authority over first-person autobiographical memory and future-oriented planning, and she claims this hub can be distributed across synthetic neural substrates and external sensory arrays without loss of selfhood. She is particularly known for deflecting split-brain objections by insisting that they are engineering problems for the hub rather than metaphysical problems for identity. I need eight to ten interview questions that avoid generic biography and do not ask for citations or reading lists. The sequence should build in difficulty: open by pressing the intuitive force of Dennett's original location question against her hub-based answer; then include at least one question that anticipates her standard reply to physical-continuity objections, paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; include one that examines how gradual replacement of biological tissue with synthetic substrates affects the stability of narrative sovereignty; include one that specifically challenges her habit of treating split-brain cases as mere coordination failures, with a follow-up designed for her likely deflection; and conclude with a question about what empirical or conceptual finding could genuinely undermine her view rather than being absorbed as just another hub-reconfiguration. Each question should turn on the actual content of her position, not on restating it.
