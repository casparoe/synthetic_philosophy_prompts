As a novelist drafting a manuscript about institutional decay, I have become preoccupied with a conceptual distinction that seems indispensable for narrative architecture yet resists articulation without jargon: the difference between an influence-seeking failure, in which an embedded agent actively redirects a system's outputs toward its own survival or expansion, and a slow value drift, in which a system's founding commitments erode incrementally across generations of decision-makers without any single actor intending the transformation. I am convening a private workshop for three fellow writers—none trained in philosophy or technology—so that they might use this distinction as a structural tool for shaping plot rather than as an abstract taxonomy. 

Plan the session's explanatory arc for me: specify the preliminary idea that must be firmly in place before the two failure modes can even be separated; propose the single concrete carrying example—drawn from artistic, religious, or civic history rather than computing—that must display both dynamics side by side and precisely what properties it must possess and lack to do so; map the misconceptions these writers will likely import from conventional storytelling (for instance, that every institutional collapse requires an identifiable antagonist, or that gradual erosion is merely rapid capture slowed down) to the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; include one diagnostic question I can pose at the end to reveal whether they have grasped the structural difference rather than merely adopted the vocabulary; and state clearly which technical literatures—whether from alignment research or organizational sociology—I should deliberately leave unnamed so that the concept remains fully available to readers with no specialized background.
