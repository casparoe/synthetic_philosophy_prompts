I'm drafting governance protocols for a federated online forum that operates on explicit rationalist norms—charitable interpretation, explicit reasoning, and institutional humility about one's own biases. I need a reusable decision procedure derived from Popper's paradox of tolerance that my moderation team can apply when a user's behavior threatens the forum's epistemic conditions. The procedure must run sequentially: first, classify whether the threat is an intolerant idea being expressed, an intolerant practice being enacted, or a structural incentive that rewards bad faith; second, test whether tolerating it would erode the specific norms that make rational discourse possible in this context; third, determine whether the response can be surgical (restricting the practice or the incentive) or must be categorical (restricting participation by the user); and fourth, check whether the moderation team itself is applying the standard selectively. At each step, specify what counts as sufficient evidence, where the procedure stops with a no-restriction answer, and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. It must handle hard cases: a user who never breaks explicit rules but systematically exploits ambiguity to exhaust interlocutors; a subgroup advocating institutional positions that, if adopted, would eliminate open inquiry; and a case where the allegedly intolerant behavior is actually a minority view challenging groupthink. Include an explicit accounting of epistemic maintenance costs—the cumulative burden that tolerating low-quality or manipulative participation places on collective reasoning capacity—and mark precisely where that concept is doing real work versus where it is only dressing up an unavoidable judgment call. Apply the procedure to three proposals: restricting a user who consistently demands others prove negative claims; quarantining a subgroup that promotes epistemic nihilism about scientific consensus; and a moderator's proposal to ban a user for being too contrarian without specifying which norms are threatened. One of these applications should yield an answer that feels wrong to me but is correct by the logic of the procedure. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—which categories of harm it is not designed to detect, and where it will reliably misfire if used outside its intended domain of preserving open, good-faith discourse. The deliverable should be the procedure in checklist or flowchart form, followed by the three worked applications, with constraints on time and expertise noted since the team will run this under pressure with no philosopher on call.
