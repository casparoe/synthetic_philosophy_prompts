As a journalist writing about philosophy of mind, I'm trying to clarify my own position in the long-running debate over whether dreams are genuine experiences—the kind of disagreement that separates Malcolm's skeptical line about first-person dream reports from Dennett's more permissive account of dream consciousness. Rather than surveying the literature, I want to see whether my intuitions about concrete scenarios cohere into a principle, and where that principle forces me to change my mind.

Here are my verdicts.

In the case of a lucid dreamer who deliberately solves arithmetic problems to maintain awareness and, upon waking, reproduces the answers with accuracy, I have no hesitation: this is an experience. There is cognitive agency, directed attention, and a coherent narrative that connects to waking capacities.

In the case of a false-awakening loop—a sleeper who experiences three successive "waking ups," each with full sensory detail and emotional relief, before actually waking and remembering only the final loop—I still say there was experience, but I am unsure whether to count it as one fragmented experience or as three discrete ones that were mostly erased.

In the case of an amnesic sleeper who is awakened during REM, immediately gives a detailed report of flying over a familiar city, but thirty seconds later denies any mental activity occurred and retains no trace, I lean toward saying an experience did occur but was not retained in autobiographical memory. The report was too structured to be pure confabulation, yet its total disappearance makes me uneasy about attributing it to me in the same way I attribute waking perceptions.

In the case of a sleepwalker who performs a complex, coordinated task—filling a kettle, turning on a stove, then returning to bed—with eyes open, no REM signature, and no subsequent report or recall, I judge there was no experience at all. This is behavior without phenomenology, and I treat it as a clear negative case.

In the case of a patient under deep propofol sedation who, upon recovery, recounts a vivid narrative of floating in a blue-lit room, despite neurophysiological indicators suggesting an absence of integrated cortical activity, I am genuinely divided. The report sounds like experience, but if consciousness was pharmacologically suppressed, I suspect the narrative was constructed upon waking rather than lived through during the procedure.

In the case of a congenitally blind subject whose dreams consist entirely of spatial echo, temperature gradients, and tactile mapping, with no visual imagery whatsoever, and who insists these states are just like being awake, minus sight, I want to say yes—this is experience—but I worry I am projecting a sighted model of phenomenology onto a mental state that might operate on entirely different principles.

What principle, if any, unifies these judgments? Specifically, does a single criterion—perhaps something like counterfactual accessibility to waking reasoning, or phenomenal ownership sustained across a temporal interval, or integration with the subject's narrative self—capture why I say yes to the lucid navigator and the blind dreamer, no to the sleepwalker, and maybe-confabulation to the anesthetic case? Or must I accept that my verdicts depend on two independent dimensions—one tracking the presence of integrated cognitive agency, the other tracking the subject's later endorsement—that sometimes come apart?

Please state the principle (or competing principles) precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by each case above, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save without artificial complexity. Then apply your best version to a case I have not listed: a subject trained in a pre-lucid marking protocol, who before sleep sets an intention to notice a specific geometric symbol should it appear, and upon waking reports only that the symbol was present, with no other dream content, no emotional coloring, and no sense of having been there. Under your principle, was there an experience of the symbol, or only a subliminal registration?
