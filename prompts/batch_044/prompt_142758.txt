"Sphex wasps run a rigid burrow-check loop: they drag prey in, check the burrow, seal it, and if the prey is moved during the check they start over—no adjustment, no learning. Philosophers call this sphexishness: mechanical rigidity with zero flexibility. But is rigidity binary? Imagine Sphex-7, a hypothetical creature that executes the exact same loop 99% of the time. However, if ambient temperature exceeds 30°C, Sphex-7 can trigger one of two hard-wired sub-routines: seal the burrow immediately or abandon it and start a new one nearby. The choice is not open-ended—both outcomes are pre-programmed, and the trigger is purely external.

In your essay, do three things. First, explain why sphexishness is usually taken to undermine free will, and be precise about whether the problem is the absence of choice, the absence of reasons-responsiveness, or something else. Second, evaluate whether Sphex-7 is genuinely less sphexish, or whether its 'escape hatch' just relocates the rigidity (e.g., to the temperature-trigger mechanism or to the binary sub-routine selection). Here you should engage with Frankfurt-style hierarchies of volition, but apply them to a case where even second-order desires are mechanically fixed. Third, defend your own view on whether graded flexibility—having some deviation points but not others—can ever be enough for moral agency, or whether free will requires a kind of openness that Sphex-7 still lacks. Be explicit about what is settled in the literature and what remains genuinely contested."

Model Answer (~750 words):

Sphexishness, named after the digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus, describes behavior that is mechanically rigid and unresponsive to contextual variation. The classic observation is that the wasp checks its burrow before dragging prey inside; if an experimenter moves the prey during the check, the wasp repeats the loop exactly rather than adapting. What's settled is that this demonstrates a lack of behavioral flexibility: the wasp doesn't learn, doesn't weigh alternatives, and doesn't modify its routine in response to changed circumstances. What's contested is whether this rigidity is best analyzed as an absence of choice, an absence of reasons-responsiveness, or a failure of some deeper condition like authorship or sourcehood.

The standard philosophical use of sphexishness is to illustrate a creature that acts without free will because its behavior is fully determined by a fixed internal program. But the deeper issue isn't just determinism—after all, a determinist can be a compatibilist. The problem is that the wasp seems unable to do otherwise in a way that reflects its own reasons. Even if every movement is causally determined, free will might survive if the agent's actions flow from its own reflective preferences. The sphex wasp lacks this: it has no reflective preferences, no hierarchy of desires, and no capacity to endorse or reject its first-order impulses.

Now consider Sphex-7. At first glance, it seems less sphexish because it has a deviation point. When temperature exceeds 30°C, it selects between sealing or abandoning. But this deviation is narrowly circumscribed. The trigger is external and non-cognitive; the options are binary and hard-wired; and there is no deliberation about which option is better. So the rigidity hasn't disappeared—it's been relocated. The wasp is still running a deterministic program, just one with a conditional branch. This mirrors a point in the free-will literature: adding a branch to a deterministic flowchart doesn't automatically create agency, especially if the branching conditions and outcomes are themselves fixed.

Applying Frankfurt-style hierarchies makes this sharper. Frankfurt argues that free will requires second-order volitions—wanting to want certain things. In Sphex-7, we could imagine a second-order mechanism that "prefers" sealing over abandoning when hot. But if that second-order preference is itself mechanically fixed—if it's just another hard-wired sub-routine—then we haven't gained freedom, we've just added another layer of sphexishness. This is the genuinely contested point: some philosophers think that any hierarchical structure, even a deterministic one, is enough for autonomy, as long as the hierarchy is integrated and not coerced. Others insist that without some openness at the highest level—without the capacity to revise one's own second-order preferences—the hierarchy is just a more complex prison.

My view is that graded flexibility is not sufficient for free will. Sphex-7 is more complex than the original sphex wasp, but complexity isn't openness. The escape hatch is binary, externally triggered, and unreflective. For moral agency, we typically require not just deviation from routine, but deviation that is responsive to reasons in a way the agent can recognize and endorse. Sphex-7 meets none of these conditions. However, I want to flag what is contested here: some compatibilists might argue that if Sphex-7's behavior is predictable and integrated with its internal states, it possesses a minimal form of agency. I think that's wrong, but it's a live debate, not a settled fact.
