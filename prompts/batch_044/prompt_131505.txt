I serve on a clinical ethics consultation service, and I have been reviewing our past recommendations on conflicts between advance directives and subsequently expressed wishes in an effort to determine whether we operate from a single principle or from several that happen to overlap in ordinary cases. In one earlier consultation, a patient with advancing Alzheimer's had executed a directive refusing artificial nutrition once she reached profound cognitive impairment; when she became non-verbal yet apparently content in a nursing facility, her family requested a feeding tube. We advised withholding it, reasoning that the directive's authority rested on the absence of any meaningful psychological continuity with its author, rather than on a merely temporary impairment of judgment.

In a second case, a patient who had directed that no mechanical ventilation be used in the event of permanent respiratory failure suffered an acute, reversible brain injury that left him temporarily unable to communicate. During the episode he signaled, via eye movements, that he wanted the ventilator continued. We recommended maintaining the directive through the temporary state, noting that an immediate preference formed under disorientation and fear should not automatically override a long-considered prior judgment, though we added in our notes that full preservation of psychological identity might weaken a directive's force in ways that temporary incompetence does not.

In a third case, a patient with locked-in syndrome following a brainstem stroke had previously refused life-sustaining measures should she become "completely paralyzed and unable to interact with others." Once she learned to communicate through eye-gaze technology and sustained coherent intellectual work and relationships, she explicitly revoked her directive and requested continued ventilation. We honored the revocation, emphasizing that her psychological and relational capacities remained fully intact despite the bodily transformation, so a present authority continuous with the past author existed and could update her earlier instructions.

In a fourth case, a patient in a persistent vegetative state had directed withdrawal of life support should he ever permanently lose consciousness. His family argued for continued care on the possibility of hidden awareness. We recommended withdrawal, holding that without any capacity for psychological life, relational engagement, or value-based reasoning, there was simply no competing present authority that could challenge the prior directive.

We now face a consultation that sits uneasily among these. A philosopher executed a directive refusing "any life-extending intervention that preserves biological life when I am conscious but permanently unable to produce natural speech or handwriting, regardless of whether I can think or communicate through other means." She subsequently developed an isolated upper-motor-neuron disorder affecting only the peripheral pathways for vocalization and manual movement; she remains fully conscious, with intact memory, reasoning, emotional life, and relational capacity. She communicates fluently through a direct neural-interface decoder, continues her philosophical work, and expresses a sustained, well-reasoned desire for mechanical ventilation should it become necessary. She contends the directive should not apply because she is not "unable to communicate," only unable to use natural modalities, and because her core values—intellectual engagement and relational connection—remain fully realizable. Her family insists that the literal conditions specified in the document are satisfied and that the directive must therefore bind.

If we want to remain consistent with our own track record, should we honor her present request or enforce the document? Which of our four precedents offers the closest analog, and does our history genuinely underdetermine the outcome? If we side with her, are we implicitly adopting a principle that psychological and communicative continuity takes precedence over literal bodily conditions specified in the text, and if we side with the family, are we implicitly committing to a principle that the precise literal conditions in the directive govern regardless of functional preservation? And what further hypothetical case—perhaps one involving intact narrative memory and reasoning but a radical, stable shift in the emotional valence attached to dependency—would best force us to declare which principle we actually hold?
