A philanthropic board that reviews global-health funding proposals has established the following record. It fully funded a program to create an open-source manufacturing protocol for an off-patent antibiotic still needed in low-income settings but ignored by commercial producers. It rejected a proposal to scale up an already-validated water-chlorination intervention in a region where three established NGOs already run nearly identical programs. It approved, with a strict phased-gate structure, a multi-country campaign to revise food-fortification regulations, noting that success could affect millions but that political tractability was entirely unproven. It rejected a proposal to deliver trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy to displaced populations inside an active conflict zone, citing the impossibility of monitoring fidelity and outcomes. Finally, it rejected a basic-science grant to study the evolutionary origins of immune tolerance, on the grounds that the pathway to measurable human welfare was too speculative.

Now consider a new proposal. It asks for support to integrate a predictive risk model for postpartum hemorrhage into routine maternity care at rural clinics, using only data that health workers already collect. The model has shown high accuracy in retrospective studies across three countries but has never been deployed prospectively at scale; the intervention requires no new pharmaceuticals or equipment, only a revised triage protocol and brief training. Postpartum hemorrhage is a leading cause of maternal mortality, yet receives substantially less funding per death averted than several infectious diseases; in addition, a well-funded global initiative already addresses the same condition through supply-side drug distribution, though not through screening, risk-stratification, or triage.

What should the board decide if it wishes to be consistent with its own precedents? Identify the single closest analog among the five earlier cases, but also articulate the two or three decision rules that could each accommodate all five prior verdicts. For each rule, state whether it approves or rejects this new proposal and explain why. Then offer your best guess as to which rule the board is actually running, with the reasoning that favors it over the alternatives. Finally, describe one further hypothetical proposal—different in kind from all six cases discussed so far—that would best distinguish your leading rule from the strongest competing one, and say what verdict each rule would deliver on it.
