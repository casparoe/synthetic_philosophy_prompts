I just finished a long, somewhat heated argument with a close friend who insists that Henry Sidgwick’s involvement with the Society for Psychical Research is nothing more than a biographical curiosity with no philosophical weight. I am convinced it is structurally significant, and I am defending this view next Tuesday in an interdisciplinary seminar. My thesis is that Sidgwick’s psychical investigations should not be read as an embarrassing departure from the rationalism of The Methods of Ethics, but rather as an expression of a methodological commitment—what I am calling structured epistemic humility about first-person authority—that parallels and actually reinforces his ethical argument. Specifically, his willingness to treat anomalous conscious states as data despite their resistance to standard third-person verification mirrors the way he treats the deliverances of practical reason: neither is self-certifying, both require intersubjective scrutiny, and neither can be dismissed without undermining the framework that makes normative judgment possible.

The audience will be about fifteen people: mostly analytic moral philosophers who regard psychical research as pseudoscientific noise; two historians of nineteenth-century British philosophy who know the SPR archives well; one philosopher of mind working on consciousness studies; and a couple of philosophy-of-science graduate students who are methodologically naturalist but open to conceptual arguments. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a full defense. Order the objections by how likely they are and how damaging they would be, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would actually use—especially the ethicists’ charge of special pleading and the historians’ concern about anachronism. For each objection, provide a concise reply I could deliver in under two minutes, then flag the weakest point in that reply. Note what I should concede up front, what I must hold firm on, and identify the one question I am genuinely hoping nobody asks—ideally one that forces me to defend the “structured epistemic humility” framing against an accusation that I am simply rescuing Sidgwick from embarrassment by redefining his terms. Throughout, please separate what is settled in Sidgwick scholarship from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material, and do not present my interpretive framing as the consensus view. Write the response in American English.
