As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy in earnest, I am trying to orient my thinking about capital punishment without simply rehearsing the familiar triad of retribution, deterrence, and the risk of executing the innocent. I want to treat this not as a settled policy debate but as a cluster of conceptual problems whose interdependencies are rarely mapped carefully.

What draws my attention is an analogy from experimental practice: certain measurements are destructive—once performed, they eliminate the possibility of subsequent verification or correction. If a wrongful execution functions analogously as an irreversible epistemic loss, then the moral and institutional analysis of capital punishment may hinge less on point estimates of false-conviction rates and more on whether our legal institutions can reliably generate, detect, and correct such errors before the irreversible step is taken. At the same time, retributive theories typically presuppose that moral desert can be attributed with a precision comparable to scientific identification, and deterrence arguments depend on causal inferences that are themselves vulnerable to modeling choices and confounding variables.

Given this framing, which open conceptual questions or lines of philosophical work at the intersection of these three arguments deserve the highest priority, and in what order? I am specifically interested in the following four candidates, but I want your reasoning for ranking them rather than a flat survey:

First, the conditions under which retributive desert can be attributed with sufficient reliability to justify an irreversible sanction, given that institutional error is not merely possible but structurally concealed in adversarial systems. Second, the philosophical status of deterrence claims when the relevant causal effects depend on contested statistical modeling and on assumptions about rational agency that may not hold uniformly. Third, whether there is a principled normative distinction between accepting false positives in capital sentencing versus in other punitive contexts, particularly once we treat execution as destroying the very subject whose status could later be corrected. Fourth, the dependency relations among these questions—for instance, whether the irreversibility argument collapses if desert attribution is shown to be inherently probabilistic, or whether deterrence becomes irrelevant if the error-structure argument is decisive.

For each question you place in the top two positions, please defend its priority: why it is upstream of the others, what kind of philosophical rather than empirical work it requires, how tractable it is for someone without legal training, and what kind of conceptual result or argument over the coming years would cause you to reorder the priorities. Which single resolution, if achieved, would most change how we should assess the remaining two lines of argument? And are there sequencing considerations—questions that should be pursued in parallel versus those that must be settled before others can be coherently addressed?

Please avoid bibliographic recommendations, reading lists, citations, or appeals to specific scholars; I am interested in the architecture of the problem and the reasoning behind an agenda, not in assembling a syllabus.
