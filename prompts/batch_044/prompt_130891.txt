As a novelist constructing a narrative about a community whose past has been both archived and remembered in incompatible ways, I have been studying an institutional protocol that strikes me as philosophically puzzling. In several national heritage bodies and public-education frameworks, there operates what might be called a bifurcated epistemic regime: a public-narrative track and a juridical-archival track. On the first track, when the sustained collective memory of an affected community conflicts with documentary, archaeological, or forensic evidence—concerning, say, the circumstances of a displacement, the origin of a cultural artifact, or the attribution of harm—the institution is formally required to present the community’s account as authoritative in exhibitions, school curricula, and official commemorations. On the second track, the same institution treats that same documentary and forensic record as sovereign for legal restitution, property adjudication, and internal scholarship. The practice does not merely present both views side by side; it assigns hierarchical precedence to memory in the public sphere and to documents in the legal sphere.

What philosophical commitments does this dual-track practice presuppose? Does it implicitly endorse a pluralist epistemology in which collective memory and archival history are incommensurable authorities, each sovereign within its own domain? Or does it rest on a moral-political theory—perhaps of epistemic reparations or representational justice—that mandates mnemonic precedence in public space regardless of evidential weight, while leaving evidential norms untouched in adjudication? Can any single coherent framework license both the precedence of memory in display and the precedence of documents in court, or does the institution quietly rely on inconsistent theories of knowledge, responsibility, and recognition? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and indicate where the bifurcation is philosophically underdetermined—whether several rival accounts of collective knowledge or justice could license the same institutional split.

Please also say what would change your mind: whether a compelling argument for the unity of epistemic authority across institutional domains, a demonstration that the two tracks serve irreconcilable moral ends, or some other consideration, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
