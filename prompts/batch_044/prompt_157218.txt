I am a doctoral student in philosophy of aesthetics, and in two weeks I will present the central argument of my dissertation chapter to our department's aesthetics workshop. I defend a reconstructed version of Hume's standard of taste that I call calibrated intersubjectivity: rather than treating true judges as universal experts who converge on a single correct judgment, I treat delicacy of taste as a culturally situated but cognitively constrained perceptual skill—analogous to expert wine tasting, but with plural, incommensurable training regimes across deep cultural divides. On this view, aesthetic disagreement can be genuine without presupposing mind-independent beauty properties, yet cross-community translation remains possible because shared affective architecture constrains how far judgments can diverge. The workshop audience comprises two senior analytic aestheticians committed to robust aesthetic realism, a Kantian scholar who holds that Hume misses the normative structure of judgment, two graduate students working on cultural relativism in art, and a visiting cognitive scientist who studies cross-cultural emotional response. They will care especially about whether my view can distinguish genuine disagreement from mere preference divergence, whether it collapses into relativism, and whether it can accommodate the normative force of saying that someone is wrong about beauty rather than merely different. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections I will actually receive, ordered by how likely they are and how damaging they could be, each phrased in the vocabulary and framing of the specific audience member who would raise it. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and the weakest point in my reply that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, phrased as the Kantian scholar would put it, with a two-minute reply and an explicit note on where that reply is softest. Finally, identify the weakest structural point in my own position—the tension between plural training regimes and the claim that disagreement can still be genuine—and advise me how to present it proactively rather than defensively.
