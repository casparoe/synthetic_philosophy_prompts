I want to design a 48-hour take-home exam question about Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project—specifically, what makes every instance an 'a'—that students cannot answer by summarizing the gridfont alphabet or the program's feature-extraction pipeline. The crux should be philosophical, not technical: I want students to confront whether style is a perceptual primitive or an emergent statistical regularity, and whether the project's success undermines essentialist theories of categories.

Please draft the full assessment package:

Embed a specific hypothetical: imagine a modified Letter Spirit system that identifies an 'a'-like glyph in an alien writing system that shares zero geometric strokes with Roman 'a's, yet preserves only relational style properties—e.g., the ratio of enclosed negative space to stroke curvature, arranged analogously. Ask students to defend or reject the claim that this demonstrates a Wittgensteinian family-resemblance account of letter categories over an essentialist one, and to address the objection that the system's reliance on weighted statistical parameters reduces "style" to optimized feature correlation rather than genuine perception.

2. A model answer of roughly 650 words that makes these specific moves: (a) distinguishes geometric-feature essentialism from relational-style recognition; (b) uses the alien-alphabet hypothetical to argue that shared stroke geometry is unnecessary for category membership; (c) evaluates whether statistical parameterization eliminates the reality of style or merely models its perceptual conditions; (d) considers a self-undermining version of family resemblance—namely, that if any statistical correlation can be labeled "style," the concept becomes vacuous; and (e) reaches a qualified conclusion about whether Letter Spirit vindicates family resemblance or reveals that style and computation are not mutually exclusive.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 total marks, structured around five must-hit points (each with a specific point value). For each point, include: the marks available; the specific move that earns full credit; the difference between an A-level execution and a B-level execution; and the typical mistake that should lose marks. In particular, separate an A answer—which must explicitly use the alien-alphabet case to separate perceptual style from geometric feature—from a B answer, which correctly describes Letter Spirit but treats style as either purely subjective or purely reducible to stroke lists. Also flag common errors such as conflating statistical weights with thermal noise analogies, asserting without argument that AI success proves there is no essence, or describing the gridfont alphabet without engaging the philosophical issue.

The prompt should assume students have access only to course concepts and should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to an exam committee document.
