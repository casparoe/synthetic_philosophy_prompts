I have been discussing with fellow students in our philosophy of science seminar whether intuition pumps genuinely advance debate or merely dress prior convictions in the costume of common sense, and we constructed an imaginary case study to see how far the rhetorical machinery could be pushed. Would you please compose a sustained parody, approximately 650 to 800 words, in the form of a deadpan peer-reviewed article submitted to a fictional journal entitled Applied Conceptual Engineering (Vol. 14, No. 3, Autumn 2024). The article should present and defend a newly invented intuition pump called the Doxastic Mirror Protocol: a hypothetical experimental arrangement in which an agent’s beliefs are causally inverted yet phenomenologically mirrored, designed specifically to elicit strong intuitions about whether mental content is fixed by causal-role relations or by intrinsic qualitative character.

Your satire should target the practice of treating highly contrived thought experiments as calibrated empirical instruments—complete with statistical confidence intervals, ethics-board approvals for imaginary participants, replication studies using marginally altered mirror angles, and grant applications for pump maintenance—while remaining legible to readers who are not specialists in the literature. The humour must emerge from exaggerating real discursive features rather than inventing implausible ones: for instance, framing intuitive disagreement as measurement error, proposing double-blind studies in which philosophers are unaware of which intuition they are supposed to have, and treating the pump’s output as intersubjectively verifiable data. Maintain a strictly formal, deadpan academic voice throughout; invent all proper names, institutional affiliations, and funding bodies rather than citing real scholars or texts; and use British spelling conventions consistently. The piece should be readable both to someone acquainted with discussions of how thought experiments persuade and mislead, and to a general reader who recognises the parody of scientific authority being applied to conceptual analysis.
