I am preparing a seminar discussion on Erwin Schrödinger's philosophical essays from the 1940s and 1950s, particularly his engagement with Vedanta and his thesis that consciousness is fundamentally unitary—that the multiplicity of minds is an illusion analogous to the Vedantic concept of Maya, and that there is in truth only one consciousness which merely appears divided. I want to press this view against conceptual developments in neuroscience and philosophy of mind that arose only after his death in 1961, and I am not asking for any bibliographic apparatus or citation of specific editions.

Specifically, I want a reasoned reconstruction of how Schrödinger would likely have responded to two post-1960 frameworks. First, the global neuronal workspace model, which treats conscious access as a competitive broadcasting process among functionally specialized, unconscious modules—implying that the unity of awareness is an achievement of information integration rather than evidence of a singular metaphysical substrate. Second, the predictive-processing or free-energy-principle account of selfhood, which explains the felt unity of the "I" as a hierarchical inference that minimizes prediction error, with no necessary commitment to a non-physical or singular bearer of consciousness. I also want you to address how he might have treated empirical findings from split-brain studies—especially cases in which the left and right hemispheres generate distinct, coherent narrative self-models—when these are interpreted through such predictive frameworks.

Please do not supply a reading list. Instead, lay out the reconstructed position: which elements of these modern theories he would likely accept as accurate descriptions of phenomenal machinery, which he would reject as mistaking the instrumental map for the territory of Atman, and whether he would regard a split-brain patient sustaining two equally stable predictive self-models as harboring two consciousnesses or as one consciousness misrepresented by a fractured cognitive instrument. I am particularly interested in whether he could consistently maintain that predictive coherence itself is merely another form of Maya, or whether the functional explanatory success of these models would compel him toward a more pluralistic concession about the distribution of awareness.
