I have been trying to think through the moral argument about insects on my own, without any formal training in philosophy or biology. The reasoning I keep encountering runs like this: there are astronomically many insects, and even if the chance that any one of them is sentient is tiny—perhaps one in a thousand or less—the sheer scale means the total expected amount of suffering or moral weight could still be enormous. But I feel stuck on a conceptual problem that seems prior to any empirical findings about insect brains.

What I want to understand is how this scale-based reasoning interacts with what sentience itself is like as a property. If sentience is strictly binary—an organism either has some form of subjective experience or it does not, with no middle ground—then multiplying a low probability across billions of individuals yields a high expected number of sentient beings. Yet it is unclear to me what that expectation actually licenses in practice: it describes the population as a whole, but my moral choices, if I have any, are directed at particular ants, beetles, or larvae I might encounter or affect. The expectation does not seem to turn any specific individual into a probable sufferer; it just tells me that somewhere in the crowd there are likely some. By contrast, if sentience is gradable—if there is a continuous spectrum from bare reflex to rich experience—then the scale argument might work differently. Instead of counting probable sentient beings, we might be aggregating tiny fractions of moral weight across an immense population, so that the total burden is large even though no single creature crosses a threshold.

Can you explain the precise conceptual difference between applying expected-value reasoning to a binary property versus to a gradable one, specifically in the context of moral patienthood? I am particularly interested in whether the argument quietly depends on a hidden assumption—perhaps about how statistical expectations should be distributed back onto individuals, or about whether moral obligations can aggregate across uncertain cases in the same way probabilities do. I do not need a literature review or empirical neuroscience; I want to understand the logical structure of the argument itself, and exactly where it might break down if we reject the idea that a population-level expectation can function as a property of each member within the group.
