I'm a junior serving as the elected student representative on my school's Experiential Learning Oversight Board, and I need help drafting a formal recommendation memo for our Thursday session. We are reviewing a pilot proposal—submitted jointly by our philosophy department and an outside partner—called the Transformative Immersion Initiative, which would allow a small cohort of seniors to spend one semester living and working within a tightly organized community whose core commitments—radical material asceticism, collective decision-making that explicitly suppresses individual career ambition, and an ethical framework that condemns competitive achievement as a moral failing—are structurally incompatible with the values and life plans these students currently hold.

The organizers argue that encountering such a life from the inside is the only way to achieve genuine ethical understanding rather than mere theoretical acquaintance, but several board members have raised what they call the transformative-experience objection: if the immersion genuinely alters a participant's preferences, identity, and evaluative outlook, then consent given beforehand is authorized by a self that will not survive the experience, while consent given afterward comes too late to justify the initial exposure. To address this concern, the proposal includes a novel procedural safeguard requiring each applicant to complete a sealed pre-experience values statement and to designate an independent advocate who may initiate withdrawal during the program, though the student retains final veto authority over that withdrawal. I have been discussing this with classmates after our philosophy unit on autonomy and informed consent, and I believe the board needs a decision that treats this consent problem as substantive rather than dismissing it as a routine risk of education, while also avoiding the assumption that procedural safeguards can fully resolve a transformation in the self that does the consenting.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the board chair. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, approve with conditions specifying what those conditions are and over what time frame, or reject—with reasoning the program organizers could actually respond to with specific revisions. The memo must include an honest, non-caricatured account of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and its tone should be appropriate for an institutional decision document rather than a personal essay or a literature review. I want the deliverable to be something the board could vote on directly.
