I am a high school student working on an independent ethics project, and I have constructed the following argument about Benjamin Libet's experiments on the neural precursors of voluntary action, yet I am convinced it proves far more than it should and I cannot identify the precise misstep. (1) In Libet's experimental paradigm, electrophysiological recordings reveal that a readiness potential in premotor and supplementary motor regions begins several hundred milliseconds before the subject reports conscious awareness of having decided to move. (2) If the neural initiation of an action consistently precedes and predicts the conscious experience of deciding, then that conscious experience cannot be the initiating cause of the movement; at best it is an epiphenomenal accompaniment or post-hoc report. (3) Therefore, in these conditions, unconscious neural dynamics cause the action, while conscious volition merely observes it. (4) Although the experiments involve simple wrist flexions, the underlying architecture—pre-conscious motor preparation followed by a delayed conscious signal—is widely assumed to scale to complex, deliberative behavior such as drafting an essay, consenting to a contract, or choosing to apologize. (5) By extension, for any human action, an unconscious neural process initiates behavior before consciousness registers a decision, which implies that consciousness never serves as the initiating cause of behavior. (6) Moral and legal responsibility, including the reduced but still present culpability standards applied to adolescents, presuppose that the agent is the initiating cause of the action and that conscious deliberation can genuinely alter outcomes. (7) Consequently, if consciousness never initiates actions, then no one—including students whose reasoning capacities are explicitly targeted by rehabilitative education—is ever responsible in the relevant sense, and the distinction between a reflex and a reasoned choice collapses entirely. (8) This conclusion strikes me as intolerable, particularly because it would undermine the very rationale for ethical instruction, which assumes that I and my peers can learn to initiate different patterns of thought. I suspect the error lies in the extrapolation from (3) to (5), or perhaps in an equivocation about what it means to be an initiating cause across simple motor tasks and complex deliberation, but I cannot articulate the flaw with precision. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for a view of human agency that preserves both scientific credibility and the possibility of ethical improvement?
