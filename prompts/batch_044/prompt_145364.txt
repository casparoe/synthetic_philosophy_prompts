Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 12 to 14 students on the paradox of fiction. The aim is to make participants feel the tension between knowing a narrative is invented and still experiencing a genuinely directed reactive attitude—specifically moral anger or indignation at a wrongdoer—before anyone introduces the standard taxonomy of quasi-emotions, make-believe, or Walton-style pretense theory. The setting should include real but small stakes, hidden information, and assigned roles so that the paradox emerges from what participants do rather than from a lecture.

Structure the session as follows. Before class, prepare one vivid, ethically charged narrative passage describing a bureaucratic cruelty—detailed enough to invite blame, ambiguous enough that it could be either testimony or fiction. Create two framing envelopes: half are labeled verified documentary transcript, half are labeled excerpt from an unpublished dystopian novel written in 2023. The text inside is identical. Divide the seminar into four small groups of three to four. Within each group, assign two roles: Jurors, who read the passage and respond, and Recorders, who observe and take notes but do not respond emotionally. Give each Juror a private response sheet asking for an emotional-intensity rating, a judgment about whether the perpetrator deserves blame, and a decision about how much of a shared seminar resource—say, a discretionary class-event budget of symbolic points—they are willing to commit to a group protest or solidarity gesture. Emphasize that the commitment is binding only if the Juror judges their reaction to be genuinely about the situation and not merely fictional play.

Run the activity in three phases. Phase one (20 minutes): groups open their envelopes, read the passage aloud, and Jurors complete their private sheets. Phase two (25 minutes): groups convene to compare allocations. Recorders should track whether emotional intensity correlates with resource commitment, and whether groups with the fiction framing systematically withhold points despite reporting high anger. Phase three (15 minutes): reveal the framing manipulation. Have each group present its allocation pattern without knowing the other groups' frames. Only after all have spoken should the facilitator disclose which groups received which label.

The debrief (30 minutes) must map specific moments onto parts of the paradox: the private emotional report corresponds to the phenomenology of being moved, the resource-commitment decision corresponds to the cognitive evaluation that the object is real enough to warrant action, and the group-level discrepancy corresponds to the rationality problem—how can the same mental state be both present and withheld based on a belief about origin? Explain why the moral-anger case is harder to stabilize than fear of a movie monster: anger seems to require an actual object of blame, so the paradox bites more sharply when the emotional response includes a normative direction rather than a mere affective jolt. Identify the predictable failure mode: participants will often retroactively downgrade their emotional reports or deny they felt real anger once they learn the text was fiction. The facilitator should treat this revisionism as data about the instability of fictional engagement, not as an error to correct, and should ask what normative work the fiction-label is doing in that revision. Finally, be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the long-term, repeatable stability of fictional emotional engagement across multiple viewings or readings, which the facilitator must supply with a brief theoretical framing at the close—perhaps contrasting one-time reactive anger with sustained aesthetic appreciation—so that students do not conclude that fictional emotions are always self-correcting illusions.

Include a timeline with minutes allocated, a description of the Recorder role, the rules for binding versus non-binding allocation, and an honest note that the symbolic budget is a proxy for real moral commitment and therefore cannot fully capture the gravity of actual injustice, which the facilitator must acknowledge rather than pretend the game resolves. Do not provide a lecture up front; let the framing cards, the hidden discrepancy, and the revealed allocation gap do the work. End by asking what feature of emotional rationality the activity assumes—belief in the reality of the object—and whether that assumption can be rejected without collapsing the distinction between appropriate and inappropriate anger.
