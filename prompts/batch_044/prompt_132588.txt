Having recently adopted a vegetarian diet for ethical reasons, I have begun revisiting religious texts and encountered an essay summarizing Maimonides' account of animal sacrifice in the Guide for the Perplexed. The summary states the following: Maimonides holds that God never commanded sacrifice as an intrinsic good; rather, it was permitted solely because the ancient Israelites were deeply habituated to pagan sacrificial cults and could not have been transitioned immediately to purely abstract worship. The sacrificial laws function as a purely instrumental accommodation—comparable to a physician prescribing a remedy that the patient wrongly believes is the cure itself—designed only to redirect idolatrous impulses toward the one God and to prevent worship elsewhere. Once prayer and non-physical devotion become viable, the Temple rituals are fully dispensable and carry no enduring spiritual significance. From this, the author concludes that Maimonides essentially anticipates the prophetic critique found in Isaiah and Amos, that he regards the sacrificial commandments as historically contingent rather than permanently binding, and that he would therefore support the complete abolition of animal sacrifice in any modern practice.

Please audit this summary claim by claim. Which parts would a careful reader of Maimonides accept as accurate, which are subtle misrepresentations—such as conflating his historical explanation with a normative rejection, overstating the dispensability of the commandments, misapplying an analogy he uses elsewhere, or importing a modern anti-ritualist stance he does not hold—and which are simply wrong? In particular, does the conclusion that Maimonides is essentially proto-vegetarian or anti-sacrificial in outlook actually follow from the accurate parts? Where the summary errs, please state what Maimonides actually maintains instead, without relying on verbatim quotations.
