I'm a clinician-ethicist serving on an institutional review board that reviews adaptive clinical-trial protocols, and I need a reusable decision procedure for evaluating whether a consent process remains valid in studies where treatment recommendations and risk profiles update in real time—driven, for example, by predictive algorithms that revise probabilities weekly. The procedure should operationalize three philosophical distinctions I actually care about: deception that goes beyond explicit lying to include selective framing of evolving uncertainty; coercion that includes not only direct threats but structural pressure tied to continued access to care; and competence that is domain-specific—understanding not just that risks exist, but how probabilistic updating works—rather than a generic mental-capacity check. I run these reviews alone, with about twenty minutes per protocol, and I must render one of three judgments—valid, invalid, or valid only with conditions—while clearly marking the points where the question is one of values rather than facts and the procedure must hand off to human judgment. Please construct an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where a negative answer stops the procedure with "invalid," and where it proceeds to a conditional approval; keep the distinctions that actually matter philosophically but drop those that can't survive a twenty-minute review; and be explicit about where the procedure will misfire or depend on judgment calls dressed up as steps. Then apply it to three protocols: first, a cancer-monitoring study in which patients consent to "adaptive risk tracking" without access to the model's feature weights or update logic; second, a trial offering free post-study specialty care contingent on enrollment and retention; and third, a case where the protocol is technically transparent—full disclosure of probabilities, no access conditions, adequate cognitive screening—yet frames risks using relative rather than absolute metrics in a way that exploits well-documented cognitive biases, so that the procedure likely returns "valid" despite my intuition that something is wrong. For each case, show the path through the checklist and the final judgment; and conclude by stating what the procedure genuinely cannot settle, particularly where transparency and non-coercion are satisfied but the moral quality of the framing remains disputed.
