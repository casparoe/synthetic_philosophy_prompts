I am preparing a single-session teaching plan for a rationalist discussion group—mostly technically trained non-philosophers with no formal philosophy background—on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarian moral reasoning, centered on the scenario in which a husband saves his spouse rather than a stranger and the charge that a fully calculated justification involves one thought too many. I want the dependency structure of the explanation mapped out precisely: the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the audience sees the objection as a structural failure of a moral theory rather than as a sentimental defense of partiality; the single carrying example that must do the work, specifying what properties it needs to include and what it must exclude so that it does not collapse into a generic intuition pump or a story about romantic loyalty; the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring—such as conflating the case with a critique of expected-utility maximization, reducing it to an emotional appeal against calculation, assuming Williams is defending selfishness, or treating it as an argument that moral agents should never count lives—together with the exact point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off. I also want a concrete check for understanding that distinguishes someone who has grasped the objection's logical structure from someone who merely finds the husband's choice relatable. Specify what parts of Williams's broader framework—particularly his attack on the morality system and his distinction between ground projects and obligations—should be explicitly bracketed as out of scope for this level, with an honest statement of what is being left out and why. Finally, separate what is settled about the objection's force and its proper scope from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—such as whether it undermines all consequentialist frameworks or only act-utilitarianism, whether it depends on a defensible theory of personal projects, and how it relates to later debates about moral demandingness—and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.
