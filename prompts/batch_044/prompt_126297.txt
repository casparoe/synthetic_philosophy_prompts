I'm working through Michel de Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals," composed around 1580 amid the French Wars of Religion, and I've reached the comparative section that moves from ethnographic description to moral indictment. In the passage, Montaigne describes peoples of the New World who, after defeating enemies in battle, do not consume them from hunger but through a ritual honoring the fallen warrior's valor: the death is swift, the body is shared communally, and the act expresses a kind of respectful acknowledgment of courage rather than hatred. He then contrasts this with practices he has observed in Europe, where citizens of the same kingdom, bound by shared language and law, subject living bodies to prolonged torture, burning, and dismemberment over disputes about religious interpretation—methods that refine cruelty and extend suffering far beyond anything found among these so-called savages. Montaigne concludes that if we call them barbarians according to some standard of cruelty, we must apply the term to ourselves with far greater justice, since our own civil conflicts display a more deliberate and inventive brutality.

What confuses me is the precise philosophical logic governing this comparison, and I hope you can help me parse it. One interpretation reads Montaigne as advancing a proto-relativist position: judgments like "barbarism" are culture-internal norms, and since the cannibals act consistently within their own ethical framework while Europeans flagrantly violate the norms they profess, there exists no neutral, transcultural standpoint from which to rank one society above another. Another interpretation holds that Montaigne is not dissolving universal standards at all, but rather invoking a minimal, cross-cultural criterion—perhaps that unnecessary cruelty or the degradation of shared humanity is the real vice—and using it to expose European hypocrisy rather than to celebrate cultural equality. This second reading seems more compatible with his broader skeptical project of undermining dogmatic certainty, especially if the skepticism targets European claims to superior virtue or knowledge rather than the possibility of moral judgment itself. Could you help me understand whether the passage is best read as a relativist argument that eliminates universal moral standards, as a skeptical mirror that leaves a thin universal standard intact while demolishing European self-certainty, or as a rhetorical strategy that serves a different purpose altogether? I am especially interested in how the structure of the comparison—moving from apparently neutral description to indictment—functions within Montaigne's larger attempt to destabilize the confidence with which his own culture asserts its superiority.
