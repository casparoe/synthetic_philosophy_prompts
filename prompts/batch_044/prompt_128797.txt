At a recent philosophy of metaphysics seminar, two doctoral students, Mara and Leo, got into a sustained argument about how to analyze modal claims involving artifacts—specifically, whether the claim that the Eiffel Tower could have been built from steel rather than puddled iron is best handled by counterpart theory or by transworld identity. I am recounting their exchange below; please adjudicate it move by move.

Mara opened with the counterpart-theoretic position: in no world is there literally the same iron tower that is now steel; rather, there is an actual-world iron tower and, in other worlds, steel-built counterparts that stand in a graded relation of similarity—matching origin, design intent, geographic location, and social function. The modal claim, on this view, expresses the existence of such a counterpart, and it avoids committing us to any controversial essential properties of artifacts.

Leo replied with the transworld-identity view: there is one and the same tower across worlds, and being constructed of puddled iron is among its essential properties. The steel-built object in the other world is therefore not the Eiffel Tower at all, but a different structure. He argued that counterpart theory replaces a clear metaphysical boundary with a vague metric—how similar must origin, design, and location be before we have a counterpart rather than a different object?—and that essentialism, disciplined by an artifact's design function, gives us a precise fact of the matter.

Mara then introduced a harder case: a distributed digital artifact, such as an open-source consensus protocol implemented simultaneously across thousands of nodes. In one world the protocol runs in Rust with one set of maintainers; in another, it runs in Python with different maintainers but identical consensus rules. Counterpart theory, she said, naturally handles this by weighting similarity across dimensions—code syntax, governance structure, network topology—without forcing a binary choice. Transworld identity, by contrast, must either declare an essential programming language (which seems absurd) or declare that the protocol survives any change (which makes identity trivial).

Leo responded that Mara had mischaracterized essentialism. Even for distributed artifacts, there is a functional essence—defined by the consensus rules themselves, not by substrate or personnel. The Rust and Python implementations are the same protocol because they share that essential functional core; a version with altered consensus rules is a different protocol altogether. He claimed this shows essential properties are not arbitrarily assigned but are fixed by design purpose, and that counterpart theory secretly relies on the same intuition—weighting some features over others—while denying there is a correct weighting.

Mara's final move was to press a sorites-style problem back onto Leo: if the essential core is defined by specific consensus rules, what happens in worlds where those rules evolve gradually—say, one parameter shifts by a small fraction in each successive world? At some point Leo must either admit a sharp cutoff (which he has no non-arbitrary reason for) or allow that identity comes in degrees, which undermines his claim that transworld identity avoids the vagueness he attributes to counterpart relations.

Please go through the exchange and rate each move: which arguments hold up, and which fail? Is Mara correct that counterpart theory handles distributed artifacts more gracefully, or has Leo shown that functional essentialism solves the problem without vagueness? Does Mara's sorites objection genuinely damage Leo's position, or does it apply symmetrically to counterpart similarity metrics? Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify anything both are missing—perhaps about the ontology of artifacts, about whether modal intuitions should be guided by linguistic practice, or about some other issue their exchange overlooks.
