As a novelist, I recently overheard an argument between two colleagues in my study group concerning a narrative experiment that mirrors a well-known puzzle in epistemology. One colleague, whom I shall call Eleanor, proposed a scenario: a character named Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday after a fair coin is tossed. If the result is heads, she is awakened once on Monday and interviewed. If tails, she is awakened on Monday, her memory is erased, and she is awakened again on Tuesday and interviewed, with no way to distinguish Monday from Tuesday. Each time she is awake, she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads.

Eleanor argued that Beauty should assign a credence of one-third to heads, on the grounds that there are three equally likely awakenings—Monday-heads, Monday-tails, and Tuesday-tails—and only one of them is paired with heads. Therefore, being awake is evidence that she is in one of the three awakenings, and the proportion favouring heads is one in three.

Her interlocutor, Marcus, replied that this confuses the number of experiences with the number of worlds. The coin is either heads or tails; the two awakenings on tails are not two separate possibilities but one possibility experienced twice. Since Beauty has no new empirical evidence about the coin itself—only the self-locating fact that she is awake now, which she already knew would happen in either case—her credence should remain one-half. He added that if she adopted one-third, she would be vulnerable to a Dutch book: she should accept bets on heads at odds that presume one-half, yet her own credence demands different stakes, which seems incoherent.

Eleanor countered that Marcus begs the question by assuming an uncentred framework. From Beauty's first-person perspective, the proposition "the coin is heads" is not the same as "the coin is heads in this world"; it is centred on her present awakening. The reference class of awakenings is not an arbitrary multiplication but the correct domain for her self-locating belief. She also noted that if Marcus were right, then Beauty's credence should not change upon being awakened at all, which seems to ignore the genuine epistemic novelty of finding oneself in a specific temporal location.

Marcus then introduced a further twist. He suggested that even if one grants centred propositions, Beauty should update by conditioning on "I am awake now" within a model that assigns prior probabilities to worlds, not to awakenings. In such a model, the prior for heads is one-half, and conditioning on being awake—an event that occurs in both worlds, though twice in one—leaves the posterior at one-half, provided one weights worlds, not experiences. He accused Eleanor of implicitly weighting experiences, which smuggles in a metaphysical commitment to the reality of each awakening as an independent evidential unit.

Eleanor responded that Marcus's world-weighting is itself a metaphysical choice, not a neutral default. She argued that in questions of self-locating belief, the evidential unit is precisely the centred experience, and that refusing to weight them equally is to privilege an external, God’s-eye view over the subject's own rational standpoint. She concluded that Marcus's position collapses into a form of epistemic externalism that denies the first-person its proper authority in belief formation.

They turned to me and asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: indicate where each party is correct, where they err, and whether either is missing a deeper point about the relation between self-locating belief and rational credence. Do not merely declare a winner; explain what each gets right about the structure of the problem, and identify any hidden assumptions—particularly regarding the ontology of centred propositions or the proper reference class for belief revision—that underpin their disagreement.
