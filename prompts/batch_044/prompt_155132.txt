Consider a small working group—two philosophers and one historian of logic—given roughly eighteen months to pursue the conceptual legacy of the White Horse dialogue and the broader School of Names (Mingjia). The familiar interpretations are well worn: the dialogue as a semantic claim about compound terms, as a metaphysical separation of universals from particulars, or as a dialectical reductio of sloppy taxonomic practice. Yet each interpretation leaves an unresolved cluster of tensions that should be sorted before any larger project begins. In particular, one can read the denial that a white horse is identical to a horse as resting on a hidden premise about whether kind-membership survives accidental modification—a premise that resembles modern sortal essentialism, but restricted to linguistic classification rather than to the ontology of the animals themselves. Before committing limited time, I want a defended prioritization. Rank the following open lines of inquiry (or revise the list if you think I have miscounted the live options): first, whether the dialogue is best reconstructed as a thesis about linguistic reference, about metaphysical composition, or about the logic of classification; second, whether the School’s broader taxonomy—such as the hard-and-white problem—implies a systematic theory of property inheritance that conflicts with Aristotelian hylomorphism, and whether that conflict can be resolved without collapsing into nominalism; third, whether these classical taxonomic concerns bear directly on contemporary debates about natural kinds and artificial categorization—for instance, whether an automated classification system that groups objects by perceptual features risks committing an analogous error when it separates color-modified instances from their base kind; and fourth, whether there is a recoverable logic of negation in the School of Names that differs from both Aristotelian and Stoic accounts, and if so whether it offers a genuinely distinct formal framework. For each line, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for scholars without deep sinological training or advanced formal logic, and what finding over the next year would cause you to reorder the priorities. I am not asking for a bibliography, a survey of Mingjia scholarship, or citations; I want an agenda that tells us where to direct our months, with the criteria—tractability, conceptual importance, and dependence relations among the questions—made explicit and the ranking defended against trade-offs.
