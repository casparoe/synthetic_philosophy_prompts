Compare the etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function, but do so through the lens of an engineered system rather than a familiar evolutionary example: consider a synthetic “dead-man switch” gene circuit in which a toxin gene is expressed unless continuously suppressed by an exogenous chemical signal, designed so that an organism escaping its containment dies. For each account, specify what plays the role of the function-determining factor—selection history versus current system contribution—and assign functions to the toxin gene, the suppressor module, and the circuit as a whole. Then examine the case of malfunction: if a mutation causes the toxin to be expressed even when the suppressor signal is present, the causal contribution of the mutated gene is identical to its contribution in the successful state, yet we still judge the circuit broken. Explain how each account accommodates, or fails to accommodate, this normative judgment. Finally, give a committed answer: which account more perspicuously explains functional attribution and malfunction in engineered biological systems, and can the etiological account legitimately substitute intentional design history for evolutionary selection history without collapsing into the causal-role view? Defend your position explicitly, and flag the strongest disanalogy between natural selection and design that threatens your conclusion.
