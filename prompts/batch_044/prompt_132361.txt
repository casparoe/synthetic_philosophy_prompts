I have recently taken on the dual role of executor for a family estate and informal archivist for a small community organization that lost its founder, and the experience has made the ethics of memory unexpectedly personal. Sorting through boxes of letters, meeting minutes, and half-burned photographs, I find myself caught between an impulse to preserve everything and a growing conviction that some silences are not archival failures but necessary conditions for the people still living. The problem is not practical—storage space—but normative: is there a moral duty to remember, and if so, does it ever yield to a corresponding right or duty to forget? I am trying to think this through without falling back on slogans about never forgetting or moving on, and I need a rigorous conceptual framework. In particular, I have begun working with two ideas that I think any adequate theory must accommodate: mnemonic debt, by which inheriting a record creates an obligation not merely to store it but to respond to it in some ethically appropriate way, and counter-memorial silence, the practice of deliberately preserving absence—an empty folder, a withheld name, a blank page—as a form of respect rather than neglect.

What I want is the full specification of what an adequate normative theory of collective and personal remembrance and deliberate forgetting must deliver in order to count as solving the problem rather than merely redescribing it. Write the desiderata: the phenomena it must explain, including why preserving a perpetrator’s private correspondence might compound harm even when it contains historical truth; why a survivor’s testimony demands preservation even when it destabilizes a community’s self-image; and why institutional archives systematically over-record the powerful and under-record the marginal, together with what a theory must say about that structural silence. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind it—especially the distinction between forgetting as erasure and forgetting as merciful release, and between archival completeness and narrative coherence. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable; I am particularly interested in whether the demand for total mnemonic transparency can coexist with a right to be forgotten, and whether the requirement that memory serve justice necessarily conflicts with the requirement that it serve psychological healing. Note which demands are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps—say, those aligned with Avishai Margalit’s ethics of memory versus those influenced by David Rieff’s suspicion of compulsory remembrance.

Finally, without producing a bibliography or asking me to consult sources, briefly indicate how the main existing approaches—an ethics that treats remembrance as a debt to the dead, a politics that treats strategic forgetting as a prerequisite for democratic reconciliation, and a psychological model that treats traumatic recall as something to be integrated rather than suppressed—fare against your list, with special attention to where they collapse distinctions you have marked as essential. I need the conceptual architecture, not a literature review.
