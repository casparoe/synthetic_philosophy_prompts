A penal-design oversight board with Scandinavian and American members has issued five rulings on facility proposals. First, it approved a Halden-style design—private cells, shared kitchens, minimal visible security, and extensive in-person family visitation spaces—despite construction costs far exceeding American norms, citing the normalization of the physical environment as essential to rehabilitation. Second, it rejected a maximum-security proposal that added cognitive-behavioral therapy classrooms but retained solitary confinement for disciplinary violations and eliminated direct family contact. Third, it approved mandatory vocational training for all residents but rejected mandatory psychological assessment as a prerequisite for parole eligibility. Fourth, it rejected a proposal that replaced physical family visitation rooms with video-only contact, even where contraband risk was documented as high. Fifth, it approved a work-release program in which residents maintained outside employment while living in secure dormitories, but rejected an otherwise identical program with unsupervised weekend leave.

Now consider a new proposal: "autonomy clusters" of eight residents with elected internal coordinators, self-managed daily schedules, and minimal staff presence, housed inside a repurposed American supermax structure with stark concrete architecture, no modifications for natural light, and no physical family visitation spaces replaced instead by monitored virtual-reality family interactions. The design offers no institutionally mandated vocational or educational programming beyond what residents organize collectively, but it cites pilot data from a Finnish facility showing reduced recidivism among participants.
