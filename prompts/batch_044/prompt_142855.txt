I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of computation, and I'm puzzled by a pattern I see across both computer-science curricula and AI governance frameworks: the Church-Turing thesis is taught and cited as an empirical conjecture about physical processes—one that could be overturned by discovering a physical oracle—yet in practice it functions as a normative filter that determines which architectures count as computation at all. Models invoking continuous analog dynamics or hypercomputation are not typically assessed empirically but dismissed as falling outside computation, while problems that are computable but intractable are treated as merely awaiting engineering improvements. What philosophical commitments about the relation between formal mathematics and physical possibility, and about the epistemic authority of a thesis that is simultaneously treated as falsifiable and definitional, does this institutional practice presuppose? Can a single coherent stance—whether conventionalist, realist, or something else—explain why the boundary is policed in both directions, or does the practice implicitly rely on incompatible commitments? And if we replaced this dual-use framing with a consistently empirical or consistently conventional interpretation, what would have to change in how departments evaluate novel architectures or regulators classify algorithmic feasibility?
