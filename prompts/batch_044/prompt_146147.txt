Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on partialism, and I now find it suspicious because the conclusion seems to erase the very distinction care ethics wants to defend. (1) Care ethics holds that moral obligations arise from concrete relationships of care and dependency rather than from abstract universal principles. (2) If obligations depend on concrete relationships, then absent any direct caring relation to a distant stranger, I have no direct obligation to assist that stranger, even at trivial cost. (3) But caring for an immediate dependent often requires sustaining conditions that depend on others: for example, caring for a child with a rare autoimmune disorder requires maintaining access to a specialized drug whose production involves miners, chemists, and regulators I will never meet. (4) Therefore, my first-order obligation to care for my child generates derivative obligations to sustain the supply chain that makes that care possible. (5) These derivative obligations are not merely prudential; if I knowingly allow the chain to collapse through negligence, I fail in my care for the child. (6) Now, modern supply chains are globally interconnected, so sustaining care for any immediate dependent requires sustaining economic and ecological conditions that affect nearly everyone. (7) Hence care ethics, taken seriously, implies that anyone with local care obligations has indirect obligations of comparable scope and urgency to cosmopolitan obligations. (8) But care ethicists explicitly reject universal obligations as the foundation of morality, insisting that the particular relation is primary. So the argument shows care ethics either collapses into the universalism it opposes, or it must deny that maintaining care conditions generates genuine moral obligations — which seems to make care ethics self-defeating in practice. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the care-ethical framework? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2), but I want to understand what I am paying if I do.
