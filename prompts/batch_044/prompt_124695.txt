In my ethics of representation seminar we've been stuck for months on whether dramatizing real dead people in historical fiction violates some kind of obligation to them. One side says the dead have no interests so there's nothing to violate; the other says we owe them accurasy or at least not turning them into mouthpeices for our politics. I think both sides are missing something: maybe the harm isn't to the dead but to the living who have to inhabit a culture where dead people are just narrative clay. But I'm not sure if that's a genuine third position or just dissolves the original debate. Is the stall actually a hidden disagreement about whether moral obligations can survive death, or is it that 'truth' means different things -- historical fidelty versus not corrupting collective memory? Or is it verbal: both camps say 'we owe them' but one means a strict duty and the other just means it'd be decent? I want a diagnosis using what each side actually says, plus whether this 'wrong-to-the-living' framing moves the needle or just adds noise. What's actually blocking progress here?
