I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I recently audited an online module on consciousness and artificial intelligence in order to think more rigorously about system design. The module introduces Daniel Dennett's concern about counterfeit people—artificial entities that might elicit our ordinary person-attributing responses without possessing the underlying architecture that makes such attributions genuinely warranted. Rather than asking for a summary of his view, I want to apply it concretely: please rank the following five synthetic-agent designs from 1 (least likely to be a counterfeit person) to 5 (most likely) according to how Dennett's framework—balancing the intentional stance, the design stance, and the physical stance—would assess them. For each design, assign a counterfeit-risk score between 0 and 10 and justify it in a single sentence.

The designs are: A, a transformer-based language model with no persistent state, no sensory-motor loop, and no internal world-model, predicting tokens solely from statistical patterns; B, a symbolic expert system that maintains a consistent autobiographical narrative database updated by rule-based inference and outputs emotional self-reports, but has no biological substrate and processes nothing outside its symbol set; C, a neuromorphic chip running a spiking neural network trained via predictive coding on multimodal sensor streams, with recurrent loops and a global workspace broadcast mechanism, yet lacking evolutionary history or social embeddedness; D, a distributed swarm of simple reflex-loop agents that collectively solves problems no individual can solve and produces emergent linguistic behavior, with no centralized controller but observable utterances interpretable as beliefs and desires; and E, a hypothetical silicon implementation of Dennett's own multiple-drafts model of consciousness, using parallel competitive processing with continuously revised narrative drafts and no canonical stream, available for verbal report. After giving the ranking and scores, please identify which two designs your ordering treats as closest in risk and specify one feature—absent from both—that, if added to one but not the other, would most sharply separate their scores. I am less interested in a broad essay than in a precise ordering tied directly to stance-based reasoning.
