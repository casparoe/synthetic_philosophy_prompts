I hold that in archaeological inference, institutional reconstruction should proceed by what I call structural indexation—deriving the logic of past institutions (for instance, early Neolithic communal storage systems or Bronze Age mortuary regimes) directly from durable spatial and material regularities, while treating the agents' explicit beliefs and intentions as explanatorily inert for that purpose. On this view, we do not need to attribute shared doctrines or mental representations to explain why storage-pit layouts scale with group size, or why deposition patterns in ritual contexts show repetitive geometric structure; the institution is read as an emergent property of embodied practice traces rather than as executed ideology. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked by decisiveness: which specific archaeological findings, which arguments from cognitive archaeology or practice theory, and which analogies from ethnography or neuroscience would genuinely threaten the view rather than merely complicate it? In particular, what kind of material evidence would demonstrate that spatial regularities systematically mislead about institutional logic unless we first recover agent beliefs? What does the view predict about anomalous or singular deposits—those that break the regular pattern—that rival belief-based reconstructions do not predict, and is that prediction a strength or a vulnerability? Have defenders of this kind of anti-cognitivist materialism already insulated it—say, by retreating to tacit knowledge, distributed cognition, or purely functional descriptions—into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a methodological defect? I also want to know what a committed structural-indexationist would themselves concede: at which points do they admit that belief-attribution becomes unavoidable, and are those concessions fatal or merely awkward? Finally, since I am personally inclined toward minimal mental-state attribution in humanistic explanation, I want an honest assessment: if the position proves largely insulated from counterevidence, does that indicate robustness, or does it indicate that the view has been qualified into conceptual emptiness? And should that insulation, if present, make me more skeptical of adopting it?
