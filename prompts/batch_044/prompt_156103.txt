I've reconstructed what I'll call "the track record" from a prominent scholar's recent essays—without attributing it precisely—yielding these verdicts: Singapore under Lee Kuan Yew (high growth, rule of law, restricted pluralism) is granted performance legitimacy; China's reform era from 1978 onward (rapid growth, social order, extensive surveillance and suppression of organized dissent) is granted it, though qualified; Pinochet's Chile (initial growth and order achieved through systematic terror and disappearances) is denied it; the contemporary UAE (extraordinary prosperity and internal order built on extreme labor exploitation with no path to citizenship or political voice for the majority) is denied it; and a hypothetical purely technocratic developmental state that achieves Scandinavian-level welfare and growth through administration alone, with no elections but robust judicial independence and freedom of expression, is granted it. There is no visible violence, no labor exploitation comparable to the UAE, and judicial independence is preserved for commercial disputes, yet all political association is algorithmically suppressed; the population reports high subjective well-being. Should this regime be granted performance legitimacy if we want to stay consistent with the five verdicts above? My reconstruction suggests at least two competing rules fit the track record: one that requires a threshold of institutional feedback (even non-electoral, such as independent courts or spaces for expression) and one that requires only that coercion remain invisible and welfare high, regardless of feedback mechanisms. Depending on which rule is actually running, the AI-governance case comes out differently. Give your best guess with reasons, then name the one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish these two rules—perhaps a regime with visible but non-lethal coercion and excellent welfare, or one with perfect welfare and explicit, non-hidden prohibition of dissent paired with robust non-political institutional independence. In your answer, please separate what is settled in the comparative-politics literature (for instance, the conceptual distinction between output legitimacy and input legitimacy) from what is genuinely contested (for instance, whether invisible algorithmic suppression counts as a rights violation that undermines legitimacy, or whether subjective well-being reports can substitute for institutional feedback), and do not present a contested interpretive claim as the consensus view.
