As a retired ecologist recently turned to philosophy, I serve on my university's Heritage and Ethics Committee. We must decide the fate of a colonial-era botanical archive (c. 1890–1940): field notebooks containing irreplaceable phenological data are interwoven with unconsented recordings of indigenous knowledge and derogatory annotations. One proposal demands full digitization and open access in the name of preserving historical and scientific truth; another urges restricted access, critical contextual framing, or even ritual retirement of selected volumes, arguing that affected communities possess a right to be forgotten and to govern their own narratives. Please draft the concise written opinion—suitable for circulation before our March meeting—that I will present. It must recommend a concrete course of action with a clear timeline (for instance, a phased release schedule, a conditional quarantine protocol, or a selective deaccession plan), and it should treat the strongest argument for the opposing view with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it. Frame the reasoning around the philosophical tension between the duty to remember and the ethical case for forgetting, but do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; apply the concepts directly. I would especially value the introduction of a precise but unfamiliar conceptual tool—something like a "mnemonic triage" that classifies records according to the distinct epistemic obligations they generate—so long as the recommendation remains firmly anchored in the practical decision before us.
