I am trying to decide whether to start a family with my partner, and we have reached an ethical impasse that I can describe but not fully resolve. We understand that if we conceive naturally, there is a meaningful chance—roughly one in four—that our child will inherit a genetic variant that produces a life of moderate but persistent suffering. By conventional measures, such a life is still well worth living, and the child, if able to reflect later, would not wish never to have existed. We also understand that if we pursue IVF with preimplantation genetic screening, we can largely eliminate this risk, but in doing so we would not be "saving" the same child who would have been born naturally; the screening and selection process determines which embryo, hence which future person, comes into existence. My partner takes this to support a strict position: because we have no moral duty to bring anyone into existence, we should either commit to the screening path—thereby ensuring that whoever we do bring about lives well—or remain childless altogether. Creating someone who suffers is wrong, he argues, while failing to create someone who thrives is not. I feel pulled in two directions. On one hand, I agree there seems to be an asymmetry between causing harm and failing to confer a benefit. On the other hand, if the naturally conceived child genuinely prefers existence to non-existence, it is hard for me to see exactly who is wronged by natural conception, and demanding IVF feels like treating potential happiness as obligatory. Does the fact that screening alters which specific person exists undermine my partner's reasoning, or is there a principled middle ground that respects the asymmetry without requiring either technological intervention or childlessness? Please begin with your bottom-line judgment in the first sentence, then lay out the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question.
