I have reached a difficult passage in Part I where Hobbes argues that geometry differs from every other science because its objects—points, lines, planes, and figures—are not found in nature but are constructed by the mind according to explicit rules. Because the geometer is the author of these constructions, Hobbes claims, he knows the cause of every property in the figure and can therefore produce demonstrations that are absolutely certain. Yet in the same work Hobbes treats geometry as the foundation for mechanics and for reasoning about real bodies in motion.

What I cannot resolve is the ontological status of these mental figures. If a geometric triangle is purely an artifact of reasoning—something the mind builds rather than discovers in the world—how can theorems about it yield true statements about actual material bodies? Does Hobbes hold that physical bodies are literally composed of these mental constructions, that they approximate them, or that there is some other bridge between the constructed figure and external reality? And is his claim that geometry is uniquely demonstrative meant to confine its objects to the mind, or to show that any science reconstructed on geometric lines inherits the same certainty?

I also want to test whether a modern analogy I have in mind is fair or anachronistic. In software engineering we build formal models and verify properties of those models, fully aware that the running system may diverge from the model; the verification is true of the construction, not necessarily of the physical hardware. Reading Hobbes, I wonder if he is advancing a similar separation: geometric proof gives necessary truths about our own constructed definitions, and when we apply geometry to physics we are essentially checking whether physical motions match our formal model rather than observing nature directly. Does the passage support this kind of "model-checking" interpretation, or does Hobbes require a stronger metaphysical link between mental construction and material reality?
