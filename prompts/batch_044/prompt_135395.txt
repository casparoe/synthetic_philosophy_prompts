As a novelist, I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a memorial symposium for my late mentor, a philosopher who spent four decades writing about transformative experience—specifically, the idea that certain lived changes restructure one’s values so radically that they cannot be rationally evaluated from the outside. The audience is divided: half are specialists in philosophy of mind and decision theory who will recognize the framework immediately; the other half are family members, museum patrons, and students who never encountered his technical work. I need a formal, polished address of about seven minutes, written in American English, that treats this concept not as decorative biography but as the structural spine of the speech. Specifically, it should shape what I can and cannot claim about knowing him now that he is gone: I must comfort those who grieve without invoking false consolations of hidden awareness or spiritual continuity, and I must honor the experts by showing precisely why epistemic humility is the appropriate posture for mourning someone whose innermost transformations we could never share. Please avoid citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts I could not be expected to recall without searching.
