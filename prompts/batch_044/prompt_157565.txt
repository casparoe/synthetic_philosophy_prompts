In philosophy of history, the standoff between covering-law and narrative explanation has persisted for generations: defenders of nomological explanation maintain that historical accounts implicitly depend on general regularities, however tacit, while narrative theorists hold that history explains by embedding events in temporal sequences and intentional structures that law-subsumption cannot replicate. Despite extensive exchange, neither camp has moved—covering-law proponents treat narrative coherence as a pragmatic heuristic for hidden regularities, whereas narrative proponents treat law-invocation as either trivial or as distorting the particularity that makes an event historically significant. My working hypothesis is that the stall reflects a deeper disagreement about explanatory partitioning—whether the boundaries of what needs explaining are discovered in the world's causal structure or constructed by the historian's practical interests—and that both sides therefore evaluate the same case studies with incompatible criteria for what counts as a complete account.
