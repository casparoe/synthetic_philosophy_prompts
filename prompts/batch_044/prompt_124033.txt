In graduate seminars on global justice, the proverb "charity begins at home" is repeatedly invoked—not as an opening observation but as a conversation-stopper—to resist the claim that wealthy states bear stringent, direct obligations to non-citizens, whether in refugee resettlement, climate reparations, or the distribution of globally scarce medical resources. Before I allow it to do that work in my classroom, I want its precise logical anatomy mapped. Please lay out, as a menu of distinct theses rather than a single interpretation, the precise claims that slogan could be expressing. For each reading, indicate: (i) whether it concerns the scope of moral obligation, the psychology of motivation, the efficacy of institutional delivery, or the normative legitimacy of state partiality; (ii) whether it amounts to a trivial empirical generalization, a defensible but limited principle of practical reason, or a false or morally arbitrary boundary; and (iii) whether, on that reading, the proverb genuinely supports a nationalist conclusion—say, that compatriots possess special agent-relative claims—or whether it remains fully compatible with a cosmopolitan distributive scheme. I am especially interested in the intermediate positions that smuggle in substantive philosophical commitments: for instance, a view that treats the state as a cooperative scheme for mutual advantage rather than as a node in a structure of global injustice; or a claim that associative duties are generated by shared political membership but are strictly non-transferable. Distinguish these from the genuinely trivial claim that agents should address local problems they are well-positioned to solve. Finally, tell me which of these readings is actually being invoked when an interlocutor uses the phrase to dismiss obligations to distant strangers, and whether that particular reading can survive the objection that national borders are, from the standpoint of moral justification, as arbitrary as place of birth.
