The debate over workfare, benefit fraud, and conditional welfare is usually framed as a trade-off between fiscal responsibility and social protection. But beneath that surface, there are deeper open questions about whether “desert” can be operationalized without massive false-positive errors in moral classification, whether verification systems create administrative burdens that function as de facto exclusion regardless of eligibility, and whether labor-supply responses to conditionality are large enough to justify the institutional costs.

Assume you are advising a policy research group with three years of funding and no mandate to produce legislative language—only to identify which unresolved problem, if settled conclusively, would most reshape how we think about conditionality and the “undeserving poor.” You must prioritize among the following four lines of work, though you may also propose one additional upstream question if you believe the list misses the critical dependency:

1. The empirical magnitude and heterogeneity of labor-supply responses to workfare versus unconditional transfers, especially among populations with unobserved disabilities or caregiving obligations.
2. The normative coherence of reciprocity-based desert: whether it collapses once we acknowledge that structural unemployment and bureaucratic misclassification make individual “effort” hard to isolate from circumstance.
3. The institutional design of verification and anti-fraud systems, specifically the moral asymmetry between false positives (denying eligible claimants) and false negatives (paying ineligible ones), and whether current systems are optimized for fiscal control rather than for accurate moral classification.
4. The conceptual stability of the “undeserving poor” as a category—whether it tracks a real moral distinction or functions primarily as a boundary mechanism that shifts with fiscal pressure and political rhetoric.

Your task is not to survey all four but to defend a strict ranking of which two deserve the bulk of the group’s effort and which should be deprioritized. Specify the criteria you are using—tractability with existing methods, normative urgency for claimants, dependency (how many other questions hang on this one), and neglectedness in current literature. For your top-ranked question, identify the specific design principle or normative implication that would change if it were answered conclusively, and explain which commonly cited implications of welfare reform are actually overrated or do not follow. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the deliverable is a defended agenda, not a survey of authorities.
