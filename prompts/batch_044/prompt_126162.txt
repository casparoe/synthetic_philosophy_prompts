You are grading a take-home examination in philosophy of science for a course on prediction, falsification, and theoretical rescue. The exam question reads as follows: Thomas Malthus maintained that population grows geometrically while food supply grows only arithmetically, implying that unless checked by preventive mechanisms such as moral restraint, positive checks including famine and disease must eventually limit growth. For nearly two centuries this seemed empirically defeated, especially after the agricultural innovations of the mid-twentieth century enabled food production to rise faster than population in many parts of the world. Some contemporary defenders argue that Malthus was right but early, contending that technological progress has merely postponed an inevitable crisis that will emerge once agricultural productivity reaches hard biophysical limits. Evaluate whether this defense is philosophically legitimate. Specifically, address whether appealing to a delayed timeline makes a theory unfalsifiable in principle, compare Malthus's predicament to at least one other long-range scientific prediction in which a correct general claim initially lacked a verified mechanism, and state the conditions under which a rescue strategy avoids being an ad hoc adjustment rather than a genuine theoretical refinement.

The student submitted this answer: Malthus clearly predicted that population would outrun food supply and cause widespread famine, and this did not happen in the industrialized world, so in a straightforward Popperian framework his theory was falsified and should be rejected. However, defenders say he was right but early, meaning technology bought time but cannot do so forever. This is analogous to Wegener proposing continental drift without knowing about seafloor spreading; Wegener was right about the phenomenon but wrong about the mechanism and the timeline, so being right but early is a legitimate category in science. To avoid being ad hoc like Ptolemaic epicycles, a rescue must predict the new conditions under which the original claim will hold, and Malthus's defenders do this by pointing to soil degradation and freshwater limits. Actually Malthus never denied that technology could improve yields temporarily; he denied that cultural norms could permanently reduce fertility, a mistake he inherited from his reading of Condorcet's critics. Because he made no specific date for the crisis, saying he was right but early is unfalsifiable, since any future failure can be claimed as the fulfillment and any present success as the delay. Comparing this to cosmological heat-death predictions is useful because both depend on physical constants that might change, but Malthus's claim depends on human behavior that can adapt through policy. Therefore the rescue is partly legitimate if restricted to a tendency claim rather than a deterministic law, yet anyone who treats it as fully legitimate ignores that the absence of a precise timeline makes the claim compatible with any outcome, which is precisely what makes it vulnerable to the charge of being ad hoc.
