For a seminar on medieval Islamic philosophy, I need a Petrarchan sonnet—ABBA ABBA in the octave, CDECDE in the sestet, with a volta at line 9—that stages Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment structurally rather than describing it. The octave should suspend the speaker in a void: no sight, no touch, no bodily memory, with enclosed rhyme mirroring sensory deprivation. At the volta, the speaker should arrive at an immediate, contentless awareness of self—an irreducible indexical—without any object of consciousness. The sestet should hold the traditional reading, in which this proves an immaterial soul, against a minimal modern interpretation in which the bare I is not metaphysical proof but an empty given. Ask the rhyme scheme to leave this tension unresolved, for instance by echoing soul against void in the closing lines, so the poem ends in ontological ambiguity rather than resolution. Do not quote Avicenna directly; paraphrase the claim that consciousness of oneself is prior to and independent of bodily sensation. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with the argument recognizes the distinction between awareness of self and awareness of body, and polished enough to read aloud. Please keep it to exactly fourteen lines.
