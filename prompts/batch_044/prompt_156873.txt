I'm a novelist working on this strange book about interioraty—sorry, interiority—and I got invited to speak next week at an interdisciplinary seminar on narrative and cognition, which is way outside my usual crowd. I was discussing this with my friend who writes poetry and she said I was being pretentious, but I think there's something real here. I've become obsessed with Dennett's heterophenomenology, the idea that we treat first-person reports of mental states not as direct windows into real conscousness but as texts describing fictional worlds—heterophenomenological worlds that exist only in the report. My thesis for the talk is that this scientific method is actually the correct aesthetic framework for writing convincing characters: instead of pretending we're describing real mental states inside people, we should treat a character's self-reports as constructing a coherent fictional mental world, which explains why unreliable narrators feel more truthful than omniscient ones. The audience will be mostly literary theorists who think consciousness studies is just neuroscience with delusions of grandeur, plus two cognitive scientists who actually read Dennett and will call me out if I misrepresent the method. I need a prep sheet for the objections. Give them to me in roughly the order they'll come—first the literary people complaining that I'm killing the emotional soul of fiction with mechanistic language, then the scientists saying I'm misusing an empirical tool for literary fluff. For each one, tell me what I should concede up front (probably that fiction isn't science and I'm not claiming it is) and what I should hold onto (that the structural parallel between methodological bracketing and narrative distance is genuinely illuminating). Point out the weakest point in my own argument—probably that heterophenomenology was designed to avoid ontological commitments about mental states, whereas fiction thrives on making exactly those commitments about characters? And include the objection I'm praying nobody raises: whether this makes all emotional identification with characters impossible, reducing literature to anthropology of imaginary beings.
