I'm a high school student in a philosophy elective, and during our unit on consciousness we argued about whether dreams are genuine experiences. One student kept repeating the line "the experience is always in the remembering, not in the sleeping" to dismiss the idea that anything truly happens to us during REM sleep, insisting that any "dream experience" is just a narrative assembled upon waking. I can tell the phrase is hiding several different claims about memory, consciousness, and verification, but I can't separate them. Could you lay out the precise theses this slogan might express—from the trivial point that we only learn about dreams through later reports, to stronger claims about whether consciousness requires a waking self-monitor, whether first-person reports are the only possible evidence, and whether the narrative structure is fabricated after the fact—and for each say whether it's plausible and whether it actually supports dismissing dream-experiences? In particular, address how this relates to the Malcolm-style worry about verification criteria versus the Dennett-style view that dreams are retrospective constructions. Finally, please say what would change your mind: would reliable real-time reports from sleeping subjects, or a convincing model of non-waking consciousness, undermine the strongest version of this view, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
