I have come to accept a strong reading of Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis: not merely that ascetic Protestantism correlated with the emergence of modern capitalism, but that cultural values can operate as genuinely autonomous causal powers in social history — directing economic behavior independently of material conditions and institutional structures, and meeting the explanatory demands we ordinarily place on structural or interest-based causes. I understand Weber as arguing that values need not be epiphenomenal; rather, they can function as what one might call 'effective reasons' that restructure incentives and action orientations from within. What would count decisively against this position? I want more than the familiar empirical objections — that Catholic regions such as parts of Italy and Flanders industrialized without Protestant ethics, that capitalism had pre-Reformation antecedents, or that East Asian economies achieved rapid growth without the relevant religious framework. I am asking for the deeper conceptual and methodological defeaters: arguments that values can never satisfy the conditions of causal explanation, such as counterfactual dependence, identifiable mechanisms, or independent manipulability; interpretations of Weber's ideal-type methodology that reduce the thesis to a heuristic comparison rather than a causal claim about historical development; and the concern that defenders have qualified the view so extensively — through notions of elective affinity, reciprocal causation, and multiple paths to modernity — that it risks predicting nothing distinctive. Please rank these from fatal to merely awkward. Which defeaters would a committed proponent of the 'culture as autonomous cause' reading concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be absorbed by further refinement? Finally, provide an honest assessment of whether this interpretation is well-insulated from refutation, and whether that insulation represents methodological modesty or a problematic slide into unfalsifiability.
