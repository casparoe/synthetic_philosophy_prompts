I'm working on a column about greedy reductionism and I keep running into Dennett's crane versus skyhook image being used like a swiss army knife in moral psychology debates. The usual reductionist move is that explaining moral belief from neurons upward is pure crane-building - each floor holds the next, no magic hanging from the sky. Critics flip it and say thats like pretending a skyscraper can design itself, so eventually you need a skyhook holding the blueprint. I was chatting with a friend in neuroethics over coffee who said both sides are just fighting over construction metaphors that dont fit normative stuff at all.

So I want a real audit, not more metaphor-mongering. Can you map out the correspondence table - what's the ground, the scaffolding, the crane, the skyhook, and the finished building when we apply this to moral psychology? Then show me where the mapping actually fractures: does it smuggle in bottom-up causation that norms lack? Does it quietly import the idea that engineers aren't part of the system? Separate the disanalogies that actually undermine the reductionist conclusion from the ones that are just cosmetic. And tell me whether the claim - that morality can be fully explained without any higher-level scaffolding - survives once the cracks are visible. If the analogy is broken, whats a less misleading image for how moral concepts sit on neural mechanisms? Also, dont bother with a bibliography - I know you cant search and I dont want guesses.
