I'm trying to work out when it's acceptable for scientists and modelers to rely on assumptions that are plainly false, judging by my own reactions to concrete cases. Galileo ignored air resistance and friction to study falling bodies, and that strikes me as perfectly legitimate—we still trust the results. Climate scientists treat the atmosphere as a continuous fluid rather than discrete molecules, and since their temperature predictions hold up, that simplification seems acceptable too. An economic forecast that assumes every actor is perfectly rational and fully informed also makes unrealistic premises, yet it predicts market movements accurately, and I think that validates the approach. By contrast, a psychology model that assumes people have unlimited working memory makes an equally false assumption but predicts almost nothing, and I feel the unrealism is genuinely damaging there rather than harmless. A medical dosage tool that treats the human body as a linear system happens to yield correct numbers for one specific patient group, but only by coincidence, and that feels wrong in a way Galileo's idealization does not. A planetary-orbit model treating planets as point masses with no atmosphere predicts positions well, which also seems fine. But I'm going back and forth on a predictive policing algorithm that assumes human behavior is fully deterministic, relies on proxy variables like neighborhood income, and achieves statistical accuracy in forecasting crime risk: one part of me says that if the predictions work, the false underlying picture shouldn't matter, yet another part thinks there's a defect that accuracy alone cannot cure, perhaps because the unrealistic assumptions feed back into the social reality being measured. Is there a single principle—maybe about whether the false parts are causally isolated from what's being predicted, or about the purpose for which the model is used—that captures all these judgments? What would it say about the policing algorithm? And if no clean principle saves every verdict, which of my reactions should I treat as the mistake?
