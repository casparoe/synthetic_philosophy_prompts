At a late dinner in Lisbon, two acquaintances of mine—Mira, who works with historical manuscripts, and Jonas, who teaches philosophy—became entangled in a dispute about what Descartes's cogito genuinely establishes. I remained silent and listened; I am not asking for advice, but for a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments they actually made.

Mira began by saying that the argument demonstrates nothing beyond the bare occurrence of cognition in the present instant. The first-person pronoun in I think, she maintained, is imported as an ontological subject; strictly speaking, all that is shown is that thinking is happening now, not that a stable, enduring thinker exists.

Jonas responded that even a momentary occurrence requires a bearer. One cannot have dancing without a dancer, or illumination without something that illuminates. Consequently, the inference from the presence of thought to the existence of a thinker is not a grammatical trick but a metaphysical necessity: actions presuppose agents.

Mira accepted the general principle but rejected its force in this case. The agent, she said, might be nothing more than a transient neural arrangement or an impersonal process. The cogito secures existence, she insisted, but it does not determine what kind of thing exists. To treat it as establishing a substantial self, she argued, is to read far more into the argument than its premises allow.

Jonas then claimed that Mira was understating the reflexive structure of the reasoning. Descartes is not observing himself from some external vantage point; he is attempting to doubt his own existence and discovering that the attempt is self-defeating. This, Jonas said, proves not merely that something exists, but that the existing thing is essentially defined by thought—since every other attribute, including bodily extension and spatial location, can be suspended by doubt. What remains is therefore not just existence but an essential nature: a substance whose essence is thought.

Mira answered on two fronts. First, she said Jonas confuses epistemic priority with ontological essence: the fact that bodily properties are currently doubtable does not entail that they are absent, only that they lie outside this particular indubitable core. Second, she drew a comparison to certain Buddhist phenomenological accounts in which experiential events occur without a persistent owner. She suggested that the first-person reference in the cogito might similarly be a linguistic artifact shaped by European grammar rather than a metaphysical discovery.

Jonas conceded that the argument does not, by itself, prove immateriality or immortality. Nevertheless, he insisted that within Descartes's own project—stripping away everything susceptible to doubt—the reasoning legitimately isolates what survives. He accused Mira of importing an alien framework and of reducing a rigorous metaphysical result to a trivial grammatical observation, when in fact the argument demonstrates that I am essentially a thinking substance, however embodied or temporary that substance may ultimately prove to be.

Please examine their exchange move by move. For each of the five stages outlined above, rate the position from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating. Then indicate who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both disputants overlook regarding the precise scope of what the argument can and cannot deliver.
