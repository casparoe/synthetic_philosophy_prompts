Here is a reconstructed passage from the opening of Berkeley’s first dialogue in the Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous (1713). Philonous asks whether Hylas can form the idea of a tree standing in a garden with no perceiver present. Hylas answers yes—he has just done so in thought. Philonous replies that in thinking it, Hylas is present to himself as the thinker, so the tree remains within a mind, namely his own, during the act of conception. He then presses Hylas to try to conceive the tree as existing with absolutely no relation to any mind whatsoever, not even as an object of thought. Philonous claims that upon examination one either slips back into conceiving the tree as perceived by oneself, or else empties the concept of all content, leaving only an empty word. From this he infers that the very notion of an unperceived material object involves either a hidden contradiction or a meaningless abstraction.

My question is not about the standard objection that Berkeley confuses conceiving-with and conceiving-without. Rather, I want help understanding what modal claim he is actually advancing. Is he arguing that mind-independent existence is metaphysically impossible because contradictory, or only that it is epistemically inaccessible because our cognitive faculties are structured by perception? And if the latter, does this weaken the argument into a claim about human psychology rather than about the nature of being?

Please explain the reasoning with precision, distinguishing the reading on which it is an argument about conceivability-as-possibility from the reading on which it concerns the necessary conditions for having an idea at all, and say how Berkeley’s separate doctrine of notions (as opposed to ideas) bears on the second horn. Then say how confident you are in this interpretation, and single out the specific point—whether about the scope of idea, the modal inference, or the role of notions—about which you are least sure.
