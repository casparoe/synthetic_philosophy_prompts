I'm working on a piece about how philosophers recyle old arguments, and I was at a pannel where two people got into it over Nietzsche's slave-revolt story. I want to understand who actually has the better read here, because both seemed sharp.

Person A said Nietzsche's point in the genealogy is that slave morality wasn't just a flip of noble values — it was a genuinely new kind of evaluation that invented the concept of evil as an active, malicious force. The nobles just had good and bad, where bad was like unfortunate or low. The slaves turned that around and made the nobles evil, which is a totally different metaphysical category. So A thinks Nietzsche is describing a creative, world-historical innovation.

Person B pushed back hard. B said that's overreading. The slaves didn't invent anything; they just took the noble's own value-scale and inverted it out of resentment. Good becomes evil, bad becomes good. B said if you look closely, Nietzsche calls it a revaluation, but that just means flipping the table, not building a new table. B also said that A is smuggling in a kind of heroic agency that Nietzsche actually denies the slaves — they're reactive, not creative.

Then A came back with this niche twist: what if the slave revolt only works because it secretly borrows the noble's own standard of strength, but applies it to suffering? The slave says I suffer, therefore I am strong in a hidden way, which is a hybrid value that isn't pure inversion or pure invention, but a kind of parasitic mutation. A said that's why Nietzsche is ambivalent — he hates it, but he sees it as genuinely new.

B replied that this parasitic mutation idea is just A's own invention, not Nietzsche's. B said Nietzsche is pretty explicit that the slaves lack the strength for any real creation; they only have the cunning of the weak.

They asked me which side was right about what, and whether A's parasitic mutation framing is a useful addition or just a distortion. Can you walk through the exchange point by point and say who's right at each step, and who is more right overall? Don't just pick a winner — tell me what both are missing.
