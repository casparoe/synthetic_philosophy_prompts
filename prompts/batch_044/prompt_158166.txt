I recently began advising a municipal partnership program in a context where officials routinely receive substantial gifts from constituents as part of an expected cycle of mutual aid, which has forced me to reconsider whether corruption denotes a universal moral failure or a category tied to a specific institutional separation between public office and private life. My own credence that Western anti-corruption norms—prohibiting substantial gifts to officials regardless of intent—are genuinely universal moral requirements rather than culturally specific conventions is about 0.30; a development ethicist colleague places hers near 0.75. Give me your own number, but more importantly decompose the judgment: (a) whether corruption necessarily involves a conflict between an official's fiduciary duty to an abstract public and a competing private obligation, or whether it can exist even when public and private roles are structurally fused; (b) whether a practice like anticipatory reciprocity—giving before a service is rendered—differs morally from retrospective gratitude within such fused-role systems, and whether that temporal structure matters independently of the obligation created; (c) whether the universal claim survives the fact that Western systems conceal analogous obligations through legal lobbying and patronage networks; and (d) whether a norm can be universal if its enforcement depends on state capacities that many societies lack. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question being resolved in the universalist direction, indicate how they combine into your overall credence, identify which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and state what conceptual clarification or empirical comparison would shift you most.
