In debates over the Epicurean claim that death is not bad for the one who dies, two analogies do most of the work. The first compares death to a long dreamless sleep: just as a sleeper is not present to suffer during unconsciousness and does not regard the interval as a harm upon waking, the dead are not present to suffer and so death is not a harm to them. The second, often associated with Lucretian symmetry, compares post-mortem non-existence to pre-natal non-existence: since I do not lament the centuries before my birth, consistency demands I not fear the centuries after my death. Both analogies are usually deployed to support the stronger claim that harm requires indexical presence — that an event can be bad for S only if S exists at that world-time to stand in the harm-relation.

I want to press this framing with a case that rarely appears in the standard literature. Suppose we accept the indexical presence condition and apply it not only to death but to a teletransportation scenario in which a perfect psychological duplicate replaces me at time t. After t, the original me is absent, yet I seem to have a legitimate grievance if the duplicate suffers terribly. If indexical presence is necessary for harm, then my suffering-through-the-duplicate is impossible, just as harm-through-death is impossible. Meanwhile, opponents of the Epicurean conclusion argue that the sleep analogy quietly imports an awakening — the sleeper returns, whereas the dead do not — and that the pre-birth symmetry ignores deprivation: death robs me of goods I would have experienced, whereas pre-birth non-existence robs me of nothing I could have had.

Can you audit both analogies with explicit correspondence tables? For the sleep analogy, map sleeper, awakening, unconscious interval, and post-awakening judgment; for the symmetry analogy, map pre-natal absence, post-mortem absence, the 'invitation' to exist, and the deprivation of future goods. Then show where each mapping breaks — especially where the indexical presence condition forces an implausible verdict on the teletransportation replacement case — and identify which disanalogies actually undermine the Epicurean conclusion versus which are merely decorative. Finally, does the conclusion that death is not bad for the deceased survive once the analogies are stripped of their hidden imports, or was the argument carrying the analogies rather than the other way around?
