I spent a considerable part of yesterday evening in discussion with a friend about the omnipotence paradox, and he concluded our exchange by offering a summary that struck me as more systematic than I could immediately assess, yet perhaps too tidy in its conclusions. I should like an audit of its accuracy, since I am unsure where his reasoning is sound and where it slips.

He presented the matter thus. The paradox, he said, is not a mere puzzle but a formal contradiction: omnipotence is defined as the ability to perform any task whatsoever; consequently, an omnipotent being must be able to create a task it cannot perform — for instance, a stone too heavy for itself to lift — which entails that there is something it cannot do, namely lift that stone, contradicting the original definition. The standard philosophical defence, he claimed, is to restrict omnipotence to logically possible actions, but he characterised this as a straightforward retreat that abandons the ordinary meaning of 'all-powerful', reducing God to a being who is merely maximally powerful within logical bounds rather than genuinely unlimited.

He then argued that the Cartesian view — according to which an omnipotent being can perform even logical impossibilities, such as instantiating a square circle — is actually more faithful to the concept, despite rendering rational discourse about God impossible. Turning to the so-called self-limitation defence, in which God might render Himself unable to lift the stone as an exercise rather than a loss of power, he maintained that this trivialises omnipotence entirely: since any agent possessed of free will can choose to restrict its own capacities, omnipotence would then apply to ordinary persons, and the concept becomes vacuous.

Finally, he offered what he called a modal reformulation: omnipotence ought to mean that, for every possible world, the being can actualise any state of affairs within that world; yet the paradox generates precisely a world containing a state of affairs — the being's failure to lift the stone — that it cannot actualise. Therefore, he concluded, the modal account collapses unless one restricts the quantifier to logically consistent worlds, which is merely the logical-possibility restriction in different dress, leaving the concept of omnipotence either contradictory or uninterestingly limited.

I am inclined to suspect that he conflates the scope of omnipotence with the nature of power exercised across time, and that he presents one particular theological position as the standard reply when it may in fact be a minority view. Nevertheless, I cannot confidently locate every distortion. Would you examine his summary with care? Please distinguish what a philosopher working in philosophy of religion would accept without reservation, what amounts to a common but misleading simplification, and what is genuinely misrepresented — particularly regarding whether the logical-possibility restriction is fairly described as a retreat, whether the Cartesian position is correctly identified as the principal alternative, and whether the self-limitation argument genuinely collapses into a claim about free will. I should also like to know whether his modal formulation is technically sound, and whether his final conclusion — that omnipotence is either contradictory or trivialised — actually follows once these corrections are made. I do not require references, citations, or a reading list; I seek only a precise accuracy audit that I can take back to our discussion.
