I'm on our hospital's clinical innovation board and we've recieved a proposal that's tearing us in half. The infectious-disease group wants to pilot a genetic-selection algorithm—basically running evolutionary pressure on resistance datasets—to generate antibiotic rotation protocols for one ward over six months. In silico it cuts resistance by about thirty percent, but the sequence it picks is opaque; clinicians can apply it but can't trace the reasoning behind any specific schedule. Our chief of medicine says that's a dealbreaker: clinical ethics requires that we be able to articulate and defend every intervention, especially if a patient dies and the family asks why we chose drug A on Tuesday. But our board ethicist—who actually has a philosophy background—argues that Darwin already showed nature achieves functional design without comprehending the engineering, and Turing showed that computation can proceed without mental understanding. So maybe demanding human-comprehensible reasoning for every protocol is just an anthropocentric hang-up that ignores these strange inversions where competence outruns comprehension.

We need a formal memo for Thursday's vote that picks a concrete course—approve with strict conditions, reject outright, or approve with an exit ramp—and includes an actual timeline and safeguards if we move forward. It needs to treat the chief's accountability worry as a serious objection, not just technophobia, but also examine whether the Darwin/Turing analogy actually holds in a setting where informed consent and legal liability depend on transparent justification. Don't just conclude "further study is needed"; give a bottom line we can vote on. And please don't include any bibliography or citation list—this is for board members who won't chase footnotes, and I need the reasoning to stand on its own.
