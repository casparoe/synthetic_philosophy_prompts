I've spent several years observing two neighboring communities that began with comparable resources and faced nearly identical barriers from outside institutions. One has gradually built a dense, self-financing network of small enterprises; the other remains largely reliant on seasonal outside employment and collective aid. In the first community, people share a firm conviction that work is a form of moral testimony rather than simple exchange, that carrying personal debt is a kind of communal failure, and that any surplus ought to be reinvested rather than consumed. At first I treated these beliefs as the obvious explanation for their economic divergence. Yet I have come to understand that this belief system was not inherited intact; it was articulated, taught, and formalized only after mainstream lenders and employers had systematically excluded the community, and the practical habits of mutual lending and pooled capital existed in an informal, adaptive form long before anyone described them as spiritual obligations. Meanwhile, the second community endured the same exclusion, but organized its response around rotating labor pools and shared resource distribution rather than individual accumulation, and never developed the same entrepreneurial density. Now I am struggling to understand what role, if any, the first community's values actually played. When members teach these beliefs to their children as timeless principles, and when they cite their outlook as the reason they built businesses, are they pointing to a real cause, or are they giving a dignified narrative to behaviors that structural exclusion essentially forced upon them? Is there a coherent way to distinguish a shared value system that genuinely drives economic action from one that merely rationalizes action already compelled by circumstance? And if we conclude that the values are largely decorative, should that diminish our respect for the outcome, or should it only change the kind of explanation we offer?
