After a long argument with a friend last night about whether old evidence can ever genuinely confirm a theory within a Bayesian framework, I keep returning to a specific pattern that our discussion never resolved. I am trying to determine whether this configuration already has an established name in epistemology or philosophy of science, or whether it is a genuinely distinct variant that needs its own label and precise boundary conditions.

Here is the pattern, described concretely. A well-established theory, call it T1, has long accommodated a dataset D, but only by treating a particular feature F of D as noise or as an artifact of measurement error. Then a rival theory, T2, arrives. T2 does not predict any novel measurements that T1 fails to predict; rather, T2 reclassifies F as the central signal and builds its explanatory apparatus around it. Once the data are redescribed in T2's conceptual vocabulary, practitioners claim that D confirms T2 because T2 "accounts for" F, whereas T1 was forced to dismiss it. But in the original measurement vocabulary shared by both theories, the probabilities of observing D are not clearly different. The confirmation does not come from a failed prediction by T1 that T2 gets right; it comes from a shift in what counts as data versus noise.

I have noticed this happen in two contexts that seem structurally identical, despite differing domains. In one case, a psychological model treats individual variation in reaction-time outliers as measurement error, while a newer model treats those same outliers as meaningful markers of cognitive state transitions. In the other, a cosmological framework treats certain anomalous background fluctuations as instrumental artifacts, while a competing framework treats them as signatures of primordial dynamics. In both cases, defenders of the newer theory cite the old dataset as confirmatory, yet they cannot point to a specific observational discrepancy that the older theory failed to predict; the dispute is over taxonomy, not over predictive success.

This seems adjacent to the classic problem of old evidence, but it is not the same: that problem asks how evidence already known when the theory was formulated can raise its posterior probability. Here the issue is not temporal priority but descriptive framing—the evidence was always "known," but its evidential status changes only after a conceptual reclassification. It also differs from ordinary underdetermination, because the theories do not necessarily share all empirical consequences in every vocabulary; they differ precisely in which descriptions they authorize.

Does this pattern have an established name? If so, how exactly does it relate to the problem of old evidence, to the predictive versus explanatory distinction in confirmation theory, and to concerns about the theory-ladenness of observation? If there is no established term, can it be named and defined in a way that clearly distinguishes it from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis adjustments, from immunization strategies, and from standard cases of old-evidence confirmation? I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
