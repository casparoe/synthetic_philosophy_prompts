Construct a thought experiment, set in a realistic university research-lab or clinical-intake context and no longer than one paragraph, in which a fully competent adult consents to a clearly described procedure. All physical and psychological risks, the exact withdrawal mechanism, and the compensation terms must be disclosed accurately and completely; withdrawal must remain genuinely available at any time with full payment retained. The deception must concern only the study’s true purpose—participants are told it measures stress tolerance under competition, but it actually measures blame attribution—while every procedural detail is truthful. The participant must also face structural economic pressure: specifically, they are long-term unemployed and the stipend equals roughly one week of basic grocery and transit costs, yet there is no direct personal threat, no physical coercion, and no hidden penalty for leaving. Design the case so that an informational account of consent (valid when risks and procedures are accurately disclosed) judges consent valid, whereas an autonomy-based account (invalid when deception about purpose or severely option-constraining conditions undermine self-governance) judges it invalid.

Present your answer in American English, using exactly these sections in this order: Scenario (about 150 words); The Three Verdicts (about 250 words)—state and briefly justify the verdict of the informational account, the autonomy account, and a transactional account that treats consent as a fair, mutually understood agreement; The Separating Feature (about 100 words)—identify the single element of the case that drives the disagreement between the informational and autonomy views; Removing the Pressure (about 100 words)—say whether the autonomy verdict changes if the economic pressure is removed but the purpose-deception remains; The Informational Counterattack (about 100 words)—show how a defender of the informational account would attack the autonomy verdict in the original case. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; paraphrase all philosophical positions without quotation marks.
