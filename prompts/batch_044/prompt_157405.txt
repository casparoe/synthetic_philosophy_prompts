I serve as a clerk to the National Commission on Democratic Legitimacy, which issues advisory rulings on whether particular collective decisions genuinely express what it calls the popular will. Our precedents are as follows. First, we endorsed a constitutional referendum that passed with sixty-two percent approval on seventy-eight percent turnout after six months of public debate, ruling that sustained deliberation combined with broad direct participation produces an authoritative expression. Second, we rejected a bill rushed through a parliamentary committee after closed-door meetings with industry lobbyists, despite polls showing sixty percent public opposition; we held that neither representative authority nor passive numerical preference can compensate for a process lacking transparency and inclusive reflection. Third, we approved with conditions a municipal zoning change enacted by a town council that had held eighteen months of open forums and revised its proposal based on feedback, even though the change contradicted a referendum from three years earlier that had drawn only thirty percent turnout; we said that an updated, deeply deliberative process reveals a more authentic will than an outdated, low-participation vote. Fourth, we rejected a petition that gathered signatures from seventy percent of registered voters within one week through viral social media campaigns demanding the immediate dissolution of a regulatory agency; we ruled that velocity and emotional contagion undermine the reflective judgment required for popular sovereignty, so magnitude alone is insufficient.

We now face a new request. A stratified micro-public of two hundred citizens, selected by sortition, has completed an eight-week deliberative protocol with adversarial expert testimony on climate policy. The jury reached eighty percent consensus on a regulatory framework that current polling shows fifty-five percent of the general population opposes. There was no broader referendum or petition; the only direct participants were the two hundred jurors, though the selection was statistically representative and the proceedings were fully transparent.

We want to be consistent with our own track record. What should we decide? Identify which of our four precedents is the closest analog, and explain whether our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. If they do, articulate the two most plausible rules that fit all four rulings, state what each predicts about this jury, and say which recommendation best coheres with our body of decisions and why. Finally, specify the one further case—described in concrete terms—that would most clearly tell those two rules apart, and state what principle we would be adopting regardless of which way we go here.
