I'm preparing a proposal for a one-semester research project in practical rationality, and I want to move past the standard framing of the sunk-cost problem. The usual debate asks whether honoring irrecoverable investments is always irrational, with economists insisting that only future costs and benefits matter and philosophers occasionally defending sunk-cost honoring as an expression of loyalty, narrative coherence, or commitment. I think this framing collapses two structurally different phenomena: retrospective commitment, where an agent treats past expenditure as a reason because it preserves diachronic continuity of her practical identity, and prospective commitment, where an agent uses an already-incurred cost as a self-binding mechanism to constrain future choices for strategic stability. My half-formed idea is that these two structures have different rationality conditions, and that the standard examples—staying in a failing business, finishing a bad meal, continuing a war—mix them together in ways that obscure whether honoring the cost is irrational at all.

Please turn this into a focused research proposal for an eight-week project suitable for a philosophy-of-action seminar paper of about 6,000 words. State the precise question: under what conditions, if any, is honoring a sunk cost rational, and does the answer depend on whether the case involves retrospective or prospective commitment, particularly when the agent at the decision point is not strictly identical with the agent who bore the original cost? Explain why the existing literature—both the economic orthodoxy and the philosophical defenses—leaves this specific distinction unsettled. Lay out the method: which kinds of cases you would construct or analyze, what conceptual distinctions you would sharpen, and how you would test whether the retrospective/prospective split actually does the work rather than being a redescription. Specify what a successful result would look like, what kind of finding would count as the project failing, and be honest about the risk that the distinction collapses back into familiar talk about identity or precommitment.
