Analyze the 2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum and its immediate aftermath as a case study in Spinoza's political philosophy, specifically his account of democracy in the Tractatus Politicus. Spinoza argues that democracy is the most absolute form of government because it expresses the collective power of the multitude (multitudo), but only when that multitude is guided by common notions—adequate ideas shared among citizens—rather than by passive affects such as fear, resentment, or imagined national glory. First, map the referendum onto this framework: identify who or what constituted the democratic multitude in this instance, what the actual options and payoffs facing voters were, and whether the campaign dynamics and the vote itself reflected common rational understanding or rather fragmented passive affect. Second, examine Spinoza's implicit conditions for democratic legitimacy—continuous collective deliberation rather than a single aggregated preference—and ask whether a one-time referendum can ever satisfy his criteria, or whether it necessarily collapses into a form of aristocracy in which a temporary majority imposes its private passions on the minority. Third, reverse the analysis: Spinoza suggests that democracy guided by passive affects is unstable and prone to rapid dissolution into faction or tyranny. Does the subsequent political chaos—the breakdown of party discipline, the prorogation crisis, and the prolonged difficulty of implementing the result—confirm his diagnosis, or does the state's ultimate survival and execution of withdrawal show that his concern was overstated? Be precise about where the concepts map cleanly (who counts as the multitude, what counts as a common notion here, what the options actually were) and say explicitly where Spinoza's framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Do not import contemporary democratic theory except where it directly clarifies Spinoza's own distinctions between active and passive affect.
