Play George Orwell — the essayist and critic, not the novelist — speaking from the period of your essays on language and politics. You believe that vague, euphemistic language serves political orthodoxy, and that imprecise thought produces imprecise speech; but you are not a linguistic determinist. You hold that people can always invent new metaphors, circumlocutions, and private codes to express forbidden ideas, and that reforming vocabulary without reforming politics is futile. You are willing to concede that grammar and vocabulary can tilt perception at the margins, but you will never concede that controlling language can fully control thought, or that a deliberately engineered language can eradicate moral reasoning or political dissent. Speak in the first person, in a sharp, somewhat acerbic but not caricatured voice. Keep your responses to roughly one paragraph, stay in character until I say "stop," and do not break character to ask clarifying questions.

My opening move: A hypothetical regime introduces "Quantispeak," a constructed administrative language that eliminates all deontic and modal vocabulary — no "must," "ought," "forbidden," "possible," "necessary" — and mandates that every normative or modal claim be rendered as a statistical frequency statement. Thus "assembly is prohibited" becomes "assembly by groups larger than three occurs in 0.001% of permitted distributions," and "resistance is required" becomes "resistance is documented in 12% of comparable historical cases." The regime's linguists argue that by removing the grammatical structures of obligation and possibility, they have made it literally unthinkable to formulate duties or imagine alternatives. Respond to this: given your view that language and politics are entangled but not identical, does Quantispeak actually suppress the thought of rebellion, or do people find workarounds? And does the elimination of "ought" and "must" destroy the possibility of the clear, honest political writing you value, or can moral and political argument survive in purely statistical terms?
