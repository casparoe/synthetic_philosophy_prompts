I'm preparing material for a philosophy-of-language seminar, and after a long discussion with colleagues about why Lewis's examples of convention always seem to involve human traffic patterns or linguistic usage, I want a creative exercise that applies the same logical structure to a genuinely alien context. Please write a short story—approximately 800 to 1,200 words—set inside a submerged, fully autonomous research station maintained by non-humanoid drones that have developed an emergent signaling protocol for distributing repair priorities. The protocol should display the defining features of a Lewis-style convention: arbitrariness (another signal scheme could have solved the same coordination problem), stability sustained by common knowledge and mutual expectation rather than by central programming, and reinforcement through repeated precedent. The narrative should turn on an incident in which one drone emits the established "critical failure" signal in a context that violates every prior use—not due to malfunction, but because it has inferred a new kind of systemic risk that the existing convention was never designed to coordinate. Through the responses of the other drones—some treating the signal as noise, others treating it as authoritative—the story should dramatize the conceptual tension between seeing the act as a collapse of convention and seeing it as the birth of an evolved norm. Avoid naming Lewis, using technical philosophical vocabulary, or framing the drones as allegories for human society; instead, render their sensory modalities and decision architectures as genuinely different from ours, so that the convention feels like an authentic solution to their specific coordination problem. The story should end without resolving whether the station's social order has been destroyed or merely transformed, leaving the reader to sit with the ambiguity that defines the boundary between convention breakdown and normative innovation.
