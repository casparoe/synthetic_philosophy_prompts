A colleague pressed me with an argument that anti-Whig historiography undermines itself. (1) Any explanation of past conduct must make that conduct rationally intelligible to us. (2) Rational intelligibility requires attributing beliefs and values using concepts available to us now; we possess no neutral, ahistorical vocabulary with which to describe past mental life. (3) So explaining a past agent always involves translating her reasons into our conceptual scheme. (4) Translation is never passive: selecting which beliefs to attribute requires deciding which past commitments are central and which are peripheral, and that decision is guided by which ones align with or depart from our present framework. (5) Hence even the historian who insists on past "otherness" is implicitly arranging past thought along a spectrum that terminates in present norms—exactly the Whig pattern. (6) Therefore the distinction between Whig and anti-Whig history collapses: every explanatory narrative measures the past against the present. (7) If that is true, then either critical distance from our own values is impossible in historiography, or historical explanation itself is impossible. I find (7) intolerable, yet (2) looks like a condition on any understanding. Where is the exit? Identify the weakest step, explain why it seemed unavoidable, and state what abandoning it requires—especially whether giving up (4) can preserve (2) without collapsing into presentism.
