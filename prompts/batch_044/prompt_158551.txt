I have recently begun advising a regional development authority that is attempting to design institutional reform in a territory divided by a long-standing political boundary, and the Korean divergence has become unavoidable as a mental reference point; I want to work through it conceptually rather than rhetorically. Take the economic divergence of North and South Korea after 1945—specifically the trajectories from the 1953 armistice through the South’s manufacturing takeoff in the 1960s and 1970s—and analyze it through the institutions-versus-geography-versus-culture debate on national wealth, but with a methodological twist I am calling the “uniform baseline problem.” By this I mean the paradox that Korea’s division supplies what looks like an ideal natural experiment—shared geography, shared language and culture, shared pre-division institutional history—yet precisely because the baseline is uniform across the two cases, the experiment eliminates the cross-sectional variation that the tripartite framework usually relies on to isolate institutional effects from geographic endowments or cultural legacies. The framework typically claims that institutions dominate once geography and culture are held constant; Korea holds them constant by construction, but in doing so it also removes the comparative leverage needed to test whether the remaining divergence is truly institutional rather than a product of unobserved confounders such as differential war destruction, the specific sequencing of land reform versus collectivization, the presence or absence of U.S. security guarantees and aid flows, and the idiosyncratic consolidation strategies of Park Chung-hee versus Kim Il-sung.

Map the episode with discipline: identify the relevant actors and decision points in the 1950s and 1960s, specify what counts as the institutional “treatment” in each regime, list the observable economic outcomes, and show where the counterfactual—what North Korea would have become with Southern institutions, or vice versa—is genuinely unobservable rather than merely inconvenient. Which features of the divergence does the framework explain cleanly, which does it force into an ill-fitting category (for instance, attributing export-oriented industrial policy purely to “inclusive institutions” without accounting for the micro-politics of elite consolidation), and which does it fail to see altogether because they fall outside its three bins?

Then run the analysis in reverse. Does Korea confirm the institutionalist claim, or does it expose a boundary condition—namely, that the argument depends on cross-national variation which Korea deliberately suppresses? Is the uniformity of geography and culture a strength for the framework or a weakness that reveals its dependence on comparative leverage it no longer possesses? Address whether this is an episode the framework anticipated, or an anomaly that shows the theory is silent on cases of perfect baseline uniformity coupled with extreme treatment divergence driven by exogenous security structures and individual leadership. Be honest about where the theory should admit that the divergence is overdetermined by factors it has no language for. Do not provide a bibliography, cite specific scholars, or offer policy recommendations for my authority; work only from the common historical record and the logical structure of the identification problem itself. I want a conceptual audit: what the Korean case teaches us about whether we can ever know, in a methodologically honest way, why some nations become rich.
