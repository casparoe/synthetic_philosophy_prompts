I am working on an independent philosophy project about environmental ethics, and I have been trying to use reflective equilibrium to determine whether my judgments about insecticides and pest control are guided by a consistent principle or are merely ad hoc. Here are my verdicts on several concrete situations.

First, applying a bacterial toxin that specifically targets the digestive system of one invasive crop pest and is documented not to affect pollinators strikes me as permissible, even if it causes suffering to the targeted insects. Second, using a broad-spectrum neonicotinoid spray that protects the same crop but kills bees and other non-target invertebrates strikes me as impermissible, regardless of economic benefit. Third, manually crushing an invasive stink bug in my garden—direct, painful, and intentional—feels acceptable to me, whereas installing an indiscriminate UV light trap in a barn that kills flying insects instantly and painlessly feels wrong, apparently because of the lack of discrimination rather than the presence of suffering. Fourth, poisoning termites inside the wooden foundation of my own house is acceptable, yet poisoning termites in a fallen dead tree in a protected woodland is not, even though in both cases they are damaging wood. Fifth, city-wide aerial fogging with pyrethrins to prevent a hypothetical mosquito-borne outbreak—when no active epidemic is documented—seems wrong to me, but the same intervention during a confirmed epidemic with rising hospitalizations seems permissible. I remain genuinely uncertain about a sixth case: deploying an aerial pheromone disruptor that confuses mating in only one invasive moth species, but requires repeated aircraft dispersal over a watershed, with unknown long-term effects on aquatic invertebrates.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving targeted functional harm, ecological substitutability, or the proportionality of collateral damage—that delivers all six verdicts, or at least the first five? If so, what does it predict for the pheromone case? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn to preserve simplicity, and why? I am particularly torn between the hand-crushing case and the UV-trap case, since both involve killing insects but yield opposite verdicts, and I want to know whether that tension reveals a hidden commitment I should make explicit.
