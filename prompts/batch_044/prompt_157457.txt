Question 3 (20 marks): In Quine’s essay Two Dogmas of Empiricism, two central empiricist commitments are challenged: the analytic–synthetic distinction, and the doctrine that every meaningful statement can be individually translated into terms of immediate experience. One defence of analyticity distinguishes framework-analytic statements—those true solely in virtue of explicit stipulation within a formal calculus, such as logical axioms—from content-analytic statements, which depend on the meanings of non-logical vocabulary. The defender claims that Quine’s arguments undermine only content-analyticity, leaving framework-analyticity intact as a matter of conventional decision; they further argue that this preservation allows reductionism to be rehabilitated, since logical truths require no empirical verification, while the remaining synthetic statements retain discrete, isolable experiential equivalents. Assess this defence.

Student’s answer:

Quine’s essay rejects the analytic–synthetic distinction and reductionism. The first dogma assumes some truths are analytic—true by meaning alone—while others are synthetic, confirmed by experience. Quine argues that attempts to define this boundary via synonymy or semantic rules fail because they presuppose the very distinction they seek to explain. The second dogma holds that each meaningful statement is individually equivalent to some set of observational reports. Quine replaces this with confirmation holism: beliefs form a web, and experience tests the system as a whole, not single statements.

The defence distinguishes framework-analytic truths—fixed by explicit rules of a formal system, such as logical axioms—from content-analytic truths, which depend on ordinary terms like bachelor. The defender claims Quine’s critique targets only content-analyticity, because framework-analyticity survives intact: logical axioms are fixed by convention, not by empirical fact. They further argue that once logical truths are set aside, the remaining synthetic statements each possess isolable empirical content that can be mapped one-to-one onto direct observations.

This defence fails. First, Quine’s rejection of the analytic–synthetic distinction is grounded in his confirmation holism: since all statements are revisable in principle, there is no principled reason to treat logical axioms as conventionally fixed rather than empirically supported. Therefore framework-analyticity cannot survive, because holism already shows that even central beliefs are answerable to experience. Second, the centrality of logical truths within the web actually means they carry greater empirical weight than peripheral synthetic claims; their resistance to revision reflects extensive direct connections to observation, not an absence of empirical content. Consequently, preserving framework-analyticity would not rescue reductionism, but would instead assign logical truths a disproportionately large share of the system’s empirical load. The defender thus conflates the argument against the first dogma with the argument against the second, and misinterprets centrality as evidence of empirical richness rather than structural importance.
