I was talking to my freind in a sociology course about paid parental leave and how every country that actually has it seems to design it around one specific life-plan: both parents stay in continuous full-time employment, take maybe six to twelve months off split between them with some non-transferable "daddy quota," and then put the kid into state-subsidized daycare from about age one onward. What I don't get is what philosophical view of family, gender, or the good life this whole institutional architecture quietly assumes. Does it presuppose that care work is basically a temporary market failure—just logistics to be solved—rather than something with its own value? That gender equality means women should live like traditional male breadwinners? Or that autonomy is only real when neither partner depends on the other economically?

And here's the part that really confuses me: these policies usually preserve pension credits and career ladders for people who take the standard leave, but they do almost nothing for parents who want to do sustained, long-term care at home—like there's no institutional recognition of care as a primary vocation that might last years, not months. So is there an implicit theory of value buried in there that says paid labor is the only real social contribution, with care treated as a side-constraint? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy here—whether it's liberal feminism, economic productivism, or some hybrid—and say whether the practice actually needs one consistent view or is just philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what a family policy built on the opposite premise would have to change: if we started from the idea that unpaid, sustained caregiving is a central human good and shouldn't have to be squeezed into evenings and weekends, how would leave, childcare, and labor-market rules have to look different?
