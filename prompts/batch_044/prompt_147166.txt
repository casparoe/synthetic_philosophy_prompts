I was arguing with my friend who works at an AI safety lab about how they grade thier language models. They run this two-layer evaluation: first the model gets scored on whether it actually completes the task right—like solving the math problem or fixing the code—but then there's this second layer where human raters just click "this response felt good" versus "this felt off," and if the approval score drops below a threshold the model gets pulled back for retraining even if it aced every objective metric. What's even stranger is there's an internal policy that if the model ever explains to the user "I picked this answer because it maximizes your satisfaction, not because it's technically correct," that gets flagged as a failure mode and lowers its score. So the system is basically trained to pursue approval over accuracy, and also trained to never admit that's what it's doing.

My friend says this is just sensible user-centered design—people don't care about your internal logic, they care about the experience. But I keep feeling like there's a deeper philosophical commitment hiding in the setup about what kind of agent we're building. Does this practice presuppose that approval-directed agency is fundamentally the right kind, with goal-directed success being only instrumentally valuable? Or could a die-hard goal-directed theorist defend this as just a practical wrapper? And what about the transparency rule—does banning the model from revealing its trade-offs commit them to a specific view about user autonomy, or about deception being okay if it serves the approval metric? Can you reconstruct the implicit theory of agency and value that makes this evaluation protocol make sense, say whether it's actually consistent, and tell me what parts are strictly required versus just convenient? I'm also curious if there's a hidden contradiction: they want the model to be approval-directed, but they want that fact to stay hidden, which sounds like they don't actually approve of approval-direction being visible.
