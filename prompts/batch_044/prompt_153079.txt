You are an expert philosopher of epistemology and an experienced academic interviewer preparing questions for a specialist colloquium podcast. Next week’s guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a precise, somewhat heterodox hybrid view: she accepts the force of the standard anti-luminosity argument for gradual belief change—such as the familiar case of feeling progressively colder—yet she rejects its global conclusion, instead defending a restricted luminosity thesis that applies only to knowledge states crossing a discrete, context-sensitive epistemic confidence threshold. She pairs this with a graded KK principle, holding that knowing that one knows requires not merely first-order knowledge but also a second-order state that itself meets that same threshold. Before drafting the interview, take a moment to think carefully about the internal architecture of her position. Briefly brainstorm the three most serious pressure points: how the threshold can be specified without circular appeal to the very knowledge it is meant to illuminate; what her view implies about the phenomenology and accessibility of knowing near the boundary; and whether the graded KK principle genuinely solves the problem of higher-order ignorance or merely relocates it to the second order. Then draft eight to ten sharp interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. Include at least one question pressing on the non-circular specification of her confidence threshold and how she distinguishes it from an arbitrary stipulation; at least one question probing her account of borderline cases just below the cutoff, especially in gradual-change scenarios analogous to the classic anti-luminosity examples; and at least one question challenging whether her modified KK principle avoids collapsing into either triviality for high-order states or an implausible regress. For each of these three, provide a targeted follow-up keyed precisely to the answer she is most likely to give—anticipate standard replies such as an appeal to salience, a denial that phenomenology tracks epistemic status, or a distinction between epistemic access and achievement, and press them. The remaining questions should turn on related but distinct issues: for instance, whether her restriction saves luminosity for mathematical or perceptual knowledge while abandoning it for empirical belief, what she says about self-knowledge in cases of expert intuition, and whether her view can accommodate collective or distributed knowledge. Avoid generic biographical questions, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, specific citations, or references to obscure named theories and texts that an interlocutor could not address from first principles. Write the questions in a formal, colloquium-ready tone that assumes familiarity with the luminosity debate and the standard objections to the KK principle, but that builds entirely on the conceptual framework described above.
