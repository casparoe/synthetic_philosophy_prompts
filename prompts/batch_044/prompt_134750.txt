Many health systems maintain two parallel institutional practices: palliative-care ethics treat severe, intractable suffering as setting a lexical boundary—no quantity of anticipated pleasure or extended life can justify leaving a patient in that state—while the same system's technology-assessment boards deny expensive treatments by computing quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), thereby treating pleasure and pain as fully commensurable on a single cardinal scale. What theory of value regarding the commensurability of pleasure and pain does this pairing presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does the practice commit the institution to lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, or is it philosophically underdetermined, compatible with pluralist or context-sensitive accounts that assign different metrics to bedside and budgetary decisions? Distinguish what the pairing strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether the two policies are consistent with one another or quietly rely on incompatible measures of welfare.
