i've been obsessing over this argument my adviser threw at me in a seminar on collective action ethics, and i can't find the exit. it seems to show that deontology, taken seriously, collapses into moral paralysis in large-scale coordination problems, but every step looks airtight.

(1) A deontological side-constraint against harming is absolute in the sense that it doesn't weaken just because more good is at stake elsewhere; that's the whole point of rejecting consequentialism.

(2) But "absolute" here must mean agent-relative: my constraint binds me regardless of what other agents do, otherwise it's just global optimization with extra steps.

(3) So in any multi-agent scenario, each agent evaluates only their own potential violations, not the aggregate result of everyone following the rule.

(4) Now imagine a coordination failure with N agents, where each agent can either (a) violate the constraint once (killing one innocent) and thereby stop N-1 other agents from carrying out N-1 violations, or (b) respect the constraint and let the N-1 violations happen.

(5) By (2), each agent sees option (a) as one violation they commit, and option (b) as zero violations they commit, regardless of the total body count.

(6) So every agent chooses (b). The result is N violations and catastrophic harm, and every agent acted with clean hand's.

(7) Worse, if even one agent breaks ranks and picks (a), they commit one violation but prevent N-1 others, yet deontology still condemns them, because they violated the constraint.

(8) So deontology not only permits the worst aggregate outcome, it actively condemns the only actions that could prevent it.

(9) But that can't be right, because then deontology is structurally self-defeating in collective contexts: it demands that agents preserve their own moral purity at the cost of multiplying harm.

(10) Therefore either deontology applies only to isolated agents—which makes it useless for politics and climate ethics—or there's a hidden slide i'm missing.

My adviser says step (3) smuggles in an assumption about how agent-relativity works, but i don't see it. I also wonder if step (7) ignores something about complicity or enabling. Where exactly does this slip? And if the answer is that deontology just is silent on collective outcomes, isn't that a huge cost for a moral theory in 2025? I want to keep (1) and (2) if possible, but i'm open to paying elsewhere if i have to.
