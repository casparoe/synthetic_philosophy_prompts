I want the full specification for a normative theory that can adjudicate whether international criminal tribunals established after total military defeat—specifically the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg and the International Military Tribunal for the Far East at Tokyo—were legitimate exercises of justice rather than exercises of victors' justice, despite applying retroactive criminal law and despite trying only defeated powers.

Write the desiderata: the phenomena, cases, distinctions, and consistency conditions the theory must handle, with the reason each is a genuine requirement rather than a camp prejudice. At minimum, the theory must account for (i) the retroactive definition and prosecution of crimes against peace and crimes against humanity in positive international law prior to 1945; (ii) why the exclusion of Allied conduct—strategic bombing of civilians in Europe and, in the Pacific theater, the use of atomic weapons—from the tribunals' jurisdiction does not reduce the proceedings to mere revenge; (iii) the distinction between collective national guilt and individual criminal responsibility, especially given that the Tokyo tribunal treated cabinet-level decision-making differently from Nuremberg's organizational-liability framework; (iv) the rejection of tu quoque defenses raised by several Tokyo defendants regarding colonialism and Allied warfare, and whether that rejection was principled or ad hoc; and (v) consistency with nullum crimen sine lege in some defensible interpretation, or a precise explanation of why that principle is overridden and by what authority.

Then inject a stress-test case that is not standard in the literature: suppose a tribunal in 1950 is convened to try the leaders of a formally neutral state that never ratified the Kellogg-Briand Pact or the 1945 London Charter, but whose regime had systematically incorporated those instruments' language into domestic constitutional propaganda before launching an aggressive war. Must your theory permit prosecution? The answer should reveal whether the theory's foundation is treaty-positivist, customary-law, or moral-universalist.

Next, flag which of these desiderata are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are negotiable preferences of particular camps (strict legal positivists, natural-law theorists, or realist critics). Identify whether any subset of the non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—if so, that unsatisfiability is itself the central result. Finally, say which single requirement does the most damage to the most popular existing accounts, and briefly—without a full scorecard—indicate how a strict-positivist view, a natural-law view, and a hybrid institutionalist view fare against the list.
