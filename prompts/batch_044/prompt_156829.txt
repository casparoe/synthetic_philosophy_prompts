Write a 90-minute final-exam essay question for an upper-level philosophy of rationality seminar on coherence arguments for expected utility maximization. The prompt must be answerable without web access and must not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.

The question should not be satisfied by summarizing Ramsey’s or Savage’s standard Dutch-book derivations of the probability and utility axioms. Instead, inject the following conceptual scenario: an agent’s preferences over compound lotteries violate the independence axiom in a structurally regular, reproducible way—call it “frame-locked independence failure,” where the framing of outcomes (gain-frame versus loss-frame) systematically shifts the weighting of common consequences without altering the agent’s ordinal ranking of sure outcomes or her probabilistic beliefs about states of the world. Ask students whether a coherence argument can be reconstructed to defend expected-utility maximization in this setting, and if so, whether it retains normative force or collapses into a consistency requirement on revealed preference alone.

Provide:
1. The exam question itself, precisely worded, making clear that students must address both the logical structure of coherence proofs and the philosophical status of framing-sensitive preferences.
2. A model answer of approximately 800 words that distinguishes three levels of philosophical response: (a) identifying that standard Dutch-book arguments for independence presuppose state-independent utility and separability of beliefs and values; (b) showing how frame-locked failure undermines the separability assumption required to derive a unique utility representation from coherence constraints alone; and (c) evaluating whether relaxing separability saves coherence at the cost of reducing the argument from a normative defense of expected-utility maximization to a merely formal consistency condition on choice behavior.
3. A marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain (e.g., the A answer must explicitly diagnose where the coherence proof breaks rather than merely noting that framing “violates independence”; the B answer may correctly describe the Dutch-book structure but fail to locate the separability failure).
4. Two common student mistakes that should cost marks: (i) conflating coherence with static consistency of revealed preference, thereby missing that coherence arguments are dynamic and diachronic; and (ii) treating framing effects as stochastic noise rather than as structurally integrated features of the preference relation.

Specify that the course level is upper-level seminar, the time is 90 minutes, and the answer should be graded out of 25 marks, with 10 marks allocated to logical reconstruction, 10 to philosophical evaluation, and 5 to clarity and precision. Do not name obscure recent papers; you may refer to standard figures by surname only (Ramsey, Savage, Jeffrey) if needed for orientation, but do not depend on specific publication dates or titles. Do not include quotation marks around any real text.
