I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been working on a governance layer for a distributed protocol that has run into a conceptual problem I can't frame precisely. The system uses a self-amending rulebook encoded in smart contracts: any rule, including the rule that specifies how amendments are made, can be changed by a qualified vote. To prevent arbitrary disruption, we added a constraint that any amendment must preserve backward-compatibility with all prior states of the system—but backward-compatibility is itself defined by the current rulebook, which is subject to amendment. This means the constraint on change can be changed by the very changes it is meant to constrain. In legal philosophy, I understand there's a long-standing puzzle about rules that govern their own amendment, sometimes discussed in connection with constitutional self-reference and logic games in which players may alter the rules during play. What I'm trying to understand is what our design implicitly assumes about the source of authority. Does encoding a fully self-amending mechanism presuppose that normative authority can be entirely internal and self-grounding, or does it smuggle in an external anchor—such as the original deployer's intent, the network's prior state, or some invariant mathematical property—that we haven't acknowledged? Furthermore, does the backward-compatibility loop commit us to a particular view about institutional identity over time: that continuity requires preserving some essential feature, and if so, is that feature itself protected by the mutable rules or standing outside them? I'd like an analysis of whether our architecture is philosophically coherent, which rival theories of rules and authority it quietly rules out, and what would have to change in our design if we replaced its implicit theory with a straightforwardly hierarchical one in which the amendment rules are permanently fixed and immune to revision.
