In a seminar discussion on well-being, two participants—Jordan and Sam—debated whether the heterogeneity of pleasures undermines hedonism. Jordan began by conceding that pleasures vary enormously: the warm satisfaction of finishing a long hike, the sharp exhilaration of grasping a proof, and the quiet contentment of reading by a fire share no obvious qualitative feature. Still, Jordan insisted that all pleasures possess a single, irreducible phenomenal tone—a kind of intrinsic hedonic gloss—that is present regardless of an experience’s object or cognitive content. Jordan compared this to vision: seeing crimson and seeing emerald differ in content, yet both share the modality of vision; analogously, different pleasures share the modality of feeling-good, even if their specific flavors diverge.

Sam pushed back in several moves. First, Sam argued that positing an undetectable gloss is just relabeling the mystery: if the gloss is so thin that it cannot be introspectively isolated from the content it accompanies, it explains nothing and risks metaphysical extravagance. Second, Sam proposed that what actually unifies pleasures is not an intrinsic feel but a functional profile: they are mental states that the subject is motivated to prolong and that reinforce the behavior producing them. Jordan immediately replied that this collapses hedonism into a form of desire-satisfaction theory, since defining pleasure by its relation to motivation makes the intrinsic value lie in the satisfaction of those structures rather than in the feeling itself. Third, Sam offered a compromise: perhaps pleasures form a natural kind not at the phenomenological level but at the neurobiological level—activation in specific reward-related neural circuits—so the heterogeneity is superficial, like crimson and emerald both being electromagnetic waves despite looking nothing alike. Jordan countered that tying pleasure to a specific neural substrate makes hedonism contingently empirical rather than conceptually necessary; if an artificial being experienced something phenomenologically identical but implemented via different circuitry, hedonism would either arbitrarily exclude it or be forced to concede that the substrate, not the experience, is what matters. Sam responded that this is precisely correct, and that a defensible hedonism should be empirical: it claims a particular kind of experience is good, and identifying that kind may require science rather than armchair introspection.

Please go through their exchange point by point—Jordan’s phenomenal-tone analogy, Sam’s functional-profile objection, Jordan’s collapse-into-desire-satisfaction reply, Sam’s neurobiological-natural-kind proposal, and Jordan’s empirical-contingency objection—rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall about whether the heterogeneity problem is fatal for hedonism, and identify what both parties are missing, such as whether the problem genuinely demands a single shared property at all, or how hedonists might distinguish qualitative heterogeneity from axiological uniformity without committing to either pure phenomenology or pure neuroscience.
