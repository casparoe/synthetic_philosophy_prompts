Two colleagues in my research group—Jordan and Riley—have been arguing over how to design the labeling protocol for a clinical image dataset that classifies scalp photographs as "bald" or "not bald" for a hair-loss diagnosis model. The dataset contains thousands of borderline cases where hair density falls in a middle range, and the group needs a consistent rule before training begins.

Jordan takes an epistemicist line: there is a precise biological threshold of hair follicle density that separates bald from not-bald; our current measurements are just too coarse to locate it. He argues that the right response is to invest in higher-resolution imaging and statistical calibration to approximate that hidden cutoff, and that labeling should aim at tracking this real boundary even if we never reach perfect precision.

Riley defends a supervaluationist view: "bald" is genuinely vague, and there is no single correct boundary to discover. Instead, there are many admissible precisifications—different reasonable cutoffs within the middle range—and the correct labeling protocol should accept all of them, flagging only the clear cases at either extreme and treating the middle range as genuinely indeterminate or conventionally assigned within a tolerated band.

The exchange went through six moves:

(1) Jordan said that if Riley is right, then every borderline patient gets an arbitrary label, which is unacceptable in clinical practice where a diagnosis determines treatment eligibility.

(2) Riley replied that the assignment is not arbitrary; it is conventionally fixed within a range of reasonable precisifications, just as "adult" is conventionally fixed at 18 despite there being no biological fact that makes 18.0001 years qualitatively different.

(3) Jordan countered that medical categories like "bald" are meant to track underlying biological conditions (androgenetic alopecia), not bureaucratic conventions, so treating the cutoff as purely conventional undermines the diagnostic purpose.

(4) Riley answered that even biological tracking involves conventional choices about where to draw the line—hypertension thresholds are revised as conventions change—so the presence of convention does not erase the underlying biology; it just means the predicate applies within a tolerated range rather than at a hidden point.

(5) Jordan then claimed that Riley's view makes error impossible: if there is no fact of the matter, then no one can be misdiagnosed as bald when they "really" are not; but clinical practice depends on being able to correct misclassifications.

(6) Riley said that is a confusion between metaphysical error and practical correction. You correct a label by showing it falls outside the adopted convention, not by appealing to a hidden metaphysical boundary. The system can still be improved without pretending there is one true line.

They asked me to referee. Can you go through their exchange move by move—rating each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating? Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing, particularly regarding how an AI labeling system should actually handle the middle-range cases in practice—whether through probabilistic outputs, human-in-the-loop review, or explicit precisification rules—without treating the philosophical dispute as settled by that practical choice alone.
