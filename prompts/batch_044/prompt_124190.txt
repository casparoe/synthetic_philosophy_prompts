There's that old computer project where people tried to get programs to invent alphabet styles from grid patterns, but I'm more interested in the conceptual gaps that are still open.

If you had to advise a tiny team — say one philosopher of mind, one cognitve scientist, and one type designer — with about three years of funding and no web access or massive corpora, which three open problems should they actually spend their time on?

Here are the candidates I'm weighing, but I'm not sure how to rank them:

1. The ghost stroke problem: can you systematically remove or warp individual strokes in a letterform and still keep its categorical identity, and does the answer shift depending on the overall style context? I'm wondering if this reveals whether letter categories are built from necessary features or from family resemblance.

2. Cross-modal recognition: why do we instantly read an 'a' in fog on glass or bent neon even when every geometric feature changes? Are we tracking some abstract structural invariant, or is it just deep pattern matching?

3. Synthetic bleed: when a generative model learns a font style and starts spitting out glyphs that sit right on the boundary between 'a' and 'e' in that specific style, does that show our categories have fuzzy edges, or just that style and category are more entangled than we thought?

4. The essence-style split: is there actually a clean separation between what makes it an a and how this particular a looks, or is the essence itself relative to style?

I'm less interested in a survey than in a defended ordering. For each of your top picks, say why it's upstream of the others, how tractible it is for this specific team without big data, and what result in the next few years would make you move it up or down the list.
