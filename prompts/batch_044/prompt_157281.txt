I've been thinking about introspection lately, partly after some threads on LessWrong and partly because I'm in a phil of mind course where we only covered the standard stuff. I'm trying to figure out what a decent theory of introspective reliability would even have to cover before I buy any particular one. Like, it's not enough to say "sometimes people are wrong about themselves" — I want the full spec, and I feel like most people skip over the hard parts.

So: what are the actual desiderata? I mean the cases it has to handle — not just the obvious ones like people misreporting why they picked a face, but also weirder ones, like the split-attention reporter running dual n-back who insists they're calm even though their galvanic response is all over the place, and they genuinely can't revise the report even when shown the data. The distinctions it can't collapse — like between being wrong about a mental state versus being wrong about its cause, or between access-transparency (you see the state) and revision-transparency (you'd recieve an update if shown contrary evidence). The results from cognitive science it can't flat-out contradict — maybe confabulatory narratives in split-brain cases, but I'm more interested in whether it has to accommodate a "high-load confabulation gradient" where error rates scale with working-memory load in a specific non-linear way. And tell me which single desideratum kills the most popular theories — maybe naive transparency views or pure behaviorist denials. Also, flag if any pair of these requirements are jointly unsatisfiable; I have a hunch that demanding both perfect first-person authority in low-load conditions and complete third-person correctability in high-load conditions might be a contradiction, but I don't know if that's just my confusion. A table is fine. Then briefly, how do the usual suspects — maybe a strong access theory, a weak self-modeling view, and a fully deflationary account — do against the list? Don't give me a bibliography or reading list, just the conceptual scoring.
