I was chatting with my writer's group last tuesday — we're all a bit obsessed with old philosophy of mind stuff — and someone brought up the frame problem, that classic puzzle about how you know what hasn't changed when one thing does. Most people illustrate it with robots but I'm drafting a novel about memory and archives so I want something more human, yet still conceptually sharp.

Could you write a short story, maybe two or three pages in feel, about an elderly archivist named Elias who curates a private library of "responsive manuscripts" — books that physically alter their text based on the emotional temperature of the room they're kept in. One winter morning Elias discovers an unfamiliar volume has materialized in the east galery, and he realizes he can't verify every other book's contents from scratch; he has to reason about which properties of the collection still hold and which have been silently invalidated. I want the mechanics to echo the frame problem precisely: the manuscripts inherit traits from their shelving category unless that category conflicts with a direct observation, in which case inheritence is blocked non-monotonically. Show Elias making specific inferences — for instance, assuming all east-galery texts are handwritten unless he sees printed type — and then show that assumption breaking without him noticing until it's too late. The story should feel melancholy, like he's losing not just books but parts of his own memory.

Start with the story itself, no preface or commentary — just drop us straight into Elias's head, and keep the narration close to his quiet, unraveling thoughts.
