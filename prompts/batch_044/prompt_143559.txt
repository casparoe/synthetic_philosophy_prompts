As a doctoral candidate in moral philosophy working on agricultural ethics and wild-animal suffering, I am preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on interspecies harm. I would like a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a concluding quatrain, with two refrains (A1 and A2) and two rhymes (A and b)—that structurally enacts a tension the seminar has circled but not resolved. The first refrain should carry the calculable logic of replacement rates: that shifting consumption from beef to chicken multiplies the number of direct deaths per calorie because of the disparity in body size and feed conversion, so that the arithmetic of suffering is not reduced but redistributed. The second refrain should carry the hidden, less calculable ledger of crop-cultivation mortality—field insects, soil invertebrates, and small vertebrates killed in planting and harvest—whose capacity for suffering is not settled science but genuinely open, making their moral weight uncertain rather than negligible. In the tercets, move from the familiar comparison of beef versus chicken, through the exposure of indirect harm in plant agriculture, and let the closing quatrain interweave both refrains without resolving whether the indirect, epistemically opaque harm outweighs, equals, or falls outside the direct harm. The poem should be philosophically precise: no false certainty about invertebrate sentience, no moralistic command to abstain, no romantic appeal to a circle of life that dissolves the problem. It should leave the tension intact, as our discussion has, and be readable as poetry to someone who knows the ethical literature without requiring citations or specialist jargon.
