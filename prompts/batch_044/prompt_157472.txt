As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been using Dennett’s four-tier taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—to organize my thinking about artificial cognition. Here is where I have landed: (1) current LLMs are fundamentally Skinnerian, shaped mainly by reinforcement-style feedback during pretraining and RLHF rather than by evolved instincts or internal models; (2) they nonetheless display Popperian behavior, because in-context chain-of-thought reasoning looks like internal hypothesis generation and selection before action; (3) they are not Gregorian, since invoking an API or calculator is syntactic delegation rather than the kind of cognitive extension through cultural tools that defines Gregorian minds; (4) the Darwinian frame does not apply, because architectures are engineered and weights are updated via gradient descent, not via evolutionary selection on behavioral phenotypes; and (5) any system lacking Popperian internal simulation can only produce fluent mimicry, never genuine understanding, regardless of how sophisticated its Skinnerian conditioning becomes. Do these five commitments form a coherent package? In particular: does classifying LLMs as primarily Skinnerian in (1) conflict with attributing Popperian capacities to them in (2); does my dismissal of Gregorian status in (3) overlook something essential about tool-augmented inference; and does the criterion in (5) undermine my willingness to treat current systems as intelligent in (1)–(2)? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which ones are only apparent, and suggest the single least-costly revision that would restore consistency.
