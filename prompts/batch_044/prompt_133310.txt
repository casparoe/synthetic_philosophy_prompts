After a long argument with a friend about whether Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture—roughly, that human extinction is especially bad because it severs our implicit dependency on future generations carrying forward our projects and relationships—can illuminate acute crises or only gradual, distant extinction, I want you to apply it to the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962. Analyze the crisis as a near-extinction event: identify the key agents (Kennedy, Khrushchev, ExComm, Soviet submarine commanders), the actual options and payoffs they faced, and what avoiding nuclear escalation preserved beyond immediate lives—specifically, the long-term scientific, cultural, and intergenerational structures that Scheffler claims we implicitly value. Then say honestly where the framework strains: does the thirteen-day time horizon undermine the idea of dependency on an open-ended future, or does it reveal that such dependency is active even in moments of immediate threat? Finally, inject a comparative twist: apply the same reasoning to a hypothetical localized interruption—say, the sudden termination of the Apollo program in a superpower conflict—and ask whether Scheffler's argument applies symmetrically to partial versus total extinction, or whether this exposes a hidden scope condition.
