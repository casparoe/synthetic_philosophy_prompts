I'm trying to distinguish manipulation from legitimate persuasion by working through my intuitive verdicts on specific influence scenarios, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture the pattern or whether some verdicts must be overturned.

Here are my judgments. A physician who presents both absolute and relative risk figures for a surgery and lets the patient weigh them is engaging in legitimate persuasion. The same physician, knowing the patient has math anxiety, who deliberately suppresses the absolute figures and reports only the relative reduction to make the treatment sound more impressive, is manipulating. A marketing team that A/B-tests button colors and deploys a high-impulsivity-triggering shade only to users with high impulsivity scores is manipulating. A debate coach who teaches clear premise-conclusion structure so listeners can track and object is legitimately persuading. A political strategist who pairs immigration statistics with subtly chaotic background imagery—carrying no informational content but priming disorder-related affect—to shift opinion is manipulating. A novelist whose immersive narrative makes readers feel deep sympathy for a character with an unpopular moral view, leading some readers to revise their own view, feels like legitimate persuasion, even though the mechanism is emotional rather than argumentative. By contrast, a friend who knows I defer to authority figures and frames a request by citing an expert I admire, while misrepresenting that expert's actual position, is manipulating.

The pair that most troubles me involves framing for communication versus framing for extraction. A financial advisor who knows her client is loss-averse and therefore describes an investment portfolio in terms of "protecting existing capital" rather than "potential growth"—both accurate descriptions of the identical portfolio—strikes me as legitimately adapting to her client's psychology. But a negotiator who knows the counterparty is loss-averse and frames a concession as "avoiding a worse deal" rather than "securing a gain"—structurally the same move applied to the same cognitive bias—strikes me as manipulative extraction. I cannot see what distinguishes them except the speaker's intent or institutional role, neither of which feels like the right kind of principle.

My tentative principle is that influence is manipulation when it reroutes decision-making through sub-personal cognitive mechanisms—loss aversion, affective priming, perceptual fluency, authority heuristics—in ways that the agent, upon reflective consideration, would not endorse as part of her own reasoning process, whereas legitimate persuasion operates within and strengthens the agent's reason-responsive capacities. On this view, the physician suppressing figures, the targeted button color, the chaotic imagery, and the misrepresented authority all qualify as manipulation because they bypass reflection. The debate coach, the novelist (perhaps surprisingly), and the financial advisor all qualify as legitimate because they either feed into reflection or adapt its expression without hijacking its machinery. The negotiator, however, is harder: he exploits the same sub-personal mechanism as the advisor, yet I want to call him manipulative.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a refinement of the one above—that delivers all of these verdicts, including the distinction between the advisor and the negotiator? If not, which verdict should I drop: the novelist's legitimacy, the advisor's legitimacy, or the negotiator's manipulativeness? Please also apply your resulting account to a case I have not considered: a therapist uses virtual-reality exposure to treat a flying phobia, calibrating scenario intensity in real time via biometric feedback (galvanic skin response, eye-tracking) that the patient cannot consciously perceive; the patient has consented to the therapy in general but not to this specific algorithmic calibration. Does this count as manipulation, legitimate persuasion, or something else, and what feature of the case drives that verdict?
