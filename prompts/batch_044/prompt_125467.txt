I was recently asked to direct a small-scale revival of a pastoral opera for a local community theater, and in preparing the production I have found myself returning to Rousseau's critique of theatrical spectacle—particularly his argument in the letter to d'Alembert that theatrical representation substitutes an artificial image for direct civic presence and thereby corrupts public morals. As I try to think through whether directing this work is compatible with the philosophical commitments I think I hold, I want a consistency check before I present my reasoning to the board.

Here is roughly where I have landed:

(1) I accept Rousseau's diagnosis: theatrical performance is structurally representational, and representation in public life tends to dissolve direct accountability by interposing an image between actor and audience.

(2) I also hold that the general will requires direct, unmediated participation by citizens in sovereign deliberation; representation of the people by deputies is therefore not merely practically necessary but philosophically illegitimate, except perhaps as a temporary expedient.

(3) At the same time, I think Rousseau permits the legislator to employ civic rituals—public festivals, communal games, collective celebrations—to shape the mores of a people, even though these events are performative and involve a staged, collective self-presentation.

(4) Finally, I want to defend Rousseau's own theatrical practice—his composition and staging of a pastoral opera intended for a rural, uncorrupted audience—by arguing that pedagogical performance before such a community does not fall under the critique, because its purpose is not to create an artificial public but to reinforce natural sentiment and social bonds.

My question is whether these four commitments form a coherent package, or whether I am helping myself to distinctions that collapse under pressure. Specifically: does the permission granted to the legislator in (3) undermine the strict anti-representationalism of (2), and if so, can I rescue (2) without abandoning (3)? Does the defense in (4) rely on a distinction between corrupting urban spectacle and pedagogical rural performance that is either ad hoc or incompatible with the general structural argument in (1)? Is there a tension between (1) and (3) regarding whether any collective performance can avoid being representational, and does that tension become more severe once I add (4), which asserts that at least one performance escapes the corruption?

Please go through these pairs, rank the tensions by severity, and for each indicate the least costly revision. I am not asking for a historical reconstruction of Rousseau's own consistency; I want to know whether my particular combination of views, including the injection of the legislator's civic rituals into an otherwise standard anti-theatrical reading, is jointly tenable. If the package is inconsistent, tell me which single commitment I should drop to restore coherence with the least damage to the overall position.
