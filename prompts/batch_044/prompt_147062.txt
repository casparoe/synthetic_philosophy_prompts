I have been going through my philosophy of language seminar instructor’s annotated problem sets on two-dimensional frameworks, and her verdicts are remarkably consistent yet resist easy classification. So far she has ruled that “Water is H2O” is necessary a posteriori, with a primary intension tracking watery stuff across worlds and a secondary intension anchored to H2O in the actual world; that rigid designators like “Aristotle” carry constant primary and secondary intensions that coincide in every world; that a descriptive name such as “the tallest spy in 2024” has a world-varying primary intension but a secondary intension fixed to whoever actually holds the role here; that “All bachelors are unmarried” shows no divergence at all between the two dimensions, being necessary and a priori in both; that Twin-Earth Oscar’s term “water” shares our primary intension but not our secondary one, regardless of whether one calls that a difference in meaning or only in reference; and that mathematical truths like “There are infinitely many primes” exhibit identical primary and secondary intensions that are both necessary and a priori.

Next week she will assess a constructed case we designed: introduce a term “flurg” via an explicit descriptive condition—“the substance that actually causes the green glow under laboratory conditions L”—where in our world L is satisfied by copper sulfate, but in nearby worlds L is satisfied by different chemicals or by nothing at all. We ask specifically about the sentence “Flurg causes green glows in conditions L”: (i) is it a priori? (ii) is it metaphysically necessary? and (iii) do its primary and secondary intensions come apart in precisely the water-like fashion, or does the descriptive baptism make the term behave more like “the tallest spy”? Predict her answers to all three, say whether she is implicitly running a Chalmers-style epistemic two-dimensionalism governed by centered-world rational accessibility, a Jackson-style metaphysical two-dimensionalism governed by conceptual containment across all worlds, or a hybrid that treats naturally kind-anchored terms differently from descriptively fixed ones, and tell me which further constructed case—perhaps an empty descriptive term with no actual-world satisfier, contrasted with a rigid designator whose reference was originally fixed by a contingent description—would best force a decision between the first two frameworks.
