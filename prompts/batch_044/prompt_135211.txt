I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and we are working through the taxonomy of alignment problems. The standard framing distinguishes intent alignment—the narrow technical challenge of making a system do what its designer meant, irrespective of whether that intention is morally sound—from value alignment, the broader challenge of ensuring that the system's objectives are genuinely correct or good. I want to test whether this dichotomy is exhaustive or whether there is a genuinely orthogonal intermediate category that gets collapsed into one pole or the other.

Specifically, I propose we consider what might be called procedural alignment: the condition that a system's internal reasoning trajectory—its chain of inference, its weighting of evidence, its reversibility and auditability—structurally matches the epistemic norms of its designers, even when both the intended outcome and the correct value ranking are already satisfied. In high-stakes domains such as medical triage or judicial sentencing, two systems could share both the designer's intent and the right normative ordering of outcomes, yet differ radically in whether their decision paths are transparent, corrigible, or subject to human verification. The question is whether this is a distinct alignment failure mode or merely a transparency condition folded into intent alignment.

With that framing in mind, please rank the following three formulations of the alignment problem by how precisely they isolate a non-redundant technical challenge, assigning each a tier—S, A, B, C, or D—where S indicates a genuinely independent problem and D indicates a largely redundant redescription. In one sentence per tier, say why it lands there.

1. Intent alignment: the system produces outcomes that match the designer's actual intentions, regardless of value correctness.
2. Value alignment: the system pursues objectives that are normatively correct, regardless of whether they match the designer's intentions.
3. Procedural alignment: the system's internal reasoning path is structurally faithful to the designer's epistemic norms and fully auditable, regardless of outcome identity.

I'm especially interested in whether procedural alignment stands alone or collapses into intent alignment once we specify intentions with sufficient granularity. A brief note on whether small-scale systems—say, narrow decision-support models rather than general agents—make the distinction more or less visible would also be welcome, since our course has focused mostly on the latter.
