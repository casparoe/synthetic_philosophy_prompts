I'm putting together a conceptual framework for a graduate seminar on democratic erosion, and I've kept noticing a configuration that does not sit neatly inside the standard vocabulary. In several contemporary regimes — patterns visible in Hungary, Poland, and Turkey come to mind, though analogues appear elsewhere — governments facing pressure to maintain democratic credentials respond not by abolishing oversight institutions but by multiplying them. They establish new constitutional courts, expand statutory grounds for judicial review, create independent anti-corruption boards with elaborate appointment procedures, or layer transparency mandates onto executive action. The twist is that each new mechanism is constructed so that it applies only to trivial categories of decision, produces recommendations that are explicitly non-binding under domestic law, or requires activation by a body that the executive controls through supermajority appointment rules. Meanwhile, older, simpler mechanisms that actually constrained power — direct parliamentary veto over primary legislation, straightforward judicial review with broad standing — are left formally intact but stripped of jurisdiction, funding, or practical reach. The result is a kind of institutional obesity: quantitative indices that count the number, formal independence, or procedural complexity of checks and balances may register stability or even improvement, while the substantive capacity of opposition parties, courts, or citizens to check executive action has clearly diminished.

Is there an established term for this specific dynamic — the strategic accumulation of formally democratic but functionally inert institutional layers, where formal density rises as efficacy collapses? I am familiar with the literature on autocratization, institutional decay, hollowing out, and authoritarian legalism, but none of these seems to capture the paradox that a regime can become more institutionally elaborate precisely while becoming less democratically constrained. Does this configuration have its own name in comparative politics or constitutional theory, and if so, how is it bounded from ordinary institutional weakening? If not, could it be named and defined in a way that distinguishes it clearly from decay (where institutions simply weaken over time) and from facade democracy (where democratic form was never intended to function)? I am not looking for a reading list or country-specific citations; I want the conceptual carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, which nearby concepts it overlaps with, and what boundary cases would show that it is not merely a sub-type of democratic backsliding in general.
