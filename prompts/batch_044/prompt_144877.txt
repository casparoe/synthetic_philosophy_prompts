In the socialist calculation debate, Ludwig von Mises argued that rational economic allocation is impossible without private property and market prices; Friedrich Hayek added that central planners cannot access dispersed, local knowledge; and Oskar Lange responded with a competitive trial-and-error pricing mechanism guided by central boards using formal equations. By the mid-twentieth century the exchange seemed to end in stalemate, yet I cannot determine whether the participants were genuinely disagreeing about a formal feature of information processing or whether they were answering different questions under the same label.

From an engineering perspective, the dispute looks as though it might concern whether economic information is centrally compressible—treatable as a state vector that can be updated by a planning board—or whether it is irreducibly distributed, like field measurements that must be read locally. Mises and Hayek appear to treat prices as signals carrying tacit, context-dependent data that resist aggregation, while Lange treats them as adjustable parameters in a computable equilibrium system. If this framing is correct, the impasse may not be verbal in a simple sense; rather, neither side may have possessed the conceptual apparatus to formalize information latency, feedback-loop stability, or the scaling properties of distributed computation, all of which would determine whether Lange's mechanism could converge faster or more reliably than decentralized markets.

But I remain uncertain about the diagnosis. Is the core disagreement epistemic—about whether local knowledge can in principle be transmitted to a center—or normative—about whether planning is desirable regardless of epistemic limits?
