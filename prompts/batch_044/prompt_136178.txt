Draft the joint statement that a Remembrance Duty camp—working in the broad tradition of Margalit's ethics of collective memory, which treats remembrance of injustice as a moral obligation owed to victims—and a Forgetting Rights camp—working in the broad tradition of Rieff's critique of therapeutic culture and compulsory remembrance, which treats the right to forget as essential to individual and collective flourishing—could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The collaboration's concrete subject is the moral governance of a hypothetical global digital memorial archive with algorithmic curation: a permanent, searchable repository of records of collective harm that can be preserved in full, deprecated but retained, or individually expunged upon verified request. Do not ask for bibliographies, direct quotations, or reading lists; identify the camps only by the conceptual traditions named above.

Structure the document as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—precise statements both sides accept—covering the reality of harms from both forced erasure and compulsive remembrance, the technical capabilities and limits of algorithmic curation, the relevance of survivor consent, and the conditions under which institutional neutrality is possible or impossible.

2. A disagreements table with two columns, each phrased in wording the respective camp would accept for its own position. Include at least: whether remembrance is primarily a duty owed to victims or a burden imposed on survivors; whether forgetting is a moral right or a form of moral evasion; and whether algorithmic curation can ever be normatively neutral.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one side. At least one crux must concern whether the biological or cultural naturalness of forgetting carries independent normative weight, and another should address whether institutional design can separate preservation from performance.

4. For each crux, specify a concrete institutional feature or case both sides agree is relevant—such as a verified survivor's request to expunge their family's trauma record, or an automated preservation protocol for undocumented atrocities—together with each camp's explicit prediction of how that case should be governed.

Keep formulations exact rather than diplomatic mush, avoid treating any part of the dispute as merely verbal unless you genuinely conclude it is, and do not invoke obscure theories outside standard philosophical literacy. End with a single line beginning Summary: that captures, in one sentence, the shared institutional framework both camps accept and the irreducible normative disagreement that remains.
