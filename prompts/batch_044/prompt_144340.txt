As a retired biologist who has recently begun reading metaphysics, I came across a summary of origin essentialism in an introductory text. The author explains that the view holds each person is essentially tied to the particular sperm and egg from which she actually developed: had a different sperm fertilized that egg, or that sperm fertilized a different egg, the result would not have been her. The summary then argues that this commitment forces the essentialist to say that any genetic mutation occurring during embryogenesis produces a numerically different person, and that the view cannot make sense of monozygotic twins, who share the very same origin yet are clearly two distinct individuals. From these points the author concludes that origin essentialism is really just genetic determinism in metaphysical dress, and that it should be abandoned.

I suspect this reasoning is too quick, but I cannot pinpoint where it slips. Would a defender of origin essentialism accept that intrauterine mutations threaten personal identity, or is there a distinction between originating conditions and subsequent genetic constitution? Is the twin case a genuine contradiction for the view, or does the possibility of organismal fission show that shared origin need not entail shared identity? And does the charge of genetic determinism confuse an essential property with a sufficient one? I would like an accuracy audit: which parts of the summary are faithful, which subtly misrepresent the position—perhaps by conflating origin conditions with genetic essence, or by treating a familiar objection as a knockdown refutation—and whether the conclusion survives once those misrepresentations are corrected.
