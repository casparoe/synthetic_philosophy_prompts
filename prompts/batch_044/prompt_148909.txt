Analyze the UK government’s decision in October 2021 to withdraw the temporary £20-per-week uplift to Universal Credit—introduced in spring 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic and affecting roughly six million low-income households—through the lens of prioritarianism, with sustained attention to the leveling-down objection. First, identify who occupied the worst-off positions both before and after the withdrawal, assess whether the cut reduced their absolute welfare or merely slowed its improvement, and determine whether any compensatory fiscal or economic effects could count as prioritarian benefits elsewhere. Then map the political justification—restoring fiscal normality and avoiding dependency—onto prioritarian reasoning: does framing the worst off as potential dependents change how much weight their interests should carry, or does it simply obscure a distributional judgment?

Next, use the episode to probe the leveling-down objection itself. Suppose the government had retained the uplift but funded it through a temporary tax on high earners that, due to behavioral responses or administrative leakage, reduced the welfare of the well-off without delivering any net gain to the worst off—would prioritarianism treat that trade as permissible, impermissible, or conceptually confused? More broadly, ask whether democratic budget politics ever makes prioritarian weighting visible, or whether the language of fairness, affordability, and work incentives systematically disguises it; and conclude by saying whether the Universal Credit withdrawal functions as a confirmation, an anomaly, or a category mistake for prioritarianism—especially regarding whether indirect leveling-down through fiscal consolidation is something the theory can recognize and condemn.
