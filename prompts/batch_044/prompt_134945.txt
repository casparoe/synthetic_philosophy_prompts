In a graduate seminar on collective rationality, two doctoral students—Mira and Jonas—got into a sustained disagreement over how an academic committee should resolve what they called the discursive dilemma, though they disagreed about what resolving it actually requires. The case they used was a three-person hiring committee judging whether to appoint a particular candidate. The committee agreed to evaluate three separate propositions: (P1) the candidate's publications meet the field's standard for rigor; (P2) the candidate's teaching evaluations indicate adequate classroom competence; and (C) the committee should recommend the candidate for appointment. Mira noted that two members affirmed P1 and P2 but denied C—arguing that rigor and teaching are necessary but not sufficient, and that the candidate's lack of collegiality, which was not one of the formal premises, should block the appointment. The third member denied both P1 and P2 but affirmed C, insisting that the publication metrics were too narrow and the teaching data unreliable, yet that the candidate's interdisciplinary potential made them worth hiring despite those reservations.

Mira argued that the committee should aggregate judgments premise-by-premise and then apply logical closure: since a majority endorsed P1 and P2, and since P1 and P2 together entail C if we accept the implicit bridge principle that meeting both standards is sufficient for recommendation, the group should conclude C. She called this discursive consistency and said that allowing the group to endorse premises that entail a conclusion while rejecting that conclusion amounts to collective irrationality, no different from an individual contradicting themselves.

Jonas replied that premise-based aggregation forces the two majority members to accept a conclusion they explicitly and reasonably reject. He said the bridge principle is exactly what is contested—those members do not accept it, so there is no entailment they are violating. He proposed instead a conclusion-first rule: aggregate directly on C, and treat the premises as explanatory postures rather than binding commitments. He added a further twist: if the committee adopts Mira's rule, members will strategically misreport their premise judgments in future votes to prevent conclusions they dislike, which he called the aggregative mirror problem—the transparency of reasoning becomes a mechanism for strategic distortion rather than rational clarification.

Mira countered that Jonas's mirror problem is overstated: strategic voting exists under any rule, and at least premise-based aggregation makes the manipulation visible. She then introduced a distinction of her own between thin and thick collective agency, arguing that committees only count as collective agents if they can be held to standards of logical consistency, which requires premise-based closure; otherwise they are merely statistical aggregates of individual opinions, and blaming them for inconsistency is like blaming a crowd for walking in different directions.

Jonas answered that this begs the question by defining collective agency in terms of logical consistency. He said that in actual institutions—universities, juries, legislatures—what matters is that the decision can be justified to those affected, not that it mirrors a deductive structure. He proposed a justificatory transparency condition instead: the group must be able to explain why each member voted as they did, but it need not resolve those explanations into a single logically closed set. He conceded that Mira's view produces cleaner formal properties, but insisted that those properties come at the cost of misrepresenting the actual reasoning of the participants.

Neither convinced the other. They asked me to adjudicate.

Please go through their exchange move by move. For each of Mira's and Jonas's main interventions—her initial premise-closure argument, his rejection based on contested bridge principles and strategic distortion, her response about strategic visibility and the thin/thick agency distinction, and his final move about justificatory transparency versus deductive closure—rate whether the move is clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, largely right, or ambiguous, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, not by declaring a winner but by identifying which underlying issue their disagreement actually turns on and whether either side addresses it adequately. In particular, address whether Jonas's aggregative mirror problem and Mira's thin/thick agency distinction are genuine contributions or merely relabelings of familiar points about strategic voting and collective intentionality.
