I am a retired natural scientist who has recently begun studying the philosophy of punishment, particularly the contrast between Norway’s Halden prison and the American system of mass incarceration. In tracking the judgments of one commentator whose lectures I have followed, I observe a consistent pattern: he praises Halden’s rehabilitative, normalized environment despite its long sentences; he condemns American mass incarceration for its punitive architecture and high recidivism; he rejects a pilot program that paired intensive cognitive therapy with routine solitary confinement for infractions, calling the punitive structure self-defeating; and he endorses community-based restorative justice for minor property offenses, provided mediation and reparation are genuine, even though no traditional incarceration is imposed.

The new case is a proposed center for non-violent drug offenses in which sentence lengths are fixed by a strict proportionality scale tied to harm caused, physical conditions are comfortable and safe, but all rehabilitative programming—therapy, education, and vocational training—is entirely optional and plays no role in release, which is automatic upon completion of the term regardless of personal change.

What verdict should I expect from him? More importantly, I want the underlying logic made explicit: please identify the principle he is most likely running, articulate at least one alternative principle that accommodates all four earlier judgments equally well, state what each principle predicts about this proposed center, offer your best guess with reasons for preferring one prediction, and specify one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the two principles.
