I am a retired field ecologist who, since leaving active research, has begun auditing philosophy of science courses and find myself stuck on a conceptual problem that I think shapes practical work I once took for granted. I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop where philosophers of biology sit beside astrobiologists and grant administrators; I want the remarks to be structured around a tension I now see but cannot resolve. The tension is this: each standard criterion for life—reproduction, metabolism, evolutionary adaptation—can be satisfied in isolation by phenomena we do not call alive, yet when we combine them we risk circularity because we select the combinations that match our single planetary sample. To keep the problem concrete, I want the speech to center on a hypothetical discovery I have been using as a thought experiment: a self-sustaining catalytic network in a subsurface ocean that replicates its chemical cycles, maintains internal equilibrium against its environment, and shifts its reaction pathways across generations, yet possesses no cells, no nucleic acids, and no discrete individuals. The philosophical idea must do real structural work: the remarks should not offer a definition, should not claim we would recognize life if we saw it, and should not collapse into a mere celebration of interdisciplinary collaboration. Instead, please write the speech as exactly five numbered points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion outside those five points. Each point should move the audience from the practical stakes of searching elsewhere to the conceptual difficulty of not knowing what would count as success, and the fifth point should close with an open invitation to tolerate ambiguity rather than resolving it. The tone should be that of someone who spent decades observing organisms in their habitats and is now genuinely unsure whether the category life picks out a natural kind or merely a familiar cluster, and it should be accessible to the non-philosophers in the room without being condescending to the specialists.
