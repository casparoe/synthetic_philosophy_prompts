I'm working through what makes a philosophical thought experiment genuinely illuminating versus merely manipulative, and I have found that my intuitive reactions form a pattern I cannot justify. Here are my verdicts, offered concretely. The standard trolley lever case strikes me as a sound intuition pump: it isolates a structural difference between redirecting a threat and using a person as a means, and I believe it genuinely advances reflection on the doctrine of double effect. By contrast, the footbridge variant—pushing the heavy man onto the tracks—feels like it exploits proximity or disgust heuristics rather than tracking any moral distinction; despite the identical outcome, I reject it as misleading. Searle's Chinese Room initially persuades me that symbol manipulation is not understanding, yet on reflection I think it cheats by localizing mental properties inside one subsystem rather than allowing them to be distributed across the whole, so I classify it as partly illuminating and partly misdirecting.

Jackson's Mary's Room, in which a scientist who knows every physical fact about color experiences red for the first time upon leaving her monochrome environment, feels compelling—she clearly acquires something new—but I now think it functions as a bad pump because it treats phenomenal knowledge as irreducibly non-physical from the outset, thereby begging the question against physicalism. Davidson's Swampman, the lightning-struck duplicate with no causal history, provokes in me a strong denial that it has mental states, which suggests that origin or causal history matters; yet I cannot defend that denial without circularity, so I treat the case as exposing an unexamined commitment rather than justifying one. Nozick's Experience Machine seems to show that we value authenticity over mere pleasure, though I worry it may only register status-quo bias, leaving me genuinely ambivalent about whether it is sound or rhetorical.

There is also a variant I have constructed that is not widely discussed: imagine replacing every neuron in a functioning brain with a silicon chip that behaves identically, running the sequence forward until no biological tissue remains, and then running the identical replacement sequence in reverse starting from an inanimate rock. If consciousness never flickers on in either direction, we seem forced either to deny that the original brain was ever conscious or to grant consciousness to the rock. I think this bidirectional replacement argument is misleading because it presupposes that consciousness must have sharp boundaries, but I also believe it exposes a real tension in functionalist commitments that cleaner examples conceal. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps concerning whether a pump isolates a structural feature that survives reversal of affective framing, or whether its conclusion remains stable when the narrative direction is inverted—that captures why I accept the trolley lever, reject the footbridge, distrust Mary's Room, and remain suspended between acceptance and rejection for Swampman, the Experience Machine, and my bidirectional replacement case? If so, state the principle precisely and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed: the zombie conceivability argument for dualism, in which we are asked to imagine a being physically identical to a conscious person but lacking all phenomenal experience. Finally, be honest about which of my listed verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and must therefore be overturned.
