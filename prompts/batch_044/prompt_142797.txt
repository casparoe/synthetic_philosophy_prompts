I sit on our university’s Student Support Allocation Committee, and last night I had a long, exhausting argument with a close friend—she works in philosophy of mind, I work in institutional policy—about whether it is even coherent to rank welfare deficits across students whose subjective experiences are structurally incommensurable. She kept pressing what she called the “translation invariance problem”: if one student navigates campus through echolocation after late-onset blindness while another manages executive dysfunction in a high-stimulation environment, they inhabit non-overlapping phenomenal spaces, and any metric that converts either experience into a common “accessibility-to-functioning” scale necessarily smuggles in a hidden ontology of the “normal” subject. The qualitative meaning of loss, she argued, is not preserved across such translations, so comparing their deficits is not merely difficult but conceptually illegitimate.

This is not abstract for Tuesday’s vote. The committee must decide whether to adopt the Cross-Domain Welfare Calibration Protocol (CDWCP), a pilot framework that assigns priority scores for limited accommodation funding by weighting estimated utility deficits across disability categories using a shared index. The designers claim the index measures only external opportunity sets, but my friend insists that the point where those sets are weighted by subjective importance is exactly where an illicit interpersonal comparison occurs. I need to circulate a formal written opinion before the meeting that takes her objection seriously rather than dismissing it, and that proposes concrete evaluative criteria reviewers can apply to the protocol’s weighting methodology. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from the conceptual structure of the problem and the institutional stakes alone.

Please draft the memo with the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. Executive Summary (about 120 words): state the bottom-line recommendation—approve with conditions, reject with an alternative allocation principle, or approve only a restricted pilot—and the single core reason.
2. The Case and the Protocol (about 200 words): describe the CDWCP, the institutional need for priority-setting under scarcity, and the designers’ claim that the index avoids direct comparison.
3. The Translation Invariance Objection (about 250 words): articulate my friend’s argument about cross-phenomenal comparison, explain why it threatens the protocol at the weighting stage, and identify the hidden normative assumption she thinks is being made.
4. The Case for the Recommendation (about 300 words): defend your recommended course using reasoning about institutional fairness, epistemic humility, and whether avoiding comparison is practically possible in resource allocation.
5. The Strongest Consideration on the Losing Side (about 200 words): present the best argument for the position you reject, treating it with intellectual honesty.
6. Implementation or Alternative (about 180 words): specify concrete next steps, a timeline if the recommendation involves action, or the structure of a replacement framework if it involves rejection.

Write in a formal, polished register suitable for circulation among university administrators and faculty ethics reviewers.
