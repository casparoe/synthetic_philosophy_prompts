I've been working through my own judgments about when a neighborhood has genuinely undergone gentrification, as opposed to mere demographic turnover, economic recovery, or ordinary commercial change, and I'm trying to see whether there's a coherent principle behind them. Here are five cases with my verdicts.

First, a working-class neighborhood sees rents climb about five percent a year for a decade. The local grocery is replaced by a boutique food hall, but no long-term renters are displaced and property values rise slowly. My verdict: this is not yet gentrification, though it is a precursor.

Second, take the same neighborhood ten years later: rents have risen eighty percent, roughly half the original renting households have left and been replaced by higher-income newcomers, and the boutique itself has been priced out by a luxury chain. My verdict: this is clearly gentrification.

Third, consider a neighborhood with the same rent spike and demographic replacement, but caused entirely by a natural disaster that destroyed much of the housing stock; wealthier residents rebuild first, and the original community cannot return because of reconstruction costs. My verdict: this is not gentrification. It is disaster-driven displacement, and the causal mechanism matters.

Fourth, imagine a neighborhood where rents and demographics are currently stable, but the city has just rezoned the area for high-rise luxury development. Investors begin quietly acquiring properties, yet no residents have left and no new construction has begun. My verdict: gentrification is already in progress, even though the harm is prospective rather than realized.

Fifth, a factory closes, causing thirty percent of residents to leave for economic reasons unrelated to housing costs. Artists then move in because rent is cheap, followed by cafes and galleries. Within five years, the remaining original residents feel culturally displaced, though no one was ever priced out of housing. My verdict: this counts as gentrification, because the neighborhood's social character has been transformed through an influx of higher-cultural-capital newcomers, even though the initial displacement was exogenous.

These five judgments feel mostly right to me, but I can see tensions. Cases two and three have nearly identical surface outcomes—rent spikes, demographic replacement—yet I treat them differently because of causation. Cases four and five both involve displacement or transformation that isn't purely market-driven price pressure, yet I call one gentrification and treat the other as prospective. And case one suggests that commercial change alone isn't enough, while case five suggests that cultural change alone might be.

What principle, if any, can capture all five verdicts? If you think no single principle can, which verdict should be overturned—should case four be rejected because harm must be actual, or case five because economic displacement must be endogenous? State your principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for a scenario I haven't mentioned: for instance, a neighborhood where a large university expands its campus, buying up housing and converting it to student rentals, raising rents for everyone else but also providing stable employment and public transit investment. Also, please be explicit about which of my five judgments your account cannot accommodate, rather than smoothing over the conflict.
