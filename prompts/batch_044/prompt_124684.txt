I was arguing with my roommate about whether tourists have to follow local queuing and tipping rules like natives do, and I think I accidentally built a reductio but I can't locate the leak. Here is the chain I ended up with:

(1) If a social practice is a stable coordination scheme with mutual expectation, widespread compliance, and low-cost participation, then anyone who steps into the relevant role inherits a directed moral obligation to follow it — call this scheme-inheritance.
(2) Tipping in US restaraunts, queuing in the UK, and local jaywalking norms in Japan are exactly such schemes: they depend on predictable behavior and are cheap to comply with.
(3) A visitor who eats at that restaurant, waits for a bus, or crosses the street steps into the role of customer or pedestrian and recieves the benefits of the scheme.
(4) So by scheme-inheritance, the visitor has the same obligation as locals: tip around twenty percent, queue properly, don't jaywalk.
(5) Crucially, ignorance of the local convention doesn't cancel the obligation, because the normative load attaches to the role within the scheme, not to your personal knowledge of it.
(6) Therefore a tourist who doesn't tip, or who jaywalks because that's normal back home, commits a genuine moral wrong — not just a social faux pas.
(7) But scheme-inheritance applies to any coordination game: specific greeting rituals, dress codes in religious spaces, left-hand eating rules, and even bribery norms where they are stable, expected, and cheap.
(8) So either we're constantly committing invisible moral violations everywhere we travel, or scheme-inheritance must be false.

I really want to keep (1) and (3) — they feel right for promises and contract-like situations — but (8) is unbearable. Is the slip between (4) and (5), where I smuggle in that ignorance doesn't excuse? Or is (5) correct and the problem is that (1) needs a knowledge or consent filter that kills (6) but saves the intuition about queuing? I feel like there's a hidden move about voluntariness versus mere role-occupancy, but I can't pin it down. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost — especially if I want to hang on to the idea that everyday conventions can generate real obligations?
