I'm on our city's Memory and Heritage Commission and we vote Thursday on a petition that's kind of stuck between ethics and archive policy. Back in the 70s a factory collapsed and the municipal archive holds about forty oral histories where survivors name specific families who owned the plant — but those families were never criminaly charged, just socially condemned. Now the descendants want what they're calling mnemonic erasure: not destroying the tapes, but permanently stripping them from the digital index and blocking researcher access, arguing that keeping these accusations searchable is a collective punishment with no end date since formal justice never ran its course. The survivor groups say there's a duty to remember exactly this kind of unprosecuted harm, and our charter mentions both preservation of testimony and protection from unjust memorialization without saying which wins.

Can you draft the short opinion memo I'd circulate before the meeting? I want it to reccomend a concrete course — approve the restriction, reject it, or split the difference with conditions — and include a clear implementation timeline, maybe within the next fiscal quarter. The reasoning should take seriously the idea that forgetting can be a moral right (not just psychological relief) and also that archives owe future citizens an epistemic debt of unfiltered access, especially where courts never spoke. Don't just restate legal definitions; treat the family's position as genuinely philosophical, in the vein of Margalit and Rieff's debates on shared memory, rather than dismissing it as sentimental.

Please say explicitly what would change your mind — which argument about memory obligations or archival ethics would push you to the opposite verdict, and where your reasoning is shakiest.
