I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I facilitate an 80-minute interdisciplinary workshop for about 16 participants—mostly engineers and a few philosophy graduate students—on conceptual issues in technology ethics. I want a fully in-person, non-lecture activity that makes the group feel the force of Kant’s universalizability tests, with particular attention to the distinction between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in will. I also want them to discover, through interaction rather than explanation, how maxims frequently smuggle in hidden indexical conditions—unstated “only when I am the agent” clauses—that remain invisible until everyone attempts to act on them at once.

Design a runnable session with clear roles or teams, interaction rules, and a point-based payoff scheme that uses no real money but creates genuine tension. Provide specific materials (cards, boards, tokens, etc.) and a round-by-round sequence. Arrange the rules so that participants first propose what seem like reasonable exception-handling rules for a shared system protocol, then adopt them simultaneously, and only then run into failure: either the system becomes logically impossible if universalized (contradiction in conception), or universal adoption undermines the very purpose the rule was meant to serve (contradiction in will). The hidden indexical should be built into the scenario mechanics—such as identical condition cards distributed to all players—so that participants experience the collapse themselves before anyone names it. Do not include an upfront lecture; the formal distinction should emerge from what happens.

Then provide a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the rounds onto the relevant parts of the universalizability test, including the point at which an implicit “only-for-me” parameter gets made explicit. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—for example, participants focusing on outcome fairness rather than the logical form of the maxim, or failing to notice that the condition applies symmetrically to everyone—and how to handle it. Finally, be explicit about which feature of Kant’s account this activity cannot reproduce—such as the noumenal/phenomenal distinction or the specific conception of rational agency as an end in itself—and therefore must be supplied by the facilitator after the experience.

Keep the prompt concrete and self-contained. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I will not have search access during the session, and the design should stand on its own. Use American English and maintain a formal, polished tone throughout.
