Over the past month, since I began collaborating with researchers in predictive modeling, I have become personally preoccupied with a conceptual tension that my philosophy-of-science reading group keeps circling but never resolves: when a large-scale system—whether a climate model, a language model, or an ecological simulation—exhibits stable predictive regularities, do those regularities count as real patterns in the ontologically robust sense defended by pattern realists, or are they merely useful fictions sustained by our choice of explanatory stance? I want to move beyond labeling people by school affiliation and instead construct a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts actual commitments on this specific issue.

Please design a branching instrument that begins with a root question about whether predictive success alone is sufficient for pattern reality, regardless of underlying mechanism. From there, split on whether physical-law instantiation is necessary for such reality; then on whether design-level functional organization can substitute for physical grounding; and finally on whether adopting an intentional stance—treating the system as if it possessed beliefs or goals—is either required to complete the explanation or is categorically inappropriate for non-biological artifacts. Each terminal node should be named after the logical combination of answers rather than after a historical figure—examples might include "physical-grounded realist," "design-stance instrumentalist," "intentional-stance pluralist," or "full eliminativist about synthetic patterns"—and should carry one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment, such as whether it forces the denial of macroeconomic cycles or, conversely, the attribution of proto-intentionality to statistical aggregates. Where the logical space yields an apparently uninhabited branch—say, one that accepts intentional-stance validation while rejecting both physical and design grounding—flag it explicitly and briefly diagnose whether the vacancy stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect. Every node should be phrased as a concrete case judgment answerable without prior knowledge of philosophical taxonomy, and the instrument should contain no requests for citations, bibliographies, or specific textual references, since it is intended for a workshop in which participants will not have access to online searches.
