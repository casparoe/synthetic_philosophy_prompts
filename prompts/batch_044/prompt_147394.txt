I am drafting an essay for a long-form ideas magazine on Jorge Luis Borges’s philosophy of time. Rather than repeating the familiar interpretation of his essay “A New Refutation of Time” as a variant of Berkeleyan idealism—time as a mental fiction that dissolves personal identity—I want to propose a less common reading. I suggest that Borges’s image of the garden of forking paths, especially as developed in his story “The Garden of Forking Paths,” supports what might be called a local presentism without global chronology: each narrative path carries its own present, but there is no universal timeline that orders all paths into a single sequence. On this view, Borges is not anticipating Everettian many-worlds branching, because he denies not merely uniqueness but the need for any container in which branches sit. The present is topological, not chronological; it belongs to a path, not to a world-line within a master history. I then contrast this briefly with the analytic philosophy of time—specifically the tension between the block universe and the moving spotlight—to argue that Borges sidesteps both by rejecting the premise that temporal relations require a linear, all-encompassing order. The essay asks whether this topological presentism can remain coherent without collapsing into solipsism, since if every path has its own now, there is no shared present in which disagreement about “now” could occur. I want the piece to be accessible to educated general readers, but I am deliberately pushing beyond the standard popular summary of Borges as “the writer who thought time was an illusion.”

Could you comment on three things? First, is this reading textually defensible—does Borges’s text actually support a topological rather than psychological denial of universal time, or am I importing contemporary philosophy of time into his fiction? Second, is the distinction I draw between Borges’s labyrinth and Everettian branching philosophically sharp, or does it conflate different kinds of non-uniqueness? Third, does the framing risk being too abstract for a magazine audience that has not read analytic philosophy of time, and if so, where should I ground the argument in concrete imagery from Borges’s stories rather than in conceptual taxonomy? I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list—only for an assessment of whether the conceptual architecture holds together and whether the essay’s central move is interesting rather than merely idiosyncratic.
