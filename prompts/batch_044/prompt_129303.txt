For an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar of about 14 students, with one 75-minute session available, design a structured classroom activity—complete with explicit rules, assigned roles, symbolic stakes, and a timeline—that makes participants feel the force of P.F. Strawson's distinction between participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude, particularly in institutional settings where role-obligations blur the line between personal and vicarious reactions.

Center the activity on a concrete fictional scenario: a university honor council has discovered that one of its members, acting strictly within an assigned procedural role, caused foreseeable but unintended harm to a student. Assign each participant a hidden role—direct victim-proxy, neutral observer, institutional-continuity representative, or the agent-role itself—plus a private reactive budget of five tokens representing emotional-moral engagement. In round one, participants must publicly allocate tokens among three visible stances: sustained participant reactive attitude (demanding a personal account), modified reactive attitude (indignation directed at the institutional role rather than the individual will), and the objective attitude (treating the agent as a management problem requiring correction or therapy rather than moral address). Allocation is visible but revisable; however, in round two, any collective shift to the objective attitude for the agent-role requires unanimous coalition among all non-agent participants to trigger an institutional closure that grants stability points but permanently removes the agent-role's obligation to offer an interpersonal apology. Those who maintain reactive engagement retain membership tokens that preserve full speaking rights during debrief, while those who build the unanimous coalition gain institutional stability but forfeit the right to demand recognition.

Arrange the rules and payoffs so that participants experience Strawson's claim that adopting the objective attitude is not merely a private explanatory shift but a form of social withdrawal that restructures the group's interpersonal fabric. The coordination problem should emerge organically: participants should find themselves able to agree intellectually on diminished personal responsibility while still being unable to collectively suspend reactive demands without a felt sense of loss or betrayal. The idea should appear in what happens—the token allocations, the failed coalitions, the silence that follows closure—before it is named in the debrief.

Provide a debrief—without naming Strawson until the mapping phase—that connects specific moments to the framework: identify when participants felt vicarious versus direct reactive attitudes, when the objective attitude felt like a loss of interpersonal recognition rather than an explanatory gain, and how the institutional framing made the objective stance appear not merely available but administratively required. Include an honest assessment of which feature of Strawson's view the activity cannot reproduce—namely, the long-term, diachronic erosion of reactive attitudes through sustained social practice rather than through a single deliberative choice—and explain how the facilitator must supply that temporal dimension verbally. Also specify what will predictably go wrong the first time: participants will likely conflate the agent's personal moral status with the legitimacy of the institutional role, and they may treat the token economy as utilitarian optimization rather than an expression of attitude; suggest concrete facilitation moves to correct this without breaking the experiential frame.

The activity should require no external readings, no citations, no bibliographies, and no web access to run.
