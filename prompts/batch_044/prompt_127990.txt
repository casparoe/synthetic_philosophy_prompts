I'm part of this interagency committee on long-range forecasting — we advise regional planning boards on whether to adopt predictive frameworks for food and water security out to 2050. There's a proposal in front of us to approve a revised "Malthusian carry-capacity index" developed by the Institute for Demographic-Ecological Modeling. The index was originally rolled out in the late 80s and widely criticized after its 1995-2005 yield-collapse predictions failed; the proponents now argue the failure was temporary, driven by synthetic-fertilizer and desalination subsidies that masked underlying exponential growth, and that those buffers are now hitting physical limits. They want us to endorse it for ten-year regional planning cycles.

Half the committee thinks adopting a "falsified" model is just special pleading — if predictions fail, the mechanism is wrong, full stop. The other half says long-run theories can't be tested on decadal timelines, and that Malthus might be right but early, with rescue conditions that dont violate standard falsifcation norms.

Please draft the committee's decision memo — recommend approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, with a concrete timeline and conditions if relevant. It should take the "right but early" argument seriously rather than treating it as rationalization, and it should also give the strongest counter-argument its due (probably that allowing rescue makes the framework unfalsifiable in practice). Write it as a document the researchers could actually respond to, not a philosophy essay.

One more thing: if the premise that this case genuinely turns on Malthusian falsifcation and long-run rescue is confused, or if you think the memo format can't handle the tension without faking a compromise, say that straight up. Dont politely paper over it. And flag any place where you're leaning on a contested reading of what Malthus actually claimed, since I'm not asking for a history lecture but I dont want hidden assumptions.
