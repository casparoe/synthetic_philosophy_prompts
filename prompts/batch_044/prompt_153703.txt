I'm working through what I actually think about deception, testing my intuitions case by case. Telling an armed intruder at the door that my roommate is not home, when she is hiding inside: clearly permissible, perhaps obligatory. Telling my elderly aunt I adore the ugly sweater she knitted: permissible, a harmless social grace. Telling a hiring manager I left my last job voluntarily when I was fired for misconduct: not permissible. Telling a dying patient who specifically asks whether her estranged son called to say goodbye, when he has not: not permissible, even though the false comfort would ease her final hours; it wrongs her by substituting my judgment for her own facing of reality. Telling a close friend her startup plan is sound when I genuinely think it will ruin her: not permissible. Writing "the check is in the mail" on an overdue invoice when it is not: not permissible, though it feels less grave than the hiring lie. Here is the pair that genuinely troubles me: telling my seven-year-old that our dead dog went to live on a farm feels protective, yet also like treating her emerging rational capacity as unworthy of the truth; meanwhile, telling a patient with advanced dementia that her long-dead husband is "just out running errands" to spare her repeated fresh grief feels clearly permissible, even kind. Both are benevolent falsehoods to vulnerable people about loss, yet my intuition splits them sharply. Is there a principle—perhaps centered on respect for rational agency, on the kind of harm deception does to practical reasoning, or on the listener's standing to demand truth—that captures all of these judgments and explains why the dementia case is permissible while the dying-patient case is not? Or must I abandon one verdict? If you propose a principle, state it precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case above, apply it to a situation I have not considered (say, a psychiatrist who temporarily affirms a paranoid patient's delusion in order to preserve the therapeutic alliance with the aim of eventually guiding him back to reality), and be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and why. Give a committed answer: take a definite position on whether a unified principle is available, do not merely lay out options for me to choose between, and give your reasons directly.
