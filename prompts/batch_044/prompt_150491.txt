I am preparing a discussion paper for an advanced undergraduate seminar in philosophy of language, and I am attempting to map the implicit commitments of a non-specialist interlocutor with whom I have been debating norms of assertion—specifically whether the governing standard is knowledge, truth, belief, or some hybrid. Over several months she has delivered consistent verdicts on four cases, and I need to determine whether they genuinely fix a single norm or underdetermine the choice. Her record is as follows: she judges a meteorologist blameworthy for asserting that ‘there will be a blizzard tomorrow’ on the strength of a sixty per cent forecast when the storm misses entirely, even though the meteorologist genuinely believed the claim; she holds that a court witness who asserts ‘the car was blue’ and is correct purely by lucky guess—lacking any perceptual basis—has done something wrong, though she hesitates between calling it a lie and a breach of some other duty; she approves without reservation of a historian who asserts that ‘the treaty was signed in March’ while admitting she is only eighty per cent certain because the archive is damaged, provided she genuinely knows the fact from other sources; and she condemns a student who asserts in a seminar that he understands Kant’s categorical imperative when he genuinely believes he does, despite having systematically misconstrued it, even though his statement touches on a technically true peripheral point.

I now wish to test her consistency with a new scenario: a sleep-deprived surgeon, operating on autopilot after a thirty-hour shift, tells a junior colleague that ‘this suture pattern is correct’; the statement is true, she genuinely believes it, but she currently lacks the cognitive capacity to know it—she could not defend or justify the claim if challenged, and her mental state is more like rehearsed behaviour than epistemic achievement. Would she condemn the surgeon’s assertion, and on what grounds? More broadly, I want you to identify the two or three competing norms—such as Williamson’s knowledge norm, a pure belief norm, a justified-belief norm, or a truth norm modified by institutional role—that fit all four of her earlier verdicts equally well. For each candidate norm, state what it predicts about the surgeon case. Then offer your best guess as to which norm she is actually running, with explicit reasoning, and finally propose one further hypothetical case—distinct from the surgeon scenario—that would best discriminate between the remaining candidates. I am particularly keen to avoid misrepresenting her position in the seminar, so please do not invent bibliographic references; I only need the conceptual architecture and the predictive logic.
