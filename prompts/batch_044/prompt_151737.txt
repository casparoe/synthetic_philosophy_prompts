I recently helped stage a workshop performance of an opera scene in which the hero dies amid visible stage machinery, exaggerated vocal writing, and a chorus that narrates the event in cool, detached language — so that the artificiality is impossible to miss. Several friends who attended were genuinely moved; one wept, despite knowing the performer and seeing the rigging. Another friend, who holds that emotional responses ought to stay calibrated to one's beliefs about reality, said that anyone who cries here is either being reflexively manipulated by sound and lighting or is failing to keep belief and feeling in proper alignment. I feel this dismissal misses something, because the grief did not feel like being tricked; if anything, the obvious contrivance seemed to make the emotional response more appropriate, not less. I am struggling to articulate precisely what is wrong with demanding that our feelings always match our beliefs about what is actually occurring. Can you help me see whether being moved by something we fully recognise as staged, implausible, and musically contrived can still be rational and valuable rather than a form of emotional indiscipline? And is the insistence that belief and affect must be co-calibrated perhaps itself an unduly narrow picture of what human responses can legitimately be?
