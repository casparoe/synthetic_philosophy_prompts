I chair a review panel for a regional foundation that funds community mental-health interventions. Recently we have begun requiring applicants to state not only what they will do, but why they are proposing it — specifically, whether their motivation aligns with what moral philosophy distinguishes as the right kind of reasons for beneficent institutional action, as opposed to reasons of liability reduction, reputation management, or anticipated political credit. We have observed that proposals driven by these latter motives often design studies differently — selecting different outcome measures, altering recruitment strategies, or shifting resource allocation — even when the surface protocol is identical to a welfare-driven proposal. The conceptual distinction is not merely decorative for us; we treat it as a filter because the kind of reason that motivates an intervention shapes its resilience when external incentives change.

Please construct a reusable audit procedure that my panel can apply to any proposal's motivation statement and supporting documentation. The procedure should translate the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction, as applied to collective institutional actors rather than to individual moral agents, into an ordered sequence of tests or diagnostic questions. For each step, specify the exact question or test to apply, what counts as a sufficient answer (pass), what counts as a failure, whether the step is checking a genuine conceptual condition or only a fallible proxy, and where mixed or ambiguous answers should be routed.

The procedure must explicitly handle three hard-case structures: (a) mixed motivation, where welfare concern and institutional self-protection are both present and genuinely intertwined; (b) explicit denial of welfare concern coupled with a study design that nevertheless serves participant welfare; and (c) explicit assertion of welfare concern coupled with design choices — budget, staffing, evaluation metrics — that primarily serve organizational visibility or risk mitigation.

Then run the procedure on the following three proposals. First, a proposal to extend crisis-text-line operating hours, justified almost entirely by reducing the foundation's liability exposure after a recent wrongful-death settlement, with welfare mentioned only as a foreseeable side effect. Second, a proposal for peer-support groups for recently unhoused young adults, framed exclusively in the language of organizational resilience metrics and stakeholder engagement outcomes, with no mention of participant well-being, yet whose design — staffing ratios, meeting frequency, confidentiality protocols — is indistinguishable from a welfare-centered model. Third, a proposal that opens with an explicit, detailed statement of direct concern for the mental health of homeless youth, but whose budget allocates sixty percent to external evaluation consultants and public-relations outreach, with direct service staff receiving minimal funding.

Finally, the procedure should identify the precise points at which it must halt and defer to human judgment because the distinction between kinds of reasons cannot be further operationalized without collapsing into either mind-reading or arbitrary line-drawing. It should also flag the specific misfire modes the procedure is vulnerable to — for instance, cases where an institution genuinely holds welfare reasons but expresses them only in bureaucratic or defensive language, and cases where liability-avoidance and welfare-promotion happen to converge on identical actions, making the reason-type underdetermined by the design.
