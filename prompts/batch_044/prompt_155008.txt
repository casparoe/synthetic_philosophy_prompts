I run this little repair cafe out of my garage every saturday, and lately we've been getting into these situtions where a fix just keeps getting worse and we can't tell if we should bail or keep going. I was reading about that dollar auction game where people bid on a dollar bill and end up spending like $20 just so they don't lose, and it clicked that's exactly what happens when we've already sunk three weekends into a broken dishwasher — we keep going not because it makes sense but because quitting feels like admitting defeat in front of everyone. But I also realized it's not just about the parts we've already bought; it's about not wanting to look like the person who gives up when other volunteers are watching, which feels like the same social trap as the auction, plus there's the thing where we've already told the owner we'll have it done by next week.

So I want an actual decision procedure me and my co-volunteers can run when we're standing around a dead appliance, something we can do in five minutes without pulling out a textbook. It needs the questions in order, what counts as a yes or no at each step, where it just stops and says quit, and where we have to actually talk about values because it's not a math problem anymore. The hard parts I need it to cover are: when the owner is standing right there expecting us to finish, when we've already promised people we'll get it done, when quitting means eating $40 in donated parts, and when there's that weird peer-pressure thing where nobody wants to be the first one to suggest giving up. Please build it out of whatever that auction theory actually says about escalation, but keep it usable by people who don't know the theory, and flag the spots where no checklist can decide for us — especially around whether it's okay to break a promise if the math says quit.

Then run it on two cases we're facing: first, a washing machine where we've already replaced the motor and the drum seal but now the control board is dead and the replacement is backordered for three months; second, an old tube radio some guy brought in because his dad built it — we've put in about 6 hours and cheap parts but it's still silent, and he's coming back next saturday expecting music. Show me where the procedure gives us an answer we won't like, and be honest about which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as rules.
