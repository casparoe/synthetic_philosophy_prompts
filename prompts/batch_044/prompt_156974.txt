Dr. Aris began by defending a qualified version of the Walzerian thesis. He argued that the moral equality of combatants holds only if soldiers on opposing sides occupy a genuinely symmetric epistemic situation with respect to the justice of their cause. In contemporary conflicts, he contended, soldiers typically operate under conditions of structured epistemic dependency: they rely on state institutions that filter, frame, and sometimes systematically distort the moral and evidentiary basis for war. Dr. Brenner interrupted to object that this conflates jus ad bellum with jus in bello; she insisted that Walzer's central claim is precisely that individual soldiers are not morally responsible for the decision to initiate hostilities, irrespective of how well or poorly informed they happen to be. Dr. Aris granted the separation of the two domains but maintained that if a soldier's ignorance is not merely accidental but manufactured—that is, produced and sustained by the very institution that deploys him—then the symmetry required for equality is undermined. The soldiers on the just side, he suggested, might labor under excusable ignorance, whereas those on the unjust side are kept ignorant by design. This asymmetry, he claimed, fractures the moral shield that equality provides.

Dr. Brenner responded that this argument still collapses epistemic conditions into liability conditions. Even manufactured ignorance, she said, does not render the individual soldier culpable for the war's initiation, since culpability requires some voluntary contribution to the wrong, and conscripted or institutionally dependent soldiers lack the requisite autonomy. She warned that if we permit institutional deception to erode combatant equality, we effectively require each soldier to evaluate the moral quality of his state's propaganda apparatus before fighting—thereby reimposing the very burden of judging jus ad bellum that the separation was meant to eliminate. Dr. Aris countered that he was not attributing culpability for initiation to the soldier; rather, he was claiming that the soldier's moral permission to kill enemy combatants is conditional on a baseline of epistemic independence, and that manufactured ignorance removes that condition. To capture the difference, he proposed distinguishing innocent ignorance from structurally induced ignorance, arguing that only the former supports the equality thesis.

Dr. Brenner's final reply was practical: she asked whether Dr. Aris's distinction can be applied without demanding that combatants investigate the justice of the cause. Any grading of ignorance into innocent and induced varieties, she argued, forces soldiers back into moral evaluation of jus ad bellum and destroys the protective separation altogether.
