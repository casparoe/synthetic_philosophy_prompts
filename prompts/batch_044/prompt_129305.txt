I'm drafting the final essay examination for an upper-level philosophy course on free will and moral responsibility. I need one question that centers on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — the regress that true moral responsibility would require being causa sui, which is impossible — but I want it pushed away from standard summaries. Specifically, students should be required to evaluate whether Strawson's regress illicitly slides between a causal-explanatory demand (that every feature of the agent be traceable to prior causes) and a normative-justificatory demand (that the agent be the ultimate source of her will in a way that grounds desert), and they should apply this evaluation to the following scenario: an adult human's neural architecture is gradually, over thirty years, replaced by functionally identical synthetic substrates, with psychological continuity preserved throughout, so that the resulting being cannot trace any of its present mental states to biological origins but can trace them only to earlier synthetic states.

Please write:
1. The exam question itself, calibrated for a 75-minute essay response.
2. A model answer of approximately 700 words.
3. A marking scheme structured around five specific intellectual moves that separate an excellent answer (A-range) from a competent but limited one (B-range), with the total out of 20 marks distributed among them, and a brief note on how students typically fail each move.

In both the model answer and the marking scheme, please clearly distinguish what is philosophically settled about Strawson's argument (for instance, the logical structure of the self-creation regress) from what remains genuinely contested (for instance, whether the argument presupposes an overly demanding conception of sourcehood, or whether the causal/normative distinction undermines the regress). Do not present any disputed interpretation — including your own assessment of the neural-replacement case — as if it were the consensus view among specialists in the free-will literature.
