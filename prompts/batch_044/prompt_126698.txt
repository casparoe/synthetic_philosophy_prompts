I'm putting together a handout for an applied-epistemics reading group—mostly longtime rationalist-blog readers—on how people actually sort themselves on conspiracy theories, not by which specific theories they endorse but by the machinery they use to evaluate institutional failure. I want a decision tree, not a list of named camps, that starts from one concrete commitment: "I accept that institutional knowledge production can be systematically wrong about a specific event without being globally unreliable." From there it should branch through diagnostic questions that separate positions by what they do with evidence, not by what they call themselves.

Please build the tree so that each node is a concrete case or claim a reader could answer without knowing the label it points to. The branches should cover at least these distinctions: (1) whether the primary failure mode is base-rate neglect—treating successful hidden coordination as common in the reference class—or structural unfalsifiability, where counterevidence is always reinterpreted as part of the cover-up; (2) whether decentralized verification networks can genuinely substitute for institutional authority, or whether they inherit the same trust-decay dynamics; (3) a niche distinction I want injected—call it "verification debt"—whether rejecting an official account obligates the rejecter to advance a competing explanation that carries predictive risk, or whether negative coherence (the official story contains internal holes) is sufficient on its own; and (4) whether belief revision is driven by an inside-view assessment of specific documents and mechanisms, or by an outside-view reference class of past conspiracies.

The leaves should include at least: strict institutionalist debunking; Bayesian skepticism anchored to low base rates; decentralized verificationism; hermeneutics-of-suspicion structuralism; and any unoccupied combinations—such as accepting verification debt while denying that reference-class reasoning applies—each with a one-sentence diagnosis of why the space is empty (incoherence, instability, or mere lack of advocates). Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate. Use a specific scenario at the first split: for instance, "Primary documents contradict the official narrative but are ignored by every mainstream outlet; do you update on the documents alone, require independent replication by a network you do not already distrust, or treat media silence as confirmatory of institutional capture?" Write it formally, as for a seminar handout, with no bibliography, no citation requests, and no dependence on obscure named theories that a small model could not be expected to recall.
