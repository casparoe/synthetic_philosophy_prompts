As a retired physical scientist who has only recently begun to study moral and political philosophy systematically, I have found myself importing conceptual tools from my earlier training into debates that philosophers typically frame differently. One such tool is hysteresis: in magnetism and materials science, a system’s state depends not merely on present conditions but on its path through prior states, and reversing the input rarely restores the original trajectory. I want to test whether collective moral memory operates analogously, and whether that framing survives disciplined contact with an actual controversy.

Take the removal of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol in June 2020, together with the subsequent campaigns to rename streets and institutions bearing names linked to the transatlantic slave trade. Analyze this episode through the lens of commemorative ethics—specifically the distinction between honorific and documentary memorialization—but keep the hysteresis analogy explicitly in view. Who possessed legitimate standing to alter or destroy the monument? Does describing the removal as 'restoring justice' obscure a path-dependent welfare judgment about whose historical suffering is made visible in public space? Does the act return the city’s collective memory to a prior, less contaminated equilibrium, or does it irreversibly push the system onto a new trajectory with its own inertia? Identify precisely where the hysteresis model illuminates the dynamics—for instance, in explaining why the toppling provoked intense backlash despite decades of prior controversy—and where it breaks down, particularly if it wrongly implies that moral identity evolves deterministically rather than through deliberative choice. Finally, say whether the episode supports the view that a society can deliberately select its commemorative regime, or whether it shows that such choices are always reactive and locked into prior paths, and note any point at which the theory of commemorative ethics remains silent rather than speaking.
