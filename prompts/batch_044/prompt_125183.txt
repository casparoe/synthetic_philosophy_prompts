I am a doctoral student in political theory working on the conceptual foundations of group characterization. I am preparing a 90-minute graduate seminar for about 14 students on whether “national character” is a coherent notion and whether any form of generalizing about peoples can be epistemically responsible without being morally unacceptable. Rather than lecturing, I want students to experience the tension themselves through structured interaction.

Please design a self-contained, runnable in-class simulation. Inject the following conceptual framing, which is not standard in the literature but captures the core problem: treat national-character claims not as statistical averages over individuals but as “modal profile ascriptions”—claims that a particular response-pattern is the characteristic or default mode of a group, stable across a range of counterfactual institutional settings. The students should discover that defending such an ascription requires holding constant certain background conditions, yet holding those conditions constant makes the claim either unfalsifiable or normatively loaded.

Structure your response in these sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. Conceptual Setup (about 150 words): Briefly explain the modal-profile framing and state the learning objective—that students should feel how fixing the conditions that make a modal claim testable simultaneously makes the claim either trivial or ethically problematic.

2. Materials and Roles (about 200 words): Specify group divisions, any handouts, and the roles students adopt (for example, “ascribers,” “institutional redesigners,” and “epistemic auditors”). The roles should generate conflict without requiring prior expertise.

3. Rules and Timeline (about 350 words): Provide step-by-step instructions for the full 90 minutes. The rules must force participants to make an initial modal ascription about a fictional but realistic national group (call it “Group K”) based on observed behavior in one institutional context; then an “institutional redesign” phase changes background conditions; then an “audit” phase requires them to either retract the ascription, restrict it to the original context, or defend its cross-contextual stability. Include a non-monetary payoff or reputation scheme (for example, public commitment tokens).

4. The Debrief (about 250 words): Map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the conceptual problem—the initial intuition that there is a “character,” the redesign that undermines it, the auditor’s dilemma, and the cost of each resolution. Do not invoke named philosophers unless essential; focus on the structural moves.

5. Predictable Breakdowns and Fixes (about 150 words): Identify what will likely go wrong the first time (for example, students conflate statistical frequency with modal default; auditors become overly adversarial; the fictional framing feels contrived) and state exactly how to correct it in the moment.

6. Honest Limits (about 150 words): State frankly which feature of real-world national-character discourse the simulation cannot reproduce and that you, as facilitator, must supply verbally—for instance, the historical weight of actual stereotypes or the lived experience of being the target of such ascriptions.

Write in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask me to search the web; the prompt should be self-contained. The tone should be formal and suitable for a doctoral student addressing a peer or mentor.
