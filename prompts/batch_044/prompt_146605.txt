I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about rational social planning and conceptual framework choice, and whether it lines up with anything like the Carnapian picture I've been circling—specifically the idea that framework selection is an external, pragmatic decision rather than an internal, truth-apt one, and that this should govern how institutions organize collective reasoning. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle that delivers them all, which ones it has to sacrifice, and what it predicts for cases I haven't listed.

Case 1: A federal agency mandates that every department must use an identical decision-theoretic framework—expected utility with a uniform risk-aversion parameter—for all regulatory choices. A department that wants to employ a non-aggregative rights-based veto for certain environmental risks is told this is "irrational" because it violates the unified calculus. Verdict: the mandate is wrong. Where practical purposes differ, framework pluralism should be tolerated rather than suppressed in the name of consistency.

Case 2: A city planning office is designing a transit system. One team uses a classical welfare-economic model; another uses a capabilities-based evaluation; they produce incompatible project rankings. Rather than forcing convergence, the office runs both analyses, makes the governing framework explicit for each decision domain, and subjects the domain-to-framework mapping to democratic oversight. Verdict: this is correct—perhaps exemplary.

Case 3: A nation-state institutes a single constructed logical language for all legal and scientific communication, banning ambiguous natural-language statutes, but explicitly preserves natural languages for poetry, religious practice, and private correspondence. The justification is purely pragmatic—reducing bureaucratic error—with no claim that the constructed language is metaphysically "truer." Verdict: this is permissible, maybe wise, provided the choice is openly pragmatic and the domains are cleanly separated.

Case 4: A planning committee refuses to approve a climate-adaptation strategy because philosophers have not settled whether intergenerational ethics should be represented in a discounted-utility or a rights-based framework. They insist that planning must halt until the "correct" ethical ontology is established once and for all. Verdict: this is a failure of institutional reasoning. Planning should proceed with an explicit, revisable framework choice rather than waiting for metaphysical closure.

Case 5: Two adjacent counties share a watershed but operate under incommensurable land-use ontologies—one treats wetlands as substitutable ecological services, the other as irreplaceable place-based entities. They successfully coordinate cleanup and flood management without merging their conceptual schemes, by specifying interface protocols rather than unified definitions. Verdict: cooperation without ontological unification is not only possible but preferable.

Case 6: A regime justifies suppressing democratic value-deliberation by claiming that value judgments are cognitively meaningless pseudo-statements, leaving only empirical-technical planning as legitimate state activity. Verdict: this is an authoritarian misuse of framework neutrality. Tolerance for conceptual frameworks does not license the elimination of the practical forums in which framework choices are made, contested, and revised.

The tension I feel is this: Cases 1 and 6 both resist the imposition of a single framework, but Case 3 seems to permit a universal administrative language, and Case 2 seems to require explicit framework selection rather than pure laissez-faire pluralism. So is my principle just "choose frameworks by practical purpose with democratic oversight"? Or is there a sharper formulation—something about separating internal framework questions from external pragmatic choices, applied specifically to political planning, with a constraint that framework selection itself must remain within a deliberative practice? And if so, does it force me to drop my verdict in Case 3 (that universal administrative language is okay) or in Case 6 (that suppressing deliberation is wrong)?

Also, please apply the resulting principle to the case I keep going back and forth on: a small, isolated community that adopts a single, rigid moral framework for all collective decisions—say, a strict utilitarian calculus—voluntarily and with unanimous consent, with an explicit opt-out mechanism for dissenters. Is this an admirable exercise in practical framework choice, or does it problematically foreclose the pluralism that my other verdicts seem to value?

Please state the principle (or competing candidates) precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, predict its verdict on the voluntary-utilitarian community, and be explicit about which of my six verdicts cannot be accommodated without ad hoc complication.
