I have been working through some puzzles in decision theory on my own—mostly from a secondhand textbook and long forum threads—after a friend mentioned the Allais paradox in passing. Last week my roommate and I tried to map out the classic choice pairs together over dinner: in the first pair, you choose between a guaranteed million dollars and a gamble offering five million with a small chance but also a risk of nothing; in the second pair, both options are risky, but the probabilities are scaled so that the relative structure looks parallel. I chose the safe option in the first pair and the risky option in the second, which apparently violates what is called the independence axiom. My roommate immediately said that makes people irrational; I said it might just mean the axiom demands more consistency than human judgment actually owes. We have been arguing about it since, and I would like you to adjudicate our exchange step by step, because neither of us is trained in this and we keep talking past each other.

Here is the back-and-forth exactly as it happened, with me reporting both sides neutrally. First, I argued that holding those preferences exposes you to a Dutch book: a clever bettor could construct a sequence of wagers that guarantees you lose money if you apply your choices consistently across differently framed versions of the same underlying gamble. Second, my roommate replied that Dutch-book arguments presuppose you are willing to enter repeated bets at precisely those odds, but a one-time life decision carries emotional weight and contextual meaning that abstract betting coherence should not override; rationality, she said, should track what you genuinely value, not bookkeeping consistency. Third, I raised the reduction principle for compound lotteries—that a rational agent should evaluate a multi-stage lottery by collapsing it to its final single-stage probabilities—so if the two pairs are really isomorphic representations, treating them differently is incoherent. She countered that invoking reduction already assumes the very independence principle under dispute, making the argument circular rather than decisive. Fourth, I noted that in experiments where people are shown the compound structure explicitly, many reverse their initial choices, which suggests the paradox is partly an artifact of how the problem is presented rather than a deep failure of reason. She responded that framing effects are precisely the kind of bias rationality ought to eliminate, so observing them does not rescue independence; it just confirms that people are systematically misled by presentation. Fifth, I asked whether allowing nonlinear probability weights—giving disproportionate psychological weight to near-certainty—could explain the pattern without declaring anyone irrational, perhaps preserving a modified form of coherence. She said that once you allow such weights you have abandoned expected utility theory altogether, which is permissible, but then you are no longer defending the independence axiom; you have replaced it with a different framework, and you should admit that openly. Please rate each of these five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, justify each rating briefly, and say who is more right overall. I also want to know whether our stalemate comes from answering different questions—me about formal coherence across representations, her about fidelity to actual human values—rather than from a single factual disagreement about rationality.
