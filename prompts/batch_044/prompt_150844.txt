I'm preparing a research paper for my philosophy seminar on the standard distinctions among positive, negative, and republican liberty, and I want to move beyond the textbook examples of slavery or direct state coercion. I am stuck on a conceptual problem that arises in modern labor relations: under at-will employment, an employer rarely exercises any actual interference with an employee's daily choices, yet retains a legally recognized capacity to terminate that employee arbitrarily and without cause. Republican theorists describe this structural condition as domination—an arbitrary power of interference—whereas frameworks of negative liberty seem to classify it as non-interference, since no actual constraint is presently exercised. Could you explain, with conceptual precision rather than historical survey, why republican liberty treats the mere presence of such arbitrary capacity as a distinct form of unfreedom, and whether this distinction genuinely captures something that negative liberty cannot? Specifically, I want to understand whether domination can exist without actual interference, and if so, whether republican liberty remains a genuinely independent category or collapses into negative liberty supplemented by risk and vulnerability.
