Consider an argument about gradual substrate replacement and the conditions under which personal identity survives a complete neural-to-silicon transition.

(1) If a conscious subject survives the replacement of any single neuron by a functionally isomorphic silicon analog—provided input-output relations are preserved and the subject reports no experienced interruption—then there is no non-arbitrary threshold at which replacement becomes fatal; identity should survive total substitution.

(2) Now suppose we perform this replacement exclusively during non-REM sleep, using interfaces that are phenomenologically transparent: the sleeping brain receives no internal signal that tissue has been altered, and upon waking the subject reports an unbroken first-person history.

(3) Repeating this nightly over several years eventually yields a system in which every neuron is silicon, yet there has never been a waking moment of discontinuity; call the waking entity in the original skull the Upload.

(4) Introduce a parallel protocol: each night, after extracting the biological tissue slated for replacement, we preserve those neurons in a nutrient bath arranged in the identical functional architecture, allowing them to continue processing in isolation.

(5) At the end of the process we possess two activated systems—one silicon-based in the skull, one biological in the vat—each with an unbroken causal-functional chain tracing back to the original subject, and each upon activation reporting the same autobiographical memory and self-reference.

(6) If survival through gradual replacement is guaranteed by the conjunction of (a) absence of experienced discontinuity and (b) preservation of functional architecture, then both systems satisfy the condition, so both are numerically identical to the original person.

(7) But identity is one-one: a single person cannot be numerically identical to two distinct, spatiotemporally separated entities capable of subsequent divergent experiences.

(8) Therefore the condition in (6) is insufficient for identity preservation; at least one chain fails to carry the original subject.

(9) Yet the two chains differ only in substrate location, not in internal functional history; rejecting the silicon chain while accepting the biological-vat chain requires substrate essentialism, which premise (1) was designed to reject.

(10) The remaining option is to deny that either chain survives—that gradual replacement, even without experienced interruption, always produces a new subject—which implies that ordinary biological aging, which continuously replaces cells, also fails to preserve identity across decades.

(11) Since (10) undermines our ordinary conviction that we survive sleep and maturation, the argument proves too much and must contain a hidden flaw.

Where exactly does it slip? Identify the specific step—whether an equivocation between functional and spatiotemporal continuity, a modal slide from "could survive" to "must survive both branches," or a quantifier shift in how (1) applies to parallel chains—explain why that step seemed plausible, and then price the alternatives: what does rejecting it cost for theories of personal identity, for the ethics of uploading, and for our belief in diachronic survival?
