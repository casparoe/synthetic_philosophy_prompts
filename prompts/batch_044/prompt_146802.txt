I just concluded a lengthy and somewhat heated discussion with a friend over whether President Eisenhower’s decision to federalize the Arkansas National Guard and deploy the 101st Airborne Division to Little Rock Central High School in September 1957 should be read as justice finally acquiring the force it had lacked, or as an admission that what we call justice is ultimately nothing more than the will of the stronger party. We kept returning to Pascal’s argument in the Pensées that justice without force is powerless, that force without justice is tyrannical, and that there is no naturally just order—only conventions that hold through enforcement and through what he describes as a mystical foundation of authority rather than through reason or nature alone. My friend insisted that Brown v. Board of Education was already morally correct before any soldier arrived; I countered that, on a strictly Pascalian reading, a judicial ruling that cannot secure compliance is not yet justice but only an unenforced claim, and that the arrival of federal bayonets revealed the Supreme Court’s authority to rest on presidential coercion rather than on any self-evident moral truth.

Please analyze the Little Rock crisis through Pascal’s framework on justice and force, but do not treat the deployment as a straightforward case of force backing justice. Instead, examine whether the episode illustrates his idea that all human justice is conventional and requires an enforced foundation of authority. Specifically: if Eisenhower had refused to act and Governor Faubus had permanently blocked the Nine, would school desegregation have been unjust in the Pascalian sense, or merely unenforced? How does the distinction between justice-as-convention and justice-as-natural-right map onto the roles of the Supreme Court, the state executive, and the federal government? Where does Pascal’s framework illuminate the crisis—particularly the risk that making justice strong risks making it tyrannical, as some Arkansans experienced the federal presence as occupation—and where does it fail, perhaps by reducing constitutional rights to mere power relations or by ignoring the moral agency of the students themselves? Finally, what does the episode suggest about Pascal’s broader view: does it confirm that justice is always posterior to force, or does it reveal a limit to that claim when an enforced convention aligns with a widely held moral conviction?
