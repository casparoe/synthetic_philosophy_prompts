Consider the philosophical debate over judging historical figures — Jefferson, Churchill, Columbus — through what has been called the man-of-his-time defence. Rather than rehearsing the familiar dialectic, I want to treat the problem as an agenda-setting exercise: there are at least four unresolved directions that could reshape both scholarship and public practice, but they are not equally worth pursuing with limited intellectual and institutional resources.

First, there is the conceptual task of specifying the epistemic threshold for moral accessibility: what evidence, social networks, or dissenting traditions must have been available to an agent in a particular historical setting for us to hold them accountable for not adopting a better view? Second, there is the asymmetry problem: does the accessibility framework apply symmetrically, so that we should praise figures for embracing unpopular moral advances that were technically within reach, or does praise operate on different criteria? Third, there is the institutional-design question: if we conclude that certain figures fail the accessibility test and therefore cannot be excused, what follows for commemorative practices — should it alter monument design, curricular framing, or museum labeling, and by what normative mechanism? Fourth, there is the boundary-condition problem: can the framework be extended to non-elite actors — enslaved people, colonized populations, women excluded from literacy — without collapsing into either condescension or an impossibly high standard?

Given these four lines, which one or two should philosophers, historians, and policy-makers treat as upstream priorities — the ones whose resolution would unlock or render moot the others? Which should be deprioritized because they are either intractable with current methods or largely ornamental once the upstream work is done? Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: dependency relations among the questions, tractability given present archival and philosophical tools, and the practical magnitude of getting the answer wrong. And please say explicitly how confident you are in this agenda, and single out the specific claim — conceptual, empirical, or normative — about which you are least certain.
