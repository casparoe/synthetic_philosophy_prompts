As a retired physicist now studying philosophy of history, I have grown skeptical of applying biological life-cycle metaphors to civilizations. Please state your credence—on a scale from 0 to 1—that the conventional “collapse” framing is conceptually mistaken for the three cases below, and that each is better understood as an adaptive restructuring rather than a systemic failure. Decompose your reasoning into: (a) the probability that the Late Bronze Age transition in the Eastern Mediterranean (c. 1200 BCE) represents network decentralization and institutional reconfiguration rather than civilizational collapse; (b) the probability that the Classic Maya decline (c. 800–900 CE) was chiefly political fragmentation with sustained cultural and demographic continuity; (c) the probability that Easter Island’s ecological crisis involved social restructuring and adaptive contraction rather than catastrophic governance failure; (d) the probability that the “collapse” concept itself commits what might be called an ontogenetic fallacy—treating societies as organisms with discrete life-cycles—thereby systematically biasing interpretation; and (e) how these terms combine, with one line of justification and a rough weight for each. Then identify which component you are least confident in, and specify what archaeological pattern or theoretical argument would most shift your overall number.
