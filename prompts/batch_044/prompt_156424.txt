I'm a high school student working through philosophy of science and decision theory independently, and I've constructed an argument against accuracy-first epistemology that seems to prove far too much. I need help identifying exactly where it fails.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) Accuracy-first epistemology holds that the sole epistemic virtue is proximity to truth, typically measured by a proper scoring rule such as the Brier score.

(2) For any proposition p about which my evidence is genuinely ambiguous, there exists some precise credence c (either closer to 1 or closer to 0) that yields a strictly better Brier score ex post than the suspended judgment of 0.5.

(3) If rationality requires maximizing expected accuracy, then for each ambiguous p I ought to adopt whichever precise extreme my evidence marginally favors, however slight that margin.

(4) Because evidence is ambiguous for nearly every empirical proposition, this reasoning applies globally.

(5) Therefore accuracy-first epistemology requires near-total dogmatism: a rational agent should hold extreme or near-extreme credences almost everywhere.

(6) Near-total dogmatism is manifestly irrational.

(7) Hence accuracy-first epistemology is self-defeating.

Which step should I reject? I am particularly reluctant to abandon (1) or (6). Does the error lie in sliding from ex post accuracy to ex ante rationality in step (3), or is there a hidden equivocation in how "expected accuracy" applies when one does not yet know the truth value? I have also been wondering whether injecting imprecise probabilities—representing ambiguity by credal sets rather than point values—blocks the argument, or whether it merely relocates the problem to the choice of aggregation rule (for instance, worst-case versus average Brier score evaluated over the set). What do the standard positions—Joyce's non-pragmatic probabilism, Pettigrew's accuracy-first framework, and the imprecise-probability critics—each have to pay to reject the offending premise?
