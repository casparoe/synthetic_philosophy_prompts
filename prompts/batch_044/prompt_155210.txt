I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I've been asked to give the opening remarks—roughly six minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop on collective cognition. The audience is mixed: entomologists, philosophers of biology, AI researchers, and graduate students, not all of whom work on consciousness. I want the speech to take up Hofstadter's dialogue about Aunt Hillary, the ant colony that debates an anteater over whether she, rather than her individual ants, might be the locus of thought. But I want to avoid both the usual reductionist dismissal and the glib holistic endorsement. Instead, the remarks should be structured around a framing I'm developing—call it fugal emergence: the idea that collective cognition operates not like a single orchestra reading one score, but like a musical fugue in which each ant enters at its own temporal scale and functional key, so that attributing any mental state to the colony requires mapping inter-level polyphony rather than simple aggregation. This should do real structural work: it should determine what I can and cannot claim—for example, I must not say the colony is merely the sum of its parts, nor that it possesses one unified, monophonic consciousness analogous to a human mind; rather, I should argue that the very question "can an ant colony have a mind?" is misframed unless we reconceive mental unity itself as temporal and functional interweaving. The speech should be intellectually serious but accessible to non-philosophers, should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should not ask the audience to take anything on authority. Please write the remarks.
