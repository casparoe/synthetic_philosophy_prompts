I live in a co-op of eight people and we tried to solve fights by having a "reaction round" every Sunday. The rule is: if you got mad at someone during the week, you write one sentence about what they did and read it out loud yourself, but then the person you blamed has to say "I hear you" and nothing else. We added this after a huge blowup about dishes, and mostly it works, but last week something strange happened. Maya was annoyed that Tom left the compost bin open, she read her sentence, Tom said "I hear you," and then everyone went back to eating brunch. But later I noticed Maya was still really cold to Tom, way more than before the round. I asked her about it and she said that saying it out loud in front of everyone made her feel like she was perfoming her anger instead of actually feeling it, and now she's kind of stuck being the "angry one" in the group. Tom, meanwhile, said he felt relieved because the ritual made it feel like the anger belonged to the group, not to him personally.

I'm wondering if we should change the format. One idea is to have people write the sentence but let a netural roommate read it, so the angry person doesn't have to perform it. Another idea is to drop the public part entirely and just do it one-on-one. But I'm not sure what's actually wrong with the current setup—maybe the problem is that we're treating resentment like a debt that gets paid off with a sentence and a response? I can't tell if Maya's lingering coldness means the ritual failed to do its job, or if it did exactly what it was supposed to do and she's just wrong to still be mad. Is there a philosophical way to think about whether blame needs to be direct and personal to count, or whether outsourcing it to a group ritual makes it more fair? Also, does Tom's feeling of relief mean he was genuinely held responsible, or does it mean the ritual let him off the hook?
