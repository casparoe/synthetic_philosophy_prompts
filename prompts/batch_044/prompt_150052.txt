I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I'm trying to referee a disagreement that broke out during our team's offsite. Two colleagues—Jordan and Riley—got into a sustained argument about whether human beings are naturally cooperative or naturally self-interested. They kept invoking two thinkers I barely know, Mencius and Xunzi, and applying them to how people behave in open-source maintenance.

Jordan opened with Mencius: people are born with innate moral sprouts—compassion, shame, courtesy, and discernment of right and wrong. Jordan pointed out that strangers still submit bug fixes, write documentation, and review pull requests for projects they don't own and will never monetize. That behavior, Jordan said, is the sprouts growing on their own. Jordan argued that Xunzi's claim that human nature is bad and must be transformed by ritual and education is just describing institutional watering: the education doesn't manufacture morality, it cultivates potential that was already present.

Riley countered with Xunzi: those sprouts are weak and unreliable. Riley noted that anonymous, unmoderated repositories quickly accumulate spam, malicious dependencies, plagiarism, and abandoned issues—behavior that only gets contained once explicit governance is added: codes of conduct, peer-review gates, maintainer authority, reputation metrics, and license enforcement. Riley said Mencius can't explain why cooperation collapses when oversight is stripped away; Xunzi predicts exactly that collapse, because without external structure people pursue private gain. Riley added that assuming innate goodness risks complacency—if we believe people are naturally decent, we'll under-invest in the structural safeguards that actually keep systems healthy.

Jordan replied that Riley was cherry-picking failure modes. Jordan observed that even in fully anonymous, ungoverned forums, people still answer questions, share debugging knowledge, and warn others about vulnerabilities without any institutional reward or punishment. Jordan also argued that Xunzi's transformation need not be top-down ritual; informal peer feedback and social learning can reshape behavior, which suggests the environment is nurturing something internal rather than imposing morality from scratch.

Riley came back that Jordan was conflating occasional altruism with stable cooperation. Riley said large-scale open-source works precisely because of structured incentives—visibility, credit, dependency tracking—not because of ungoverned kindness. Strip away the metrics and gates, Riley claimed, and the tragedy-of-the-commons degradation follows quickly. The sprouts, if they exist, aren't strong enough to sustain complex collaboration without scaffolding.

Jordan's final move was that Riley had set up a false dichotomy. Jordan said Xunzi's framework treats morality as entirely external, but in practice people internalize norms—maintainers enforce rules because they genuinely care about code quality, not just because they're incentivized to. So external structure works only because it connects to an internal sense of right and wrong, which is Mencius's core point: the internal and external are interdependent, not opposed.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate Jordan's opening claim, Riley's counter, Jordan's reply, Riley's reply, and Jordan's final move—each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right—and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall about the human-nature question as it applies here, and identify what important point both parties are missing (for instance, about how Mencius and Xunzi actually differ on the role of environment versus innate potential, or about whether one view is genuinely incompatible with structured governance).

Please write the answer in American English. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a brief statement of the overall winner and the core reason.
