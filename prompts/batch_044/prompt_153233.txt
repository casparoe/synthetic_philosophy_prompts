I am trying to determine whether my moral judgments align with generalism or particularism by testing them against concrete cases, and I want to know whether a single principle can unify them. Here are my verdicts. First, when a physician withholds a terminal diagnosis to prevent an imminent suicide, I judge the deception permissible; I take the patient’s autonomy to constitute a genuine reason against deception, but in this context it is silenced—not merely outweighed—by the immediate risk of self-harm. Second, when a job applicant lies about having been fired, I judge it impermissible; here I think autonomy is not silenced at all—the employer’s entitlement to truthful information remains fully active—so the wrongness lies in violating an unsilenced reason. Third, when a friend asks for my opinion of their clearly unworkable business plan and I lie to spare their feelings, I also judge it wrong, yet autonomy is barely engaged; instead, I think friendship itself generates a reason for honesty that is present and decisive, not silenced but dominant. Fourth, in allocating one scarce vaccine, I think we should favor the younger patient if they will live substantially longer, since lifespan is a reason; but if the older patient personally developed the vaccine, I think we should favor them instead, and I do not regard lifespan as present-but-defeated—I regard it as silenced by a contribution-based consideration that operates differently. Fifth, when a dying patient asks whether their estranged child called and I lie to comfort them, I judge this wrong, and I think compassion is genuinely present but fails to silence the reason against deception, whereas in the physician case it succeeds. Is there a coherent generalist principle—perhaps one that distinguishes invariant moral reasons from context-sensitive conditions of silencing—that delivers all five verdicts? Or must I abandon one? Please state the principle with sufficient precision that each clause is tied to the specific case motivating it, predict its verdict on an unlisted case—specifically, refusing to return a borrowed rare book because the lender has recently become cruel—and identify honestly which of my five judgments the principle cannot accommodate.
