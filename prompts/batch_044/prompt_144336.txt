I'm currently taking a seminar on philosophy of death and meaning, and our creative assignment asks for a short story illustrating Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction is harmful not only because of the deaths it entails, but because it undermines the present significance of long-term projects that depend on the expectation of future human generations—his idea of an afterlife of humanity continuing beyond any individual life. Rather than a generic apocalypse narrative, please center the story on an invented practice called sequential cartography: each generation adds one precise layer to a single, ever-expanding map of an imaginary continent, with the tradition holding that the map's value lies specifically in its never-completed, multi-generational form. The protagonist should be the tradition's final practitioner, who has just received credible evidence that human extinction is imminent within her own lifetime. Focus on how this news does not merely cancel future layers, but retroactively destabilizes the meaning of the layer she is now drawing—showing, through her internal reasoning and the physical details of the map, why the loss of an indefinite future audience or continuation can hollow out present purpose rather than leaving it intact.
