I was arguing with a friend about Bradley's regress and why propositions need some kind of internal unity — he kept falling back on the old glue analogy, where relations are like adhesive that sticks relata together, but then you need more adhesive to stick the adhesive, and you're stuck in an infinite regress. He said this shows relations can't be real, so propositions have to be unified by something like a Fregean concept rather than an external relation. Lately I've been wondering if the analogy is doing all the heavy lifting, especially since modern materials science has these self-curing epoxies and cross-linking polymers that bind without a separate binder — the molecule literally ties itself into it's substrate. So I'm trying to audit the analogy properly: what plays the role of the bricks, the glue layer, the curing agent, the substrate? Where does the mapping actually hold for logical relations versus physical adhesion? And where does it break — do relations have the same kind of separability, spatial location, or need for a substrate that glue does? If we take the "self-curing" version seriously, where the relation binds it's relata without needing a further relation, does that actually solve Bradley's problem or just rename the unity we're trying to explain? I want to know whether the conclusion — that propositions need non-relational unity — survives once all the disanalogies are on the table, or if the glue picture was smuggling in the answer all along. Also, is there a better analogie that doesn't import all this physical baggage?
