In a philosophy of mind reading group, a participant keeps returning to a compressed claim: Gödel proves that in any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic, there is a true statement the system cannot prove—therefore minds are not algorithms and science can never be complete. She uses it both to dismiss mechanistic theories of cognition and to reject the aspiration for a final physical theory. Rather than simply object, I want to bring a careful disambiguation to our next meeting. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan might be expressing? For each reading, say whether it is a direct mathematical consequence, a defensible analogical extension, or a non sequitur that borrows authority from logic. Specifically, distinguish: (i) the mathematical result itself—what Gödel actually established about formal axiomatizations of arithmetic, and whether the unprovable statement is model-relative or absolute within a fixed interpretation; (ii) the argument about minds—whether it assumes cognition is a single consistent formal system, whether it confuses syntactic incompleteness with epistemic or computational limitation, and whether it addresses the distinction between a particular algorithm and the class of all computable functions; (iii) the argument about scientific completeness—whether physical theories are formalized in the requisite arithmetic-rich way, and whether Gödelian incompleteness corresponds to empirical underdetermination, to unprovable physical laws, or to something else entirely. Where scholars genuinely disagree—for instance, on whether Gödel's own philosophical writings about mind and mechanism represent a legitimate inference or a category mistake—separate the settled logical facts from the contested philosophical interpretations, and do not present any one extension as the consensus among logicians or philosophers of science.
