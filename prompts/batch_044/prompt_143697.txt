Two museum curators are arguing whether an AI system that generates real-time ambient soundscapes tailored to each listener's live biometric stress markers can count as genuine art on Tolstoy's account. (1) Curator A says it can: the system transmits an emotional state—calm, melancholy, resolve—without the vanity, fashion, or craving for acclaim that Tolstoy condemns, and it infects the listener with feeling immediately, without the distortion of professional technique. (2) Curator B replies that Tolstoy requires true art to unite human beings in universal brotherhood, whereas biometric personalization makes every experience private and incommensurable, isolating listeners rather than binding them into a shared life. (3) A counters that thousands of users share the same generative model and emotional palette, so brotherhood is preserved at scale; the medium remains collective even when the acoustic instance varies. (4) B responds that Tolstoy demands the artist's conscious moral purpose—to foster union through sincere feeling; an algorithm has no moral intent, so its effect is mere sensory manipulation, not art. Adjudicate move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then identify what both curators overlook about the parts of Tolstoy's framework that resist translation into algorithmic production. Please respond in three sections: first, "Adjudication of the four moves" (about 250 words), rating moves 1 through 4; second, "The residue Tolstoy leaves behind" (about 150 words), on what neither curator captures; third, "Verdict" (about 50 words), stating which curator is closer to Tolstoy's position and why.
