I take the view that there is no geometric essence that makes every lowercase “a” an “a”; instead, recognition works through analogical mapping inside a font-specific style space, where each form is defined relationally against the other members of its alphabet rather than by invariant local features. This is closer to Hofstadter’s gridfont idea than to feature-list theories: an “a” in one family may share almost no contour with an “a” in another, yet both are recognized because they occupy the same structural role in their respective relational networks. What would have to be true for this relational-style account to be wrong? I want an honest exposure map: which results in perceptual psychology or computer vision would be fatal—say, evidence that human readers deploy context-invariant feature detectors that work identically across radically different typefaces—versus merely awkward, such as the success of purely feature-based OCR? Would a defender have to concede that cross-script literacy (recognizing Latin “a” alongside Cyrillic “а” that share shape but not relational role) undermines the analogy? And is the view protected by the retreat that any apparently invariant feature is just a compressed relational summary, in which case has it become unfalsifiable in a damaging way? Rank the defeaters from fatal to cosmetic, say what the position’s own defenders should concede, and indicate whether the account predicts anything that a feature-based rival genuinely does not.
