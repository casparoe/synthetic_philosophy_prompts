I'm drafting the take-home final for a graduate seminar on philosophy of value and longtermism. I want one essay question about Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the claim that much of the value of our present activities depends on our expectation that human life will continue after we die, so that human extinction would be bad in a way that exceeds the sum of individual deaths. I need the question to go past summary. It should require students to (i) reconstruct the conjecture precisely enough to separate it from ordinary consequentialist concern for future generations; (ii) apply it to an injected variant: humanity survives biological extinction, but only as a small, causally isolated population of digital minds that share no language, culture, or projects with present people; and (iii) evaluate whether this "thin survival" satisfies the conjecture, with explicit attention to whether biological continuity, cultural transmission, or mere causal continuation is doing the work.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 points that separates an A from a B. The scheme should allocate specific points to: (1) accurate reconstruction of the conjecture and its non-instrumental, structural role; (2) a clean distinction from consequentialist or contractualist reasoning about descendants; (3) analysis of the thin-survival case across at least two dimensions of continuity; (4) a defended verdict; and (5) identification of an internal tension, specifically the asymmetry with pre-existence — why the absence of prior generations does not seem to undermine the value of past projects in the same way extinction undermines ours. Note that a B answer typically covers (1) and (2) adequately but treats (3) as a binary yes/no, misses the asymmetry in (5), or collapses the conjecture into generic future-oriented altruism. List common errors that should cost marks — for instance, conflating the conjecture with Parfit's repugnant conclusion, treating survival as purely a matter of headcount, or ignoring the pre-existence problem altogether — and indicate the point deduction for each.
