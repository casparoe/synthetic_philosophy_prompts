I'm working through a conceptual tension in Spinoza's political philosophy that sits at the intersection of his metaphysics of power and his institutional design. In his late political treatise, Spinoza describes democracy as the form of imperium that comes closest to absolute sovereignty, because the collective body retains the right to command rather than alienating it entirely to a monarch or aristocratic council. Yet he simultaneously restricts full citizenship to a specific subset—excluding women, servants, foreigners, and those without independent means—while maintaining that the democratic council must be composed of citizens capable of rational deliberation about the common good.

Rather than treating these exclusions as merely historical prejudices that modern readers should discard, please argue for a reconstructed Spinozist position that takes them as structurally significant. Specifically, defend the view that Spinoza's democracy should be understood through a distinction between extensive and intensive sovereignty: extensive sovereignty belongs to the entire multitude—including those excluded from formal citizenship—as the living substrate that generates the state's potentia, whereas intensive sovereignty belongs only to the rational council that directs this collective power toward genuinely common ends. On this reading, the exclusions are not arbitrary limits on democratic participation but conditions for preserving the rational coherence of deliberation without dissolving the underlying collective capacity that makes the state possible in the first place.

In developing this argument, address three points. First, explain how Spinoza's notion of potentia multitudinis differs from modern liberal conceptions of popular sovereignty, particularly regarding whether sovereign power is constituted by procedural representation or by an actual collective capacity to act in concert. Second, show why the council's restricted membership does not contradict the claim that democracy is the most natural form of state, given that the multitude's power remains the foundational substrate even when it is not directly expressed in legislative deliberation. Third, draw out the implications for evaluating modern democratic pathologies: specifically, argue whether a polity that maximizes inclusive voting and formal representation but permits factional or irrational deliberation has, on genuinely Spinozist grounds, weakened rather than strengthened its democratic character by diluting intensive sovereignty while leaving extensive power intact but misdirected.
