Williams’s integrity objection targets the way utilitarianism requires agents to regard their own deepest commitments and relationships as having merely instrumental value in the service of aggregate welfare. The canonical illustration is the “one thought too many” case, in which a husband can save either his wife or an unrelated stranger from drowning. If the husband saves his wife only after concluding that moral rules permit him to prefer her so long as the cost to overall utility is not excessive, then he has introduced one thought too many into his reasoning. The appropriate motivation is direct and non-calculative: he saves her simply because she is his wife and his life is bound up with her.

This objection is closely connected to the demandingness problem, since both highlight the burden that impartial morality places on private life. Where Williams breaks new ground is in showing that the real damage is not quantitative—how much is asked—but qualitative: the agent is alienated from the ground projects that constitute his identity. Integrity, on this view, is the preservation of those identity-constituting attachments against the encroachment of moral arithmetic.

Nevertheless, Williams is not claiming that intimate relationships generate special moral duties that can be weighed against impartial ones. Rather, he holds that in domains governed by deep personal attachment, the appropriate reasons are non-moralized. The husband does not apply a competing principle; he acts out of love. Thus, the integrity objection demonstrates that utilitarianism fails because it cannot recognize the non-instrumental value of ground projects without translating them into weights within an aggregate sum.
