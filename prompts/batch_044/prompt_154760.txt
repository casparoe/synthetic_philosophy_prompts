In a recent forum thread on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, a commenter kept falling back on the compressed line that phenomenal consciousness is "just an illusion — essentially the refrigerator-light problem applied to introspection." He used it simultaneously to undermine first-person authority (if consciousness only seems continuously present because we only check for it under access conditions, then reports of constant phenomenal presence are artifacts) and to dissolve the hard problem (if there are no phenomenal properties, physics leaves nothing out). I think that one phrase is smuggling several distinct theses, and I want them separated before I reply.

Can you lay out, as an explicit menu, the precise claims that could be packed into that slogan? For each reading, specify: (a) whether it eliminates phenomenal properties, misrepresents them, or targets only our judgments about them; (b) whether it needs a phenomenal-concept strategy to explain why we think we have them; (c) whether it genuinely dissolves the hard problem or merely relocates it into an error theory about introspection; (d) whether it actually undermines first-person authority about current pain or pleasure, and if so on what grounds; (e) whether the reading is trivially true (all observation involves access conditions), substantively defensible, or false; and (f) whether the refrigerator-light analogy actually fits that reading or is just borrowing rhetorical force.

In particular, I want to know if there is a coherent intermediate position — one that grants there is something it is like for me now, but denies that this "something" has the intrinsic, non-physical, continuously present properties that generate the hard problem — and whether that counts as illusionism or collapses into representationalism. Also, if illusionism is correct in any form, what happens to the intuition that I cannot be wrong about whether I am in pain right now? Must every plausible illusionist treat that intuition as a false belief, or is there a version that treats it as a true belief about a misrepresented physical state?

Please present the readings as a list I can quote back, and for each say whether it actually earns the commenter both of his conclusions — dismissing first-person reports and dissolving the hard problem — or whether it achieves only one, or neither, at the cost of some other commitment he hasn't acknowledged.
