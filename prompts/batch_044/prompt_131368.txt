Consider the institutional pairing found in Germany and the Nordic countries, where university tuition is fully tax-funded and charged directly to no student, yet admission to high-demand programs—medicine, law, psychology—is tightly capped and allocated almost entirely by prior academic performance metrics (secondary-school leaving grades, GPA equivalents, standardized thresholds). Contrast this with the US and UK model, where tuition is high and financed mainly through individual debt obligations that typically persist regardless of post-graduation income, but where admission is decentralized and technically open to anyone able to secure credit or institutional aid, with price and borrowing capacity serving as the de facto rationing mechanism.

Both arrangements are routinely defended as promoting equality of opportunity, yet they filter access through radically different gates: accumulated prior merit in one case, willingness and ability to assume long-term private debt in the other. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory—or theories—that makes each pairing coherent, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well.

Specifically: What view of educational justice does the free-but-merit-rationed system presuppose? Must it hold that prior academic achievement is a legitimate basis for distributing scarce life-chances, and if so, does it need a particular account of desert that can survive the fact that such achievement tracks family resources, school quality, and social background? Or can the practice be justified on purely efficiency or social-investment grounds without any commitment to moral desert? Conversely, what does the debt-funded, open-access model presuppose about personal responsibility, risk-bearing, and the status of education? Does it require the view that an eighteen-year-old can meaningfully consent to decades of financial obligation, or that higher education is primarily a private good whose benefits ought to be captured by the beneficiary?

Then examine whether the two systems, despite their surface opposition, share deeper commitments: for instance, that universities are fundamentally sorting institutions whose job is to credential pre-existing talent rather than to transform it; that intergenerational fairness permits one cohort to fund another through taxation or to burden it through debt in specifically asymmetric ways; or that equality of opportunity is satisfied so long as the filter—grades or credit—is formally available to all. Identify where each practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories of fairness, autonomy, or social welfare—and where it strictly rules out alternatives. Finally, describe what concrete institutional features would have to change if we replaced the implicit theory with a different one: for example, if access were allocated by lottery, by demonstrated social need, or by a pure social-investment calculus that ignored both prior grades and private debt capacity altogether.
