I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of logic working on epistemic representations of bounded rationality, and I'm struggling to articulate the precise nature of the problem of logical omniscience for my dissertation prospectus. As I understand it, in standard Kripke-style epistemic logic—particularly in normal modal systems—the knowledge operator is closed under logical consequence: if Kφ and K(φ → ψ) hold, then Kψ must hold. Critics treat this as psychologically unrealistic because actual agents fail to recognize remote logical consequences. Recently, however, I've been examining resource-sensitive frameworks that restrict derivational depth or impose working-memory constraints on inference, and it strikes me that these models don't actually eliminate logical omniscience; they merely relocate it inside a bounded derivation tree. Within the agent's permitted resource envelope, closure still holds instantaneously—there's no procedural gap between knowing the premises and knowing the conclusion, only a boundary on how far the tree extends. So I'm wondering whether the canonical "problem" of logical omniscience is mischaracterized as a psychological defect. Might it instead be a formal artifact of treating knowledge as a set of worlds rather than as a computational process? In other words, is the real issue not that agents "know too much," but that our semantics conflate semantic entailment with cognitive accessibility, and resource-bounded models only mask this by truncating the entailment relation rather than restructuring it? I'd value any clarification on where this line of reasoning collapses, particularly regarding whether neighborhood semantics or awareness structures avoid this truncation problem rather than merely displacing it.
