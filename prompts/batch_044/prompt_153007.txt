Design a 75-minute seminar activity for ten graduate students in philosophy of science that forces them to experience the tension in updateless decision theory—specifically, why an agent with identical copies across branches might rationally precommit to a policy and then refuse to update on her local observation, even when Bayes’ rule seems to demand it. Do not begin with a lecture on the view; arrange the rules, roles, and payoffs so the group runs into the problem before anyone names it.

Frame the exercise as a “Binding-Policy Game” across three parallel rooms. Before any information is revealed, each student secretly selects a strategy card (Cooperate or Defect) that binds all of her “copies” in every room. After cards are locked, each student is randomly assigned to exactly one room and shown only that room’s outcome, which depends on a hidden moderator: whether the room’s “epistemic culture” weights observed frequencies or prior commitments. The student must then choose whether to revise her strategy for future rounds based solely on her local observation, or to maintain the original binding policy across all copies—including those she never observes. Structure the payoff matrix so that local Bayesian updating maximizes the individual room’s score, but maintaining the global precommitment maximizes total welfare summed across all copies. Use explicit roles—Copy-Agents (the students), a Moderator who controls information flow between rooms, and an Auditor who tracks cross-room consistency—and attach small real stakes (point totals that convert to a shared snack budget).

In the debrief, map specific moments onto UDT concepts: the instant a Copy-Agent realizes her local update would harm her unobserved twin corresponds to the refusal to condition on indexical evidence; the Moderator’s refusal to reveal cross-room totals mirrors the agent’s lack of access to other branches. Be explicit about the predictable failure mode—students will likely conflate the refusal to update with mere stubbornness or group loyalty—and instruct the facilitator to redirect by asking what evidence, exactly, the local observation provides about the other rooms. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the formal derivation of expected utility across copies, and the precise distinction between evidential and causal decision theory that underwrites the paradox; the facilitator must supply those verbally.

Prescribe the response in this order:
1. Setup and rules (approx. 250 words): setting, roles, timeline, payoff structure, information constraints.
2. The activity in play (approx. 200 words): round-by-round flow, decision points, and how the hidden moderator operates.
3. Debrief and mapping (approx. 250 words): linking game moments to UDT, handling the predicted failure mode, and the feature the activity cannot reproduce.
4. Honest limitations (approx. 100 words): what will likely go wrong in execution and what the facilitator must add.

Keep the prompt focused; do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
