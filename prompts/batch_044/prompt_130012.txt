Over the past several months I have been weighing whether to accept a permanent position abroad, knowing that my father is entering a period of declining health and that my regular presence has become woven into the structure of his daily life. The dilemma is not only practical; it forces me to ask whether I have a reason to stay that is genuinely my own, or whether any such reason would be something imposed upon me from the outside regardless of what I want. Recently, at a dinner unrelated to my situation, two friends fell into an extended argument about a hypothetical case very near to mine, and I have been unable to stop replaying their exchange. I am asking you to adjudicate it point by point, not out of sympathy for my circumstances but because I want clarity for my own thinking.

The case involved a man named Leo, who for the past decade has lived near his aging father and contributed informally to his care, though never through any explicit promise or agreement. Leo has now received an opportunity to relocate permanently for work he finds deeply meaningful. He feels no desire to remain; his strongest practical motivation points away. His father is not demanding that he stay, but he is genuinely dependent on Leo’s presence in ways that exceed practical help—Leo’s visits organize his father’s sense of time and social connection.

Friend One began by arguing that if reasons must be internal—tied to Leo’s actual desires or to projects that serve his existing motivations—then Leo has no reason to stay. His career desire is robust and internal, while staying serves no desire he currently possesses. Friend Two responded by granting the internalist premise but distinguishing between role-based obligations, which do depend on accepting a social script, and what she called relation-constitutive reasons, which arise because Leo’s practical identity has been formed through years of specific interdependence with this particular father. She claimed that Leo’s agency is not fully separable from that relation in the way Friend One assumes, and that Leo’s second-order preference not to become someone who abandons dependency can itself generate first-order reasons without collapsing into mere emotional manipulation. Friend One then countered that this is just bootstrapping—using a manufactured preference to justify itself—and that guilt, which Leo might feel if he left, is a psychological cost rather than a rational justification; treating emotional discomfort as normative force confuses affect with reason. Friend Two answered by introducing the idea of a moral remainder: even if Leo’s father explicitly released him from any obligation, there would remain a rational loss, a practical damage to the fabric of care that is not reducible to guilt. She added that promises show why waivability does not prove internality, since promises can be waived yet remain the standard example of reasons that do not depend on current desire; therefore the fact that Leo could be released only shows the obligation was conditional, not that it was never real.
