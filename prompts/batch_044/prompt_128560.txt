I am preparing an extended essay for my A-level politics coursework on the boundary problem in democratic theory — specifically, who rightfully belongs to the demos. To move beyond purely abstract discussion, I want to examine a hypothetical case: the proposed Thames-Basin Assembly, a democratic body with binding authority over water allocation, flood defence, and long-term ecological management across a river system that crosses national borders and affects populations who are not currently resident within its administrative zone. The Assembly must settle its membership rules. One proposal limits voting rights to present adult residents within the zone. Another extends membership to all living persons whose vital interests are causally affected by upstream decisions, regardless of nationality or location. A third allows future generations to be represented via appointed guardians. A fourth permits designated advocates to represent non-human riparian ecosystems. Weigh the principal arguments for and against these principles of inclusion — particularly the all-affected-interests principle, the all-subjected principle, and a stakeholder principle grounded in sustained territorial presence — and address the specific tension that arises when a non-resident downstream community suffers severe impacts whilst resident voters face only minor, diffuse ones. Then reach and defend a clear conclusion about which principle, or which combination, ought to determine membership in this Assembly. Finally, explain whether your verdict would change if the affected parties included not only distant human communities but also predictive modelling systems commissioned to represent the preferences of unborn generations. The essay should be rigorously argued, should avoid appeals to authority or citation, and must defend a specific position rather than ending indecisively.
