I am preparing a philosophical profile for a graduate seminar on consciousness and modal epistemology, and I want to check whether the cluster of views I have adopted over the past year is genuinely consistent before I present it. The position I have arrived at combines standard commitments in the philosophy of mind with one somewhat idiosyncratic thesis I developed in conversation with colleagues. Rather than a lecture on each view, I need an audit: please examine the set below, identify which pairs are in genuine tension versus merely apparent tension, rank the conflicts by severity, specify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency for each, and finally indicate which single change to the set would eliminate the greatest number of conflicts. Please be explicit about where my reasoning relies on unstated premises that the other commitments do not supply.

Here is my profile. (a) I am a physicalist about all non-phenomenal mental states: cognition, intentionality, and functional organization are entirely physical or functional in nature. (b) I take the conceivability of full philosophical zombies—beings physically identical to conscious humans but lacking any phenomenal experience whatsoever—to provide strong though defeasible evidence for their metaphysical possibility, and therefore to pose a serious challenge to physicalism about consciousness specifically. (c) I hold that phenomenal concepts are cognitively isolated in a distinctive manner: they involve a direct mode of acquaintance that cannot be fully translated into physical or functional descriptions, which explains why the explanatory gap persists despite our complete physical knowledge. (d) I reject panpsychism and maintain that there is nothing it is like to be a simple thermostat, a rock, or a basic particle—phenomenal consciousness requires a specific organizational threshold that simple systems do not meet. (e) I also defend a niche claim: that affective partial zombies—beings physically and functionally identical to us but lacking only the phenomenal character of affective states such as pain, pleasure, and emotional valence, while retaining full sensory phenomenal consciousness—are not merely conceivable but metaphysically possible in nearby worlds, because the neural substrates underlying affective and sensory phenomenal properties are dissociable. (f) Finally, I believe that if such affective partial zombies are genuinely possible, then the standard conceivability argument against physicalism is undermined: the physicalist can explain full zombie conceivability as a failure to imagine the integrated physical substrate correctly, but this explanation collapses for partial zombies where only one substrate is missing, which suggests that full zombie conceivability is not a reliable guide to possibility after all.

Please work through these commitments systematically. I am especially concerned with the interaction between (e) and (f) with (a) and (b): does accepting the possibility of affective partial zombies force me to abandon either my physicalism about non-phenomenal states or my reliance on zombie conceivability? I also want to know whether (c) and (d) can stand together given that my rejection of phenomenal properties in simple systems seems to require a threshold principle that my account of phenomenal concept isolation does not obviously provide. Rank all tensions by severity, indicate the least costly revision for each, and conclude with the single commitment whose removal would most cleanly stabilize the rest of the package.
