I am preparing a single seventy-five minute seminar for advanced undergraduates on Karl Jaspers’s fourfold taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—and on whether a nation can bear guilt across generations in any sense that is not merely metaphorical. Rather than dispersing the session across familiar historical illustrations, I want it organized around one sustained carrying example, and I want to introduce a conceptual stress point that standard textbook treatments usually suppress: the problem of democratic authorization, specifically whether political guilt can attach to citizens who never consented to the state’s actions and may have actively opposed them, and whether that attachment therefore covertly depends on a theory of tacit consent that Jaspers never articulates.

Plan the explanation as a teaching sequence rather than as the explanation itself. First, lay out the precise dependency order in which the four categories must be introduced—what must be stabilized about one before the next can be introduced without collapse, and why criminal guilt must be secured first as an individual-attributive baseline. Second, specify the single concrete scenario that should carry the entire seminar: describe what institutional and temporal features it must possess (e.g., a democratic polity whose foundational charter was enacted through systemic exclusion, now confronting demands for collective acknowledgment generations later) and, just as importantly, what it must lack (e.g., any living perpetrator, any straightforward legal liability, any homogeneous citizenry) so that the four categories are forced apart rather than merged. Third, map the predictable misconceptions—particularly that political guilt is only distributed criminal guilt, that metaphysical guilt reduces to survivor’s guilt, that intergenerational transmission requires literal inheritance of blame, and that democratic membership necessarily implies authorization of state acts—and state exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted, with reference to which category is being taught at that point. Fourth, indicate where the democratic-authorization tension should emerge: after which categories are secured, before which misconception it functions as a stress test rather than a source of confusion, and how the carrying example should be framed so that students feel the pressure on Jaspers’s category of political guilt without resolving it by retreat to moral or metaphysical guilt. Fifth, design one diagnostic question for the end of the session that would reveal whether a student has grasped the distinction between attributive and associative dimensions of collective responsibility without importing individualist moral psychology. Finally, state explicitly what must be left out at this level—any appeal to specific legal doctrines of state succession, to detailed historiography of reparations debates, to religious frameworks of original sin, or to bibliographic surveys of the post-war German context—and say so openly, explaining why each omission protects the conceptual architecture rather than simplifying it.
