I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar working through theories of biological function, and I've realized my own intuitions about specific cases don't line up neatly with either the standard etiological or the causal-role framework. Rather than committing to one camp, I want to see whether there's a coherent principle—perhaps a modified hybrid—that captures my judgments, or whether I have to abandon at least one of them.

Here are my verdicts. The human heart clearly has the function of pumping blood; that feels secure on any adequate account. Now consider a synthetic ventricular patch engineered from induced pluripotent stem cells, implanted in a patient, contracting rhythmically and moving blood, but with absolutely no evolutionary selection history behind it. I still think it's correct to say its function is pumping blood, so I'm not a pure etiologicalist. Next, take the human appendix: its evolutionary origin is murky, but it currently maintains gut microbiome reservoirs after infection. I think that immune-related contribution counts as a genuine function, even if selection for it was indirect or weak. Fourth, imagine a bacterial strain that acquired a viral gene through horizontal transfer; the gene now encodes an enzyme essential for metabolizing a synthetic pollutant, yet this metabolic role was never selected for in the bacterial lineage. Despite its causal indispensability, I feel uneasy attributing a biological function here—it seems more like an accidental capacity. Fifth, consider a flightless cave beetle whose vestigial wings have high surface area and now dissipate heat in a warm environment; flight was their evolutionary history, but thermoregulation is their present causal contribution. Here I want to deny that either flight or heat dissipation is the present function: flight is obsolete, but heat dissipation feels like a maintained byproduct rather than a true function, even though it is systematically useful. Sixth, suppose a non-coding "junk" DNA region in a single individual is co-opted during development to regulate a stress-response pathway; the mechanism is real and reproducible in that organism, but it hasn't been fixed at the population level. I lean toward denying that this region has a biological function, despite its active causal role.

Is there a single principle—maybe one that distinguishes design, selection, and population-level stabilization in a way the standard dichotomy misses—that delivers all six verdicts? In particular, how should it treat the synthetic heart versus the horizontally transferred enzyme, since both lack evolutionary history but I judge them differently? And what does the principle predict for a case I haven't listed: an artificial organoid grown in vitro that is then subjected to directed evolutionary selection for a metabolic task it was never originally designed to perform? Please state your proposed principle with precision, map each clause to the case that motivates it, list its verdicts on the unlisted organoid case, and be explicit about which of my six judgments it cannot accommodate without revision. Finally, say how confident you are in the reconstruction and single out the point where you are least sure—whether that's the boundary between designed and evolved functions, or the weight of population-level versus individual-level causal contributions.
