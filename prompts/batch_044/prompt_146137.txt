A colleague asks for my philosophical assessment of whether her consent to a clinical trial was genuinely valid. The trial tests an experimental cognitive-enhancement compound and requires continuous biometric monitoring. The informed-consent documentation contains no false statements: every data-collection protocol is described with technical accuracy. However, the privacy risks are presented exclusively through relative-frequency framing—for example, stating that unauthorized exposure occurs in "3 of every 1,000 participants"—rather than through absolute-probability or natural-frequency formats that her specific cognitive profile processes more reliably. She has high verbal and domain competence—she is a linguist—but documented low statistical literacy, and she now realizes that the framing architecture caused her to systematically underestimate the risk despite comprehending every individual clause. Separately, her employer, aware of the trial, communicated that advancement to a security-clearance role requires demonstrating "organizational transparency commitment." No explicit enrollment mandate existed, but she experienced the message as determinative of her career trajectory and enrolled partly for that reason. She notes that she was neither lied to nor physically coerced, yet suspects that the interaction between the framing design and her competence asymmetry, combined with this structural economic pressure, undermines autonomy in a way that conventional binary accounts of deception and coercion fail to capture. How should I advise her? Specifically, can one defend a philosophically rigorous distinction between permissible persuasive framing in consent procedures and a form of structural deception that exploits predictable, domain-specific cognitive vulnerabilities? And does her employer's indirect but career-determinative signaling constitute coercion in a morally relevant sense, even in the absence of an explicit threat?
