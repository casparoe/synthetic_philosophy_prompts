I am preparing a feature for a science magazine on rational disagreement. A colleague has passed me the following summary of Aumann's agreement theorem, taken from an unnamed popular book on decision theory: 'Aumann showed that two rational Bayesians with identical priors who possess common knowledge of their posterior beliefs must eventually converge on exactly the same probabilities for every event. Consequently, persistent disagreement in science or politics can arise only from hidden differences in prior belief or from some failure of rationality; the result also proves that Bayesian updating eliminates all subjective elements from belief formation, since rational agents are mechanically compelled to identical conclusions. Furthermore, recent extensions demonstrate that the theorem applies without alteration to Dempster-Shafer belief functions, so long as the agents share a common frame of discernment.' Before I quote this, I need to know which parts are faithful, which involve subtle misrepresentations — for instance, whether common knowledge of posteriors is correctly characterised, whether the result genuinely applies to non-Bayesian belief revision, and whether it removes subjectivity in the way claimed — and whether the inference that scientific disagreement therefore signals bias or irrationality actually follows. Would you examine the summary sentence by sentence, say what a specialist in formal epistemology would accept, correct, or reject, and indicate whether the conclusions about disagreement and subjectivity survive those corrections?
