I am writing to ask for your help with a conceptual tension I encountered while serving on an interdisciplinary review panel for a large neuroscience-engineering grant. The applicants propose to build a neuromorphic processor that replicates cortical architecture at the single-neuron level, arguing that because both biological brains and digital machines are fundamentally information-processing systems, achieving human-level cognition is mainly a problem of scaling up parallel connections until we match the brain's numerical capacity. One of my co-reviewers, a neurophysiologist, objected that this framing treats synaptic transmission as if it executed discrete symbolic instructions with perfect fidelity, when in fact neural signaling is noisy, probabilistic, and operates through statistical ensemble patterns rather than deterministic logical steps. I am trying to determine whether this disagreement is merely about implementation details—suggesting we just need better redundancy algorithms—or whether it reveals a deeper conceptual mismatch about what kind of system the brain actually is.

In particular, I keep returning to the comparison von Neumann drew in his later reflections between the brain and the computer, where he emphasized not only analogies but also stark differences: the brain's components are orders of magnitude less precise than electronic switches, yet it achieves remarkable reliability through statistical redundancy, and its operations may depend on a form of mathematics that is not purely logical or arithmetic. The engineers seem to assume that we can take the logical architecture of computing and simply embed it in noisy biological-style hardware, while the neurobiologist seems to imply that cognition might emerge from a fundamentally different organizational principle—one that cannot be captured by scaling up discrete processing units. I would value your analysis of whether von Neumann's own examination of neural tissue actually supports the view that the brain-computer analogy licenses this kind of direct engineering translation, or whether his recognition of statistical, analog-style processing undermines the project's foundational premise.

More concretely, as I prepare my evaluation, should I treat the statistical-noise objection as a practical engineering hurdle that can be overcome with error-correction and parallel redundancy, or as a philosophical indication that we are using the wrong conceptual model of mind altogether? I am especially interested in whether there is a coherent middle position—perhaps that the brain implements something like computation, but not the kind of computation that can be reverse-engineered by replicating neuron counts in silicon—and what that would mean for judging the proposal's intellectual merit rather than just its technical feasibility.
