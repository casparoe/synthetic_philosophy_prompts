I was discussing moral philosophy with a colleague over lunch—he works in law, I work in software, and neither of us has any formal training in ethics—when he summarized Michael Walzer’s well-known view on the moral equality of combatants. His account ran roughly like this: Walzer insists that just-war theory must enforce a complete separation between the justice of entering a war and the justice of conduct within it. Because ordinary soldiers almost never decide whether their state goes to war, they carry no moral responsibility whatsoever for the cause they are fighting for; consequently, soldiers on both sides acquire exactly identical rights and permissions, including an unconditional right to kill enemy combatants. This equality, he said, applies even to soldiers who volunteer with full knowledge that their cause is unjust, and it entails that an unjust combatant is never morally worse off than a just one—only less fortunate. From these premises he concluded that, on Walzer’s framework, no individual soldier can ever be guilty merely for participating in an unjust war, since all moral fault lies exclusively with the leaders who launched it. I want to check whether this specification is sound before I accept it. Could you audit the summary claim by claim—indicating which parts Walzer or his defenders would accept as accurate, which are subtle distortions or overstatements, and whether the conclusion that soldiers are always blameless actually follows from the accurate parts? I am particularly unsure where Walzer’s separation of jus ad bellum and jus in bello ends and where it is being treated as an absolute moral firewall that wipes out all individual culpability, so please be explicit about those boundaries.
