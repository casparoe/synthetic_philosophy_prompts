Write a villanelle on Nyāya epistemology and the four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda—structured so that its form enacts the orthodox refusal to grant anupalabdhi (non-apprehension) the status of an independent fifth source of valid cognition. Use two refrains that alternate through the five tercets and converge in the final quatrain. Refrain A should voice the Nyāya position that absence is never directly given but is always mediated through a present perceptual failure (for instance, “The bare floor only shows the missing pot”). Refrain B should voice the rival claim—advanced in schools such as Advaita Vedānta—that absence is immediately cognized without mediation (for instance, “The empty room knows its own emptiness”). Map each of the five tercets, in order, onto one member of the classical Nyāya five-member syllogism: pratijñā (the thesis that only four sources yield pramā), hetu (the reason that absence lacks an independent object-correlate), udāharaṇa (the illustrative case of seeing bare ground), upanaya (the application of that case to all absence-claims), and nigamana (the conclusion that anupalabdhi is reducible to pratyakṣa). Within this scaffolding, let the first four tercets each invoke one of the four recognized pramāṇas—perception in the first, inference in the second, comparison in the third, testimony in the fourth—so that the syllogistic defense of four-source epistemology is carried out through the very sources it affirms. The rhyme scheme should follow the standard villanelle pattern (A1bA2; abA1; abA2; abA1; abA2; abA1A2), with the refrains passing each other until the closing lines place them side by side without synthesis. The poem should be philosophically precise enough that a reader familiar with Indian epistemology recognizes the specific debate over whether prāmāṇya is svataḥ or parataḥ and the auxiliary role of tarka, yet it should remain coherent verse for someone who has never encountered Nyāya.
