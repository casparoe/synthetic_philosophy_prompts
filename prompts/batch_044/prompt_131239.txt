In a course I am taking on philosophies of history, one of my colleagues has begun using an updated version of Spengler's organic analogy: rather than comparing a civilisation to a biological organism that ages and dies, she treats it as a dissipative structure in the sense developed by Ilya Prigogine, maintaining internal order only by continuously exporting entropy into peripheral environments—through resource extraction, colonial administration, or the outsourcing of waste and labour. She claims that the cyclical patterns Polybius and Ibn Khaldun observed are not instances of biological senescence but thermodynamic inevitabilities: once the channels for entropy export are blocked or saturated, the structure collapses and a new one crystallises elsewhere. She deploys this analogy to argue that linear-progress narratives—the 'arrow of history' as cumulative knowledge and institutional improvement—are illusions sustained only by temporary, energy-intensive stability, and that Spengler and Toynbee were essentially describing the same dissipative lifecycle in biological language.

Second, identify where the mapping breaks—do civilisations possess bounded membranes or quantifiable entropy flows, and does the analogy smuggle in the normative assumption that collapse is a natural, value-neutral phase transition rather than a humanitarian catastrophe? Third, contrast it with the linear-progress analogy it is meant to displace—history as compound-interest accumulation of technology and norms—and ask whether the dissipative picture genuinely entails cyclical inevitability, or whether it could accommodate a linear trajectory that merely sheds inefficient subsystems. Finally, tell me whether either analogy can actually carry the historical conclusions drawn by Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler or Toynbee, or whether both are doing rhetorical work that outstrips their structural similarity.
