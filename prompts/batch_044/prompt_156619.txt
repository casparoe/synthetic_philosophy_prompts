I have been preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception, and in our discussions of cognitive penetration we find ourselves perpetually returning to the familiar repertoire—the ambiguous figure, the tree seen as tree, the expert radiologist interpreting a scan. I should like to move the students into somewhat less trodden conceptual terrain.

Give me an example of a conceptual issue within the cognitive-penetration debate—one that does not lean on the standard face-perception or figure-ground illustrations—in which the boundary between genuine perceptual restructuring by an acquired concept and what is merely enhanced attentional selection or post-perceptual judgement becomes genuinely unstable. To render the issue sharp, the case should involve a subject who has recently acquired a highly technical conceptual scheme (rather than a long-calibrated perceptual skill) and for whom there is a plausible rival explanation in terms of sub-personal predictive-processing adjustments. I am especially interested in scenarios where the phenomenal report is ambiguous between "the stimulus now looks different to me" and "I now notice features that were always present but previously ignored", since this ambiguity lies at the heart of the dispute with the modularist about the direction of explanatory fit. A carefully constructed philosophical thought-experiment is wholly acceptable, provided it is sufficiently detailed to expose the dependency relations between concept-possession, phenomenal character, and whether the concept genuinely penetrates the perceptual state or merely selects its outputs.
