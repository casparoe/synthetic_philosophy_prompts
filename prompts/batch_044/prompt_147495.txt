Since retiring from experimental physics, I have been studying epistemology with some care, and I wish to check a line of reasoning that has occurred to me regarding the Gettier problem. As I understand it, Gettier demonstrated that a belief can be justified, true, and yet fail to be knowledge whenever the justification, though sound in general, does not causally or logically account for the particular truth of that belief—the canonical case being Smith's inference about the job candidate with ten coins, where the premises supporting the belief turn out false, yet the conclusion remains true for entirely independent reasons. It strikes me that experimental science is systematically vulnerable to precisely this structure: a measurement apparatus may be well-calibrated (justified) and yield a correct value (true) only because two independent errors happen to cancel, so that the calibration record does not in fact explain why this particular reading is correct. If this is indeed a Gettier situation, then it would seem that any knowledge claim resting on a single instrumental chain is epistemically defective. To remedy this, I propose a principle of epistemic triangulation: a scientific belief counts as knowledge only if it is supported by at least two methodologically independent lines of evidence whose justifications do not share common failure modes, thereby ensuring that the truth of the belief is explained by the justification rather than merely coincident with it. Before I develop this further, I should like to know whether my identification of instrumental error-cancellation with Gettier-style epistemic luck is philosophically sound, and whether the triangulation condition I have outlined addresses the specific logical structure Gettier identified, or whether it instead solves a different problem altogether.
