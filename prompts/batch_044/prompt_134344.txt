Analyze the 2006 reclassification of Pluto by the International Astronomical Union through the lens of Frege's puzzle about identity statements. Begin by mapping the case onto Frege's framework: 'Pluto is Pluto' is cognitively trivial, but the statement that Pluto is a dwarf planet—or that Pluto is the same celestial body once called the ninth planet but now falling under a different taxonomic category—carried genuine informational weight and provoked widespread public resistance. Clarify what the referent of 'Pluto' is, whether it changed, and whether the cognitive shock came from a shift in the sense of 'planet'—from a broad observational category to a precise dynamical definition—rather than from any change in reference. Then introduce a further distinction: treat 'planet' either as a natural-kind term with a putative underlying essence or as an institutional kind constituted by the IAU's conventional criteria. Examine how each reading changes what Frege's puzzle predicts about the informativeness of the identity statement and about the legitimacy of the public's attachment to the old classification. Conclude by assessing what the Pluto episode shows about the puzzle itself—whether it demonstrates that identity statements involving institutionally defined categories have a different epistemic profile from Frege's astronomical examples, or whether it confirms that cognitive value always tracks a divergence in sense regardless of whether the kind is natural or conventional.
