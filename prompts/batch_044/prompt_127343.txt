I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of art and cognitive aesthetics, currently drafting a chapter on the phenomenology of negative affect in fictional engagement. During a seminar discussion last week, a colleague pressed me on the paradox of tragedy: if artworks like certain tragedies or elegiac compositions genuinely induce profound sadness, grief, or dread, why do we seek them out rather than avoid them? I noted that the canonical answers—Aristotelian catharsis, Hume's account of the conversion of sympathetic distress into aesthetic pleasure, and the family of psychological-distance theories—all seem to treat the negative affect as a problem requiring neutralization or transformation. I'm increasingly convinced that this framing is mistaken, but I need help articulating the alternative precisely.

Specifically, please argue that we enjoy tragic art not because sadness is canceled out, but because it is processed through a decoupled emotional-monitoring pathway—what I am calling an aesthetic fidelity mode—in which intense negative valence is registered not as an action-demanding threat but as a high-fidelity signal of successful emotional modeling within a safe, non-instrumental context. On this view, the satisfaction we derive is not the opposite of sadness but a distinct, co-occurring reward tied to the accuracy and depth of our affective tracking. I want a rigorous development of this position that directly addresses the obvious objection: if the sadness remains fully present and phenomenologically unconverted, then labeling the overall experience enjoyable merely renames the paradox rather than solving it. Please avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to specific textual authorities; I am looking for a focused philosophical argument that explains why this monitoring-pathway account avoids collapsing back into the familiar catharsis or distance frameworks, and that clarifies what exactly distinguishes the affective structure of tragic enjoyment from ordinary pleasure.
