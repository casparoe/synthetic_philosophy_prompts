Consumer-protection statutes in many jurisdictions grant buyers a mandatory “cooling-off” window—usually several days—to cancel contracts signed in their own homes without penalty, treating an immediate decision as potentially non-binding on a later self. Meanwhile, behavioral-design programs for retirement savings exploit hyperbolic discounting by locking in contribution escalations that take effect only after future pay raises, letting a present planner bind a present spender without ever requiring the spender’s contemporaneous consent. What philosophical theory of diachronic agency and moral responsibility do these paired practices jointly presuppose? Must they hold that hyperbolically discounted preferences are rationally defective, or can they instead be explained by a split-authority model in which only the “cooler” temporal stage possesses normative standing? Reconstruct the implicit commitments, distinguish what is strictly necessary from what is merely compatible, and say whether a single coherent account of weakness of will can justify both the legal protection and the architectural binding, or whether they quietly rest on contradictory views of the present self’s authority.
