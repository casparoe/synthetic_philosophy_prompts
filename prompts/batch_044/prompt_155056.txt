At a workshop on reproductive ethics, two colleagues argued about whether liberal eugenics can resist Julian Savulescu’s principle of procreative beneficence. (1) The first claimed that once parents may genetically select for welfare-promoting traits, they are morally required to select the best available child—otherwise they deliberately choose a worse life for no reason—so the liberal “permission” to enhance collapses into an obligation. (2) The second replied that liberal eugenics draws a firm line at the treatment/enhancement boundary: parents must correct disease-related deficits, but going beyond that baseline is supererogatory, not obligatory, because no harm is done by leaving a child at species-typical functioning. (3) The first objected that for continuous cognitive traits—such as working-memory capacity or verbal fluency—the concept of “species-typical functioning” is statistically defined, not normatively grounded, so the boundary is arbitrary and cannot bear the moral weight placed on it. (4) The second then proposed that liberal eugenics should instead permit only “threshold” interventions that secure adequate functioning, while prohibiting “positional” enhancements aimed at competitive advantage; this, she argued, preserves freedom without sliding into maximisation. (5) The first responded that “adequate” still requires a normative specification of which cognitive profiles count as sufficient, and that any such specification will implicitly favour certain conceptions of the good life—making the liberal framework less neutral than it claims. Please adjudicate each of the five moves: rate them from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing about the relationship between statistical normality and moral obligation in this context.
