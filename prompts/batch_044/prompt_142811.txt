I keep noticing a structural pattern in modern media that feels like a variant of something Kierkegaard diagnosed in the press, but I cannot tell whether the variant has been named or whether it collapses back into his existing categories. As I understand Kierkegaard’s critique, the press helps form an abstract “public” — a collective that holds opinions without any individual being personally committed to them — and this is bound up with “leveling,” the dissolution of qualitative distinctions into quantitative averages. The pattern I see now adds a reflexive layer: individuals build distinct personal brands, bylines, and voiced personas (podcasters, columnists, commentators with recognizable voices), yet algorithmic aggregation and metrics-based curation combine these voices into a collective “mood,” “consensus,” or “discourse” that no single branded individual actually endorses. This aggregated result is then treated as an authoritative external force — “the reaction,” “the conversation,” “what people are saying” — even though it is composed of the very voices being aggregated. Meanwhile, the branded individuals can disavow the collective result while continuing to feed it, so the appearance of individual authorship and commitment is preserved, not dissolved, but functions precisely as fuel for an anonymous opinion-forming mechanism. Two concrete instances: a news outlet running a “what the internet is saying” feature composed of aggregated tweets and commentary from named writers, none of whom individually holds the synthesized view presented; and a podcast ecosystem where hosts develop strong personal stances, yet audience metrics and recommendation algorithms merge these stances into a generic “audience sentiment” that is cited back to the hosts as if it were an independent social fact.

Is there an established term — in Kierkegaard scholarship or in later philosophy of media and public life — for this specific configuration, in which personal branding and algorithmic aggregation combine to produce a phantom collective authority? How exactly does it differ from Kierkegaard’s own account of leveling and the phantom public, and from later concepts such as manufactured consent or the spiral of silence? If there is no precise term, what conceptual boundaries distinguish this phenomenon from ordinary leveling, and what should we call the illusion that individual authorship remains structurally intact even as it is dissolved by the aggregation mechanism it feeds?
