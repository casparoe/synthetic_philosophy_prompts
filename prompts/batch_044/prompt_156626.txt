I'm delivering the toast at my mentor's retirement dinner—Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent four decades in sociology of religion and economic history. The room is split exactly in half: one side is filled with historians of social science and theorists of causal explanation who have spent years arguing over Weber, and the other side is her family, university trustees, and old friends who know she was important but have never read a word of the Protestant Ethic thesis. I need a five-minute speech that does real philosophical work, not just decoration.

The structural problem is this: Elena never believed that "culture caused capitalism" in any straightforward billiard-ball sense, and she spent her career attacking that caricature of Weber. Instead, she developed a distinction—mostly in her later essays—between what she called push causation and frame causation. On her view, values like the Protestant ethic don't operate as efficient forces that shove economic behavior in a particular direction; they operate as conditions of causal visibility, making certain practices thinkable and others literally invisible to the actors involved. Her best-known case study was not Calvinist Geneva but sixteenth-century Venetian double-entry bookkeeping: she argued that contractual trust among merchant partners didn't cause bookkeeping to spread like a virus, but constituted the interpretive frame within which bookkeeping could appear as a rational response rather than a suspicious novelty. I want the toast to honor exactly this contribution—the refusal to treat values as pseudo-physical causes—without either dumbing it down into "religion makes people work hard" for the family, or lapsing into jargon-heavy exposition that loses them entirely.

Please write the speech so that the frame-causation idea shapes what I can and cannot say. I should not claim she "proved" Weber right or wrong; I should not offer a bibliography or suggest anyone read specific texts; and I should not fall back on a generic "she followed her passion" narrative that ignores her actual intellectual project. Instead, build the toast around one concrete image—perhaps the ledger itself, or a moment from her classroom teaching—that lets both audiences grasp why explaining with values is philosophically treacherous and why her refusal to simplify it was a form of intellectual integrity. The tone should be warm, affectionate, and celebratory, landing as genuine tribute rather than as a miniature lecture, but the experts in the room should hear every serious point about explanatory overdetermination and recognize that I haven't betrayed her work for sentiment.
