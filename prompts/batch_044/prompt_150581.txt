I've been trying to pin down what makes some predictions avoidable and others self-defeating, using a cluster of cases modeled on the Death in Damascus story as test cases for my own judgments. In the classic version, a merchant learns that Death expects him in Damascus tomorrow, flees to Aleppo, and finds Death there—who explains he was surprised to see him in Aleppo, since he was expecting him in Damascus. My verdict is that the merchant's flight doesn't show irrationality but rather closes a causal loop: his very attempt to avoid Damascus delivers him to the meeting point, so the prophecy is fulfilled through the avoidance mechanism rather than despite it. By contrast, if a hurricane forecast warns Miami residents to evacuate and they do so successfully, I see no paradox at all—the prediction was conditional on inaction, and acting on it breaks the chain cleanly.

Where my intuitions start to split is when the predictor seems to embed my reasoning about the prediction inside its forecast. If a perfect brain simulation predicts my chess move, and I try to randomize, but my randomization is itself inside the simulation, I think I'm genuinely stuck—the escape attempt is part of the model's inputs. But if a genetic counselor predicts heart disease contingent on my diet and I change my diet, that feels like ordinary successful avoidance with no logical tension. The conflict peaks in a variant where an omniscient observer predicts I'll be in room A at noon, I deliberately choose room B, and the observer is waiting in B saying they knew I'd come there. I want to say my choice was free and the prediction about room A was false, yet the observer's presence in B suggests my reasoning was anticipated—so either freedom is compatible with perfect foreknowledge only when the knowledge doesn't causally determine the act, or I'm forced to deny that I could have stayed in A. Is there one principle—maybe about whether a prediction is causally open, counterfactually stable, or embedded in the agent's decision procedure—that captures all these verdicts? If not, tell me which verdict I should drop and why, and apply the resulting view to the case I keep oscillating on: a prophecy that I will betray a close friend, where learning the prophecy makes me hypervigilant and I ultimately don't betray them. Does this count as successful avoidance, or does my hypervigilance become part of the fulfillment conditions?
