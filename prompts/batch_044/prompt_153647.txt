Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a phenomenal disjunctivist about perceptual experience and a common-factor representationalist about illusion, focused specifically on the argument from illusion as reconstructed through the “epistemic-parity challenge”: the claim that if a subject undergoing a causally matched hallucination can make the same first-person judgments about her mental state as a veridical perceiver, then the two states must share an explanatory core that direct realism cannot accommodate.

Structure the document in three parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the formal structure of the argument from illusion (the inference from phenomenological similarity to ontological sameness), the existence and causal matching of hallucinations, the ordinary epistemic authority of veridical perception, and the burden on disjunctivism of explaining why introspection does not reveal the relational nature of veridical experience if such relations are constitutive. Second, a disagreements table with one column for the disjunctivist and one for the representationalist; each column should state, in wording its own side would accept without reservation, the precise position on: (a) whether phenomenological similarity entails a shared mental kind; (b) whether relational properties (e.g., direct acquaintance with an external object) can figure in phenomenal character without being introspectively transparent; (c) whether the argument from illusion equivocates between epistemic and metaphysical indistinguishability; and (d) whether a negative account of hallucination—as a failure of perceptual relation rather than a positive representational state—is conceptually coherent. Third, for each of these disagreements, identify the crux—the sub-question such that resolving it would move at least one party—together with a concrete case or conceptual test both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance each side’s prediction. Likely cruxes include whether simplicity or “naturalness” favors relational over representational ontologies of mind, whether first-person authority requires access to the metaphysical kind of one’s state, and whether causal matching is better explained by shared internal representational structure or by shared causal mechanisms operating on different ontological substrates; as a shared test-case, both should consider a subject with complete neurophysiological self-knowledge but no external-world access who cannot distinguish veridical perception from perfect hallucination, with the disjunctivist predicting that this limitation reveals only epistemic opacity about metaphysical kind and the representationalist predicting that it demonstrates the explanatory priority of internal representational structure. Conclude by assessing whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal—for instance, whether disagreement over whether hallucination possesses “content” depends on stipulative definitions rather than substantive ontology—and specify exactly which part, with reasons. The statement should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, and dependence on obscure texts; formulate every position with philosophical precision, avoid straw-man characterizations, and ensure that each column represents wording its proponents could sign.
