I have been trying to think through, on my own, whether it really makes sense to say that a nation or people can be guilty of wrongs committed generations ago, long after every direct perpetrator and victim is dead. I have come across Karl Jaspers’s distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, but I am less interested in sorting wrongs into boxes than in knowing what a coherent theory of transgenerational collective culpability would actually have to deliver—beyond rhetoric—to count as adequate.

Could you lay out the full desiderata? I want the specification of what such a theory must be able to do: the phenomena it needs to explain (for example, why we sometimes feel obligations to apologize or make reparations, and why we do not feel them for every historical wrong), the distinctions it must preserve (such as between inheriting a burden and inheriting blame, or between a collective that persists through institutional rules versus one that persists only through shared culture or memory), and the cases it must get right (a nation whose population has turned over entirely but whose constitutional structure remains; descendants who actively and publicly repudiate the past; a collective that dissolved and was later reconstituted under the same name). For each requirement, explain why it is genuinely required and not merely a prejudice of one camp.

Please also address whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—if so, that tension itself should be part of the result. Then identify which single requirement does the most damage to the usual candidates people reach for, whether that is analogizing nations to individual persons, treating guilt as purely institutional, or distributing blame across current members like an inherited debt.

If it helps, briefly score the main contenders—individualist analogies, institutional-continuity accounts, and distributed-liability models—against this list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring, since the conceptual work is in the requirements.

One constraint that matters to me but that I rarely see addressed properly: the theory should not depend on any metaphysically mysterious claim that national identity remains literally continuous across generations, yet it also should not reduce collective guilt to a statistical summary of individual guilts, because then the moment membership turns over completely the guilt evaporates. I do not know whether that pair of demands can be satisfied together; if they cannot, say so explicitly and show where the contradiction lies.
