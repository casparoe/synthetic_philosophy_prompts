I'm drafting my philosophical profile for a rationalist reading group and want a consistency check before I present it. My commitments on mind and consciousness are as follows:

(a) I accept a substrate-independent functionalism: mental states are individuated entirely by their causal-functional roles, so consciousness could in principle be realized in silicon, biological tissue, or distributed architectures.

(b) I endorse strong multiple realizability: human pain and a correctly organized silicon analog are not merely analogous but the same mental kind realized differently.

(c) I reject both Searle's Chinese Room argument and biological naturalism: syntax by itself is not sufficient for semantics or consciousness, but syntax coupled with sustained global-workspace dynamics, recurrent integration, and appropriate information-flow topology is sufficient.

(d) I hold that phenomenal consciousness is a functional kind—there is nothing more to being in pain than playing the pain-role—while also maintaining that phenomenal concepts carry a special first-person epistemic status that generates a genuine explanatory gap.

(e) I interpret that gap as purely epistemic, not ontological: it signals a limitation in our third-person conceptual apparatus rather than the presence of a non-physical property.

(f) I deny that present-day large language models are conscious, not on substrate grounds, but because they lack the sustained recurrent dynamics and globally integrated workspace that my functionalist account requires.

(g) I further hold that mental representation is teleofunctional: representational content is fixed by selection history or appropriate learning history, so that a system with identical dynamics but no proper etiological background lacks genuine intentionality.

Please audit this set. Which commitments pull against each other, which conflicts are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity. In particular, examine whether (c) and (g) are jointly stable: if organizational dynamics are sufficient for consciousness, why does representation additionally require teleological history, and does that undermine my denial of consciousness to LLMs in (f) given that they possess extensive training histories? Also check the consistency of (b) with (f) given (a). Finally, address whether calling the explanatory gap "genuine" in (d) while labeling it "purely epistemic" in (e) involves a contradiction, and if not, how it dissolves. Please respond in American English in a formal, polished register suitable for a philosophy seminar.
