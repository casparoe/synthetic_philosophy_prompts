As a retired physicist who has recently begun auditing seminars in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I am helping an instructor design the final examination for an upper-level course on meaning and representation. I want one essay question that works with Douglas Hofstadter's tripartite distinction—frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—developed in Gödel, Escher, Bach, but that cannot be answered by simply reciting the textbook taxonomy. The prompt should embed a specific, somewhat unfamiliar scenario so that students must apply the framework rather than reproduce it.

The scenario is this: consider a synthetic gene-regulatory circuit engineered into a bacterial strain. The circuit contains a DNA sequence that functions not as a direct causal trigger but as an interpretive frame: it specifies rules for translating local chemical gradients—say, pH and reactive-oxygen levels—into one of two stable phenotypic states, either toxin-neutralization or sporulation. An external engineer can read the sequence and recover the design intention, yet the organism possesses no conscious access to the frame, to the chemical signals interpreted as outer messages, or to any putative inner message. The question should ask students to map Hofstadter's three layers onto this system, to say where meaning genuinely resides, and to identify precisely where the analogy to Hofstadter's canonical examples—such as phonograph records or genetic codes—collapses.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, calibrated for about forty-five minutes and aimed at advanced undergraduates or beginning graduate students; (2) a model answer of approximately six hundred words; and (3) a marking scheme that separates an A-level response from a B-level response by listing the specific conceptual moves that must be present—such as recognizing that the frame message operates at the level of interpretation rules rather than causal mechanisms, avoiding the error of attributing an inner message to a non-conscious system, and noting the contested status of whether an outer message requires an intentional recipient. In the model answer and scheme, please distinguish what is settled within the framework from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who work on these issues, and do not present any disputed interpretation of Hofstadter's view as though it were the consensus.
