Write a deadpan bureaucratic compliance memo, approximately 600 words, formatted as numbered regulations with occasional footnotes in the voice of a government office called the Department of Non-Consequential Governance. The memo should announce a new protocol for resolving conflicts between absolute duties—specifically, a scenario in which an agent must simultaneously honor an inviolable prohibition against deception and an inviolable prohibition against allowing foreseeable harm, with both duties derived from the same universalizability audit that originally justified adopting them. The satire should turn on the paradox of deontology itself: the rules are adopted and weighted entirely by reference to their long-run compliance outcomes and social stability metrics, yet once adopted they must be applied without any consideration of particular consequences; when the rules inevitably conflict, the resolution mechanism (a "moral residue" allocation formula, a "threshold" exemption for catastrophic cases, or a lexical priority ranking) is defended only by its expected aggregate utility, which quietly reintroduces the consequentialist reasoning the framework officially rejects. Do not name any living philosopher or cite specific texts; instead invent precise bureaucratic terminology—e.g., "Universalizability Impact Assessment," "Deontic Overload Protocol," "Residual Duty Ledger"—that sounds technically real. Keep the tone flat, procedural, and dead serious; the humor should come entirely from the internal contradictions being managed with spreadsheets and filing deadlines. A reader familiar with the structure of Kantian justification should recognize that the memo is reproducing the standard objection—that deontological rules are justified like rules-utilitarian rules but executed like categorical imperatives—without the memo ever acknowledging it. End with a form for employees to report "unresolvable deontic conflicts," where the only available resolution boxes are "Accept Catastrophic Outcome" and "Invoke Consequentialist Emergency Override," the latter requiring the employee to sign a statement admitting they have abandoned the framework.
