I was talking with some friends last weekend about why a country can have piles of gold or printed currency and still have people struggling to get bread, and one of them mentioned that an old Scottish philosopher—David Hume—wrote essays arguing that money itself is not real wealth. From what I gathered on my own, he thought money works more like oil in an engine: it makes trade run smoothly, but adding more oil does not make the engine more powerful. He also described something like a natural balance, where if one place exports more goods than it takes in, precious metal flows there, prices rise, and eventually the flow reverses—so trade balances correct themselves without anyone planning them. I am not a student of economics, but these two ideas struck me as strange and important: the claim that money is only a symbol, and the claim that imbalances fix themselves through this flow mechanism.

I would like you to write a short story, in American English, that takes these Humean ideas seriously but does not simply lecture about them. Set the story in a fictional island city-state called Meridian, where the local currency consists of heat-bonds—paper notes whose value is tied to the temperature of a central public furnace that burns imported coal. Trade between Meridian's districts happens not with coins but through physical pipes that carry heated air; when one district sends out more goods than it receives, the excess energy literally flows back through the pipes, warming the receiving district's bonds and cooling the sender's. The protagonist should be an ordinary furnace-tender—someone with no formal training—who notices that when the government dumps extra coal into the furnace to stimulate the economy, the bonds do not buy more bread; they only make everything hotter and more expensive. I want the narrative to show, through her daily work and a specific crisis—perhaps a sudden coal surplus followed by a pipeline blockage—where Hume's oil-for-the-wheels analogy and the self-correcting flow idea actually explain what she sees, and also where the analogy strains or breaks because the heat is real, physical, and dangerous in ways that metal money is not.

Please keep the story focused on her discovery rather than on historical figures, avoid any request for citations or further reading, and make sure the philosophical tension is embedded in the plot and the setting rather than delivered as exposition. I am curious whether a fictional world built around these mechanics can make the old argument feel fresh, and I would like the answer to be written in polished American English.
