I am trying to map my own considered judgments about well-being and simulated experience, not to defend a theory but to see whether a coherent principle lies behind them. Here are six verdicts, with one pair I find especially hard to reconcile.

First, the classic hidden machine: I could enter a perfectly blissful simulated life with no awareness of the artifice. I reject this for my own life—not because the feelings are defective, but because they stand in the wrong relation to reality.

Second, the transparent machine: I know I am in simulation and freely choose to remain in an artificial but pleasant environment for decades. I do not think this is irrational or harmful, though I still hesitate to call it the best possible human life.

Third, partial immersion: I spend weekdays in an ordinary real world and weekends in a flawlessly designed simulated paradise, with full knowledge of the division. This strikes me as not merely permissible but perhaps optimal.

Fourth, the achievement-filter: I perform real actions—say, composing music—but an interface ensures that every attempt feels like effortless triumph regardless of actual quality, and I never learn the true results. This seems worse for my well-being than the full hidden machine, despite involving more “real” action.

Fifth, the post-hoc narrative implant: after living an ordinary real life, I am given false but coherent memories of having achieved something genuinely difficult and valuable. This improves my well-being, and I do not regard it as a corruption comparable to the achievement-filter.

Sixth, the terminal exception: I am dying in pain, and I may choose either genuine but emotionally distant family presence or a simulated, perfect reunion. Here the simulated option seems fully acceptable, perhaps preferable.

The tension is sharp: full deception is rejected, yet transparent and partial forms are accepted; the achievement-filter is condemned more severely than full deception, yet the post-hoc implant is welcomed. Is there a unified principle—perhaps concerning temporal structure, autonomy over one’s narrative arc, or the distinction between process and retrospective evaluation—that captures all six? If not, which verdict should be revised?

Also, apply your best principle to a seventh case I have not listed: a child raised entirely within a transparent, benevolent simulation who is told the truth only upon reaching adulthood. Does the principle predict that this life was deficient, acceptable, or superior?

State the principle precisely, indicate which of my judgments it saves and which it forces me to abandon, and conclude with \answer{...} enclosing either the principle’s core clause or the name of the verdict that must be overturned.
