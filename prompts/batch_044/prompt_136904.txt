I facilitate a graduate seminar on theories of well-being—about 14 students, one 90-minute session. Design a structured in-class simulation, not a lecture or discussion, that makes the group run into the force of Nozick’s experience-machine objection from the inside rather than hearing it named afterward. The specific conceptual target is this: mental-state theories (hedonism, informed-desire satisfaction) have trouble distinguishing a preference that tracks real achievement from one that has been adaptively formed to value mere experience, and the experience-machine intuition draws its power from that ambiguity rather than from a simple “contact with reality” slogan.

Set up a two-round resource-allocation game. In round one, each student receives ten “well-being points” to split between “experience modules” (guaranteed pleasant mental states, no external verification required) and “achievement tokens” (require peer-verified task completion, risk failure). Before round two, assign hidden roles via sealed cards: half the group receives an “adaptive-preference card” stating that achievement tokens are permanently unavailable to them due to a fabricated structural constraint, but they are not told that their preference shift in round two is being monitored; the other half receives a “genuine-preference card” with no constraint. Both groups then reallocate their ten points. The payoff is real but small: the final allocation determines which of two take-home essays they receive—one defending mental-state theories, one defending objective-list theories—plus a coffee voucher tied to the achievement-token count.

The rules should be arranged so that, by round two, the adaptive-preference group tends to shift heavily toward experience modules without recognizing that their valuation has been shaped by the hidden constraint, while the genuine-preference group splits more evenly or retains achievement investment. Only after allocations are locked and recorded should roles be revealed, and the facilitator should then ask each group to defend their round-two split before knowing who held which card.

The debrief must do four things. First, map the round-one/round-two shift onto the distinction between brute preference and informed desire, and onto the mental-state/objective-list boundary. Second, explain what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—for example, that the adaptive-preference group may feel manipulated rather than philosophically enlightened, or that participants will try to reverse-engineer hidden roles during round two, or that the coffee-voucher incentive may crowd out reflective allocation—and say how to handle each failure without breaking the simulation’s internal logic. Third, say honestly which feature of Nozick’s original argument the simulation cannot reproduce, namely the first-person phenomenological difference between living inside a simulated world and tracking actual achievement, which the facilitator must supply verbally in the debrief rather than expecting it to emerge from the rules. Fourth, note what the activity specifically cannot show about hedonism, since the hidden-role mechanism is structurally closer to an objective-list or informed-desire framework than to a pure mental-state view.

Write this as a runnable protocol: explicit rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the payoff scheme in detail, a debrief script outline, and the honest limitations note. If the hidden-role mechanism actually collapses the distinction I am asking you to stage—for instance, if it presupposes that adaptive preferences are always detectable, always illegitimate, or that mental-state theories can never accommodate them—say so plainly rather than designing around the problem. I want direct pushback if the premise is confused or the task as posed can’t be done well.
