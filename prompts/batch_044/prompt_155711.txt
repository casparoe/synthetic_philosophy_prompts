A recent essay on democratic boundaries summarizes a proposed solution to the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos—under the heading of a "reciprocal authorization constraint." According to this summary, the familiar "all affected interests" principle should be rejected because, taken seriously, it collapses into an impossible global demos whenever climate, trade, or monetary policies are at stake, since nearly everyone on earth is affected in some degree. In its place, the essay proposes that membership should be governed by mutual legislative accountability: an individual belongs to the demos only if they stand in a relation where they can both be bound by coercive norms and reciprocally contest and authorize those norms. The summary claims this excludes temporary visitors and future generations—who lack the standing for reciprocal authorization—while including all long-term residents regardless of formal citizenship. It further asserts that this constraint is essentially identical to the "all subjected persons" principle defended in democratic theory, merely translated from the vocabulary of subjection into that of authorization. Finally, the summary concludes that democratic closure arguments—which hold that citizens alone have the right to determine membership rules—are revealed as circular once the constraint is accepted, because they presuppose the very boundary between citizens and non-citizens that they claim to justify; therefore, the boundary problem dissolves into a clear, non-arbitrary distinction with no further circularity.

I suspect this summary gets several distinctions wrong, though I cannot say exactly where. Is the characterization of the "all affected interests" principle fair, or does it overstate the collapse objection by ignoring the ways its defenders limit the relevant kind or magnitude of affectedness? Is the "reciprocal authorization constraint" genuinely equivalent to the "all subjected persons" principle, or does it smuggle in normative conditions—such as the capacity to contest—that defenders of the latter principle might reject or that subject-only accounts do not require? Does the charge of circularity against democratic closure actually land, or does it misrepresent how closure theorists justify membership rules—perhaps by treating citizenship as a prior institutional fact rather than a conclusion to be derived? And does the claim that the boundary problem "dissolves" survive once these corrections are made, or does it depend on one of the conflations in the summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a plausible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation, and tell me whether the essay's conclusion follows from the parts that survive correction.
