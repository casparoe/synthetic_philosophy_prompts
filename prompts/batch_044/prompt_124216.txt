As a novelist accustomed to constructing narratives where absence can be either a void or a presence, I am struck by the philosophical stall over tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries value, and that once these are removed nothing further is needed for a good life. Its opponents concede that suffering is bad and craving destructive, yet insist that positive goods—beauty, understanding, love, achievement—possess intrinsic weight, and that a life of pure nullity lacks the content required for flourishing. Despite this apparent clarity, neither camp has yielded ground in years; each restates its premises as if they were conclusions. I hypothesize that the deadlock is not substantive but structural: the sides may be answering different questions—one about the cessation of harm, the other about the constitution of a narratable life—under the single heading of "well-being." Diagnose this impasse using what each side actually says in reply to the other. Is it verbal, turning on "value" versus "flourishing"; an unacknowledged disagreement about which mental states serve as data for ethical theory; a bedrock intuition that a life must have dramatic content to be complete; or a hidden formal question about whether absence can be a positive state rather than a privation? Identify precisely where the reasoning stops, and say what—a conceptual distinction, a thought experiment, or a reframing of the question—could count as genuine progress.
