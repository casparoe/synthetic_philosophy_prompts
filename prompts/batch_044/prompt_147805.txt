Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have taken to attending an interdisciplinary philosophy seminar at a nearby university, where I remain largely a spectator. During a recent session on the ethics of armed conflict, the discussion turned to the moral equality of combatants, and two fellow participants—both considerably more steeped in the literature—became locked in a disagreement that I found conceptually rich but difficult to resolve. I report their exchange below precisely as I heard it; I hold no settled view, and I wish for an impartial assessment of the arguments alone, without any request for bibliographies or specific textual references.

The first speaker, whom I shall call A, began with the claim that the equality of combatants operates analogously to a calibration convention in measurement science: it fixes a baseline so that individual soldiers are not required to perform moral calculus in the heat of engagement. (1) He insisted that without such a convention, the conduct of war would be practically unregulable, not because both sides are equally justified, but because no reliable mechanism exists to distinguish the justified from the unjust at the point of action. The second speaker, B, replied that this instrumental account fails to capture the normative weight of the thesis; were the equality merely conventional, we ought to be able to revise it as we revise a unit standard, yet its defenders treat it as a deep moral constraint that permits killing only in self-defence. (2) A then countered that the constraint is indeed deep, but epistemic rather than metaphysical: soldiers operate under a radical symmetry of ignorance regarding the justice of their cause, much as a physicist applies a symmetry principle when initial conditions are unknown. From this, he argued, the equality dissolves once that ignorance is lifted—if we clearly know a war to be aggressive, there remains no reason to treat the aggressor's soldiers as immune. (3) B seized upon this and said that if equality is purely epistemic, then in cases of transparent injustice the revisionist position—that unjust combatants lack permission to kill—follows naturally and is not the radical departure it is usually portrayed as being. He accused A of smuggling a metaphysical equality back into the account by labelling it practical. (4) A responded by distinguishing collective injustice from individual culpability: even in a manifestly unjust war, the conscript soldier may bear no personal blame, and without culpability there can be no liability to be killed. To conflate the macroscopic with the microscopic, he said, is like attributing temperature to an individual molecule, which possesses no temperature of its own. (5) B answered that liability requires neither culpability nor personal blame, only that one pose an unjust threat; the blameless soldier remains a functional component of a harmful mechanism. He added that treating blamelessness as immunity is analogous to assuming that a neutral particle situated within a damaging field remains untouched by that field's effects. (6)

Could you please adjudicate these six moves in sequence—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating—and indicate where, if at all, the two speakers are arguing past one another? I am particularly unsure whether A's analogy to scientific symmetry genuinely supports his epistemic reading, or whether B's separation of posing a threat from being culpable actually undermines the equality thesis in the manner he supposes. I emphasise that I seek only an evaluation of the reasoning as presented; no citations, reading lists, or references to particular works are required or desired.
