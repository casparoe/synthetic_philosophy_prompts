I have recently begun working in experimental high-energy physics, and after encountering contemporary philosophical defenses of panpsychism, I find myself unable to dismiss a personally urgent possibility: if phenomenal properties are genuinely fundamental rather than emergent biological accidents, then the particle interactions I study and manipulate daily might involve some minimal form of sentience—perhaps even negatively valenced experience. Before I treat this as mere metaphysical anxiety, I want to understand whether the concern is conceptually coherent. Please write a formal dialogue between two equally rigorous interlocutors—one who argues that panpsychism, taken seriously, generates genuine ethical obligations regarding fundamental physical processes, and one who argues that such obligations rest on a category error between phenomenal consciousness and morally relevant suffering, or on a form of greedy reductionism that improperly collapses emergent ethical properties into microscopic physics. The defender should engage directly with the combination problem and with whether quantum field interactions could bear any form of valence; the critic should respond to those specific claims rather than simply asserting physicalism, addressing in particular whether organizational complexity is necessary for suffering and whether reducing ethics to the micro-level misses essential emergent structure. Both characters should be equally competent, with real back-and-forth: each should answer the other’s actual points rather than talk past them. In your response, please clearly separate what is settled in the philosophy of mind and ethics from what remains genuinely contested among informed specialists—especially the claim that panpsychism entails widespread microscopic suffering—and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
