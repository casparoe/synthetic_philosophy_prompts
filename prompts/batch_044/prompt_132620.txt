My organization's ecological-intervention review board has ruled on five proposals, and I need both a consistency-based recommendation on the sixth and an honest diagnosis of which principle we are actually running. Our precedents: (1) we approved poisoning an invasive rat population on an offshore island despite painful deaths, because the rats were invasive and the intervention protected a ground-nesting seabird colony; (2) we rejected draining a seasonal wetland to suppress mosquito larvae, on the grounds that the amphibian larvae killed were not invasive, the predator density in the wetland was low, and the suffering reduction was speculative; (3) we approved distributing an oral sterilant among feral pigs, accepting that current individuals would live shorter, somewhat hungrier lives in exchange for averting a large number of high-suffering future generations; (4) we rejected a proposed controlled burn of a grassland intended to prevent a probabilistic future catastrophic wildfire, because the certain, immediate suffering of the animals burned outweighed any speculative future reduction; (5) we approved actively breaching a beaver dam to collapse a wetland, after independent data showed that the wetland's extreme prey density generated intense predation-pressure suffering, and that the resulting shrubland supported substantially lower biomass with measurably lower predation rates, even though the collapse killed some amphibians and insects.

The new proposal concerns a dense monoculture of an invasive grass that now supports an enormous population of native small rodents suffering severe starvation and high predation pressure. A selective fungal pathogen, applied over two growing seasons, would thin the grass and shift the area to sparse shrubland with lower total biomass and lower predation rates. The pathogen does not affect birds, large mammals, or invertebrates, but rodents that ingest it suffer prolonged respiratory distress before death or recovery, and the transition kills some native insects and small amphibians. The grass is clearly invasive; the rodents are native but overabundant because of it.

If we want to be consistent with ourselves, should we approve? Identify the two or three principles that jointly explain our five rulings—candidates might include something like "intervention is permitted only to replace a demonstrably high-suffering ecosystem state with a lower-suffering one, with collateral harm acceptable as a side-effect," "direct harm to existing sentient beings is permissible only for invasive-species removal or for reducing future births, never for ecosystem restructuring alone," and "certain immediate suffering always outweighs speculative or distributed future suffering, regardless of scale." Say which principle best explains our actual track record, what it predicts for this fungal-pathogen case, and name the single further case—perhaps one involving a non-lethal density-reduction method that lowers rodent numbers without killing any individuals and without changing the ecosystem type—that would force us to choose between the principles. Also identify our closest analog among the five precedents, and state clearly what principle we would be adopting if we approve versus if we reject. I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule extraction, the divergence of predictions, and the decisive distinguishing case.
