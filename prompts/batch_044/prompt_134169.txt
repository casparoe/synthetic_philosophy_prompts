Please help me understand a reconstructed passage from Isaac Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (Scholium to the Definitions, Book I), in which he discusses a bucket suspended by a long cord, twisted tightly, and then released. At first both the vessel and the contained water are at rest relative to one another, and the water's surface is flat. As the vessel begins to turn, it gradually communicates motion to the water until both share the same rotation and the surface becomes concave, rising at the sides. Newton then asks us to consider the case in which the vessel is suddenly halted: the water continues to swirl for some time, and its surface remains concave, even though it is now at rest relative to the vessel's walls. From this he infers that the concavity is not an effect of motion relative to the bucket, but reveals true circular motion in absolute space.

My difficulty concerns the logical transition from this observation to the anti-relationist conclusion. Newton seems to treat the concavity as an intrinsic dynamical signature of absolute rotation—an effect that occurs whenever bodies move in true circles, regardless of their relation to surrounding matter. But suppose we accept, purely for the sake of analysis, a relationist ontology in which all motion is defined relative to other bodies. Even then, the relationist could observe that whenever the water acquires a particular velocity distribution relative to distant celestial bodies, its surface becomes concave, and that this correlation is stable and lawlike. Could Newton's argument therefore be interpreted not as a direct demonstration that absolute space exists, but as showing that the relationist is compelled to treat the global configuration of distant matter as causally efficacious in producing local inertial effects? In other words, does the bucket prove that relationism is false, or does it merely establish that rotational dynamics involve correlations with distant bodies that any adequate metaphysics—substantivalist or relationist—must accommodate?

I would like a careful unpacking of the argument's structure, particularly regarding how Newton moves from the persistence of concavity during relative rest to the claim about true motion, and whether his reasoning necessarily assumes that inertial effects cannot be relationally grounded. Please do not invoke bibliographic references to later commentators such as Mach or Einstein, and avoid citing secondary literature; I am interested in the internal logic of Newton's reasoning itself.
