I have been trying to understand whether a low-impact AI could be designed to avoid side effects without anyone first writing down a complete account of human values. The difficulty I keep running into is that the phrase "side effect" seems to smuggle in exactly the value judgments it is supposed to avoid. If the assigned task is something like "increase agricultural yield," then redirecting a river is technically outside the task, but calling it a "side effect" rather than a legitimate method already assumes that preserving the original river course matters. Without a full specification of what matters, the boundary between the task and its side effects looks arbitrary.

That said, I recently came across the idea that one might sidestep this problem by using a purely physical baseline—measuring how much the AI disturbs a local thermodynamic equilibrium or an entropy distribution relative to the counterfactual world in which the AI never acted. If impact is defined only as deviation in energy redistribution or particle-position variance, then it seems we could penalize large physical changes without ever deciding whether those changes are good or bad. So my question is this: is the argument that value-free side-effect avoidance is impossible actually based on a confusion between normative evaluation and physical measurement? Or is there a deeper reason why even a thermodynamic baseline cannot be chosen without implicitly committing to some values about what should be left alone?
