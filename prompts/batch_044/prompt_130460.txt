So I've been stuck on this Platonia dilemma thing — 20 people, one sends a telegram, gets a billion — and then I ran into these Luring Lottery setups people use to probe superrationality, where agents try to correlate without talking. For decades teh debate about whether to send the telegram or cooperate in symmetric games feels like it's just looping: causal decision theorists say your choice doesnt affect the others, evidentialists say symmetry means you should reason as if you're all linked. But nobody ever shifts. Is this a real disagreement about rational choice, or mostly verbal — different meanings of 'should' — or are they literally solving different problems, one about single-agent norms and one about population dynamics, just using the same words? I also threw together a weird variant I'm calling the mirror-box lottery: half the participants are real people, half are perfect simulations running the exact same code, and the payout depends on cross-world correlation instead of actual messages. Under that framing the causal camp and the superrational camp seem to be optimizing for completely different targets, which makes me think the original stall might come from hidden assumptions about what counts as 'you' across worlds. What's the diagnosis — hidden formal disagreement, bedrock intuition about symmetry, or an ill-posed question? And what would actually move it: a distinction between agent-indexed and world-indexed reasoning, a formal result on correlation without causation, or is progress impossible?
