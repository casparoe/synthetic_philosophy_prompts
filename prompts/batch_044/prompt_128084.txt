I spent thirty-five years in field ecology and conservation biology before retiring, and only in recent years have I turned seriously to philosophy. I am trying to organize my thinking on a question that my scientific background makes unavoidable but that philosophical treatments rarely address with ecological precision: if a large tract of wild habitat is destroyed, does the aggregate suffering of the affected animal populations decrease, increase, or remain ambiguous?

Please construct a taxonomy—preferably a branching tree—of the positions on this question. The root should split on the claim: "Large-scale habitat destruction produces a net reduction in wild animal suffering." Each internal node must be labeled with a specific claim or diagnostic case that divides the positions, phrased so that a respondent can choose a branch without already knowing the philosophical label that awaits.

Your tree must incorporate the following ecological distinctions as branching criteria:
- Life-history strategy: whether the dominant affected populations are r-selected (high reproductive output, density-independent juvenile mortality) or K-selected (low output, high parental investment), since this determines whether welfare gains come mainly from never-born individuals or are swamped by survivor distress.
- Mortality regime and demographic compensation: whether post-disturbance mortality is density-dependent or density-independent, and whether remaining populations in refugia exhibit demographic compensation (increased reproduction or survival in sinks) that offsets any welfare reduction achieved by lowering overall numbers.
- Source-sink welfare spillover: whether survivors displaced into sink habitats experience lower welfare than they would in the destroyed source habitat, so that the root-level calculus misses a hidden welfare cost.

At every leaf, provide either a recognized label (if the position has one) or mark it "unnamed," followed by exactly one sentence identifying its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. Flag any two leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining the overlap. Also identify at least one logically possible combination of answers that occupies no leaf; diagnose whether it is unoccupied because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Write for someone with basic evolutionary ecology but no specialized philosophical training. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the instrument should be usable without web access. Keep the tone formal and precise.
