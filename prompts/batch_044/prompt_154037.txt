For a graduate philosophy-of-action seminar final (three-hour sit-down exam, answer one of three essay questions, 40 percent of module grade, approximately 1,200–1,500 words expected): design one essay question on sphexishness, mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and free will. The question must not be answerable by summarizing the standard wasp-burial example or by reciting Frankfurt’s hierarchical model of the will. Instead, inject and turn on the following conceptual distinction—behavioral sphexishness (repetition of fixed motor patterns regardless of changed environmental conditions) versus architectural sphexishness (mechanical fixation of the hierarchical weighting schema by which an agent assigns salience to reasons, even when first-order outputs are fully variable). Present a specific case: an agent whose first-order preferences are revisable through deliberation, but whose second-order mechanism for weighing long-term against short-term reasons is causally locked—analogous to a wasp that can vary its digging motions but cannot alter the burial-trigger sequence. Ask whether this agent lacks free will in a way that a merely behaviorally rigid agent does not, and require students to address whether the sphex analogy breaks at the representational level (because the agent represents its own rigidity) or survives it.

Provide a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme. The model answer must contain: (i) precise definitions of both types of sphexishness; (ii) analysis of why architectural rigidity might be more threatening to free will than behavioral rigidity, given that free will is often tied to hierarchical self-governance; (iii) evaluation of whether representational access to one’s own fixed weighting mechanism restores flexibility or deepens the problem; (iv) a clear verdict on whether the sphex analogy holds and what that implies for compatibilist accounts. The marking scheme should separate an A answer—which makes all four moves, explicitly notes that Frankfurt-style hierarchical models are vulnerable to architectural sphexishness in a way they are not vulnerable to behavioral sphexishness, and addresses the representational-level break in the analogy—from a B answer—which collapses the distinction, treats the case as standard weakness of will, and misses the hierarchical point. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; evaluation should be entirely conceptual.
