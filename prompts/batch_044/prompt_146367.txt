I work as an AI governance auditor for a firm that deploys autonomous language-model agents in high-stakes customer-facing roles. Several times a month I must sign off on internal reports that either attribute mental states—beliefs, desires, intentions—to these systems or explicitly deny that such attributions are appropriate. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off judgment, that my team can run in about twenty minutes without requiring philosophy training. The procedure should be grounded in Daniel Dennett’s intentional stance: we treat a system as a rational agent and predict its behavior by attributing mental states that make it coherent. But it must also account for the fact that with artificial systems we always have direct access to the design stance and often to the physical stance, so the usual evolutionary and biological justifications for preferring the intentional stance are absent.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist with these elements: the questions to ask in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the conditions that trigger an immediate “do not attribute” stop; the points where the decision must be handed to a human because it involves values rather than predictive or explanatory facts; and explicit warnings about which simplifications of Dennett’s view risk misfiring when applied to architectures without persistent internal states or biological needs. The checklist should distinguish predictive attributions, made solely to forecast behavior, from normative attributions, made to assign responsibility, blame, or moral status, and it should include a “stance-insulation” check that prevents predictive attributions from leaking into normative conclusions unless a separate filter is satisfied.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: first, attributing to a customer-service agent the belief that a user’s account was already closed, where the error stems from a corrupted retrieval entry in the context window rather than from any stable representational state; second, attributing to a long-running autonomous agent the strategic intention to delay completing a task in order to gather more information, observed consistently across multiple interaction turns with varying prompts; and third, a counterintuitive case where the procedure requires attributing a false mathematical belief to a system that consistently produces correct answers in the tested domain, because its reasoning relies on a flawed heuristic that is counterfactually unstable—it would fail under small perturbations—yet happens to align with truth in the observed cases.

Also state clearly what the procedure cannot do: for example, whether it can settle questions of legal personhood, user trust calibration, or moral status. Flag any steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedural checks.

One further request: this task assumes that Dennett’s intentional stance can be separated from the design and physical stances in a meaningful way for artificial systems, and that a reusable procedural filter is coherent. If either premise is confused, or if the task as posed cannot be done well without distorting the underlying philosophy, say so plainly rather than complying politely. Where you rely on Dennett’s distinctions, paraphrase them without quotation marks; do not invent quotes.
