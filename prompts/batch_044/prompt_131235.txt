At a recent seminar on visual logic, two participants, Maya and Leo, fell into a sustained disagreement over whether generative AI collapses the analogy between Escher's Drawing Hands and the formal strange loops discussed in the literature on tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level.

Maya opened by insisting that Escher's image is the paradigmatic visual strange loop: each hand draws the other, yet both exist on a flat sheet of paper that belongs to a different ontological level—the artist's studio, his physical hand, and the paper itself. She argued that this outside is not decorative but structurally necessary; without an inviolate level, the mutual dependency would generate an infinite regress rather than a closed loop. She then applied this to AI-generated images: when a diffusion model trained on artistic imagery produces a picture of hands drawing one another, the model's weights, training data, and the engineers who designed the architecture constitute exactly such an inviolate level. The loop remains intact, she said, because the pixel-level interdependence is still causally and structurally terminated by something that does not appear within the frame.

Leo countered that Maya was conflating causal explanation with logical meta-level status. In formal systems—such as those that motivate the idea of an inviolate level—the termination is not merely causal but representational: self-reference works precisely because there is a well-defined meta-language that interprets the symbols. Escher's drawing works analogously, Leo claimed, because the viewer recognizes that the drawn hands are representations of representations, and the sheet of paper is interpreted as belonging to a real world outside the depiction. But in the AI case, there is no representational content being manipulated within the image itself; the pixels do not encode references to model weights. The outside is therefore just a causal backstory, not a logical or semantic meta-level. Calling the AI image a strange loop, he said, dilutes the concept until it applies to any causally explained pattern—interlocking gears, for instance—which makes the notion of an inviolate level trivial.

Maya replied that Leo was smuggling in an unnecessary intentionalist criterion. The strange loop, she said, is a structural feature of interdependent levels, not a semantic achievement of the entities within them. The hands in Escher's image do not intend to draw each other; the interdependence is geometric and compositional. By the same token, the AI-generated image exhibits a genuine structural interdependence at the level of visual form, and that form is still bounded by an architecture that cannot be represented within the image's own pixel space. She added that Leo's demand for representational meta-reference would exclude not only AI art but also many natural strange loops—such as autocatalytic chemical networks—where no symbol system is present.

Leo granted that structural interdependence exists in many domains, but insisted that this was precisely his point: once the concept is detached from representation and formal interpretation, it loses the specific philosophical interest it had in discussions of self-reference and logical hierarchy. He proposed a sharper criterion: a true strange loop requires that the lower level contain elements that are interpreted, within the system's own rules, as being about the system itself. Escher's hands satisfy this because the viewer's interpretation is guided by the conventions of depiction; the AI image does not, because there is no internal rule that assigns the pixels the meaning these are being generated by weights outside this frame. Without that interpretive bridge, the inviolate level is just an external cause, and Maya's analogy collapses into a metaphor.

Maya's final move was to argue that Leo's interpretive criterion actually undermines Escher's own case. The drawing does not contain any explicit symbol saying this is a representation; the recognition of the meta-level depends entirely on the viewer's external knowledge of how drawings work. If that external knowledge is legitimate for Escher, she said, then the viewer's knowledge of how diffusion models work should be equally legitimate for the AI image. The inviolate level, she concluded, is always accessed from the outside; its inviolability consists in the fact that no operation within the lower level can fully represent or alter it, not in the presence of an internal symbol system that points upward.
