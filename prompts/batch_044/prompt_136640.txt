I am preparing a research proposal for an interdisciplinary workshop on the metaphysics and epistemology of consciousness, and I keep returning to a tension that seems under-explored in recent literature. The standard formulation of epiphenomenalism—the view that phenomenal properties exert no causal influence on physical events—generates what has long been called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if conscious experiences do not cause anything, then our judgments about them (for instance, that we are currently experiencing pain, or that there is a particular shade of crimson present) appear to be either causally disconnected from their targets or systematically unreliable. Most familiar responses either deny the causal closure of the physical domain, propose some form of causal overdetermination, or retreat to an error theory about introspective reports. But I am less interested in whether epiphenomenalism can be saved than in what the paradox reveals about the prior conditions for phenomenal judgment itself.

Specifically, I want to understand which open conceptual questions in this vicinity are genuinely upstream of the others, and where limited philosophical and scientific resources should be directed over the next several years. For instance: is the judgment paradox primarily a problem about the transparency of introspection—that is, whether we access phenomenal properties directly rather than via representational intermediaries—or is it separable from transparency debates and better framed as a problem about the analogical and conceptual mechanisms that allow phenomenal vocabulary to map onto physical descriptions? Another line asks whether resolving the paradox requires settling the metaphysics of consciousness first, or whether one can make progress on judgment-formation mechanisms independently, perhaps by treating phenomenal concepts as a special class of indexical or recognitional capacities. If you were advising a small working group—philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists with perhaps three years of sustained collaboration—how would you rank these and related open questions? Defend the ordering: which questions are tractable with current conceptual and empirical tools, which depend on answers to others, and what kind of result (conceptual, experimental, or formal) would most shift your priorities? I am writing this for strategic planning rather than for a survey, so I care about the reasoning behind the agenda, not about bibliographic coverage.
