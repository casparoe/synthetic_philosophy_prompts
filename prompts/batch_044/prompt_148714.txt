I have noticed a recurring structural failure in designs for self-correcting reasoning systems, and I am unsure whether it is a recognized variant of a known problem or something that needs its own label. The pattern is this. Begin with an agent that can evaluate its own inferences and flag potential errors; call this the audit function. To prevent the audit function itself from being systematically wrong, we want the agent to audit the auditor. Applied recursively, this yields either an infinite tower or a halt that grants the top-level auditor unconditional trust. The case that interests me is neither extreme but a precise middle configuration: the agent is built so that each audit level is trusted conditionally on the level above it functioning correctly, with the base reasoning layer trusted only if audited, and the top layer trusted only if the agent can verify its own reliability. In practice, this produces a specific deadlock. The agent applies its audit rules confidently to individual object-level beliefs and even to pairs of beliefs, but whenever it attempts to apply the same rule to the claim that its audit hierarchy as a whole is reliable, the conditions for application become circular or collapse into an unconditional axiom that disables further correction. The result is not global skepticism—the agent remains active and corrects local errors—but a structural blind spot: it can never legitimately endorse its own overall reliability, even with a perfect track record, because the endorsement would require using the very mechanism whose reliability is in question. This resembles what is sometimes called the Löbian obstacle, yet that term is usually tied to a specific modal-logic result about an agent believing in its own rationality and deriving contradictions. The phenomenon I describe is architectural rather than purely logical: it concerns layered verification structures and the point at which conditional trust becomes either circular or axiomatic. Is there an established name for this middle-configuration deadlock—perhaps in the literature on reflective consistency, epistemic bootstrapping, or self-modifying systems? If not, can it be distinguished precisely from the standard Löbian obstacle, from infinite-regress arguments in epistemology, and from the problem of the criterion? I am especially interested in its boundaries: does it apply only to hierarchical architectures, or does it also arise in flat, probabilistic self-models where confidence is distributed continuously rather than layered?
