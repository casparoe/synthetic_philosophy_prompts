I'm drafting a feature on the current resurgence of "deep history" synthesis and its critics, and I want to move beyond the familiar Harari-versus-Diamond-versus-Graeber-and-Wengrow scorecard. My working thesis is that these three grand narratives—cognitive-revolution universalism, geographic determinism, and anarchist anthropology—share an unexamined commitment to what I am calling structural irreversibility: the idea that the transitions to agriculture, state administration, and writing create path dependencies that make prior organizational forms structurally unavailable, even when societies appear to revert to them.

Below are my draft notes. I am asking specifically for conceptual comments, not bibliographic ones. First, do Graeber and Wengrow's documented cases of seasonal dual governance—communities that shift between hierarchical and egalitarian arrangements according to the calendar—actually undermine this irreversibility thesis, or do they merely demonstrate local oscillation within an irreversible macro-trajectory? Second, if Diamond's framework is modified to allow societies to reshape the environmental selective pressures acting upon them—creating feedback loops between culture and ecology—does the determinism collapse into unfalsifiability, or can it be reformulated without losing predictive content? Third, does my framing of shared irreversibility implicitly commit me to a functionalist view of social complexity that neither Harari's cognitive framing nor Graeber and Wengrow's emphasis on conscious political choice would endorse?

The feature is aimed at an educated general audience. I want to avoid treating these authors as representatives of "progress" versus "cyclicality," and instead ask whether the debate about human history has been asking the wrong question about what it means for a social form to disappear. Where does this framing hold, where does it overreach, and which of the three authors is most vulnerable to the critique of hidden functionalism?
