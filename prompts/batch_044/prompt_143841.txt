I sit on an ethics review board for documentary-style historical dramas, and we need a reusable decision procedure for evaluating invented scenes—fabricated dialogue, compressed timelines, assigned private motives—involving real people who are deceased and have no legal representatives. The guiding idea is not legal harm but representational fidelity: we owe the dead not literal accuracy in every detail, but preservation of the causal and moral architecture of their actual lives as those lives figure in the shared historical record accessible to viewers. Write a step-by-step procedure, with ordered tests, that a non-philosopher on the board can apply to a proposed scene. The procedure should distinguish permissible narrative compression (omitting failed preliminary trials, merging minor figures) from impermissible structural distortion (inventing private beliefs that reverse the explanatory logic of a documented public decision). Include worked applications to three cases: a scientist whose failed experiments are elided for pacing; a political leader whose invented religious crisis is used to explain a documented policy reversal; and a borderline case where the procedure yields a result the board should likely override on other grounds. Flag precisely where judgment cannot be eliminated, what distinctions the procedure deliberately collapses, and what it is unable to settle.
