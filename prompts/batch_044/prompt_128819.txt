There's a pattern I keep noticing in evolutionary psych arguments and I don't know if it has a name. Someone explains why people are wary of outsiders by saying that long ago strangers usually meant danger, so being wary kept you alive. Then a study shows people are actually quite open with strangers they meet online, and suddenly the explanation becomes: no, the instinct only works for in-person meetings, and the internet is too new for it to recognise. But then another study finds people are wary of strangers online after all, and now the explanation flips: actually the instinct works everywhere, it just gets confused by screens because they share superficial features with real encounters. Meanwhile if people turned out to be very friendly in tiny tribal groups, they'd say it's adjusted to group size. So the same idea can be narrow or broad, well-tuned or misfiring, depending on what the data needs, and each version sounds okay on it's own, but together they make the theory impossible to catch out. It's not quite just-so storytelling, and it's not exactly unfalsifiability in the Popper sense, since each version does make predictions. It's more like a specific kind of theoretical flexibility. Does this have an established name? If not, can you coin one with clear edges — what counts, what doesn't, and how is it different from just adding a normal exception or saying the enviroment has changed?
