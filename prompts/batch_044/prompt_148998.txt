I was observing a discussion between two economists about whether Friedman’s defense of unrealistic assumptions—the idea that predictive success can justify descriptively false premises—holds up under modern macroeconomic practice. One argued that the defense collapses once you distinguish prediction from policy evaluation: the Lucas critique shows that models calibrated to historical moments can fail under intervention precisely because their unrealistic microfoundations (rational expectations, a representative agent) are structurally wrong, so the so-called F-twist licenses false confidence in counterfactuals rather than genuine methodological innocence. The other responded that this misreads both Friedman and modern practice: DSGE models use unrealistic assumptions precisely to achieve interventional stability, and the F-twist applies exactly here—if the model’s out-of-sample predictions, including post-intervention dynamics, hold up, the descriptiveness of the microfoundations remains irrelevant. The first speaker countered that true out-of-sample counterfactual testing is almost never available; calibration fixes parameters to reproduce observed moments, which means assumptions and predictions are entangled rather than separable, so invoking predictive success is circular. The second speaker replied that structural estimation, not pure calibration, addresses this by identifying parameters from cross-equation restrictions, preserving a meaningful separation between assumption and prediction. The first then claimed that if the separation were real, altering a microfoundation—say, replacing rational expectations with adaptive learning—should not require full recalibration, yet in practice it always does, which demonstrates that the assumptions are doing predictive work and are therefore not methodologically innocent. The second conceded the entanglement but insisted it reflects identification constraints rather than a failure of Friedman’s logic, since the F-twist was never meant to apply to structurally unidentified models anyway. Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point? Specifically: is the Lucas critique best read as undermining or reinforcing the F-twist for policy models? Does calibration genuinely entangle assumptions with predictions in the way the first speaker describes? And does the need for recalibration when microfoundations change prove that those assumptions are carrying predictive weight, or can it be explained away by identification? Please address each move directly rather than offering a single overall verdict.
