You are an expert philosopher of mind and affective science, with particular strength in conceptual analysis and the taxonomy of mental states. Before writing, take a moment to brainstorm: list the three standard positions on emotion—cognitivist or judgment-based accounts, perceptual or feeling-based accounts, and hybrid or constructivist accounts—identify exactly where they talk past one another, and sketch one niche conceptual distinction that could reframe the debate without requiring empirical data you do not have.

Now write a polished, two-page research proposal suitable for a one-semester independent study grant or a dissertation prospectus supplement. The project asks: should philosophical analysis abandon the unitary category “emotion” and instead distinguish between “affective registrations”—fast, body-based, non-conceptual evaluative states—and “emotional stances”—slow, conceptually structured, socially accountable commitments to treat a situation in a specific normative light? The central claim to investigate is that the classic debate between judgment theories, perceptual theories, and feeling theories stalls because each camp correctly describes one of these two structures but wrongly assumes that structure exhausts emotional life, creating pseudo-problems about rationality, appropriateness, and moral responsibility.

Your proposal must contain:

1. A precise statement of the philosophical question, framed so that it has a determinate structured answer (e.g., yes, the distinction holds and dissolves specific puzzles; no, the two structures are inseparable in principle; or yes, but only for a subset of cases) rather than an open-ended survey.

2. An explanation of why the existing trichotomy fails to settle the question—specifically, how judgment theories capture the normative accountability of emotional stances while missing the non-conceptual urgency of registrations, how perceptual theories capture bodily immediacy but cannot account for socially regulated emotional displays, and how hybrid accounts conflate the two without explaining their interaction.

3. The method: since this is a philosophical rather than empirical project, describe exactly what you will do. This should include conceptual analysis of emotional reports in ordinary language, examination of boundary cases (for example, ritual grief lacking personal belief, bodily arousal without object-directed evaluation, and shame directed at norms the subject does not endorse), construction of decision criteria for when a registration becomes a stance, and assessment of whether rationality norms apply differently to each structure. Provide a realistic month-by-month plan for one semester (approximately four months).

4. What a successful result looks like—e.g., a defensible taxonomy with clear conditions for membership in each category, plus a diagnosis of at least two classic puzzle cases that the unitary view misdescribes—and what counts as failure, such as the distinction collapsing under analysis, or every actual emotional episode turning out to be an inseparable fusion of both structures.

5. An honest scope assessment: explain why this is tractable in one semester (it relies on conceptual reasoning and case analysis rather than laboratory work or large-scale data), identify the precise point where you must stop (for instance, before extending the taxonomy to clinical classification or to collective emotional practices), and flag any applied questions—such as moral responsibility for actions driven by registrations versus stances—that lie beyond the project’s boundary.

Constraints: Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests. Do not invoke highly niche named theories, obscure historical figures, or specific textual passages that a general philosophical model could not reliably discuss; you may refer to well-known positions in general terms, but paraphrase without quotation marks if you mention specific arguments. Keep the entire proposal answerable through conceptual reasoning alone—no web access should be necessary. Write in a formal, academic register appropriate for a grant or fellowship committee, and conclude with a brief paragraph on why this reframing matters for broader debates in philosophy of mind.
