After a three-hour argument with a friend last night—she insisted that if my grandmother, now in advanced dementia, smiles when spoon-fed and no longer recognizes her children, then the advance directive she signed a decade ago refusing artificial nutrition in exactly this condition should be treated as a dead letter—I want to convert my opposing view into an operational tool. I serve on a hospital ethics consultation service; we field two or three conflicts per month between documented advance directives and a patient's current expressed preferences, we must deliver a signed recommendation within forty-eight hours that the attending physician and the legal surrogate must both endorse, and I have clinical ethics training but no legal background. I need a reusable decision procedure, not an ad hoc opinion.

The principle to operationalize is that the authority of the earlier self is conditional rather than absolute: it binds only when the later self's preference falls outside the "zone of reflective accessibility"—defined here as the range of preferences that can be reconstructed, through interpretive charity, as consistent with the earlier self's documented second-order values, weighted by the reversibility and severity of the intervening cognitive change. Within the zone, the directive governs; outside it, the current preference governs, but only after passing procedural checks.

Please construct a step-by-step procedure my team can execute in under one hour. For each step, specify the exact question, the indicators that count as a sufficient answer, the point at which the procedure terminates with a recommendation to honor the directive, the point at which it terminates with a recommendation to follow the current preference, and the explicit points—clearly marked—where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be referred to the committee's values rather than resolved by clinical fact. The checks must cover: (1) reversibility of the cognitive change (with specific clinical markers); (2) biographical triangulation (whether the current preference aligns with at least two independently documented values from distinct life stages); and (3) affective congruence (whether the emotional quality of the current state coheres with the narrative implied by the directive).

Apply the procedure to two cases. Case one: a seventy-four-year-old with Alzheimer's who executed a directive refusing artificial nutrition upon failure to recognize her children; she now fails to recognize them, eagerly accepts spoon-feeding, and appears content, while her family argues that she always prioritized autonomy over comfort. Case two: a sixty-one-year-old with recurrent severe depression who executed a directive refusing antidepressant medication if she ever requested it during an episode; she is now in such an episode, explicitly refusing medication, and her psychiatrist documents that in three prior remissions she consistently expressed gratitude that her directive had been overridden.

State honestly what the procedure cannot settle—such as conflicts between family members with equal legal standing, ambiguity in the directive's threshold conditions, or cases where biographical records are missing—and identify the specific misfires it will produce. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation you anticipate I will find unwelcome. End with a single final line beginning exactly with "Summary:" followed by one sentence stating the core logic of the procedure.

Summary: The procedure honors an advance directive only when the patient's current preference lies outside a reconstructible zone of consistency with the earlier self's values, after checking reversibility, biographical triangulation, and affective congruence, and it explicitly marks where clinical facts end and normative judgment must take over.
