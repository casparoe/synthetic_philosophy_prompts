I'd like a dialogue between two philosophers of science—call them Aris and Blake—debating whether Dutch-book coherence is a genuinely normative constraint on scientific belief, or only on literal gambling behavior. Set the exchange around a specific, somewhat unconventional case: a climate-hydrology model of a river delta that predicts either gradual salinization or an abrupt, physically undefined regime collapse, where intermediate states are not merely unknown but structurally indeterminate within the model's own equations. Aris should defend the Dutch-book position, arguing that any researcher who allocates funding, advises policy, or publishes risk assessments is implicitly staking resources on outcomes, and that coherence applies to whatever well-chosen observable partition—say, salinity thresholds by a target year—the agent actually treats as decision-relevant. She should invoke the classic pragmatist idea that belief is measured by willingness to bet, but extend it: even if the full ontology is fractured, the scientist still holds precise implicit credences over measurable indicators, and refusing to articulate them leaves her vulnerable to a combination of bets that guarantees loss regardless of which observable outcome occurs.

Blake should push back as an equally competent interlocutor, not a strawman. He should grant that betting markets can be constructed for observable indicators, but argue that the Dutch-book argument smuggles in two questionable presuppositions: that every rational belief state must be representable as a single precise probability function over a fixed sample space, and that the stakes in scientific judgment are symmetric and fungible like monetary wagers. He should press the case that in this delta model, the researcher faces what he terms "ontological discontinuity"—the state space itself fractures—so demanding precise probabilities across an exhaustive partition forces her to resolve a metaphysical question about system identity before she can even form beliefs. He should ask whether Aris's coherence requirement therefore compels scientists to feign precision they do not have, and whether an agent who assigns imprecise or suspended credences is genuinely exploitable, or is instead refusing to enter a betting framework that misdescribes her epistemic position. The exchange should contain real back-and-forth: Aris should distinguish ontological ambiguity from decision-relevant ambiguity, while Blake should address Aris's implicit-bet point by asking whether all resource allocation reduces to quantifiable credence, or whether some scientific commitments are normative rather than probabilistic. End without declaring a winner, leaving intact the tension between coherence as rationality and coherence as misrepresentation. Keep the language technically informed but accessible, avoid any citation of specific papers or page references, and do not ask for bibliographies.
