I retired from a marine biology lab a few years back and since then I've been trying to read philosphy properly, though I still think like an experimentalist. Lately I've been stuck on archives and power — who gets recorded, the silences in the sources, that kind of thing. A friend in my reading group walked me into this argument and now I can't find the exit.

(1) If an archive systematically omits group G because of structural power, then any historical claim about G drawn solely from that archive isn't just incomplete but epistemically unjustified — the silence isn't neutral absence, it's active erasure.
(2) To repair that erasure you need a source of knowledge about G that is independant of the archive, since the archive itself contains no data on G.
(3) But if you already have such an independant source, then the archive is redundant for knowing about G; and if you don't have one, the claim stays unjustified.
(4) So the critical view that "archives are unreliable about the excluded" either forces us to abandon all archive-based claims about excluded groups, or it forces us to treat archives as just one input among many — which dissolves the special critical status of "the archive" as a site of power.
(5) Yet if we dissolve that status, we can't explain why archival silence is worse than ordinary missing data.
(6) Therefore the critique is self-defeating: either it collapses into general scepticism about history, or into bland pluralism about sources, and neither supports the strong claim we started with.

My friend says step (2) is where the slip happens — that correction doesn't need an independant source, just a hermeneutic of suspicion applied inside the archive itself. I don't fully buy that, but I also don't want to land in (6). Is there a quantifier shift or modal slide hiding between (1) and (4)? Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me?
