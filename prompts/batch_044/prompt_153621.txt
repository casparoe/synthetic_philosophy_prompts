Consider the following examination question from a graduate seminar on value, meaning, and the future:

Samuel Scheffler argues that human extinction would be bad in ways that go far beyond the deaths it causes, and he introduces what he calls an afterlife conjecture to explain part of this badness. Explain the conjecture: specify what Scheffler means by afterlife, clarify what he is not claiming, and show why he thinks the prospect of extinction undermines the meaning of present activities rather than merely causing distress about them. Then address this variant: suppose a pathogen eliminates all human life in 2150, but autonomous preservation systems maintain every cultural, scientific, and artistic artifact with perfect fidelity, and even continue certain research programs without human direction. Would Scheffler's argument still imply that lives led in 2100 are severely undermined? Give reasons.

Below is a student's answer:

Scheffler's afterlife conjecture is the claim that much of what makes our lives meaningful depends on the belief that humanity will continue after we die. By afterlife he does not mean personal religious survival, but a collective secular continuation: future generations will exist and carry on the social, scientific, and artistic projects in which we now participate. The conjecture explains why human extinction is catastrophic beyond the deaths involved: extinction removes the background conditions that justify long-term commitments such as raising children, building institutions, or pursuing knowledge that will not be completed in one's lifetime.

In the variant case, where autonomous systems preserve all artifacts and continue research algorithmically after 2150, Scheffler's argument still applies with full force. The problem is not archival loss but the absence of living human agents to participate in the projects. Even perfect preservation does not restore the collective afterlife, because there are no future people whose lives our efforts might affect.

There is, however, a subtle point about the structure of Scheffler's reasoning. He treats our attachment to this collective continuation as analogous to the desire for personal legacy: just as an artist values her work being seen by later viewers, we value humanity persisting to witness and extend our efforts. From this analogy it follows that if autonomous curators not only preserve but actively extend our scientific theories, they might partially substitute for the missing biological posterity, mitigating the loss of meaning. Nevertheless, Scheffler insists that the dependence is constitutive rather than merely psychological. Our projects are structured around the assumption of future human participants, not just around a preference for transmission. Therefore, while the silent-extinction scenario might be somewhat less devastating than total erasure—since the algorithms preserve the form of continuation—it still undermines the meaning of lives in 2100, because the living chain is broken.
