A friend studying moral psychology summarized Bernard Williams and Ruth Benedict for me as follows: Benedict divided societies into guilt-cultures, regulated by private conscience, and shame-cultures, regulated by public exposure; Williams then reversed the usual moral ranking by arguing that shame is actually the deeper, more internal emotion, since it operates through an internalized audience rather than through fear of actual spectators, and therefore binds us more closely to our real selves. From this, my friend concluded that shame is the morally superior emotion and that, because emotional norms are institutionally malleable, a culture can deliberately choose to cultivate shame over guilt through education and public ritual. I suspect this collapses several distinctions philosophers usually keep separate. Is this a fair summary? Please go claim by claim: which parts accurately reflect Benedict's anthropology and Williams's view of the internalized other, which are standard popularizations, which subtly misrepresent the arguments—for example by turning descriptive categories into normative prescriptions, by overstating Williams's comparative evaluation of the two emotions, or by treating institutional design as straightforward emotional engineering—and whether the conclusion that a society can "choose" its dominant moral emotion survives once the summary is corrected.
