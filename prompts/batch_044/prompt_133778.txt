I am composing a scene in my novel in which a jaded professor of intellectual history administers a take-home final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Hume's political economy. I need you to draft the examination question, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a detailed marking scheme. The question must concern Hume's essays 'Of Money' and 'Of Interest,' specifically his claim that an augmentation of the money supply does not instantaneously raise prices but first stimulates industry through a gradual, uneven diffusion. To prevent students from merely summarizing the quantity-theoretic narrative, the prompt should require them to apply Hume's framework of gradual adjustment to a constructed counterfactual: a closed, agrarian economy suddenly issues a large volume of paper notes convertible not into specie but into claims on anticipated tax revenues—an instrument Hume never discusses. A superior answer should reconstruct Hume's implicit assumptions about the velocity of circulation, determine whether his gradualist mechanism survives under this credit-backed issuance, identify the precise point at which nominal and real activity diverge, and evaluate whether this divergence undermines his broader anti-mercantilist argument that national wealth consists in labor and commodities rather than in accumulated paper. The marking scheme should be out of 25 marks, with explicit allocations for reconstructing the gradualist argument (5 marks), isolating the velocity assumption (5 marks), analyzing the paper-credit counterfactual (7 marks), diagnosing the divergence point (5 marks), and assessing the impact on anti-mercantilism (3 marks). For each criterion, specify the particular misconception—such as treating Hume as a strict metallist, ignoring the temporal lag in price adjustment, or conflating his view with modern monetary neutrality—that should lower a grade. Do not ask students to provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from Hume's texts unless they have been verified against a primary source; otherwise, paraphrase his positions. Write in formal American English, suited to an academic setting within literary fiction.
