I'm preparing materials for a graduate seminar on philosophy of science and long-range forecasting, and I've been struck by how often the Malthus case comes up in discussions of whether a theory can be "right but early" rather than simply wrong. To structure the debate precisely, I'd like you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The first camp—call them the Boundary-Condition Rescuers—holds that Malthus correctly identified a structural population-resource mechanism that was genuinely suspended (not refuted) by a transient, fossil-energy-based agricultural subsidy and the associated demographic transition; they maintain that "right but early" is a coherent epistemic category for long-run equilibrium predictions, provided one specifies the observable conditions under which the mechanism reasserts itself. The second camp—the Transition Falsificationists—holds that roughly two centuries of sustained divergence between population growth and subsistence limits constitute a direct falsification of Malthus's predictive core, and that "right but early" is an immunizing maneuver unless it carries a non-arbitrary temporal cutoff after which the absence of the predicted equilibrium counts as failure.

Please produce a document the two camps could jointly publish. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims: include agreement on the basic Malthusian mechanism (positive and preventive checks), on the historical fact of the post-1800 divergence in per-capita resource availability, and on the logical distinction between mechanism failure and boundary-condition relaxation. Then present a disagreements table: each row should state one precise point of remaining divergence, phrased so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position. Identify at least two cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—such as whether the demographic transition represents a temporary interruption or a permanent structural break, and whether the absence of a specified temporal cutoff renders "right but early" unfalsifiable in principle. For each crux, specify the empirical case or argument both sides agree would bear on it (for example, the trajectory of global agricultural energy return on investment over the coming half-century, or the demographic and agricultural response to a sustained, multi-decadal decline in fossil-fuel subsidies), and record in advance each camp's prediction. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the formulation exact enough that neither side can dismiss it as a strawman, but avoid framing either view as obviously irrational.
