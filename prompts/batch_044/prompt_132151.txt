Compare the level-splitting response to higher-order defeating evidence with the uniform-calibration response. The former holds that a rational agent may maintain her first-order belief while downgrading the reliability of the reasoning process that produced it, thereby allowing a gap between first-order confidence and second-order assessment; the latter insists that first-order credence must be revised downward in direct proportion to the strength of the higher-order defeater, so that no such gap can persist. Apply both frameworks to the following case, which introduces an asymmetry rarely present in standard textbook examples: a mathematician working in analytic number theory has constructed a proof of an intermediate lemma. She then encounters strong peer disagreement—three independent specialists each claim the proof contains a subtle error—yet she also possesses well-supported meta-evidence that formal peer review in pure mathematics carries a far lower base rate of false negatives than peer review in empirical disciplines, and that her own previously calibrated cognitive profile (exceptionally high symbolic working-memory capacity, validated against objective proof-verification benchmarks) predicts above-average accuracy in precisely this kind of symbolic error-detection. How does each account accommodate the interaction between this domain-specific reliability data and the direct peer disagreement? Assess which framework yields the more rational revision policy for her first-order belief in the lemma, and determine whether the formal-versus-empirical asymmetry she invokes functions as a legitimate calibrator or as an ad hoc rationalization that one of the two positions is structurally better equipped to expose.
