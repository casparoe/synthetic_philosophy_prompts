I'm constructing a decision tree for a philosophy-of-cognitive-science seminar that sorts responses to the claim that analogy-making is the fundamental mechanism of cognition—associated with the framework developed by Hofstadter and Sander—into distinct positions, without relying on author names to do the sorting. The tree should start from a single root commitment ("Analogical mapping is prior to, and not derived from, symbolic representation") and then branch through concrete diagnostic questions that split positions on genuinely different answers. Each branch node should be phrased as a yes/no or either/or claim that a respondent can answer without knowing which leaf it produces.

The required branches, in order, are:
(1) Whether analogical mapping can operate directly on perceptual surfaces, or whether it always requires an intermediate abstract representation;
(2) Whether "slippage"—the gradual mutation or relaxation of a concept as it is extended analogically—is a feature that drives conceptual innovation or a failure mode that undermines analogical validity;
(3) Whether analogy can generate genuinely novel conceptual structures that were not implicitly present in the source domain, or whether it only recombines pre-existing structures;
(4) Whether linguistic capacity is a downstream product of analogical cognition, or whether language provides the representational scaffold without which analogical mapping cannot occur.

The leaves must include at least: (A) a strong analogy-first position that accepts all four "pro-analogy" answers; (B) a perceptual-surface-only variant that rejects intermediate abstraction but denies slippage is productive; (C) a symbolic-computational rejection that denies analogy is prior; (D) a linguistic-constructivist view that makes analogy derivative; and (E) at least one unoccupied combination of answers, which should be labeled "unnamed" and diagnosed in one sentence (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored).

For every leaf, provide: the combination of answers that leads to it, the name (or "unnamed"), and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—the consequence that defenders of rival views find hardest to accept.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves that share identical answers across all four branches but differ only in emphasis or rhetorical framing, and explain briefly why the tree cannot separate them. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the tree built from logical commitments rather than from a catalog of named theories.
