Consider a restricted version of the no-free-lunch results applied specifically to polynomial-time learners, and an argument that seems to use them to dissolve the rational basis for all empirical reasoning.

(1) Wolpert's no-free-lunch theorems establish that, averaged uniformly over all target functions f: X → Y, every learning algorithm exhibits identical off-training-set error.

(2) This equality is preserved when both the learner and the target class are restricted to polynomial-time computable functions, provided the averaging remains uniform over that restricted class.

(3) Scientific induction—approximated here as polynomial-time Bayesian updating with an Occam-biased prior over computable hypotheses—is one such learner.

(4) Its systematic mirror—an algorithm that, given the same data, always outputs the negation of every inductive generalization that a standard learner would endorse—is also polynomial-time computable.

(5) By (2), summed over all polynomial-time target worlds, the mirror and scientific induction have exactly identical predictive-error profiles.

(6) Rational preference for one reasoning method over another requires that the method have lower expected error in the actual world, not merely equality across an arbitrarily distributed set of worlds.

(7) Any attempt to break the tie by weighting the actual world more heavily—by appealing to its simplicity, lawfulness, or consistency with our prior beliefs—already employs the inductive framework whose justification is in question, and is therefore circular.

(8) Hence there is no non-circular basis for assigning a higher prior to the actual inductive world than to its anti-inductive mirror.

(9) Therefore scientific induction is not rationally preferable to anti-induction.

(10) If scientific induction is not rationally preferable, then no empirical belief formed through it is justified.

(11) But (10) is absurd: we clearly possess justified empirical beliefs.

(12) So one of (1) through (9) must fail, yet the chain feels valid.

The argument seems to slide from an averaging result to a global epistemic conclusion, but I cannot locate the exact slip—whether it is an equivocation between uniform average and actual-world expectation, a hidden dependency of the mirror on the induction it negates, or an unjustified rationality requirement in (6). I want to preserve (1), (2), and (11) if possible, and I am willing to reject (7) if that is the least costly option, though I worry that giving it up may force me into a conventionalist or rationalist grounding for induction that I wanted to avoid. Which step should be rejected? Explain why it looked plausible, what rejecting it costs—especially whether it allows a non-circular weighting of worlds or undermines the demand for global comparative justification—and whether the argument can be repaired by substituting a weaker claim that still blocks (10).
