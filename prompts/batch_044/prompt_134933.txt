Write a dialogue between a deontologist who holds an absolute prohibition on torture and a consequentialist reasoning with explicit probabilities, set in a "fuzzy ticking-bomb" scenario: analysts are 65% sure an attack is imminent, 45% sure the detainee has actionable knowledge, and 25% sure coercion would yield accurate intelligence rather than fabrication. The deontologist should argue that smearing probabilities across a categorical boundary doesn't dissolve the boundary but reveals that the consequentialist is already treating human dignity as a weighted commodity. The consequentialist should respond by pressing whether an absolute prohibition can survive its own epistemic costs—specifically, whether refusing to act on 65% confidence is itself a decision with foreseeable victims, and whether that refusal can be justified without implicitly weighing lives. Have them actually engage: each should answer the other's specific point rather than restating their opening position. Keep both voices equally sharp; neither should be a strawman.
