I serve as deputy chair of our university's Senate Sub-Committee on Doctoral Appeals and Epistemic Accountability, and we are convening next Tuesday to resolve an unusual appeal. A philosophy doctoral candidate—whose dissertation concerned modal epistemology—failed her oral defence not because of factual errors in her answers, but because the examiners found her reasoning 'insufficiently decisive'; she now appeals on the grounds that the examiners themselves lack the epistemic standing required to render such a judgment. Specifically, she argues that since none of the examiners can rule out being brains in vats fed deceptive sensory input about her speech and responses, they cannot claim to know her performance was inadequate; she terms this the 'symmetry of sceptical pressure', contending that any institutional decision applying a high-stakes knowledge standard to a candidate must be satisfied by the decision-makers themselves, or else the standard collapses into arbitrary authority. Our regulations allow appeals on procedural or epistemic grounds but remain silent on whether radical sceptical arguments undermine the validity of examination outcomes, and my co-chair is anxious that accommodating this claim could open the door to every failed candidate invoking Cartesian doubt.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Tuesday's meeting: recommend a concrete course of action—uphold the failure, overturn it, or remand for a reconvened defence with specified procedural safeguards—within a clear time frame, treat the candidate's sceptical argument with philosophical seriousness rather than dismissing it as implausible, and include an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The memo should address whether an institutional body can coherently apply epistemic standards to a candidate that its own members could not meet under sceptical scrutiny, and it should be written in formal British English suitable for circulation among senior academics and external examiners.
