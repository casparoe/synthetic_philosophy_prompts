As a novelist drafting a character who experiences intense satisfaction both in direct sensory moments and in what I am calling “displaced pleasures”—the peculiar affective states taken in strangers’ triumphs she will never witness, or in the recursive awareness that her own enjoyment is being observed—I have become preoccupied with the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, not as a historical footnote but as a live conceptual puzzle: what, if anything, do pleasures have in common? I want you to specify the full list of desiderata that any adequate theory resolving this problem must satisfy. The theory must account for canonical cases—gustatory pleasure, the relief of pain, aesthetic absorption—without reducing them to a single qualitative feel that clearly fails for malicious or vicarious enjoyment; it must preserve the distinction between pleasure and mere desire-fulfillment, since a satisfied desire can leave one cold while an unsatisfied one can feel sweet; it must explain why “shadow pleasures,” those lacking both bodily localization and a clear intentional object, still belong in the category rather than being reclassified as cognitive satisfactions; and it must remain consistent with the observation that pleasure can persist even when every explicit preference is frustrated. For each requirement, give the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind it—why dropping it costs us the phenomenon rather than just a camp’s preference—and mark which are non-negotiable (without which we are no longer talking about pleasure at all) versus which are negotiable commitments of particular traditions, such as the insistence on phenomenological uniformity or the rejection of any pluralist ontology. If any subset of these desiderata proves jointly unsatisfiable—say, if explaining displaced pleasure requires abandoning the very unity that makes hedonism attractive—identify that tension explicitly and show where the fracture occurs; that is the result I care about most. I am not asking for a bibliography, named positions scored against one another, or citations I could not check without access; I want the criteria themselves, weighted by conceptual force. Please also say what argument or counterexample would change your mind and lead you to conclude the heterogeneity problem is genuinely unresolvable, and identify the weakest point in the framework you construct—the place where a critic arguing for pure family resemblance is most likely to break it.
