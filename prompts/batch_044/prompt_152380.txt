I write for a publication that tracks debates at the intersection of political philosophy and public policy, and I am preparing a feature on whether compatriots carry special moral claims that justify preferential treatment in resource distribution, immigration rules, or cultural preservation. The standard framing—universalist cosmopolitanism against associative nationalism—feels exhausted to me; readers tend to sort themselves by team rather than by the actual commitments they hold about concrete trade-offs. I want a diagnostic decision tree with six to eight nodes that classifies positions on this question by asking respondents about specific cases, not about which label they prefer. Each branch should split on a concrete scenario—such as whether a state may prioritize severe domestic poverty relief over an equally severe foreign famine, whether cultural preservation can justify exclusionary immigration even when the culture is defined civically rather than ethnically, or whether shared democratic institutions generate obligations stronger than universal human duties but weaker than kinship ties—so that someone can navigate the tree without knowing any philosophical names.

The tree should be constructed from the logical space first: map the combinations of answers, then assign actual positions to the leaves, including pure cosmopolitanism; a strong nationalist view that grounds priority in shared history or culture; liberal nationalism that treats compatriot preference as a permissible tie-breaker within universal duties; and a hybrid institutional-cosmopolitan view that accepts associative attachments but denies they generate distributive priority. You should also identify any logically possible but apparently unoccupied combinations—perhaps a view that accepts deep associative obligations yet rejects all state-level partiality, or one that justifies compatriot priority purely on efficiency grounds without any moral weight—and diagnose briefly whether each gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Specify the audience as scientifically literate general readers, avoid undefined jargon, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or further reading.

The deliverable should not be a neutral catalog: take a definite stance on which distinctions actually do the sorting work—argue, for example, whether the root split should concern distributive versus non-distributive domains, or identity-based versus institution-based association, and defend why your chosen root question is the right starting point. Explicitly flag which positions the tree cannot separate despite differing in metaphysical grounding, and explain whether that practical convergence undermines the distinction or merely conceals it. Where your taxonomy injects or relies on less familiar conceptual moves—such as treating democratic co-membership as generating associative weight without ethnic content, or separating tie-breaking permission from distributive weighting—make those commitments explicit and give your reasons for treating them as central rather than peripheral.
