Question: In philosophy of science, ontological commitment is often constrained by parsimony: we should avoid multiplying entities beyond what our best theories require. Consider a case of symmetric inaccessibility. Two ontological schemes, designated Alpha and Beta, are empirically equivalent with respect to every observation that is structurally available within the framework, but they differ only in commitments regarding a domain that is shielded not by practical limitation but by a formal boundary condition—specifically, a sector that the governing equations explicitly decouple from all observable dynamics. Some philosophers argue that in such cases we should identify the schemes and adopt the sparser ontology; others hold that agnosticism is the only warranted response. Now suppose the inaccessibility is perfectly symmetric: from within Alpha the hidden sector of Beta is unreachable, and vice versa, with no asymmetry in explanatory power or predictive success. Does this symmetry provide an independent reason to identify Alpha and Beta ontologically, or does it instead weaken the standard parsimony argument? Defend your answer, taking care to distinguish epistemic indistinguishability from ontological identity.

Student answer: The student begins by noting that parsimony counsels against positing entities that perform no explanatory work, and observes that in the symmetric case neither hidden sector can ever explain observable phenomena within the opposing scheme. They then claim that because Alpha and Beta stand in a relation of mutual translation—similar to the kind of ontological relativity discussed by Quine—the hidden sectors are merely notational variants and should be collapsed into a single ontology. The student further argues that agnosticism is inappropriate because treating the hidden sectors as genuinely distinct possibilities implicitly grants them ontological weight, which conflicts with parsimony. At the same time, the student insists that epistemic indistinguishability is not generally identical to ontological identity, illustrating this with quantum particles that share all observable properties yet remain numerically distinct. They conclude that in the present case identification is forced specifically because there is no relational structure capable of distinguishing the hidden domains, so the principle of the identity of indiscernibles applies directly.
