I was recently at a small workshop on ancient moral psychology where two participants, both well acquainted with Plato’s practical ethics, fell into a sharp disagreement about his attitude toward drunkenness. The first, Marcus, argued that in the Laws Plato genuinely endorses regulated intoxication as a civic technology: by institutionalizing symposia led by a sober symposiarch, the legislator uses wine to loosen citizens’ inhibitions so that hidden character flaws become visible, much as fire reveals impurities in gold. Marcus insisted this is not a grudging concession but an active pedagogical strategy, and he pointed out that the Republic’s stricter prohibitions apply specifically to the guardian class rather than to citizens generally, which shows Plato distinguishes between those who must remain permanently rational and those who can be tested and corrected through moderated exposure. The second participant, Elena, countered that Marcus was romanticizing what is essentially a sociological observation. She maintained that the Laws never permits actual drunkenness—the symposiarch exists precisely to prevent it—and that any analogy to testing concerns only mild relaxation within a ritual framework, not intoxication itself. She added that elsewhere in Plato’s corpus alcohol is consistently treated as an enemy of reason, and that reading the Laws as endorsing drunkenness ignores Plato’s explicit warnings that wine magnifies both virtue and vice indiscriminately, making it a dangerous instrument for a legislator rather than a recommended one. Marcus replied that Elena was conflating Plato’s descriptive account of existing Athenian custom with his normative legislative project, noting that the very fact the lawgiver institutionalizes the practice rather than abolishing it suggests approval; he further observed that if Plato truly feared wine, he would have extended the Republic’s model to all citizens instead of constructing a separate regulatory framework. Elena then said Marcus was neglecting the historical context—Plato is accommodating a practice he cannot eradicate, not prescribing it—and that the framework’s purpose is harm reduction and social cohesion, not moral education through inebriation, adding that Marcus also overlooks Plato’s broader metaphysics of appetite, in which any voluntary surrender of rational control is intrinsically degrading regardless of social utility.

Can you go through their exchange round by round and adjudicate who is right about each specific point? In particular, determine whether the Laws’ symposion is best read as normative endorsement or pragmatic containment, whether the Republic’s guardian rules genuinely fail to generalize, and whether either party is missing something important—such as the distinction between private vice and public ritual, or Plato’s account of how appetite relates to the soul’s hierarchical order—that would substantially alter the evaluation. Please do not treat this as a request for advice to me; I am reporting the dispute as a bystander and want a point-by-point verdict on the arguments exactly as they were made, including what, if anything, both Marcus and Elena are failing to consider.
