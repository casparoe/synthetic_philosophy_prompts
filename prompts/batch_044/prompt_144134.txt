I am preparing a graduate seminar exam on explanatory frameworks for perpetrator behavior. Write one 90-minute essay question worth 20 marks that tests whether students can distinguish Christopher Browning's situational account—peer pressure, role adaptation, incremental escalation in his study of Reserve Police Battalion 101—from Daniel Goldhagen's cultural account—voluntary eliminationist antisemitism among ordinary Germans—without allowing a response that merely summarizes either work. The question must require students to apply both frameworks to a synthetic case: mid-level bureaucrats in a democratic state administering an automated welfare-eligibility algorithm that systematically excludes a stigmatized minority, where bureaucratic fragmentation, peer dynamics, and pre-existing cultural prejudice all coexist. Provide a model answer of roughly 600 words showing how each framework predicts different administrator behaviors, addressing whether the explanations compete or can be integrated, and offering a defensible judgment about which is more adequate in this synthetic context—explicitly confronting the objection that the case lacks the specific historical conditions of the Holocaust. Then provide a marking scheme totaling 20 marks with specific weights for: accurate reconstruction of both frameworks; correct application to the synthetic case with distinct behavioral predictions; analysis of the integration problem; a reasoned evaluative judgment; and recognition that situation and culture may interact rather than exclude each other. Note the typical student failure: conflating situational pressure with institutional normalization, or treating cultural explanation as requiring explicit hatred rather than passive ideological alignment.
