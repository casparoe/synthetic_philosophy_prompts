ok so our constitutional review panel has been issuing advisory opinions on church-state setups for about a decade and now we're stuck. heres our track record:

- we said england can keep bishops in the lords and use tax money to repair cathedrals, as long as its framed as heritage preservation not worship subsidy.
- we said germany's kirchensteuer is okay because citizens can formally opt out by leaving their registered church, even though that costs them sacramental rights.
- we said france can ban headscarves for public school teachers but not for students, drawing the line at state agents vs private citizens.
- we said the US can't pay direct salaries to clergy of one denomination, but can let religious orgs use public school buildings after hours on the same terms as anyone else.
- we rejected a proposal where citizens could direct 0.5% of income tax to religious bodies but secular humanist groups were left off entirely.

now there's a new proposal: a country with no official church wants taxpayers to direct 1% of income tax to either a registered religious community or a registered secular ethical organization. but only groups with continuous legal registration since before 1950 qualify. that covers the big churches and one old humanist federation, but excludes newer evangelical movements, recent muslim associations, and any secular groups founded after 1950.

what should we recommend if we want to stay consistent? which of our five precedents is the closest analog? i can see at least two principles that fit our record: one that protects historical continuity and cultural preservation, another that demands equal access regardless of how old the group is. tell me what each predicts about this 1950 cutoff, which one you think we're actually running, and name the hypothetical case that'd finally split them. also if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the call, say so and explain what principle we'd be adopting either way.
