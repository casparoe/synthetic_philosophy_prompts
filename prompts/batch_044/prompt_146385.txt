I am a novelist presently drafting a historical fiction in which an archive of neural recordings is found to contain not forged documents but interpolated memories—gaps between authentic recollections filled with synthetic experience using principles analogous to the colour phi phenomenon. In discussion with fellow writers, I have begun to wonder whether this fictional mechanism is not, in fact, a literal description of ordinary cognition.

Since the colour phi effect demonstrates that conscious visual experience is reconstructed retrospectively—our brains generate the perception of continuous motion only after receiving discrete frames—it strikes me that all historical awareness must be similarly retrospective and constructed. Given this, if every moment of consciousness is already an implicit revision of discontinuous data into a coherent narrative, is there any substantive philosophical difference between an Orwellian rewriting of history and a Stalinesque erasure? Or does the distinction collapse once we recognise that there never was an unmediated, original experience to corrupt—only a perpetual, neurologically necessary reconstruction that state revisionism merely renders explicit?
