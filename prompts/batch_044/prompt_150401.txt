The exam asked students to work through Nietzsche's three modes of history in the second Untimely Meditation—monumental, antiquarian, and critical. Specifically, I wanted them to address whether monumental history's 'deception' is best understood as an epistemic failure (like making false causal claims about repetition) or as a practical, life-promoting illusion that doesn't claim literal accuracy; and whether antiquarian preservation can really be kept seperate from critical judgment, given that deciding what counts as worth saving already involves evaluation.

Here is the answer exactly as written: Nietzsche distinguishes three ways history can serve life: monumental, antiquarian, and critical. Monumental history looks at great past deeds to inspire present action. It can deceive us, though, because it picks out exceptional moments and makes them seem like direct models we can copy, which is an epistemic problem since those moments were unique and history doesn't actually repeat in that causal way. Antiquarian history preserves the past out of reverence, keeping traditions alive by collecting and safeguarding artifacts and customs in a basically passive way. Critical history judges the past and breaks away from it when it becomes a burden, serving life negatively by destroying what no longer helps us grow. The antiquarian and critical modes are basically seperate functions: preservation is conservative and non-judgmental, while criticism is active and destructive, so they don't overlap at all. All three are useful, but monumental is the riskiest because its deception involves false causal claims about history repeating itself.
