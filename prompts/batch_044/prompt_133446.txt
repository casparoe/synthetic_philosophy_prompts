Student answer:

Berkeley's master argument, presented in the First Dialogue between Hylas and Philonous, attempts to show that material substance is literally inconceivable. Philonous asks Hylas to try to form an idea of a tree existing in a garden with no one present to perceive it. The attempt necessarily fails because any idea of the tree is itself an idea within a perceiving mind, which demonstrates that the notion of an object existing unperceived is contradictory. This directly targets materialism by proving that mind-independent matter is impossible.

Certain reconstructions strengthen the argument by adding a premise of perceptual invariance—the claim that if matter existed, its sensible properties would remain constant regardless of observer. A modern materialist can reject this invariance for secondary qualities like color and sound while retaining primary qualities such as extension and motion as genuinely mind-independent. Berkeley blocks this retreat in the Principles by denying the primary-secondary distinction altogether; once that distinction collapses, there is no hidden substrate left to defend.

What is frequently missed is that the master argument already contains an implicit premise about temporal continuity. For an object to exist during moments when no human perceives it, its perception must be sustained by an omnipresent mind. Therefore the argument does not merely establish that unperceived matter is inconceivable; it simultaneously proves that God must be the continuous perceiver who maintains reality between human observations. Without this theological component, the master argument would leave dangerous gaps in existence during universal inattention, so Berkeley's case against materialism is inseparable from his proof of divine perpetual perception.
