Write a deadpan bureaucratic memorandum of approximately 650 words, formatted with standard memo headers (To, From, Date, Re, Classification), in the voice of a mid-level official at the fictional Ministry of Historical Continuity in the Republic of Velmor. The Republic was administratively created in 1992; the memo addresses regional governors about maintaining the state’s official 800-year continuity narrative. The document should detail, in exhaustive administrative prose, four operational areas: (1) linguistic standardization—promoting a dialect codified in 1995 as the “ancient national tongue” and suppressing regional variants in school examinations; (2) ritual manufacture—updating the guidelines for the National Harvest Festival, invented in 1983, including the correct embroidery patterns for “traditional” costumes designed in 2001; (3) census categorization—revising the decennial survey forms so that citizens select from ethnic categories created by the 1998 Administrative Ethnography Project, and explaining how to handle respondents who claim identities that predate the categories; (4) monumental rehabilitation—procedures for renaming public spaces after pre-1992 figures who have been retroactively promoted from local outlaws to national founders, including a checklist for removing contradictory archival references. Introduce an invented bureaucratic metric, the “Continuity Coefficient,” calculated from years of family participation in state rituals, language-test scores, and census-category consistency, with a sliding scale for immigrants who may enroll in “retrospective heritage workshops” to accumulate points toward full civic belonging. The satire must be sustained and deadpan: the author should never wink, never use irony markers, and never state that the traditions are invented or the community imagined; the absurdity must arise solely from the meticulous administrative logic applied to the construction of a nation. The target is the theoretical claim—associated with the study of invented traditions and imagined communities—that modern nation-states depend on bureaucratic, educational, and commemorative machinery to produce the appearance of ancient, continuous, and homogeneous identity. The memo should reward a reader familiar with those arguments without requiring knowledge of specific texts, and should avoid all citations, bibliographies, named scholars, and verbatim quotations. Keep the language formal, precise, and institutionally plausible.
