I serve as the lead mechanism-classification officer at a reliability-testing consortium for autonomous distributed systems. Every week my team must decide, within thirty minutes and without consulting external literature, whether a newly detected anomaly is an instance of a previously catalogued failure mechanism. The stakes are sharply asymmetric: a correct "same mechanism" classification lets us apply an existing containment protocol and avoid a mandatory three-week fleet shutdown; a misclassification of a genuinely novel failure as familiar risks propagating an unmitigated error mode. The conceptual difficulty is that mechanistic identity is not reducible to shared error messages (functional equivalence) or to shared physical parts (structural correspondence). Drawing on the philosophy of mechanism—where identity involves organizational continuity among entities and activities, together with the stability of counterfactual dependencies under intervention—I need an operational decision procedure that engineers without philosophical training can run consistently.

Please construct a reusable procedure: an ordered sequence of no more than six tests or questions, each specifying what counts as a sufficient "yes" or "no," where a negative answer stops the process and mandates full root-cause investigation, and where ambiguity forces an explicit hand-off to human judgment because the remaining dispute is about risk tolerance rather than mechanistic fact. The procedure must distinguish four axes—component correspondence, organizational closure, functional profile, and counterfactual dependency preservation—and must state which axes may be simplified or dropped in a time-pressured setting without destroying the core concept.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

1. A mesh-networked swarm navigation module exhibits timing desynchronization with the exact error-signature profile of a prior hierarchical-controller failure, but the organizational architecture is peer-to-peer rather than centralized, and the counterfactual profile shows that removing any single node does not eliminate the failure, whereas removing the central controller did eliminate the prior one.

2. A bio-hybrid actuator fails under thermal stress with phenotypic output identical to a previous purely mechanical actuator failure, yet every physical component and causal intermediary differs; however, the organizational boundaries (the entity-activity mapping) and the intervention-response patterns are isomorphic to the catalogued case.

3. A cognitive-architecture classification module reproduces the same misclassification pattern documented six months earlier, including identical intermediate representations, but the feedback loop that mediated error correction in the original case is absent in the new architecture, altering how the error propagates across nearby-world variations.

In each application, state the procedure's verdict, flag any step where judgment replaces algorithmic output, and ensure that at least one case—specifically case 1 or 2—produces a verdict your team will resist (either forcing an unwanted shutdown or permitting an unwanted containment). Finally, specify exactly what the procedure cannot do: which forms of mechanistic novelty it is blind to, which simplifications distort the underlying philosophical account, and under what conditions the team should override the output entirely.
