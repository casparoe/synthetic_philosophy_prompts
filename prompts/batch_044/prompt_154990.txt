I am taking a graduate seminar on philosophical accounts of value and the good life, and over the past few weeks we have been applying Schlick’s framework—on which life gains meaning not through the attainment of some external purpose but through full absorption in an activity pursued for its own sake, like play—to a range of concrete cases. Our instructor has delivered consistent verdicts across the following examples: a professional cellist who practices eight hours daily solely to maintain her orchestra seat lacks meaning; the same cellist, after retirement, playing identical repertoire alone at home for no audience and no reward, has meaning; a pharmaceutical researcher running clinical trials exclusively to satisfy regulatory milestones and secure promotion lacks meaning; that same researcher, after leaving the industry, repeating the experiments in a private garage out of pure intellectual curiosity, has meaning; a chef preparing an elaborate tasting menu for paying customers and viewing the work purely as labor lacks meaning; and the same chef cooking an identical meal for close friends, savoring the craft itself, has meaning. Next week we will examine a new case: a high-school mathematics teacher spends her evenings over many months developing an unconventional proof of a minor number-theoretic conjecture. She expects no publication, has no audience, and describes herself as genuinely captivated by the logical structure; yet she also admits that the remote possibility of eventual recognition, should she succeed, helps sustain her through periods of frustration, though she insists she would continue even if that hope vanished entirely. Predict our instructor’s verdict—does this teacher’s activity have meaning on Schlick’s view?—and lay out the competing rules that fit her earlier track record equally well. One interpretation might hold that any instrumental purpose present in the agent’s psychology, however faint or backgrounded, contaminates the activity and robs it of play-like meaning; another might hold that only a dominant or sustaining instrumental purpose does so, allowing incidental or remote motives to coexist with genuine meaning. Give a committed answer: which rule best explains her prior judgments, what she should say about the teacher, and specify a further case—perhaps an artist who accepts commissions she genuinely loves, or a volunteer whose service is partly sustained by the feeling of virtue—that would decisively separate the two interpretations.
