I am preparing a thirty-minute colloquium presentation defending a revisionist reading of Strawson's reactive-attitude framework. My central claim is that the canonical reactive-versus-objective binary obscures a structurally distinct intermediate stance—which I term the prognostic stance—that we routinely adopt in institutional settings where behavior is predicted, managed, and preempted through data-driven models (for example, in algorithmic risk assessment or predictive mental-health screening). Unlike reactive attitudes, prognostic stances lack the emotional phenomenology of resentment or gratitude; unlike the purely objective attitude, they retain normative weight, carrying demands for justification, accountability, and corrective intervention. I argue that this stance is not a diluted reactive attitude but a separate category, and that recognizing it undermines the standard compatibilist argument: determinism in modern institutional contexts does not push us toward Strawsonian objectification, but rather toward this normatively loaded prognostic posture, which preserves moral concern without requiring libertarian free will.

The audience is a joint philosophy-cognitive science colloquium of about twelve people: four moral philosophers for whom Strawson's canonical essay is settled and largely exhaustive of the conceptual landscape; three philosophers of predictive processing who are skeptical of importing technology-studies examples into classic metaphysical debates; two legal scholars focused on algorithmic accountability; and one hard determinist who regards any revision of Strawson as an evasion of the real problem. The talk will be followed by a forty-minute Q&A.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually receive from this specific mix. Order them by how damaging they are likely to be to my proposal, not by logical sequence. For each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary and conceptual commitments of the subgroup that will raise it. Provide a concise reply suitable for about ninety seconds of speaking time, and explicitly flag the weakest point in that reply. Identify the one objection I am most hoping nobody asks—specifically, the challenge that exposes the tension between my claim that prognostic attitudes are genuinely normative and my reliance on institutional practices that several audience members will regard as merely instrumental or bureaucratic rather than morally engaged. Finally, for each of the four subgroups, specify one concession I should make in my opening remarks rather than try to defend in the Q&A, and sketch briefly what that concession should sound like. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; I need a speaker's prep sheet, not a literature review.
