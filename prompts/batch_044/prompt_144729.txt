Following a long and inconclusive argument with a friend about whether Buddhist anattā merely weakens or fully dissolves the grounds for moral responsibility, I want to examine a stricter reading than the one usually offered in philosophy of religion. Rather than treating no-self as a variant of Western bundle theory that still permits narrative coherence, suppose we take the Abhidharmic picture literally: experience consists of momentary, impersonal dharmas with no enduring substrate and, crucially, no coherence conditions that could support narrative identity across time. Please describe the most important implications of this stricter anattā—one that denies both substantial and narrative continuity—for three specific issues: the logic of moral apology (can a causal stream apologize without owning its past actions?), the justification of retributive punishment (if there is no continuing subject of harm, what remains of desert?), and the coherence of personal growth or spiritual progress (how can a practitioner improve if there is no self to improve?). Focus on the conceptual structure rather than textual scholarship, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
