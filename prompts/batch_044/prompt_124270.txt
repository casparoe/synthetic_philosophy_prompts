I'm working through my own intuitions about epistemic justification by testing them against concrete scenarios rather than rehearsing abstract labels. Here are my verdicts. If I accept a mathematical result because I can derive it from axioms I do not further justify, the result feels fully justified; the bare commitment at the bottom does not seem to travel upward and infect it. If I believe there is a coffee cup in front of me because I see it, the justification terminates right there in the experience itself, not in another belief. If I believe a distant city was flooded on the basis of a single news report, I feel tentatively justified but also that I have taken on an epistemic debt—should the source fail, my belief collapses, and I have not distributed that risk. If ten independent sources report the same flood, my confidence jumps, yet the justification still feels horizontal, spread across a network of mutual support rather than anchored downward; it is stronger than the single-source case but structurally different from the mathematical or perceptual ones. If I hold that causing unnecessary suffering is wrong, I can defend it only by appeal to other moral judgments and to my emotional responses, with no non-moral bedrock; nevertheless I treat it as justified enough to guide action, though the structure feels circular rather than linear.

The cases that keep pulling me in opposite directions are these. Imagine a belief B1 supported by B2, which is supported by B3, extending infinitely backward with no loop and no first member. At first this feels like standing on an infinite ladder—no real support at all. Yet if I imagine actually traversing each link and finding each genuinely supportive, I cannot quite declare B1 unjustified; it simply never lands on anything self-evident. By contrast, my belief that my childhood memories are broadly accurate is sustained by family accounts, photographs, and my present identity, each of which could in principle be questioned but none of which are; there is no external foundation, yet the circular coherence feels stable enough to live with. So an infinite linear chain feels shaky, while a circular web feels acceptable, even though both lack an ultimate ground. Is there a coherent principle—foundationalist, coherentist, infinitist, or a precise hybrid—that captures all of these verdicts, or must some be overturned? If you defend a hybrid, specify exactly what distinguishes an acceptable anchor from an acceptable node from an acceptable open-ended chain, and apply your principle to a case I have not listed: what should I say about my belief in the existence of other minds, which I hold largely because others behave as if they have them, and that belief in turn helps justify my interpretation of their behavior?
