I am a philosophy professor presenting to an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind workshop. My thesis is that the standard Malcolm-Dennett framing of whether dreams are experiences depends on a false dichotomy between occurrent phenomenal states and post-hoc narrative fabrication. I defend a hybrid account: during REM sleep the brain sustains structured neural representations that possess intentional content but lack occurrent phenomenal character—thereby avoiding Malcolm's verificationist criterion for experience—while upon awakening these representations are reconstructed through a memory mechanism that retroactively introduces phenomenology into the report. This means dream reports are neither direct descriptions of sleeping consciousness, as critics of Malcolm often assume, nor pure confabulations playing back inert records, as a common reading of Dennett's alternative suggests.

The audience is precise: about twelve analytic philosophers of mind who take verificationist worries about first-person authority seriously; two cognitive neuroscientists who regularly invoke REM neuroimaging and dream-decoding results; and three phenomenologists who treat first-person dream reports as authoritative data for consciousness studies. They are collectively skeptical of middle positions that borrow vocabulary from both camps without resolving the tension.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet for this audience. I need the following, written in American English and without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

First, list the seven or eight objections I will actually face, ordered from most damaging to my position to least damaging. For each, indicate which subgroup is likely to raise it, phrase it in the vocabulary that subgroup uses, and provide a concise reply appropriate for about two minutes of speaking time. Include a brief note on where each reply is weakest or concedes ground.

Second, identify the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—the dreaded question—explain precisely why it threatens my account, and advise how I should respond if it arises.

Third, tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks to lower the temperature, and specify which element of my argument must land cleanly for the proposal to survive the session.

The objections should target my specific hybrid claim about proto-experiential dispositions and retroactive phenomenological reconstruction, not generic objections to dream skepticism or to neuroreductionism.
