I'm working through Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur, specifically a passage in which she treats happiness not as a static possession but as a dynamic equilibrium that must be actively sustained. She contends that human beings require certain sustaining illusions—particularly about the durability of intellectual passions and the enduring value of study—to ward off the lethargy and ennui that accompany complete transparency about one's own limitations. At the same time, she insists that these illusions must be rationally managed rather than blindly embraced, since unexamined delusion collapses into misery. To develop this claim, she draws a comparison with physical motion: just as a body in motion does not persist without continuous impetus, a happy life does not persist without continuously renewed, self-aware illusion. She deploys this comparison against religious asceticism, arguing that a program that seeks to eliminate all illusion also removes the very impetus necessary to make life bearable.

I'm struggling to understand exactly what philosophical work the comparison is doing. Is she importing her own interpretation of Leibnizian vis viva into moral psychology, or is she relying on a more generic mechanics of forces? How precisely does the correspondence run—does the illusion serve as the impetus, does rational self-management serve as the directing constraint, and does the collapse into misery correspond to the dissipation of motion? Where does the analogy strain, given that physical impetus is typically external or determined by mass, whereas psychological impetus appears self-generated and reflexive? And does her conclusion—that happiness is an actively maintained state incompatible with perfect self-knowledge—follow from the dynamics she describes, or does it depend on independent premises about human psychology?

Please help me understand the passage: lay out the correspondence table she seems to intend, distinguish the disanalogies that matter for her argument from those that do not, and assess whether her critique of asceticism survives once the physical model is audited. If the analogy is carrying more weight than it should, what is a more precise framing of her claim about happiness, illusion, and rational self-management?
