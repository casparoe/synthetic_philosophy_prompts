Write a dialogue between two moral philosophers—Dr. Aris Thorne and Dr. Lena Okonkwo—who defend opposing positions on whether the numbers should count in rescue ethics, in the tradition of John Taurek’s argument that we lack reason to save the greater number when each person has an equal claim to be saved. The exchange should be rigorous and balanced: neither speaker should be a strawman, and each must respond directly to the other’s actual arguments rather than talking past them.

Frame the debate around the following non-standard distinction. Thorne should defend a strict anti-aggregationist view that rejects not only the summation of welfare across persons, but also any selection procedure—such as a lottery—whose probabilities are weighted by group size. He should argue that weighting by size smuggles aggregation back into the decision through procedural fairness, violating the separateness of persons. Okonkwo should defend a “pairwise non-aggregative lottery” view: we compare individuals one-to-one, assign equal rescue priority to each, and when direct comparison yields no ordering we employ a fair procedure that gives every person an identical ex ante chance, regardless of cluster size. She should argue that this preserves anti-aggregationist commitments without yielding the counterintuitive result that we may save one rather than fifty.

To prevent the dialogue from collapsing into familiar slogans, embed the following concrete scenario and conceptual twist. The case is sequential, not binary: a single vessel can make exactly one trip now, and—only if that trip is brief—might make a second trip later. The nearby cluster contains three people in calm waters; the distant cluster contains thirty in a storm, but reaching them consumes all fuel and eliminates any second trip. Crucially, introduce “interdependent survival” within each cluster: the three share no essential resources and could in principle be rescued individually, whereas the thirty are linked by a single shared life-raft and water supply, so saving any subset requires saving all thirty. Have Thorne and Okonkwo debate whether this structural interdependence undermines the lottery approach or instead reveals that anti-aggregationism tacitly relies on an unrealistic moral atomism. They should also address whether the possibility of a second trip introduces a form of “dynamic aggregation” across time—counting future lives saved against present ones—that the anti-aggregationist must reject, and whether rejecting it commits one to an implausible presentism about moral reasons.

The dialogue should run approximately 800–1000 words, with genuine philosophical back-and-forth: each speaker should anticipate an objection, answer it, and then have the other identify a specific flaw in that answer. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for page numbers. Keep the language precise but accessible, suitable for an advanced undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy. The deliverable is the dialogue itself, not a summary or commentary upon it.
