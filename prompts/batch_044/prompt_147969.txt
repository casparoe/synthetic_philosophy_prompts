I am writing a position statement for a philosophy of science seminar on temporal asymmetry, and because the topic has become personally urgent after a recent disagreement with a colleague, I want to test whether my commitments actually cohere before I present them. My views are these. I accept that the physical arrow of time is entirely grounded in the Past Hypothesis—the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state—and that there are no fundamentally time-asymmetric laws of dynamics; statistical mechanics does all the explanatory work. At the same time, I am convinced that causal asymmetry is conceptually primitive: for one event to cause another is necessarily for it to precede it, and this directional feature is not reducible to correlations, counterfactual dependencies, or entropy gradients. I am also a B-theorist about ontology: there is no ontologically privileged present, no moving spotlight of presentness, only tenseless earlier-than and later-than relations arranged in a four-dimensional block. Nevertheless, I insist that there remains an objective metaphysical direction to time—earlier-to-later is not symmetric—without this entailing any phenomenological flow. On the psychological side, I hold that our asymmetrical relation to past and future—remembering what we call the past, anticipating the other direction—is fully explained by the entropic gradient together with evolutionary selection for predictive tracking, leaving no residual explanatory gap that requires phenomenal primitives or a special phenomenology of passage. Finally, I maintain that in a hypothetical local region undergoing sustained entropy decrease, the causal order would not reverse: causes would still precede effects in exactly the same orientation, producing only thermodynamic anomalies rather than a flipped causal structure. Do these five commitments hang together, or are there hidden fractures? I suspect there may be tension between my reduction of the physical arrow to boundary conditions and my insistence on primitive causal asymmetry, and I wonder whether my B-theoretic denial of a moving present conflicts with my claim that there is an objective metaphysical direction. But I want a full audit: examine every pair, distinguish real conflicts from merely apparent ones, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each specify what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. I also want to know which single revision, if I made it, would resolve the most problems with the least damage to my overall picture.
