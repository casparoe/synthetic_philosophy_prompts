I am a retired experimental physicist who, after leaving the laboratory, has begun studying philosophy of logic and science with some seriousness. Recently, in a discussion group, a fellow reader mentioned Lewis Carroll's dialogue between the Tortoise and Achilles, in which Achilles attempts to justify a simple deductive step and is led into an infinite regress of ever-higher rules of inference. The problem struck me as analogous to a difficulty I encountered in my own career: the calibration of fundamental measurement standards, such as the historical transition from the physical meter bar to a definition grounded in the speed of light, where the defining measurement itself seems to presuppose the very unit being established.

I would like you to write an essay that examines whether Carroll's tortoise genuinely undermines any non-circular justification of deduction, or whether available responses—including appeals to pragmatic self-support, semantic ascent, or evolutionary accounts of rationality—successfully arrest the regress without begging the question. Your essay should weigh the main arguments on both sides, but it must not end in agnosticism: defend a specific verdict, such as that the regress is fatal, that it is dissolved by distinguishing use from justification, or that it rests on an implausible demand for external validation of basic inference. In making your case, please draw the comparison with scientific calibration explicitly: does the practical success of a self-referential standard in physics suggest that circularity in justification is harmless, or does it indicate that the demand for non-circular justification was misapplied to logical rules in the first place? Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in your reasoning rather than in scholarly apparatus.
