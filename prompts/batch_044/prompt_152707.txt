David Lewis's analysis of convention explains persistent behavioral regularities—such as driving on the right or using a particular currency—as solutions to coordination games sustained by common knowledge of mutual expectations. Critics have long objected that this framework cannot distinguish a genuine convention from a mere habit, or from a pattern maintained by coercive asymmetry, since all three can generate identical equilibria. To press this issue, introduce the following refinement: call a regularity a reflexive convention only if the participants share not just first-order common knowledge that they conform, but second-order common knowledge that they are mutually accountable for maintaining the regularity, and that this very accountability is recognized as part of the practice itself.

Please describe the most important implications of this reflexive-Lewisian framework for understanding the evolution and fragility of democratic norms—specifically norms of peaceful concession and loyal opposition after electoral defeat. Take a definite, committed position: argue either that the reflexive condition successfully distinguishes conventions from habits and coercion, and therefore explains why democratic norms collapse precisely when second-order accountability erodes, or that it fails because it imposes epistemic demands that real-world political actors rarely meet, making the distinction theoretically neat but practically empty. Give clear reasons for your choice, anticipate and respond to the strongest counter-argument, and avoid merely cataloguing competing interpretations without defending one.
