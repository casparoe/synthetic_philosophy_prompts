I'm writing a diagnostic exercise for a graduate philosophy of history seminar on overdetermination and multi-causal explanation. Rather than asking for a literature survey, I need a taxonomy in tree form—built from logical space, not from a catalog of named theories—that sorts positions by how they answer questions about a concrete case.

Frame the root case consistently: in 1989 a regime collapses, and historians advance three apparently sufficient explanations—long-term structural economic decay, a contingent diplomatic blunder, and a rapid mass-mobilization cascade. The tree should branch on three logical distinctions that a student can answer without recalling scholar labels: (i) whether causal overdetermination is genuinely possible in history, or whether every apparent case resolves into one dominant sufficient condition once background conditions are fully specified; (ii) whether accepting overdetermination undermines counterfactual reasoning—specifically, whether it remains coherent to ask whether the collapse would still have occurred absent the diplomatic blunder if economic decay was already sufficient; and (iii) whether narrative coherence requires selecting a single dominant turning point, or whether a historical account can integrate multiple sufficient causal arcs without reducing one to the other.

From these branches derive exactly six leaves. Four must correspond to occupied positions: (A) resolutionist monism, which denies that overdetermination survives full causal specification; (B) counterfactual eliminativism, which accepts overdetermination but treats counterfactual questions about the past as malformed; (C) modal pluralism, which preserves counterfactuals by indexing each explanation to a distinct modality of historical change (structural, contingent, agential); and (D) level-sensitive pluralism, which holds that the three explanations are non-competing because they operate at different explanatory scales. The remaining two leaves should be logically permitted combinations that appear unoccupied; label one 'unnamed' and diagnose in one sentence whether its emptiness stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The other should represent a view that distinguishes 'causal overdetermination' from 'narrative overdetermination'—the claim that even when two causes are each sufficient, a historian must still select one as the dominant narrative thread, not because the other is causally redundant, but because narrative intelligibility demands a determinable turning point.

For every leaf provide its name (or 'unnamed') and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—the specific claim its defender must accept. Also flag any pair of leaves that your branching questions fail to separate, with a brief explanation of why the answer patterns collapse. Do not include bibliographies, citations, named scholars, or requests for further reading; the instrument should be usable by a student who answers from judgment about the case rather than from memorized labels. Limit the tree to four branching nodes or fewer, and phrase each node as a direct question.
