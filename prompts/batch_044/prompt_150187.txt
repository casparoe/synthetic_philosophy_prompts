I am a final-year secondary-school student pursuing an independent study in the philosophy of cognition and artistic creation, and over the past semester I have been working closely with my mentor on Douglas Hofstadter's model of conceptual variation—the view that genuinely creative acts can be analyzed as adjustments of parameters within an abstract conceptual space rather than as arbitrary departures from a theme. My mentor applies this framework not only to music and visual art but also to arguments and formal systems, and she has developed her own extension of it, one that distinguishes continuous parameter sliders from discrete modality toggles that shift the representational format without altering underlying relations. I have recorded her verdicts across a range of cases, yet I find that her track record remains jointly consistent with several competing interpretations of what counts as a legitimate variation.

Here is the record as she has given it to me. She judges that slowing a melody by half and transposing it to a new key, with every intervallic relationship intact, counts as a genuine variation on the original theme. She likewise accepts a painting whose every hue has been rotated continuously around the color wheel, provided the compositional geometry is untouched. She denies variation-status, however, to a poem in which each noun is replaced by its direct antonym despite an unchanged syntactic skeleton, and she rejects a narrative in which every named character is arbitrarily substituted with one drawn from an unrelated story, even though the plot architecture is preserved. She further accepts uniform scaling of a three-dimensional sculpture to twice its size, but she denies the status to a mathematical proof that derives the contradictory conclusion from the original while employing the identical axiomatic base. Throughout these judgments she has hinted at her extended framework involving discrete modality toggles, yet she has never explicitly stated whether activating such a toggle—say, translating an argument into an entirely different notational system—preserves or destroys variation-status.

The problem is that her yes-cases and no-cases seem equally well explained by at least three rules: a structuralist rule that cares only about preservation of deep relational architecture; a continuity rule that permits only gradual, parametric adjustments within a single representational medium; and a recognizability rule that requires an informed observer to place the result within the same conceptual neighborhood without explicit decoding instructions. Her own toggle-extension introduces a fourth possibility, one that might treat representational-format shifts as permissible only when no discrete toggle is flipped.

Next week I must present a new case for evaluation, and I want to anticipate her reasoning precisely rather than guess. The case is this: a well-known philosophical argument originally formulated in informal natural language—concerning moral obligation under conditions of partial ignorance—has been reconstructed in formal predicate-logic notation with entirely different symbolic vocabulary. Every inferential step, quantifier scope, and modal operator is preserved exactly; there are no logical omissions or errors, but the surface form is unrecognizable to anyone lacking the translation manual. I need you to predict whether she will classify this reconstruction as a genuine knob-twiddled variation of the original argument. More importantly, I want you to lay out the competing rules—structural preservation, parametric continuity, conceptual-neighborhood recognizability, and her own toggle-modified version—showing explicitly how each handles both her earlier verdicts and this new formalization case, and then identify the single further hypothetical case that would most sharply adjudicate among them. My aim is not merely to forecast her answer but to understand the architecture that actually governs her judgments.
