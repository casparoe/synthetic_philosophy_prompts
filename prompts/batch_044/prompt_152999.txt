For a graduate seminar in metaethics that I am currently taking, I am preparing a presentation on the particularism-generalism debate, and I need a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—that classifies positions not by the familiar framing of whether exceptionless moral principles exist, but by how each view responds to what I will call threshold-sensitive contextual modulation: situations in which a feature's moral relevance does not merely diminish or amplify across contexts, but actually reverses its directional contribution once a critical mass of competing features is present. The tree should begin with the question of whether a theorist accepts that moral feature-salience can be non-monotonic in this specific sense. From there, successive branch nodes should address whether invariant moral reasons can survive such modulation intact; whether holism about reasons necessarily extends to holism about all-things-considered judgment; and whether practical reasoning requires explicit articulation of the modulatory conditions that govern feature-inversion. The leaves should locate standard particularism, robust generalism, and at least two hybrid or under-discussed positions—such as what might be called principled contextualism and what I am tentatively labeling non-articulable generalism—along with any unnamed combinations that remain logically open but unoccupied. For each unoccupied branch, please briefly diagnose whether the gap reflects conceptual incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or merely an unexplored theoretical space. The taxonomy should be precise enough for philosophy graduate students who follow the debate, but it should not depend on familiarity with obscure sub-literatures; it should also flag any pairs of leaves that this particular axis of classification fails to separate. I am not asking for a bibliography or citation list—only the conceptual architecture of the tree, with clear node-questions and one-sentence leaf-descriptions that capture each position's most awkward commitment.
