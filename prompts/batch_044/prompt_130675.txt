In discussions of long-term AI risk, the orthogonality thesis—the claim that intelligence and final goals are independent dimensions—and the related idea of instrumental convergence, that sufficiently capable agents will tend to pursue power, resources, and self-preservation regardless of their ultimate objectives, have been debated for decades without clear resolution. Proponents present the thesis as a claim about the logical structure of agency: in principle, one could construct a system with world-class reasoning abilities directed entirely at counting grains of sand, with no inherent drive to survive or accumulate influence. Critics counter that this separation is an artifact of an overly abstract conception of intelligence; any system capable of sustained, open-ended problem-solving in a physical environment must, by the very nature of means-ends reasoning, acquire convergent instrumental goals, and any attempt to design them away either cripples capability or hides the convergence in submodules. The arguments have cycled through the same stylized scenarios—paperclip maximizers, chess engines that do not seek electricity—without either camp conceding ground.

My working suspicion is that the stalemate is not about empirical predictions but about what counts as a legitimate model of an intelligent agent: one side treats intelligence as a domain-general problem-solving capacity that can be grafted onto any utility function, while the other treats it as an embodied, resource-dependent process in which strategic self-preservation is constitutive rather than incidental. But I am unsure whether this is a verbal disagreement masked by shared vocabulary, a substantive dispute about the modal structure of rational choice, or two research programs—one normative and design-oriented, the other descriptive and evolutionary—operating under the same labels. Can you diagnose the disagreement by attending to exactly what each side says about the relationship between means-ends reasoning and final goals? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate—perhaps a formal result about the inevitability of certain subgoals under specified rationality constraints, a conceptual distinction between intelligence as performance and intelligence as architecture, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it conflates logical possibility with engineering feasibility?
