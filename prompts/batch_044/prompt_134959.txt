For a seminar in moral philosophy I am writing down my current position on whether numbers should count in rescue and allocation cases, and I want a consistency audit before I present it. Here are my commitments:

(1) I accept the anti-aggregationist view—associated with Taurek—that in direct, one-off rescue dilemmas where I can save either one person or five, the five do not collectively present a stronger moral claim; each individual's claim is of equal weight and they are not additive, so I am not morally required to save the greater number.

(2) I think our contrary intuition that we should save the five tracks an evolutionary quantity-sensitive heuristic rather than a genuine moral reason, and I take this debunking to undermine aggregation in direct cases.

(3) I hold that moral theory should be unified: there should not be one decision procedure for private rescue and a separate, compartmentalized procedure for institutional allocation unless that division is itself derivable from the same underlying principle.

(4) In institutional contexts—e.g., distributing a scarce vaccine—I believe it is correct to prioritize the option that saves more lives, but I want to justify this without aggregating moral claims; instead I appeal to equal ex ante procedural fairness, where each citizen has an identical claim on institutional protection, so distributing risk equally across the population maximizes expected survivors without treating lives as fungible moral units.

(5) When harm is unavoidable outside any institutional framework, I think a fair lottery with equal per-capita chances is appropriate, and the fairness of that procedure does not depend on the relative sizes of the groups involved.

(6) I maintain that the strength of a moral claim is fixed entirely by properties internal to the claimant and does not vary with the presence, absence, or number of other claimants.

Do these hang together? I am especially concerned that (3) combined with (4) reintroduces aggregation through procedural language, and that (2) might also undermine the rational basis for (4). Meanwhile, (1) together with (5) might seem to conflict—if numbers never matter, why structure the lottery around equal individual chances rather than treating each group as bearing one collective claim?—though I suspect that tension dissolves once the procedural rationale is made explicit.

Please audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the real tensions by severity, specify for each what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs, and deliver your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Then provide the reasoning.
