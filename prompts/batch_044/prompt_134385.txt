I have recently become personally invested in how societies institutionalize accountability for historical violence, partly because of a family connection to postwar reconstruction and partly after visiting several memorial sites that seemed to operate with entirely different logics of guilt, victimhood, and state obligation. I am trying to think through the comparative landscape of what scholars call Vergangenheitsbewältigung in Germany, Japan’s fragmented postwar remembrance of empire and war responsibility, Turkey’s contested negotiation of the Armenian genocide, and the American South’s long transition from Lost Cause mythology to recent monument removals and curriculum battles. Rather than asking for a literature review, I want to understand which open conceptual problems in this comparative field actually deserve sustained philosophical and political attention over the next several years, and which are largely settled or downstream.

Specifically, I am interested in an agenda that treats comparative memory not as a ranking exercise—who has faced the past better—but as a problem of mnemonic architecture: how remembrance obligations are distributed across federal and local jurisdictions, how they interact with contemporary citizenship rights, and whether there is a defensible metric for what I will call commemorative density, meaning the degree to which a state’s legal, educational, and spatial institutions are structurally oriented around a specific historical wrong. Among the open lines of work, which should take priority? For instance, is the upstream problem the normative basis for intergenerational liability in collective memory—that is, whether citizens who did not participate in a wrong can be compelled to maintain its remembrance—or is it the empirical-political question of which institutional forms actually alter civic identity rather than producing backlash? And how should we weigh denialist regimes, where the state actively suppresses remembrance, against cases of over-commemoration, where remembrance risks becoming a substitute for material redistribution?

Please defend your prioritization: identify two or three open questions or research directions that you think are most worth pursuing, explain the criteria you are using—tractability for philosophers and political theorists rather than historians, upstream dependence, and relevance to current policy disputes—and indicate what kind of evidence or argument over the next few years would cause you to reorder them. I am especially interested in whether there are implications widely discussed that do not actually follow from the comparative data—for example, whether Germany’s model is genuinely exportable—and whether there is an underexplored line of inquiry regarding the temporal limits of commemorative obligation: at what point does remembrance become an inherited performance rather than a living moral practice? I want a defended agenda, not a survey or bibliography, focused on what should command attention if one had limited resources—say, a small research group or a policy fellowship—to devote to this domain.
