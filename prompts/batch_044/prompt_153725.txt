I've been working through the handicap principle for a course on evolutionary explanations of culture, and my study partner and I cooked up this argument that seems to prove morality can't actually exist if the principle applies to it. She mentioned counter-signaling - that high-quality people sometimes hide their quality on purpose - but I don't see how that saves us. Here are the steps we wrote out:

(1) The handicap principle says a signal is honest only if its cost is unavoidable for low-quality individuals but affordable for high-quality ones.
(2) Moral acts like risking your life for a stranger are extremely costly.
(3) So if moral acts are handicap signals, they honestly advertise that the actor has high moral quality.
(4) But if they honestly advertise quality, then anyone who does them knows they'll gain reputation.
(5) So performing a moral act always involves knowing you'll get a reputation boost.
(6) But if you do something partly for reputation, it isn't genuinely moral - the motive is strategic, not moral.
(7) Therefore if the handicap principle explains moral behavior, then no moral behavior is genuinely moral.
(8) But clearly some people do genuinely moral things.
(9) So either genuinely moral acts exist and the principle doesn't apply to them, or it applies and morality is just reputation management.
(10) Most evolutionary psychologists want to apply the principle without denying morality.

I feel like the slip is somewhere between (4) and (6), maybe an equivacation about what "knowing you'll benefit" does to your motives? But I'm not sure. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (2) and (8) if I can, since I think moral acts really are costly and that real morality exists. Also, is there a hidden scope shift I'm missing?
