I've come around to this view that honoring sunk costs is never actually irrational, but not for the usual signaling-commitment reason people talk about. Instead I think it's tied to diachronic agency: if an agent treats past commitments as totally disposable the second they're suboptimal, there isn't a persistant planner there, just a sequence of momentary optimizers. So honoring sunk costs is rational in a constitutive sense — it preserves the very agent that made the plan. What would actually count against this? I want the defeaters ranked: what's fatal, what's just awkward, and what defenders of this view would themselves concede is damaging. For instance, is there formal work showing any decision rule with this property collapses into standard EU maximization with an odd discount factor? Or empirical cases where institutions that abandon sunk-cost projects end up with stronger long-run coherence, not weaker? Also, is the view already insulated — like, any time someone drops a bad project we just say 'that wasn't real agency' — and is that insulation a problem?
