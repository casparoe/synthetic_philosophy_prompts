In discussions of Dewey’s democratic theory, one frequently encounters the claim that a democratic community functions like a scientific laboratory: citizens formulate policy-hypotheses, enact them as social experiments, observe outcomes, and revise collective beliefs through public deliberation and feedback. This analogy is often used to defend democracy’s epistemic superiority over authoritarian alternatives, on the grounds that it institutionalizes fallibilism, distributed observation, and iterative correction. Audit this analogy rigorously. First, provide an explicit correspondence table—what in democratic politics plays the role of the hypothesis, the experimenter, the control group, the measurement instrument, the peer-review mechanism, the replication study, and the criteria for falsification? Second, identify precisely where the mapping breaks: can democratic citizens genuinely “blind” themselves to partisan interests in the way researchers blind subjects and evaluators? Is there a functional analogue to pre-registration that prevents post-hoc rationalization of policy failures? Does the analogy quietly import the assumption that social outcomes are as isolable, quantifiable, and causally transparent as variables in a controlled setting? Third, evaluate whether these disanalogies undermine the epistemic defense of democracy, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative conclusion all along. If the laboratory comparison collapses under scrutiny, suggest whether a different analogy—perhaps to open-source debugging with distributed error-correction, or to evolutionary selection with varied environmental testing—better captures Dewey’s point without smuggling in the misleading apparatus of experimental control.
