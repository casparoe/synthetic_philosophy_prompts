I hold that cultural values can operate as autonomous causal variables in macrohistorical explanation, rather than being reducible to material conditions, class interests, or post-hoc rationalizations. Specifically, I accept a qualified Weberian claim: that the internalization of ascetic Protestant values—methodical worldly discipline coupled with the notion of a vocational calling (Beruf)—exerted downward causal pressure on economic behavior in parts of early modern Northern Europe, producing patterns of capital accumulation, reinvestment, and risk-calculation that are not fully accounted for by concurrent shifts in property law, banking technology, or class structure. For this to be genuinely causal rather than merely correlational, I assume three conditions must hold: the values were psychologically internalized by agents as action-guiding reasons; these reasons were not fully substitutable by material incentives or institutional rules alone; and the semantic content of the value-complex remained stable enough across generations to sustain a continuous motivational chain linking theology to economic practice.
