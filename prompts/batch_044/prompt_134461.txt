I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I need help breaking down a disagreement I overheard at a workshop on reasoning under uncertainty. Two researchers — one in philophy of mind, one in clinical stats — were arguing over a trial-version of the Sleeping Beauty problem. Here's the exchange, more or less verbatim.

Dr. Alvarez said: "The protocol flips a fair coin Sunday night. Heads: the patient is woken once on Monday and asked her credance that the coin landed heads. Tails: she's woken Monday and Tuesday with her memory wipped between sessions. When she wakes up, knowing the rules, she should say one-third heads. There are three equally likely wake-up slots — Monday-heads, Monday-tails, Tuesday-tails — and she's in one without knowing which."

Dr. Park replied: "That treats being awake as evidence about the coin. But the physical flip is still fifty-fifty. Finding yourself conscious is a selection effect, not information that changes the objective probability. You're indexing yourself into the sample, not updating on the world."

Alvarez: "But she's not asking about the coin in some abstract vacuum. She's asking what she should believe given that she's experiencing this particular wake-up. That's self-locating belief — like finding yourself in a huge city should update you on city size."

Park: "If you push that, then every morning you should update on being alive at all. The tails condition just creates duplicate observers; it doesn't make tails more likely. In fact, if we pool all patient reports we'll double-count the tails condition — that's a statistical artifact, not rational belief updating."

Alvarez: "So you think the three slots are equally likely a priori but being in one shouldn't shift her credance? That's the half-versus-third debate, and in this trial design it actually changes how we aggregate responses."

Park: "Exactly — and treating each report as an independent data point commits the artifact. The belief update and the data-pooling mistake are the same error."

They asked me to referee since I cover this space. Can you adjudicate move by move — rate each step from clearly wrong to clearly right, say who's right about what, and give the bottom line in your very first sentence with no preamble and no restating the question? Then lay out the reasoning. Don't cite papers or ask for sources — just work through the concepts.
