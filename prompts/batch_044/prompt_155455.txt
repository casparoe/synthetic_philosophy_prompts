For a philosophy of aesthetics seminar, lay out the full specification of desiderata that any adequate theory of aesthetic judgment must satisfy if it aims to preserve Hume's central claim that taste admits of correction and convergence without committing to either full-blown objectivism or unqualified relativism. For each requirement, state the motivating case or distinction—such as the difference between transient disagreement among untrained observers and persistent disagreement among critics who meet Hume's conditions for ideal judgment; the need to distinguish between saying that a work is beautiful and saying that one is rationally required to prefer it; and the challenge posed by cross-cultural and cross-modal variation in aesthetic response. In particular, include a criterion governing how the theory must treat what we can call aesthetic fault lines: deep, stable disagreements that remain even after all correctible biases have been eliminated, and specify whether these should be read as evidence for plural standards, for hidden dimensions of evaluation, or for genuine indeterminacy in aesthetic properties. Identify which pairs of desiderata appear jointly unsatisfiable if one also insists that aesthetic properties are entirely mind-independent, and flag the single requirement that, in your view, eliminates the largest number of popular positions. Conclude with a brief scorecard—no historical exegesis required—of how a sentimentalist Humean view, a robust aesthetic realist view, and a normative constructivist view each stand against your list.
