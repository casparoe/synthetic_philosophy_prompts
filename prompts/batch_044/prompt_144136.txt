Write a satirical parody in the form of a deadpan internal memorandum from the legal and conservation department of a fictional international museum, addressed to a partner institution that has requested to borrow an object referred to throughout only as "the bronze figure and its underlying substrate." The voice should be relentlessly formal, like an actual museum loan agreement, but the writer should be visibly unable to decide whether the borrower is receiving one item or two, and every clause should turn on a live philosophical dispute about coincidence, essential properties of form versus matter, and persistence conditions.

The memo should weave in specific bureaucratic anxieties that map onto the metaphysics: a liability section asking who owes what if the statue is melted in transit and only clay remains; an insurance rider for what the document terms "persistence failure"; a clause about accidental remolding and whether that constitutes breach of contract or merely a change of state; and a brief aside describing the museum's national holiday honoring the original mold-maker. The satire must stay deadpan — the writer never breaks character to explain that they are discussing philosophy — and the absurdity should arise naturally from applying contract logic to questions of material constitution. Write this as continuous prose only, in the manner of a long, slightly frantic email or letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables whatsoever. Aim for about 700 words.

Please include, either at the opening or woven into the conclusion, a brief statement of what would change your interpretation of the contract's core logic: specifically, which metaphysical argument — for example, that the statue is essentially its form, or that coincidence is impossible — if shown to be wrong, would force you to restructure the liability clauses, and identify the precise point where your mapping of legal obligations onto material constitution is most vulnerable to collapse.
