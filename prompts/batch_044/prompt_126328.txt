As I work on a novel about an archivist whose testimony is systematically discounted, I have settled into the following philosophical profile on epistemic injustice and need an audit before I let it govern the narrative logic. I hold that testimonial injustice is fundamentally a wrong done to a person in her capacity as a knower, not a mere distributive failure of credibility; that hermeneutical injustice should be understood as an active foreclosure of interpretive possibilities by dominant narrative structures, rather than an innocent gap in collective concepts; that every instance of such injustice is, at its root, an aesthetic failure—it fractures the coherence of a life story for both subject and witness; that no malicious individual intent is necessary, since structural patterns alone can reproduce these deficits; and that the proper remedy is therefore purely institutional—redistributing platforms and interpretive authority—without demanding personal apology or attributing individual moral guilt. Which of these actually conflict? I see a potential contradiction between treating the harm as essentially aesthetic and treating it as a distinct moral wrong to a knower; I also worry that describing hermeneutical injustice as an active foreclosure clashes with my denial that intent is required. Rank the tensions by severity, distinguish real conflicts from merely apparent ones, and indicate the least costly revision—preferably one that preserves the aesthetic framing, since that is driving the novel's architecture.
