I serve on the Long-Range Institutional Planning and Endowment Committee of a large research university, and we must decide by the end of this quarter whether to adopt a binding governance rule: every academic unit applying for multi-decade capital funding must submit strategic forecasts accompanied by explicit numerical probability ranges—specifically, 90 percent confidence intervals—for key outcomes such as enrollment shifts, interdisciplinary research breakthroughs, and long-range societal impact metrics. The administration's risk office argues that without such quantification, cross-unit comparison and fiduciary oversight are impossible; several senior faculty representatives, drawing on philosophy of economics and decision theory, object that many strategic uncertainties are not merely unknown in magnitude but structurally resistant to cardinal measurement, and that mandating precise numbers will institutionalize false precision rather than reduce it. One of our economic advisors circulated a brief invoking John Maynard Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty, but the brief appears to conflate Keynes's logical-relation theory of probability—developed in his 1921 Treatise on Probability—with Frank Knight's later economic taxonomy, and it further asserts that Keynes held long-run expectations to carry literally zero evidential weight, being governed entirely by convention or animal spirits. I need the committee's formal decision memo to engage Keynes's actual framework with philosophical seriousness: particularly his claim that probabilities can be ordered by comparative weight of argument even when they resist numerical assignment, and his view that rational belief is not reducible to frequency or subjective betting odds. At the same time, the memo must treat the risk office's concern as legitimate—namely, that purely qualitative assessments can mask arbitrariness, strategic ambiguity, and political bargaining—rather than dismissing it as managerial overreach.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before our March meeting. It should recommend either adoption, rejection, or conditional adoption of the numerical-confidence mandate, paired with a concrete implementation timeline—say, a phased rollout over two budget cycles—and explicit criteria governing exceptions. The reasoning should identify precisely where the advisor's summary misrepresents Keynes's view of evidential weight, clarifying that Keynes did not treat long-run expectations as weightless conventions but as judgments supported by non-quantifiable yet rationally comparable arguments; it should also explain how Keynes's framework distinguishes measurable risk from genuine uncertainty without collapsing into skepticism about all forecasting. The memo must include an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the opposing side: if you recommend conditional adoption or rejection, address directly how the university can maintain cross-unit accountability and prevent qualitative reports from becoming rhetorical exercises; if you recommend full adoption, explain how the mandate can be designed to avoid forcing false precision onto genuinely Keynesian uncertainties. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; the committee needs reasoning it can act on, not further references.

The deliverable should conclude with an actionable bottom line rather than an unresolved compromise—perhaps a tiered reporting structure in which quantifiable risks are submitted with standard numerical ranges, while non-quantifiable strategic uncertainties are documented through a structured weight-of-argument appendix requiring units to specify the evidential breadth, analogical support, and vulnerability to surprise for each forecast. This mechanism should be presented not as a philosophical ornament but as an institutional design feature that honors Keynes's epistemology while preserving the risk office's legitimate demand for transparent, comparable reasoning across units. Write the memo in formal institutional prose suitable for circulation to administrators, faculty senators, and trustees, with clear headings, a one-paragraph executive recommendation at the outset, and a closing implementation schedule.
