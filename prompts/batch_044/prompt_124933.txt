I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I tried to formalize the Gettier problem into an argument, but it now seems to prove that empirical knowledge is impossible. (1) Knowledge is justified true belief, and Gettier cases show that justification and truth can come apart accidentally—the belief is true, but not because the justification was sound. (2) To fix this, we need a no-accident condition: the belief must be true in virtue of the justification, with no hidden gap or external correction separating the reasoning from the fact. (3) Imagine a "systematic Gettier" agent: every belief she forms contains a hidden reasoning error, yet an unknown mechanism corrects every error, so all her beliefs happen to be true; by (2) she has no knowledge, since each truth is accidental relative to her reasoning. (4) The same structure applies to any ordinary empirical belief I hold, because for each one there is a logically possible scenario in which my reasoning contains a hidden error that is corrected by some unknown mechanism, leaving the belief true but disconnected from my justification. (5) If (2) requires ruling out all such hidden-correction possibilities, and I obviously cannot rule them out for every belief, then none of my empirical beliefs are true in virtue of my justification. (6) So either I have no empirical knowledge at all, or (2) is not actually required. (7) But (2) seemed necessary precisely to solve Gettier cases. Which step should I reject—the modal slide in (4), the epistemic demand in (5), or the framing of (2) itself? And if I give up (2), what do I pay in terms of explaining Gettier-style luck?
