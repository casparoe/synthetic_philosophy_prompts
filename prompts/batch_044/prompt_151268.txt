I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind working on the metaphysics of consciousness and the epistemology of introspection. In my dissertation research I am trying to move past the standard dialectic between illusionists and realists about phenomenal consciousness—the familiar exchange in which illusionists claim that qualitative experience is a kind of representational error, and realists respond with the phenomenal concept strategy or transparency arguments. I think both sides miss a structural distinction that might restructure the debate without collapsing into either eliminativism or dualism.

Here is the distinction I have in mind. Rather than treating illusionism as the claim that there are no phenomenal properties, I want to formulate it as a claim about what I will call phenomenal framing: the feature of introspective judgments that presents their objects as immediately given, non-inferential, and intrinsic to the subject. On this view, mental states certainly have functional and representational properties, but phenomenal framing is a cognitive-architecture mechanism that systematically misclassifies those states as belonging to an ontologically primitive qualitative category. The illusion is therefore not in the content of experience itself, but in the framing mechanism that generates first-person authority and the appearance of irreducibility.

Please argue that this reconstructed illusionism—directed at phenomenal framing rather than at phenomenal properties per se—can answer the phenomenal concept strategy objection without conceding that there are irreducible phenomenal concepts. Specifically, show how treating framing as a functional property of introspective mechanisms allows the illusionist to explain why phenomenal judgments feel authoritative without granting them special epistemic privilege, and address whether this move avoids the charge that illusionism is self-refuting because the judgment that framing is illusory must itself be framed.
