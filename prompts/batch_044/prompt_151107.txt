For an independent study project in philosophy of mind, I have been following the debate sparked by the “stochastic parrots” critique of large language models, and I find myself stuck on its structure rather than its examples. Critics emphasize that these systems produce fluent text without embodied reference or direct causal connection to the states of affairs they describe; defenders reply that emergent reasoning, in-context learning, and consistent benchmark performance demonstrate a functional form of understanding that does not require human-like embodiment. The exchange has continued for years, yet neither camp appears to update in response to the other, and the discussion often collapses into trading convincing outputs against convincing failures without theoretical movement.

I want to understand what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute about where to draw the boundary of the word “understanding”; a substantive disagreement about which conditions—causal history, functional role, representational fidelity—are genuinely necessary for cognition; or are the two sides answering different questions—perhaps one about etiology and one about behavioral competence—while speaking as if they share a single subject? To test this, I have been considering whether a distinction imported from epistemology might help clarify things: between “representational tracking,” in which a system’s internal states must be specifically sensitive to the particular conditions they purport to represent (such that, in nearby possible worlds where those conditions differ, the states differ accordingly), and “predictive coherence,” in which a system need only maintain consistent, useful outputs across contexts without that specific world-sensitive linkage. Would applying this distinction to language models resolve the debate, merely rename existing positions, or reveal that the sides are indeed talking past one another? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would actually count as progress—an empirical result, a formal conceptual clarification, or a reframing of the original question—rather than another round of illustrative examples and counterexamples?
