I take the view that the paradox of deontology—where strict agent-centered constraints appear to license catastrophic outcomes—is dissolved once we stop treating moral reasoning as a single metric over world-states and instead recognize a double-ledger structure: one ledger tracking outcomes (the consequentialist domain), the other tracking non-fungible conditions of agent-integrity that regulate what it means for this particular agent to act. On this framing, refusing to kill one to save five is not a failure to minimize harm but a refusal to convert one's own agency into an instrument of aggregation; the moral cost is real but belongs to the outcome-ledger, while the constraint belongs to the integrity-ledger, and there is no single currency in which both must be traded. What would have to be true for this resolution to fail? I want the defeaters mapped and ranked: arguments showing the two-ledger distinction collapses into hidden consequentialism or ad hoc stipulation; concrete cases where agent-integrity conditions seem to shift with outcomes in ways the view cannot accommodate without arbitrary line-drawing; formal results from decision theory or moral psychology that show practical reasoning necessarily integrates all considerations into a unified preference ordering; and developments in normative ethics that render agent-relative reasons redundant. Say which of these defenders of the framing should themselves concede count against it, which they would dismiss, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample is met by reassigning it to the wrong ledger, is that insulation a virtue or a fatal flaw for a view of this kind? Include an honest assessment of whether any of these defeaters have arguably already appeared.
