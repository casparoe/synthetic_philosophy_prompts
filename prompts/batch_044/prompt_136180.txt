As a novelist whose books have long turned on the architecture of cities that refuse to bury their dead properly—stories in which monuments lie more precisely than archives—I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at the inauguration of a comparative memory institute. The building will house, in adjacent but structurally unequal galleries, the institutional remains of Germany’s postwar Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan’s bifurcated war narratives, the suppressed Armenian archives of Turkey, and the recently dismantled statuary of the American Lost Cause. The audience will be divided: half are historians, archivists, and descendants for whom these rooms are not metaphor; the other half are municipal officials, donors, and neighbors who need to understand why comparison itself is not an insult to particular suffering.

The philosophical idea that must shape the speech—not ornament it—is the distinction I want to call mnemonic jurisdiction versus mnemonic inheritance. Jurisdiction is the state’s claim to own and regulate collective remembrance through law, curriculum, and urban planning; inheritance is the unscripted transmission of trauma through family silence, folklore, and the accidental preservation of objects that escape institutional control. I want the argument to proceed from the claim that these two modes are not complementary but structurally antagonistic: every act of jurisdiction tends to displace inheritance, and every act of inheritance undermines the coherence that jurisdiction demands.

The difficulty is that I must navigate this tension without offering false equivalence. Germany’s perpetrator-memory, Japan’s split victim-perpetrator narrative, Turkey’s enforced amnesia, and the American South’s romanticized defeat are not variations on a single theme but incompatible regimes of truth. At the same time, I must prevent the audience from retreating into an easy pluralism that treats remembrance as a private aesthetic preference—each culture remembers in its own way, and therefore no comparison is permitted.

Please write this speech: approximately fifteen minutes, addressed to this mixed room. It should be possible for a listener with no specialist knowledge to follow the conceptual architecture, yet precise enough that a historian of comparative memory culture recognizes I am not simplifying the differences between these four cases for narrative convenience. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the reasoning that stands on the distinctions introduced here rather than on imported scholarly authority. And please do not invoke any theoretical framework or named school of thought beyond those I have named, since the argument should depend on the structural tension between jurisdiction and inheritance rather than on citation.
