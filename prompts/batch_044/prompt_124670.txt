Since I recently joined a local land-use committee, a dispute there has made me want to think more rigorously about tradition and reform. At our last meeting, two members—call them A and B—debated whether to approve demolishing an old railway viaduct that is no longer used for trains but now serves as an informal pedestrian bridge and a nesting site for a rare local bird. A invoked Chesterton's fence: we do not know all the reasons this structure has survived in its present form, and tearing it down risks destroying ecological, social, or historical values that we have not yet identified. B replied that this misapplies the metaphor: Chesterton's fence concerns practices or institutions that have persisted because they solve identifiable problems, whereas this viaduct was never deliberately preserved for any purpose; its current ecological role is accidental, not a reason it was kept. A then appealed to Burkean prescription: long-standing arrangements embedded in local life carry a presumption in their favor, and physical landmarks that shape a community's self-understanding fall within the small local associations Burke called little platoons. B countered that Burke's doctrine of prescription applies to constitutional and moral institutions, not to every physical artifact; treating accidental preservation as morally binding confuses Burke's cautious reformism with mere inertia and stretches his idea of local attachment into an indiscriminate preservationism. A responded that B smuggles in an unmotivated distinction: if the physical environment is part of the fabric of local life, then removing a familiar landmark without compelling justification violates the continuity that Burke thought necessary for social stability. B said this erases the very difference between principled conservatism and resistance to any change whatsoever. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say who is more right overall. Please also state how confident you are in this adjudication and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
