Here is an argument I have been developing for a project on non-human causation in historiography, and it now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion undermines the very distinction I want to preserve. (1) If a force is causally indispensable to a historical outcome—meaning the outcome would not have occurred without it, and its mechanism is not reducible to contemporaneous human intention—then it qualifies as a historical actor in the minimal sense required by explanation. (2) The bacterium Yersinia pestis in the fourteenth century, the 1815 Tambora aerosol forcing, and present anthropogenic climate change each satisfy this condition: they altered mortality regimes, labor structures, and state fiscal capacity through mechanisms that operated independently of any human design at the point of impact. (3) Therefore each is a historical actor in this minimal explanatory sense. (4) Once an entity counts as an actor in explanation, denying it any form of agency is a category mistake: agency here is simply the non-derivative capacity to initiate change that other actors must accommodate rather than direct. (5) But if these entities possess agency, then environmental determinism is not an error of over-attribution but a correct recognition of distributed agency; and if agency is genuinely distributed in this way, moral evaluation—blame, praise, accountability—must be distributed as well, since agency without the possibility of accountability is conceptually incoherent. (6) It is clearly absurd to assign moral blame to a bacterium or to a volcanic eruption. (7) So either we reject (1) and claim that causal indispensability is never sufficient for actor-status, which seems to collapse all material causation into pure social construction; or we reject (4) and treat “actor” as a merely metaphorical term, which renders explanatory appeals to germs, climate, or volcanoes empty; or we reject (5) and sever agency from accountability, but then we forfeit the normative reason to resist determinism in the first place. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2), and I am prepared to weaken (5), but I want to know the precise cost of each option and whether there is a hidden equivocation—perhaps between explanatory efficacy and moral agency, or between causal independence and intentional direction—that makes the slide from (3) to (6) only apparent.
