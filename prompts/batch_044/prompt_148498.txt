Consider the institutional practice of “dual-register heritage designation” adopted by certain regional preservation boards, especially in contexts where colonial archives and local oral traditions diverge. Under this practice, a site may be listed in the Archival Register on the basis of documentary evidence—say, land deeds and administrative logs showing that a particular building served as a colonial revenue office—or in the Mnemonic Register on the basis of sustained community testimony that assigns the same building a contradictory significance, such as being a clandestine organizing space for resistance. When both registers apply to the same site but the narratives conflict, the board does not merge the entries, adjudicate between them, or subordinate one to the other. Instead, it issues separate physical plaques, maintains two parallel catalog entries, and prohibits either narrative from being presented in official materials as the “true” or “complete” history that supersedes the other.

What philosophical commitments about the relationship between collective memory and historical record does this duplication practice presuppose? Does it require an epistemic pluralism according to which archives and living memory generate equally valid but incommensurable truths about the past? Or does it rest on a domain separation—perhaps that archival history answers questions about what causally occurred, while collective memory answers questions about present identity and moral belonging—so that they do not genuinely conflict? Could the practice instead be sustained by a purely pragmatic or political theory of heritage management, without any deep commitment about the nature of historical truth? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or family of theories) that best explains why conflict is resolved by parallel preservation rather than by resolution or hierarchy; distinguish what the institution strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; and say what would have to change in its reasoning, its physical display rules, or its funding priorities if it adopted the view that, whenever collective memory and the archival record conflict, the record should always give way, or conversely that memory should always prevail.
