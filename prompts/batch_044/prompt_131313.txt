I am a doctoral student in political philosophy currently enrolled in a seminar on the ethics of migration and the normative structure of redistributive welfare states, and I have been asked to moderate the colloquium Q&A session for an invited speaker who defends a position she calls reciprocity-first conditionalism. Her argument, developed in a recent monograph we have been discussing in the course, is that the moral urgency of open borders must be lexically ordered behind the prior institutional achievement of what she terms thick civic reciprocity — the shared normative framework that makes high-tax, high-transfer welfare schemes politically legitimate. She distinguishes sharply between entry rights, which she treats as morally substantial but limited, and standing rights to full membership in distributive schemes, which she regards as contingent upon prior integration rather than automatically conferred by physical presence. Crucially, she denies that her view is merely an empirical warning about fiscal strain; instead, she treats democratic solidarity as a cooperative scheme among co-authors of shared institutions, such that rapid demographic change without mediated integration erodes the motivational infrastructure of redistribution itself, not merely its budgetary feasibility.

For this session, I would like eight to ten interview questions that prevent her from simply restating this architecture and instead press where the literature on migration and welfare-state legitimacy actually applies pressure. The sequence should move from conceptual clarification to normative challenge, with each question paired with a follow-up keyed to her most probable reply. Please include: first, a question that probes whether her motivational infrastructure claim functions as an empirical prediction about voter psychology or as a normative condition for legitimate coercion, with a follow-up for the case in which she insists it operates as both; second, a question directed at asylum seekers whose home societies have collapsed precisely the reciprocity conditions she prizes, anticipating her likely appeal to a distinction between temporary protection and full distributive standing, followed by a follow-up asking whether that two-tier structure reproduces the exclusion she claims is only provisional; third, a question about the operationalization of integration — specifically, how we determine that reciprocity has been achieved without smuggling in assimilationist benchmarks or culturally parochial norms, with a follow-up addressing her probable retreat to procedural rather than substantive criteria; and fourth, a concluding question that asks what institutional failure or empirical finding could actually falsify reciprocity-first conditionalism, as opposed to merely demonstrating that integration is more difficult than anticipated. The overall arc should make sustained use of her entry-versus-standing distinction, avoid generic biographical openings, and maintain the formal tone appropriate for a philosophical colloquium.
