I'm taking a seminar on medieval philosophy of religion, and we've been working through Maimonides' treatment of the commandments, particularly animal sacrifice. The secondary literature I've encountered tends to split quickly between an "accommodationist" reading—sacrifice as a tolerated relic of pagan practice that God permitted only because the Israelites could not yet abandon ritual slaughter—and a "symbolist" or "functionalist" reading that insists the practice retains some permanent spiritual or communal purpose in Maimonides' ideal legal framework. But neither camp seems able to hold together the well-known discussion in Part III of the Guide, where sacrifice is explained as a divine concession to a people steeped in idolatrous worship, and the systematic codification in the Mishneh Torah, where sacrificial law is presented as part of an ideal Torah regime without any explicit indication that it is provisional or second-best. I want to avoid simply picking a side based on which text I find more authoritative.

So I'm trying to write the full specification: what desiderata must any adequate philosophical interpretation of Maimonides' stance on animal sacrifice satisfy? I need the criteria with the motivating tension or case behind each—for example, the need to reconcile a genetic explanation of the commandment's origin with its normative status in an ideal legal system; the need to respect his broader distinction between laws that serve an intrinsic intellectual end and those that serve an instrumental or remedial end; the need to account for his explicit view that prayer can functionally substitute for sacrifice in the absence of the Temple, without allowing that functional replaceability to collapse into the claim that the animal element was never meaningful in the first place; the need to distinguish sharply between explaining why a practice was instituted and justifying why it remains binding; and the need to remain consistent with his theory of prophecy and the structure of divine law, which assigns some commandments to human weakness and others to direct intellectual perfection. I also want to include a more specific criterion: any adequate view must specify whether the "concession" applies to the institution of sacrifice as such, to its particular material form, or only to the psychological state of the Israelites at the historical moment of its institution, and whether a community fully capable of pure intellectual worship would, on Maimonides' own framework, be committed to retaining, transforming, or abolishing the commandment.

Please lay out the full list, flag any pair of desiderata that you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable—I have a particular suspicion that "complete reconciliation with the normative legal texts" and "no residual normative weight for the sacrificial form itself" may pull in opposite directions—then briefly score the three main strategies (the pure accommodationist, the symbolist, and a two-level functionalist account) against your criteria, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating tensions rather than on the scorecard. Finally, apply the resulting principle to the case I haven't listed: if Maimonides were confronted with a sustained community that had genuinely outgrown all ritual need and practiced only contemplative worship, would his framework require him to treat the sacrificial commandment as abolished, as optional but still valid, or as obligatory but now emptied of its original rationale?
