I am designing a session for a philosophy-of-mathematics seminar and want to ground our discussion of intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism in a literary text rather than in formal proofs. I have in mind Edwin Abbott's Flatland, whose basic narrative is widely known: A Square, a two-dimensional narrator living among polygons and circles, is visited by a Sphere from Spaceland; he demonstrates the third dimension only through transient circular cross-sections of changing radius; A Square attempts to preach this revelation to the Circles and is ultimately imprisoned as a dangerous heretic. The usual philosophical readings treat the Sphere as an analogue to religious revelation or to Kuhnian paradigm shift, but I want to pursue a more technically precise interpretation—one that treats Abbott's geometry not as metaphor for faith, but as a dramatization of the intuitionistic rejection of non-constructive existence claims.

Specifically, read the novel through what I will call an intuitionistic phenomenology of dimension: the Sphere asserts that a third axis exists, yet he never provides A Square with an executable, step-by-step procedure for constructing that axis from within two-dimensional geometry. Instead, he offers only fleeting, unrepeatable traces—circles that grow and shrink—much as a classical mathematician might assert the existence of an object that has never been constructed inside the system at hand. From this angle, A Square's failure to convince the Circles parallels the intuitionist's refusal to accept existence without a method of construction, and the failure of the law of excluded middle in domains where no such method is available. I am especially interested in whether the "dream of Lineland"—where A Square tries and fails to persuade a one-dimensional being of a second dimension—dramatizes something like a Brouwerian choice sequence: a mathematical extension that is not predetermined but must be freely generated over time, and that therefore resists demonstration from within the lower-dimensional formal system.
