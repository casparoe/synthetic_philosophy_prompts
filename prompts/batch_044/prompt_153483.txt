I am putting together a problem set for an advanced philosophy of science seminar, and I have grown tired of watching students recycle the same overdetermination examples—firing squads, redundant light switches, simultaneous forest fires—that appear in every introductory text. I want a case that remains entirely armchair, requiring no empirical research, yet is structurally complex enough that it cannot be resolved by reflex.

Give an example of a conceptual issue that arises specifically when counterfactual-dependence accounts—those that analyze causation in terms of modal counterfactuals holding across possible worlds—and interventionist accounts—those that analyze causation in terms of invariant relations surviving surgical interventions on structural models—are applied to one and the same scenario. The scenario should involve redundant institutional authorization: a regulatory decision that requires concurrent approval from three independent committees, each of which issues authorization based on a shared but unobservable evidentiary report, and where the decision is ultimately enacted even though any two committees alone would have sufficed. Your example should highlight not merely a disagreement about which committee is "the" cause, but a deeper divergence about what kind of fact the causal relation itself is—whether it tracks modal dependency or structural invariance.

Once you have constructed the case, take a committed position on which framework captures the causal structure more faithfully. Defend that verdict by showing how your chosen account handles the redundancy without collapsing into either epiphenomenalism or causal pluralism, and explicitly identify the precise conceptual cost the opposing framework must pay in accommodating the scenario. If you genuinely believe the case cannot be adjudicated, state that directly and specify exactly what each side must surrender.
