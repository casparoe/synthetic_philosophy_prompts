Last night I had a long and rather heated discussion with a close friend about whether mathematics genuinely requires actual infinities, or whether all talk of them can be reconstructed in potentialist terms. He offered me a summary of what he takes to be the standard case for actual infinity—centered on Cantor's diagonal argument and Hilbert's hotel—and then appended a finitist rebuttal that he thinks most textbooks suppress. I am writing because I want to know whether his summary is something I should take seriously or whether it distorts the mathematics and the philosophy.

Here is how he put it. Cantor's diagonal argument, he said, proves that the real numbers are not just larger than the naturals but form a genuinely completed, actually infinite totality that no enumeration can exhaust; this is not merely about the failure of any particular list, but about the existence of an absolute cardinality that stands beyond all possible counting. Hilbert's hotel then illustrates the counterintuitive properties of such completed infinities: a fully occupied infinite hotel can accommodate a new guest by shifting every occupant to the next room, which shows that in the infinite realm the part can equal the whole, violating the Euclid principle that the whole is necessarily greater than the part. From this, my friend concluded that actual infinity is not just strange but conceptually incoherent—if accepting it forces us to abandon basic logical principles about part and whole—so we should adopt a strict finitist or potentialist framework in which there is no completed set of all real numbers, only an indefinitely extendable process of constructing them, and Hilbert's hotel is merely a misleading parable that illicitly reifies an unending procedure into a static object.

He seemed confident, but I noticed that he moved very quickly from describing Cantor's result to interpreting it as an ontological proof of a completed totality, and he treated Hilbert's hotel as if it were intended as a reductio ad absurdum of infinity rather than an illustration of its arithmetic. Before I commit to either side, I want an accuracy audit. Which parts of his summary would a mathematician or philosopher of mathematics accept as fair? Where does he conflate Cantor's technical result with a metaphysical interpretation? Is he right that finitism rejects the premises of Hilbert's hotel rather than just finding it paradoxical, and does his claim that actual infinity violates the part-whole principle survive once the distinction between finite and infinite sets is properly kept? Finally, does his conclusion—that we should reject actual infinity—actually follow from the corrected version of his premises, or does it depend on the misrepresentations?
