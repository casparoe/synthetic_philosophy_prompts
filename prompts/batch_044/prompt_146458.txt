I'm a PhD student in political philosophy at Leeds and I've been trying to think about Machiavelli's fortune and virtù in a way that doesn't just recycle the same old prince-stories. I want to analyse the 2016 Brexit referendum and the negotiation chaos that followed through that lens, but not in the lazy 'Boris Johnson is like Cesare Borgia' way. Specifically, I want you to treat the referendum result itself as a sudden event of fortuna — the kind of flood that breaks its banks — and then ask what virtù was actually available to the British state afterwards. Not just individual politicians like Theresa May or Boris Johnson, but whether an unwritten, convention-based constitution can exercise something like collective or distributed virtù, or whether it's actually an anti-virtù structure because it lacks instituional dykes to build. Did the flexibility of the British system help it adapt to the shock, or did the dispersion of authority mean nobody could take decisive action? Going the other direction: does Brexit show that Machiavelli's model of personal princely agency is useless for modern parliamentary democracies, or does it reveal that institutions can do the adapting he thought only a single ruler could do?

Please don't just tell me whether Boris was a good prince. I want you to map the theory onto the institutional mechanics — who had standing to respond, what the options were, where Machiavelli's framework is silent — and say where the mapping breaks. I'm particularly interested in whether the 'restoration' framing of Brexit (taking back control) hides a welfare judgement about whose suffering counts, though I'm not sure that's a Machiavellian move. Also, please be explicit about what would change your mind: what specific feature of Machiavelli's texts — say, his treatment of republics in the Discorsi — or what detail about the Brexit timeline would make you reverse the claim that the UK lacked virtù? And if you think the whole application is forced, say so — I need the reasoning, not just a verdict.
