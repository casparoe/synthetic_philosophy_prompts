I have recently inherited a ceramic sculpture made by my late grandmother, and in deciding whether to have it repaired with a visible modern epoxy or to preserve the fractures as part of its history, I realised that my intuition about whether the repaired object is still the same piece depends on distinctions for which I lack the vocabulary. I understand the analytic debate about material constitution — the statue and the clay, coincidence, and persistence conditions — only at second hand, but I am fluent in the vocabulary of art conservation: substrate, provenance, structural continuity, reversibility of intervention, and the ethical distinction between restoration and reconstruction. Restate the main philosophical positions on the statue and the clay — that the statue is identical to its material substrate, that it is constituted by the substrate without being identical to it, and that it is a separate object governed by form-related persistence conditions — using only that conservation vocabulary, as precisely and systematically as you can. For each position, report what translates cleanly, what must be distorted or forced to fit, and what has no counterpart whatsoever in conservation practice. Then give me the residue report: does conservation ethics, particularly the principle that any restoration ought to be reversible and distinguishable from the original material, reveal an unstated assumption in the philosophical debate about coincidence and identity? Or is there something essential in the philosophers' argument — perhaps about modal properties or about the possibility of coincident objects — that conservation practice implicitly denies but never needs to articulate? I want the translation to be honest, not a smuggled analogy, and I want to know whether the failure to carry something over is a defect in the conservation framework or a sign that the philosophical claim was less substantive than it appeared.
