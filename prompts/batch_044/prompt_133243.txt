I have been discussing with colleagues a family of philosophical scenarios used to examine whether moral responsibility requires that an agent genuinely could have acted otherwise—the principle of alternative possibilities. In the familiar versions, an agent makes a decision, and there is a hidden controller—perhaps a neuroscientist or a counterfactual mechanism—who stands ready to force the same decision should the agent show any sign of deviating, though in the actual sequence no intervention occurs. I understand these cases are designed to show that responsibility can persist even when alternative possibilities are absent in one specific sense. Recently, however, I have noticed a structurally analogous but apparently distinct configuration. Here, there is no discrete controller waiting in the wings. Rather, the agent's environment has been shaped over time—by predictive algorithms, institutional architectures, or cumulative feedback loops—so that by the moment of deliberation, all alternatives except the one eventually chosen have been gradually eliminated or rendered practically inaccessible. The agent experiences no direct coercion and deliberates freely within the remaining option space; yet if they were to try to diverge, they would find the path already absent, not encounter an active blocker. Is there an established term for this specific arrangement, in which the absence of alternatives arises from ex ante structural closure rather than from a counterfactual intervener? How exactly should it be distinguished from the standard intervention cases with respect to the principle of alternative possibilities, and if it is not separately named, what defining features and boundary cases would allow us to treat it as a distinct category rather than a peripheral variant?
