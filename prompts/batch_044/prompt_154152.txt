As a retired physical chemist who has recently begun reading philosophy of mind, I have observed a specific pattern in experiments and clinical practice that I cannot quite place within the usual frameworks. In sensory studies, volunteers rating the pungency of an identical chemical compound report it as noticeably harsher when they are shown a molecular structure they can genuinely parse—say, a benzene notation they understand—than when they are shown the same structural information rendered in an unfamiliar symbolic system, even though in both conditions they are explicitly told the substance is synthetic. The propositional content is the same, yet the perceptual shift occurs only with the comprehensible format. A parallel case arises in radiology: experienced clinicians detect a subtle tissue density in a scan displayed with the standard colour map they have learned, but miss the identical feature when the palette is inverted, despite being verbally warned of its presence. In both examples, information seems to reach perception not merely by being believed, but only when it is carried by a representational vehicle that the subject can interpret from within. Is there an established concept for this precise phenomenon—this dependence of perceptual alteration on grasp of the representational format rather than on belief content alone—and, if so, how is it distinguished from ordinary expectation effects, linguistic relativism, or the general thesis that cognition penetrates perception? Should the literature treat it as a subtype, or does it fall outside current taxonomies entirely? If no term exists, I should welcome a precise coinage with clear boundary conditions: when does the effect collapse into mere misunderstanding of the display, and when does it genuinely alter the sensory experience?
