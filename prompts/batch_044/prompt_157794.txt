Consider Harold Ramis's Groundhog Day (1993) as an unwitting philosophical experiment in the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies — particularly its symmetry claim that prenatal non-existence is not a harm — but apply a non-standard framing: treat Phil Connors's temporal loop as a phenomenological analog to the state Epicurus describes, in which linear time collapses, outcomes are predetermined, and the subject retains full consciousness and memory yet is deprived of any narrative terminus. In this reading, the loop becomes a controlled examination of whether harm requires experiential presence or can instead consist in the truncation of a project that needs an endpoint to be meaningful.

Your task is a critical reading in both directions. First, show where the film illuminates the Epicurean position: does Phil's suffering in repetition come from the absence of death (and thus of a completed life-arc), or from something structurally different? Analyze the suicide montage, the piano-lesson sequence, and the final broadcast as potential evidence for either interpretation. Second, show where the film resists the Epicurean: if Phil escapes not through accepting non-existence but through moral self-correction that finally earns him linear time, does this vindicate the deprivation account — that what we rationally fear is not experiential pain but the impossibility of authentic self-completion without finality?

Take both the film's emotional architecture and the philosophical argument seriously. Note precisely where the analogy breaks down — for instance, Phil retains causal efficacy and cross-iteration memory in ways the dead do not — and do not reduce the work to a mere illustration. Conclude by saying what this particular dramatization contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot capture, specifically regarding whether the harm of death, if any, lies in experience, in deprivation, or in the structural impossibility of a completed self without a terminus.
