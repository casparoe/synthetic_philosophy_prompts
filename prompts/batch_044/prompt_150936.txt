I work as a senior review officer at a bilateral development agency, and every quarter my unit must sign off on country-level causal narratives submitted by field offices—reports that claim a specific factor (institutions, geography, culture, or some interaction) explains why one nation is wealthy or poor relative to peers. We cannot run new regressions; we have about two hours per report, access only to the document and standard reference tables, and we must issue a formal pass, conditional pass, or reject with written justification. The reports routinely confuse correlation with mechanism, treat historical contingency as deterministic, and slide from “this factor is present in rich countries” to “this factor caused the richness.” I need a reusable evaluation procedure—an ordered sequence of tests with explicit pass/fail/uncertain criteria—that my reviewers can apply without being philosophers or econometricians.

The procedure must be built around the specific methodological limits of macro-historical causal inference: the problem of overdetermination at the national-unit level, the instability of counterfactuals when the “intervention” is an institution or cultural pattern rather than a discrete treatment, and the distinction between a structural cause (invariant across time for that unit) and a configurational cause (dependent on interaction with other units). Design the steps in order, stating for each what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with an outright rejection (especially for what we call “galactic overdetermination”—asserting one dominant cause when the record shows multiple sufficient pathways), and where it must hand the decision to a human reviewer because the issue is one of values or interpretive judgment rather than logic or evidence. Include a specific “downgrade” pathway: when a claim survives but only by retreating to a weaker, non-causal formulation such as “facilitating condition” or “enabling context.”

Then apply the procedure to three submitted narratives. First, a report arguing that Norway’s wealth is primarily caused by its early adoption of parliamentary institutions, comparing it only to Denmark and Sweden. Second, a report arguing that Mali’s poverty is geographically determined by landlockedness and tropical climate, treating colonial institutional disruption as a secondary interaction effect. Third, a report arguing that Japan’s post-war growth was culturally caused by “group-oriented values”; here the procedure should yield an answer the field office will resist—specifically, that the claim must be downgraded not because culture is irrelevant, but because the counterfactual required to establish national-level causation is historically unstable, forcing the report into the “facilitating condition” category.

Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of unconventional but valid arguments it will systematically misclassify, what questions about distributive justice or policy priority it leaves entirely untouched, and why it is a filter for narrative coherence rather than a substitute for empirical research design. Mark every point where judgment is ineliminable, and include one worked example of each kind of outcome—pass, conditional pass with downgrade, and reject.
