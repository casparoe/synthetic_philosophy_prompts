In recent philosophical and technical discussions of large language models, I have noticed a recurring dialectical pattern that I cannot place within the standard taxonomy of attitudes toward mental attribution. Analysts routinely invoke belief- and desire-language to explain specific model behaviors—describing a system as believing that p or wanting to avoid q in order to render an otherwise puzzling output intelligible—yet, in the very same discussion, they deny that such attributions are literally true, insisting that the model is merely a stochastic token-predictor lacking any representational architecture that could sustain propositional attitudes. The shift is not mere equivocation: it is systematically correlated with the direction of explanatory need. When the task is predictive or interpretive, the intentional stance is adopted without apology; when the task is normative—assigning responsibility, assessing deception, or evaluating epistemic authority—the stance is withdrawn and replaced by an eliminativist or instrumentalist gloss.

Consider two concrete instances. First, a safety evaluation reports that a model harbors false beliefs about pharmacological interactions to explain why it generates dangerous medical advice in certain prompt configurations; in the report's ethical appendix, however, the same authors state that because the model possesses no beliefs, it cannot be said to deceive or mislead. Second, a philosopher of mind argues that predicting conversational dynamics requires attributing to the model a stable set of preferences and background assumptions, yet in the metaphysical section of the same essay asserts that no such stable states exist, since the model's weights do not encode discrete propositional contents.

Is there an established term for this specific configuration—where the intentional stance is deployed selectively as an explanatory resource for local behavior prediction, but systematically suspended as an ontological commitment whenever normative or metaphysical stakes arise? If Dennett's original framework allows one to adopt or abandon the stance based on predictive utility, does this pattern represent a legitimate application of his view, or does it constitute a distinct failure mode—perhaps a kind of stance bifurcation that undermines the coherence of the attribution? If no established concept captures it precisely, I would ask you to coin a term, provide a precise definition that distinguishes it from ordinary pragmatic inconsistency, from the as-if interpretation of mental talk, and from straightforward eliminativism about AI mental states, and indicate where the boundary cases lie: for example, whether a researcher who consistently uses belief-language for prediction but never for responsibility is exhibiting the phenomenon, or whether the phenomenon requires an explicit contradiction within a single argument.
