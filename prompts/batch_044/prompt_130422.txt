Compose a Petrarchan sonnet — octave rhyming ABBAABBA, sestet rhyming CDECDE, with the volta falling between lines eight and nine — spoken in the voice of G. E. Moore. The subject should be his claim, developed in his best-known ethical work, that both beauty and friendship are intrinsic goods, and that they exemplify what he called "organic unity". Use the octave to voice a precise objection: that friendship is valuable only for the pleasure and security it produces, and that beauty is valuable only for the sensory enjoyment it yields — in other words, that both are merely instrumental to something else. At the volta, let Moore reply that this misses the point: the value of a friendship, or of an aesthetic experience shared between friends, belongs to the whole complex and is not equal to the sum of the values of its parts taken in isolation; to analyse them into discrete pleasures is to commit the error of treating an organic unity as a mechanical aggregate. Let the rhyme scheme do structural work: the enclosed rhymes of the octave should suggest parts being paired off and separated, while the interwoven sestet should resist the closure of a couplet, mirroring the idea that value here is not added up but entangled. The poem should end on line fourteen with a question or image that leaves open whether "organic unity" names a genuine emergent property or merely marks the limits of our analysis. Write it for an audience that has just debated whether intrinsic value can be decomposed into constituent goods, so that the formal turn from octave to sestet enacts the conceptual turn from reductionism to holism. Do not quote directly from Moore's texts; render his intuitionism, his distinction between intrinsic and instrumental value, and his critique of hedonistic analysis in your own language, with enough accuracy that a reader familiar with early analytic ethics recognises the position immediately.
