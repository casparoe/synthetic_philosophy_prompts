I am not a trained philosopher; I have been working through questions about reading and interpretation on my own, and I recently started a small informal study circle with friends who are similarly self-taught. For our last session I composed a short exam question and received one written response. I am acting as the grader, but I want my evaluation to be precise and defensible rather than impressionistic. Please assess the answer below: assign it an explicit grade (for instance, B+, B, B-, or an A-F equivalent) and then provide point-by-point comments that justify that grade. Identify what is accurate and well-reasoned, flag any places where the reasoning overreaches or slips, and explain why. Do not invent errors that are not present, and do not substitute a general summary for a line-by-line evaluation.

The exam question was:

Consider a novelist who writes a scene in which a protagonist chooses loyalty to a friend over honesty to a stranger. The narrative details surrounding the choice—descriptions of tone, physical setting, and the protagonist's interior state—are rendered with warmth, emotional proximity, and implicit approval. After the book is published, the novelist states in an interview that she intended the scene to show that loyalty is a dangerous form of self-deception. Some readers argue that the text itself supports admiration for loyalty; others insist that the author's explanation should settle the matter. How should we weigh the novelist's later statement? And is there a meaningful distinction between asking what the author meant to embed in the work during composition and asking what the work actually conveys to a careful, independent reader?

The student's answer:

The student begins correctly: a reader never has direct access to an author's private mental states; the public text is the only object available for interpretation. They observe that literary works regularly produce meanings their creators did not anticipate, and that an author's retrospective interview should be treated as one piece of evidence among others rather than as an authoritative decree that ends discussion. They invoke the intentional fallacy—the error of judging or interpreting a work primarily by comparing it to the author's private aims—and use this to argue that the novelist's statement should not be treated as decisive.

They then claim that Roland Barthes made exactly this point in his essay The Death of the Author, arguing that the author must be entirely eliminated from interpretation. From this they conclude that retrospective authorial commentary is not merely weak or non-decisive evidence but completely without interpretive relevance: a purely biographical fact that should be ignored altogether.

Applying this to the scene, they note that the narrative's warmth and emotional proximity indicate that the work conveys admiration for loyalty. They therefore argue that the correct reading is that loyalty is admirable in this context, and that the novelist's contrary statement should be dismissed. They add a further claim: if the novelist genuinely intended to condemn loyalty but failed to realize that intention in the text, then the intended meaning is still present in the work, only "blocked" or hidden by poor execution. The failure of execution, they say, does not erase the presence of the intended meaning, even though a reader should still interpret the text according to what it actually conveys.

Please grade and assess.
