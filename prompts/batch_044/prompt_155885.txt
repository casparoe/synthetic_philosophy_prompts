I am presenting my paper, "Moral Translation and Pragmatic Convergence: A Quinean Model of Cross-Cultural Evaluation," at a philosophy department colloquium next Thursday. My central claim is that moral disagreements between radically different linguistic communities are best understood not as conflicts over a shared domain of moral facts, but as instances of Quinean indeterminacy of translation applied to normative vocabularies: each community embeds its values in a holistic manual of social coordination that is underdetermined by behavioural evidence. I argue that moral values should therefore be treated as regulative postulates—analogous to Quine's treatment of mathematical entities—whose warrant lies not in correspondence to an independent moral reality, but in their predictive utility for stabilising cooperative behaviour across contexts. I further propose that cross-cultural moral criticism should be reconceived as a pragmatic comparison of coordination stability rather than as truth-tracking evaluation.

The audience will be about twenty faculty and postgraduate students: half are moral realists (including two non-naturalists who defend irreducible normative properties), three are specialists in Quine's philosophy who regard his occasional remarks on ethics as underdeveloped and possibly inconsistent with his naturalism, two applied ethicists concerned with human rights discourse, and the remainder are general analytic philosophers with little patience for relativism.
