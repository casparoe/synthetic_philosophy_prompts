I am preparing my defense for a seminar presentation to the evolutionary dynamics working group next Thursday. The audience is precise: two evolutionary game theorists who have published extensively on reciprocal altruism and consider Tit-for-Tat the baseline explanation for cooperation in iterated prisoner's dilemma settings; several behavioral economists who run high-noise, multi-round human-subject experiments and care more about empirical fit than about evolutionary stability proofs; and a philosopher of biology who is generally skeptical of adaptationist explanations and will probe whether my argument smuggles in normative assumptions about rational agency. Over coffee with colleagues last week I sketched my view and was told it sounds like a minor variant of Nowak's work, which convinced me I need to distinguish it clearly. My thesis is that cooperation in the iterated prisoner's dilemma is sustained not by reciprocal punishment but by what I call forgiving-oblivious strategies—policies that do not condition future cooperation on an opponent's previous move—because real agents with bounded rationality face an epistemic opacity of defection: they cannot reliably parse intentional defection from stochastic noise at a computational cost lower than the expected payoff of cooperation, making attempts at reciprocity unstable. I claim this explains persistence of cooperation in noisy empirical environments better than standard tournament models, but my presentation currently lacks a formal derivation of the opacity condition from a specified bounded-rationality framework. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as the evolutionary theorists, the behavioral economists, and the skeptical philosopher would actually phrase them. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest; provide a concise response appropriate for a thirty-minute Q&A; and identify the objection I am most hoping nobody asks—the one targeting my informal treatment of epistemic opacity—together with how to defend it on the fly rather than hoping it is missed.
