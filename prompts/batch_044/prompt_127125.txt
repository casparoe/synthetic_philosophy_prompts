I'm taking an upper-division seminar on nationalism and political theory, and I have been trying to formalize an objection to the constructivist account of nations—roughly the view, associated with scholars like Hobsbawm and Anderson, that modern nations depend on invented traditions and shared narratives rather than on ancient ethnic substrates. I constructed the following argument, which I now suspect proves far too much, but I cannot locate the precise misstep.

(1) If a collective's coherence depends entirely on deliberately fabricated narratives, standardized languages, and selective amnesia about its origins, then there is no pre-political people that possesses inherent rights or obligations prior to those narratives; the collective is, in an important sense, a self-authorizing fiction.
(2) Nations satisfy this condition: their traditions are largely modern inventions, and their members will never meet one another.
(3) Therefore, appeals to national sovereignty or collective cultural rights are circular—the nation is invoked as the ground of the right, but the nation only exists because the right is invoked.
(4) However, the same analysis applies recursively. The intellectuals, bureaucrats, and educators who construct national narratives are themselves constituted by smaller-scale invented communities—academic disciplines, professional guilds, and even families—each sustained by shared myths, ritual repetition, and selective memory.
(5) So if being invented in this way nullifies a collective's moral legitimacy, then the constructivist's own critical standpoint collapses, because it is produced by structures that are equally invented.
(6) By the same token, every social bond—from citizenship to friendship to family loyalty—is dissolved, since none can point to a non-constructed origin.
(7) Yet we treat obligations within families and local associations as genuinely binding, and the constructivists themselves do not deny those obligations.
(8) Hence the inference from constructedness to illegitimacy proves too much: it yields either a global moral nihilism about all collective life or a self-undermining skepticism.

Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? I want particularly to preserve both the idea that national identity is genuinely constructed and the idea that family obligations are not illusory. Is the error an equivocation between being constructed and being fictitious, or is the recursive move in (4)–(5) a non sequitur that only looks like pressure? If I block the slide at (1), must I concede that some collectives have a natural substrate after all?

Please also indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
