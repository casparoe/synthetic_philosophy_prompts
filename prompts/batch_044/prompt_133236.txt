I am leading a ninety-minute seminar for philosophy-of-science undergraduates who have completed one introductory statistics course. The goal is not to have them compute Simpson's paradox, but to understand it as a structural failure of aggregation for causal inference: specifically, why an association that holds in every subgroup can vanish or reverse in the pooled data, and what that implies about the relationship between statistical patterns and causal claims. Design the explanatory sequence: identify the conceptual dependency that must be established before showing the reversal—likely the idea that association is partition-dependent rather than invariant—then specify the single carrying scenario (it must involve two groups with a hidden allocation mechanism driven by a third variable, and it must avoid medical or vaccine contexts that trigger prior beliefs, and avoid pure numerical tables without narrative structure). Map the misconceptions in the order students will form them: that correlation at any level implies causation; that the larger-N pooled result is epistemically privileged; that stratifying on the confounder always yields the "true" effect; and the more subtle error that if reversal occurs in all subgroups then population-level causation is ontologically empty. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted. Provide one end-of-session diagnostic question that tests whether they grasp the structural point—something that forces them to reason about a new partition, not recall the example. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level (for instance, full causal-graph notation or the formal conditions for collapsibility) and justify the omission. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
