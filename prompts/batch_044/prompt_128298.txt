I'm a journalist who covers science, philosophy, and institutional ethics for a major publication, and next month I'm delivering the opening remarks—about five to six minutes—at a workshop I'm co-hosting with a university philosophy department, titled After the Scandal: Professional Roles and Moral Accountability. The audience is split between scholars of Confucian ethics and comparative moral philosophy on one side, and working science writers, editors, and researchers who have just lived through a serious credibility crisis in our field on the other. I want the remarks to treat Confucian role ethics as a live framework for understanding institutional failure, not as decorative cultural reference; the idea should structurally determine what I can and cannot claim about blame, repair, and returning to work.

Specifically, I want the speech to turn on a conceptual synthesis I've been developing: Confucian rectification of names (zhengming) insists that roles such as editor, reporter, or mentor carry specific obligations, but I am convinced these obligations are not best understood as abstract social scripts or duties. Rather, they are constituted by what I call reciprocal vulnerability—to genuinely occupy a professional role is to be made specifically vulnerable by those who depend on your judgment, and it is that vulnerability, not institutional authority, that generates the ethical demands. This is not standard textbook Confucianism; it injects a care-ethical sensibility into role ethics in a way that needs to be tested. The remarks should use this idea to frame accountability: if vulnerability is constitutive of the role, then the defense that one was merely following professional protocol cannot function as a moral shield, since the role itself includes exposure to those harmed by one's choices. At the same time, the speech must not collapse into the claim that every professional must become a private moral hero; there must be a principled boundary between legitimate role-bound discretion and culpable evasion.

Please write the remarks in a voice precise enough that the philosophers do not wince and accessible enough that the practitioners do not check out. Take a committed position—either this synthesis saves Confucian role ethics from becoming an apologia for bureaucracy, or it fails because it demands an unsustainable moral heroism from ordinary professionals—and build the speech around that verdict with reasons, rather than balancing options neutrally. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; paraphrase conceptual moves without quotation marks if you are unsure of exact wording. Finally, include a brief closing note identifying where the argument takes liberties with standard Confucian interpretations or relies on the injected reciprocal-vulnerability framework, since I want the audience to distinguish traditional commitments from my own synthesis.
