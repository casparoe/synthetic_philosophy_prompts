I'm on the advisory pannel for the state long-range planning board, and we have to vote Thursday on whether to reinstate the old 1982 Harrow resource framework into active policy planning. Harrow predicted a sharp Malthusian-style collapse in regional food and energy capacity by 2005—didn't happen, got officially falsified and shelved in '98. Now a faction on staff says it was just "right but early": the green-revolution subsidies and cheap fossil inputs were temporary rescues, not real disproof, and with those buffers gone the 2040 trajectory lines up almost exactly with Harrow's curve. They want us to adopt it for the new rationing and land-use protocols.

The opposing faction says this is the worst kind of post-hoc immunization—if every failed long-run prediction can be saved by inventing a "delayed feedback loop" or "hidden subsidy" that bought time, then no forecast is ever truly falsifiable, and we're just using old authority to justify present austerity. They want permanent retirement.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It needs a concrete reccomendation—either pilot the framework in one district for five years with strict exit conditions, or kill it and explain why rescue moves like this undermine predictive integrity. The reasoning should take the "right but early" idea seriously rather than mocking it, but also address whether there's any principled line between a legitimate temporal rescue and special pleading. Don't ask for citations or a bibliography; I need a document the board can act on, not an academic survey. Also avoid framing it as just "what did Malthus say"—the philosophical problem is whether long-range theories can be falsified at all when the predicted outcome is always just around the corner.
