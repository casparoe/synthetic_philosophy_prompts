I have been working through Grice's distinction between what is said and what is conversationally implicated, particularly in contexts where the Cooperative Principle is apparently flouted. It seems to me that since implicature is anchored in the speaker's M-intention—the reflexive intention that the audience recognize a particular belief—any inference drawn by the listener must either coincide with that intended belief or fail to qualify as an implicature altogether. From this, I want to derive a stricter consequence: if a listener derives an inference that the speaker never intended, we should not describe this as a misrecovered implicature, but rather as a listener-side construction that lies entirely outside Grice's pragmatic framework. On this reading, miscommunication specifically about implicature becomes conceptually impossible: either the listener grasps the speaker's intended implicature, or there was no implicature present to be miscommunicated in the first place. I am trying to apply this reasoning to AI-mediated dialogue systems, in which an intermediate model may rephrase an original utterance before delivery, and I wonder whether Grice's account obliges us to treat every listener-derived inference in such chains as either a faithful recovery of the original speaker's meaning or as something that falls outside the domain of implicature entirely. Can one formalize this entailment within Grice's calculus—treating the speaker's reflexive intention as both necessary and sufficient for the existence of an implicature—such that any listener error is necessarily reconceived as error about what was said rather than about what was implicated? Or does Grice's framework already contain the resources to allow that a listener can genuinely misrecover an intended implicature, without thereby generating a spurious new one?
