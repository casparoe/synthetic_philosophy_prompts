To move beyond the standard first-order diagnosis, one of my colleagues introduced a distinction that I think is worth testing across the literature: first-order sphexishness, in which an agent performs stereotyped behavioural loops that are impervious to local feedback, and second-order sphexishness, in which the very mechanisms for strategic revision are themselves rigidly canalised, so that repeated failure cannot restructure the underlying architecture. I would like to see how this two-level framework applies to contemporary philosophical accounts of free will.

Please rank the following four positions according to their overall capacity to resist both forms of sphexishness without collapsing into an implausible libertarianism or an overly permissive compatibilism: (1) Dennett’s evolutionary-scaffolding account, which treats free will as an emergent design capacity shaped by cumulative selection; (2) a Frankfurt-style hierarchical compatibilist model, in which freedom is defined by the coherence between first-order desires and reflectively endorsed second-order volitions; (3) an agent-causal libertarian view, according to which a non-event-causal substance initiates actions that break deterministic chains; and (4) a predictive-processing framework, where action is driven by precision-weighted prediction-error minimisation rather than by explicit deliberative representation. For each position, assign a tier—S, A, B, C, or D—reflecting its resistance to sphexishness at both levels. Then, in one or two sentences per position, justify the placement, explicitly addressing both first-order behavioural flexibility and second-order meta-cognitive adaptability. Write your answer in formal British English, avoid bibliographies or specific textual citations, and treat the evaluation as an exercise in conceptual analysis rather than historical exegesis.
