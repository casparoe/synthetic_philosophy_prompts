Read Ursula Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the problem of interpersonal utility comparison — not just asking whether the child's suffering is "worth" the city's happiness, but whether that weighing is even conceptually coherent. Most readers treat it as a straightforward utilitarian horror story, but i want you to take the philosophy in the weeds: does the story smuggle in an assumption that joy and misery share a common scale, and do the citizens who stay actually perform a calculus or just accept a framework that lets them pretend the comparison works? Look at the moment they learn about the child and the moment some leave. Does Le Guin dramatize something like aggregation of preferences, or does she show that seeing the child breaks the comparability rather than just tipping the balance? And here's the twist: ask whether the ones who stay suffer a kind of corruption in thier own wellbeing precisely because they know they're part of the comparison — something the formal literature on utility comparison usually ignores. Don't reduce the story to an illustration; say where the fiction pushes back on the philosophy and where the philosophy exposes something the story leaves unsaid.
