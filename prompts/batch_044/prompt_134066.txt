Consider the cluster of institutional practices in classical Athens: the Assembly’s power to vote for the execution of an entire male population (Mytilene), to reverse that decree within a single day upon emotional reconsideration, to authorize a massive overseas military expedition (Sicily) despite explicit warnings from experienced commanders, and then—through the separate popular body of the dikasteria—to try and execute an individual philosopher (Socrates) for corrupting the youth and for impiety, all without constitutional separation of powers, without professional judges, and without any procedural safeguard preventing a narrow majority from deciding matters of life and death. The same citizen body could thus shift overnight from punitive severity to collective mercy, from strategic overreach to the scapegoating of generals, and from philosophical dissent to capital verdict, each time through direct majoritarian vote.
