Build a decision tree that sorts philosophical positions on whether individuals have moral reason to act—abstaining from meat, reducing carbon-intensive consumption, or voting in large elections—when single actions are causally inefficacious in collective harms. The root node should ask whether collective harm is decomposable into discrete individual contributions at all. From there, branch by harm-structure: continuous aggregation (each unit adds imperceptibly), threshold aggregation (outcome depends on crossing a boundary), or winner-take-all (only pivotal actions matter). Within each structural branch, split again by the source of obligation: counterfactual difference-making, participatory complicity in an unjust practice, or role-based institutional duty. Every leaf must name the position if standard (e.g., act-consequentialist rejection, expected-utility aggregation, practice-based solidarity, role-obligation) or mark it unnamed, and must carry one sentence on its core claim and one sentence on its most awkward commitment—such as having to treat safe-district voting as purely symbolic, or denying that climate footprints are ever individually blameworthy. Include at least one empty branch combination and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent (for instance, denying decomposability while insisting on strict individual causal liability), unattractive, or merely unexplored. Phrase every node as a concrete case judgment—e.g., “If one vote in a million changes nothing, is a wrong still done?”—so that answering requires no prior knowledge of the labels. Limit the tree to eight leaves or fewer, and ensure the three domains (climate, meat, voting) are all represented among the branches.
