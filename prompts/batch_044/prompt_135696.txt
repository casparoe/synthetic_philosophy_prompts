As a retired biologist who has only recently begun working through philosophical texts with any systematic care, I have been struck by the gap between clinical descriptions of mortality and the conceptual arguments philosophers offer about its disvalue. I am preparing a discussion for a small philosophy-reading group composed mostly of scientists, and I want to read Leo Tolstoy’s novella *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* through the lens of the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies—specifically through the version that turns on the symmetry between prenatal and posthumous non-existence and on the claim that there is no subject present to suffer harm once life has ended. At the same time, I want to inject a distinction that is rarely made explicit in popular treatments: the difference between death as a discrete terminus (the state of being dead) and dying as an extended biological and social process of decline, alienation, and role-loss. Ivan’s terror seems directed less at annihilation itself than at the progressive dismantling of his professional and domestic identity, his bodily betrayal, and his exclusion from the social rituals that once defined him.

Could you offer a reading that takes both Tolstoy’s narrative and the Epicurean conceptual framework seriously—not reducing the novella to an illustration of the argument, nor distorting the argument to fit the plot? I am particularly interested in which scenes—such as the moment of recognition in the black sack, or the final reconciliation with his family—illuminate where the Epicurean claim holds and where it fails to address what actually horrifies Ivan. Does the novella suggest that the harm of mortality lies not in the state of non-being but in the anticipatory knowledge of that state, or in the degradation of agency during the process? Furthermore, does Tolstoy dramatize something about the social construction of death—the way Ivan’s colleagues treat his absence as an administrative vacancy—that philosophical arguments about individual harm tend to miss? I would like an honest assessment: where the philosophical lens genuinely clarifies a scene, where the work resists that reading and the critic must concede the mismatch, and what the novella might add to the debate about whether death is bad that purely conceptual analysis cannot supply. I am not asking for bibliographic references or citations; I want an argument that can stand on its own for a group that will not have the text in front of them but knows the story’s major beats.
