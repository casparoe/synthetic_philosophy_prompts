Right, I'm a retired biologist — spent years on viral quasispecies — and only lately have I got into philosophy, mostly about how nations remember defeat. I'm stuck on the lost-causes idea: the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo Polje, Dunkirk. There's a concept in evolutionary theory called error catastrophe, where mutation rates climb past a threshold and the quasispecies loses its master sequence and collapses. I want to analyse the Kosovo myth — the 1389 battle that Serbian nationalism turned into a sacred loss — through that lens. Treat the myth as a replicaton: every retelling, from epic poems to church sermons to 1980s nationalist speeches, is replication with mutation. Has Kosovo hit error catastrophe, or is it a highly conserved master sequence with only surface drift? Compare it briefly to Dunkirk, where the framing of the evacuation as a miraculous deliverance seems stabilised with little mutation, and to the Confederacy's Lost Cause, which I think has genuinely degraded into incoherence. Where does the biological anologue actually illuminate national memory, where does it break because myths don't have fixed fitness lanscapes, and what does the comparison tell us about why some defeats stay potent for centuries while others fade?
