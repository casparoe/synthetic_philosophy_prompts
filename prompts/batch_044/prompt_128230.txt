I am a high school junior in an elective on Enlightenment philosophy, and our class just finished reading selections from Émilie du Châtelet's essay on happiness. In my study group we have been arguing about whether her hierarchy of passions—study ranked above glory, love, and sensual pleasure—describes a real psychological law or merely defends the life of a scholar. Rather than ending with another summary, I want to present a poem that forces the idea to work structurally.

Please write a villanelle in which two refrains carry the philosophical load. The first refrain should capture her claim that study is the only passion that is fully self-sufficient, requiring neither another person's approval nor a favorable bodily state, and therefore never decays into disgust. The second refrain should capture the dependency and eventual exhaustion of the other passions—glory, which borrows its light from an audience; love, which hinges on reciprocity; and pleasure, which depends on a body that tires. The poem should follow the ABA rhyme scheme with the refrains in the A positions. Through the five tercets, the two refrains should alternate and circle past one another without resolving, tracking how these two conditions for happiness keep intersecting but never collapsing into one. In the final quatrain, both refrains should appear together, standing side by side in co-presence rather than synthesis, reflecting her view that happiness needs the stable, prejudice-resistant foundation of study even while the other passions remain genuine but unstable supplements. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with her argument recognizes the specific distinctions—especially that study alone escapes the corruption of prejudice and external opinion—without becoming a didactic lecture. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface, commentary, or restatement of the prompt, and write in American English.
