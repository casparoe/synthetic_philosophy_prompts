I serve as the student representative on the Interim Governance Committee of the Meridian Residential School, a small boarding program for upper-secondary students in the sciences and arts. Next Wednesday we must circulate a recommendation for a binding referendum scheduled before the term ends: the faculty and administration have proposed a Distributed Learning Compact that would allow up to forty percent of coursework to be completed remotely, altering the residential character of the school. The philosophical problem is the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos?—because the committee is split on the franchise. Should voting be limited to currently enrolled students and full-time faculty; should it include parents, who pay tuition and live with the consequences; alumni who graduated within the last decade and maintain mentoring relationships; or the exchange students present for only one semester?

I want the memo to recommend one concrete franchise rule with a clear implementation timeline, and to treat the strongest opposing view honestly rather than dismissing it. To keep the reasoning from being generic, please engage specifically with an argument raised by one of my co-members, which she calls the dormancy objection: she claims that alumni and future students hold only a dormant stake in present governance, and that direct voting rights for them would fracture the diachronic unity of the current student body, so their interests should be channeled only through non-binding advisory proxies. At the same time, take seriously the counter-argument that parents are not mere outsiders but co-subjects to the school’s disciplinary and financial authority, and that excluding them repeats the classic error of treating affectedness and subjection as separable when they overlap in institutional life.

Please take on the role of a senior advisor in democratic theory and institutional ethics, focusing on conceptual clarity rather than legal citation or historical bibliography. Before drafting the memo, spend a brief moment brainstorming how synchronous subjection, asynchronous affectedness, and representational dormancy interact in this specific case. Then produce the formal document: a direct recommendation (approve one franchise rule, reject it, or approve with conditions), reasoning that a skeptical committee member could actually respond to, and an explicit acknowledgment of the best consideration on the losing side. Finally, I invite plain pushback: if framing a school referendum through the boundary problem is a category mistake, or if the dormancy objection is incoherent as I have presented it, say so clearly and do not politely construct a memo on a confused foundation.
