I've been thinking about the torture vs dust specks thing, but I wanna push past the usual intuition-pumping exercises you see everywhere. So here's a position I'm tentatively holding, and I want you to be honest about how exposed it is.

The reason we don't equate one person being tortured with a billion people getting a dust speck in the eye isn't just scope neglect or some bias toward vivid suffering. I think there's a real structural feature of moral agency here—when harm is spread across billions of separate causal chains, no single agent holds enough of it for the harm to register as genuinely 'thier' in the way torture is. Call it the fragmentation thesis. On this view, aggregation isn't just psychologically hard; it's ontologically different because the agent-victim relation gets diluted below a threshold of ownership. The suffering adds up, but the moral accountability doesn't, or at least not in a linear way.

What would count against this? Not the standard 'but suffering is suffering regardless' reply—I'm looking for the real defeaters. Would it take experiments in moral psychology showing people treat distributed harm identicly when agency is made explicit? A formal result in collective action theory showing that group intentionality preserves individual ownership after all? Or maybe some case where we intuitively blame everyone in a massive chain for a tiny contribution, which would show the threshold idea is ad hoc? I also want to know if I've just qualified the view into unfalsifiability—if I say 'below the threshold it doesn't count,' is that a problem for a view like this, or is that exactly what a structural thesis should do? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones defenders of fragmentation could honestly concede without collapsing the whole thing.
