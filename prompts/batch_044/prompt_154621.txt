I'm preparing the syllabus for my graduate seminar on conceptual change, and a tension has emerged that I think would make for a rigorous exercise in conceptual architecture. Douglas Hofstadter's model of creativity as "knob-twiddling"—the sliding of parameters within an existing conceptual frame to generate analogies, metaphors, and variations—strikes me as powerful for explaining scientific analogy or mathematical concept-stretching. Yet I keep returning to creative acts that appear to move in precisely the opposite direction: not loosening a concept's dimensions but suddenly fixing them in a configuration previously unthinkable, generating significance through an unexpected rigid boundary rather than through parameter variation.

Write a polished dialogue between two equally sophisticated interlocutors—Dr. Sera Lin and Dr. Jonas Reed—debating whether Hofstadter's model is exhaustive or whether there exists a genuinely distinct mode of conceptual creativity that Reed calls "frame crystallization." Lin should defend the view that even apparently rigid creative achievements—such as the axiomatic closure that produces non-Euclidean geometry, or Duchamp's presentation of a factory-made urinal as a finished artwork—are ultimately decomposable into complex, higher-order knob adjustments and inter-frame slippages. Reed should argue that these cases involve a structural inversion: the creative move is not variation within a pre-given conceptual space but the imposition of an unexpected constraint that retroactively redefines the space itself, producing originality not by analogy but by boundary-lock.

The exchange should contain real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues: Lin must directly address Reed's claim that "locking" cannot be reconstructed as sliding without losing the phenomenological specificity of the act, and Reed must engage Lin's counter that all apparent crystallization is merely a rapid, invisible sequence of parameter shifts. They should probe whether their disagreement is merely descriptive or carries normative weight—does recognizing frame crystallization change how we assess originality, authorship, or conceptual progress, or is it only a different vocabulary for the same underlying mechanism? Please write in a formal philosophical register, with neither party serving as a strawman, and do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or appeals to texts I should consult.
