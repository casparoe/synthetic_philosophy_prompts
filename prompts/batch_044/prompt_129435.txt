Analyse the 1971 Nixon shock — the suspension of dollar-gold convertibility and the collapse of the Bretton Woods fixed-exchange system — through Burkean conservatism and Chesterton’s fence, but apply them via a distinction rarely made in either literature: between an ‘epistemic fence’ (an institution that persists because prior generations solved a problem current actors no longer grasp) and a ‘functional fence’ (one that persists only because it currently performs an obvious task). Map the Bretton Woods architecture onto this distinction: was the gold-exchange standard primarily an epistemic fence against the competitive devaluations and capital-control chaos of the 1930s, or merely a functional constraint on American deficit spending? Did Nixon and his advisers — particularly given their awareness of the Triffin dilemma — understand the problem the fence had solved, or did they treat it as an obsolete functional barrier? Then say what Burke’s argument from prescription and Chesterton’s principle each imply about whether the dismantling was reckless, and where both theories fail to fit: Bretton Woods was an engineered international regime of only twenty-five years, lacking the organic continuity and local embeddedness that Burke treats as the source of institutional wisdom. Does that mean the framework is misapplied, silent, or does it reveal that global monetary institutions can never be genuine ‘fences’? Conclude with what the episode demonstrates about whether Chesterton’s principle extends to deliberately constructed, transnational economic architecture.
