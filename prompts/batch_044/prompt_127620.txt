I serve on my university's governance committee for interdisciplinary institutes. We must recommend a selection mechanism for the new Institute for Synthetic Judgment — a body that will award three-year research chairs across six departments. The provost has proposed a ranked-pair veto system: each department ranks candidates; pairwise comparisons determine a social ranking, but any department can veto a candidate in its own field if that candidate is not in that department's top three. A faction of political scientists argues this is strategy-proof within departments but vulnerable to cross-departmental manipulation; a faction of economists insists that any deterministic, non-dictatorial mechanism with three or more outcomes in its range and an unrestricted domain of preferences must be manipulable — the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result — so we should abandon the search for strategy-proofness and instead design for transparency and auditability. Meanwhile, the philosophy department chair has submitted a minority report arguing that the theorem's conditions fail here in a structured way: departmental veto rights restrict the domain so that not every linear ordering over candidates is admissible as an input profile, and that if we formalize this restriction precisely — she proposes calling it domain localization — we may recover a strategy-proof rule provided we also weaken the range requirement so that no more than two candidates can ever be in contention across departments simultaneously. Draft the committee's decision memo: recommend whether to adopt the ranked-pair veto mechanism, approve it with modifications, or reject it in favor of an alternative you specify, with a concrete implementation timeline if adopted. The memo should engage the economists' appeal to Gibbard-Satterthwaite seriously — do not dismiss it — but also assess whether the philosophy chair's domain-localization argument actually escapes the theorem's scope, paying particular attention to whether the range still contains three or more alternatives once cross-departmental pairings are counted. Include an honest account of the strongest strategic-risk objection to your recommended course. Do not provide bibliographies or citations; reason from the concepts themselves.
