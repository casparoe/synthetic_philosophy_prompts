I'm working through materials for an independent study on comparative logic, and my discussion group has been stuck on how to interpret Gongsun Long's claim that a white horse is not a horse. Most introductory accounts present it as a puzzle about compound descriptions—essentially asking whether adding a color term changes the kind being referred to—but I think that framing misses what the School of Names actually meant by the separability of attributes. Rather than treating color and shape as properties that inhere in a single substance, the Mingjia seem to treat them as ontologically distinct realities that happen to coincide in experience: the form that makes something a horse and the color that makes it white are not unified inside one thing, but are separate realities that occur together without composing a genuine composite. If that reading is right, then 'white horse' does not name a kind at all, but only a temporary conjunction of two independent existences.

Here is my confusion: if attributes are separable in this strong metaphysical sense, then every object we perceive is just an unstable aggregation with no internal principle of unity. Wouldn't that push the School of Names toward a radical mereological nihilism in which there are no real composite entities, only fleeting coincidences? And if so, wouldn't their own practice of disputation—arguing specifically about the white horse—presuppose that this compound has enough stability to serve as the subject of a true or false statement? Or is there a coherent reading of this separability thesis that preserves the distinction between form and color without dissolving all compound reference into illusion?
