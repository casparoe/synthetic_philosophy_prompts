Analyze the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora and the ensuing 1816 "Year Without a Summer" as a case study in the attribution of historical agency to non-human climate forces, with explicit attention to the dangers of environmental determinism. Employing an actor-network framework—treating volcanic aerosols, anomalous temperature regimes, and harvest collapses as "actants" that modify relations among other entities—map the relevant players (climate perturbations, agrarian producers, grain markets, state relief institutions, and cultural producers such as the circle at Villa Diodati), the options available to human actors (market regulation, reserve release, migration, and symbolic adaptation), and the ways in which the climate event altered the feasibility or cost of those choices. Then specify at least one feature of the episode—such as marked regional disparities in famine mortality under comparable meteorological stress, or the particular convergence of conditions that yielded Mary Shelley's Frankenstein—that a strictly deterministic account cannot explain without recourse to pre-existing institutional structures or deliberate human decision. Conclude by evaluating whether framing Tambora as an agent-like historical actor clarifies the causal architecture of the crisis or instead obscures the policy and distributive choices that mediated its damage, and indicate what this case demonstrates about the conceptual limits of climate agency.
