Since my father’s death last autumn I have been trying to understand whether grief is best conceived as a judgment about the world, a bodily feeling, or some mixture of the two. In that effort I came across a passage from William James’s essay “What is an Emotion?” (1884) that is central but hard to parse, and I need help working through it.

James writes:

“Common sense says, we lose our fortune, are sorry and weep; we meet a bear, are frightened and run; we are insulted by a rival, are angry and strike. The hypothesis here to be defended says that this order of sequence is incorrect, that the one mental state is not immediately induced by the other, that the bodily manifestations must first be interposed between, and that the more rational statement is that we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful, as the case may be. Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth.”

Just before this he distinguishes these “standard emotions”—those with distinct bodily expression—from other feelings (certain intellectual pleasures or aesthetic responses) that seem bound up with mental operations yet have “no obvious bodily expression” for their consequence.

My difficulty is this. Does James’s argument require that the cognitive appraisal—the “perception of the exciting fact”—lies entirely outside the emotion, serving only as its trigger, so that the emotion itself is nothing but the feeling of bodily disturbance? Or does he allow that the appraisal remains part of the emotional state, merely insisting that it is insufficient without the accompanying bodily state? And how should we apply his framework to grief, where there is both an explicit judgment (“he is dead and will not return”) and a diffuse bodily state—does James force us to say that the judgment is not part of the grief proper, or only that it is not its emotional core? In particular, his claim that without bodily states the perception would remain “purely cognitive” seems to sit uneasily with his admission that some feelings lack bodily expression altogether; I want to understand how these two parts of the passage fit together.

Please help me understand the passage in light of these questions, paying particular attention to the tension between judgment-based, perception-based, and feeling-based accounts of emotion.
