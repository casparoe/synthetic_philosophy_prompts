My roommate's in a sociology PhD and we've been bickering about whether "national character" is even a coherent thing to talk about. Over the past few months she's made these calls on different examples: she said it's totally fine for a historian to describe 19th-century Prussian bureaucratic culture as risk-averse and rule-bound, but flat wrong to say "Prussians are naturally rule-bound"; she liked an anthropologist comparing Japanese and American apology norms in institutional settings, but hated it when a travel blogger claimed "Japanese people just have a soul that apologizes"; she said using "French secularism" in political analysis is okay if you tie it to specific laws and schools, but rejected a vlogger saying "the French are just rude people"; she was cool with a psychologist linking Danish social trust to welfare-state structures, but not okay with someone using the same numbers to argue "Danes are genetically trusting"; and she approved a diplomat saying "German industrial culture values precision" in a trade memo, but said it was unacceptable when a politician used "Germans lack imagination" in a campaign speech.

Now she's reviewing a working paper for her journal club. The paper uses survey data across regions to argue that Brazilian business culture tends to prioritize personal relationships over contractual formalism, and the author frames this explicitly as "part of the Brazilian national character shaped by colonial patrimonialism." I want to know what she'll say — approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions? More importantly, what rule is she actually running? I can see at least three that fit her past verdicts: one that cares about institutional framing vs essentialist framing, one that cares about academic vs popular context, and one that cares about comparative qualification vs absolute attribution. Which one best matches her track record, what does each predict about this Brazilian case, and what further case — maybe the same claim in a diplomatic cable vs a TikTok, or framed with "cultural script" instead of "national character" — would actually split the rules apart?
