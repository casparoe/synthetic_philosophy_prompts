I was arguing with a friend last week about decision paralysis and mentioned Buridan's ass, but then we got sidetracked into this other pattern I keep noticing and now I'm wondering if philosophers actually have a name for it.

The setup is like the classic case—two options genuinely tied on every relevant dimension, no hidden reason to prefer either. But instead of freezing, the agent picks one based on something totally arbitrary: which button is closer to their thumb, which option was listed first, whatever. The strange part is what comes after. Once the choice is made, they dont just accept the arbitrariness; they start treating that random factor as if it were the decisive reason all along, building a little justification around it. Its not exactly post-hoc rationalization, because they arent just defending a choice they already made—they seem to genuinely develop a preference that literally wasn't there before the pick. I mentioned this to my friend and she said it sounds like satisficing or maybe cognitive dissonance reduction, but to me it feels structurally different: satisficing is about stopping search, not about inventing a principle after an arbitrary stop, and dissonance is usually about a bad choice, not about upgrading a neutral tie-breaker into a real reason.

So is there an established concept for this specific move—equal preference broken arbitrarily and then the break-point retroactively promoted into a genuine preference? How exactly does it relate to Buridan's original problem, and where are its edges—would it still apply if the arbitrary factor was completely unconscious, or if the agent later admitted it was random?
