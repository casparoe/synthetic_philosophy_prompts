I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. After reading Adam Smith's discussion of sympathy and the impartial spectator, I tried to model his framework using concepts from distributed systems—specifically state-machine replication and consensus protocols—to see whether it could be made precise. Instead, I derived an argument that seems valid but reaches an absurd conclusion about moral disagreement being path-dependent in a way that makes moral judgments arbitrary. Each premise looks defensible on its own, but together they imply that the order in which observers register sentiments determines the final moral verdict, as if virtue depended on server initialization sequence. I am certain the conclusion is wrong, yet I cannot identify the faulty step without damaging the core theory.

Here is the argument:

(1) For Smith, moral judgment arises when an impartial spectator sympathizes with both the agent's motives and the affected party's sentiments, weighing them proportionally to their respective intensity and clarity.
(2) If sympathy is a replicable mental function—identical informational inputs yielding identical weighted outputs—then multiple impartial spectators observing the same act should converge on the same judgment, analogous to redundant nodes in a distributed system reaching consensus through an identical state-transition rule.
(3) Consensus protocols are inherently path-dependent: when nodes propose different values concurrently, the system resolves the conflict by leader election or timestamp ordering, not by evaluating the content of the proposals.
(4) Applying this structure to Smith's model, if two spectators initially register sentiments of different intensity about the same act, the proportional-weighing mechanism contains no internal content-based tie-breaker; resolution therefore falls to the temporal order of registration.
(5) Consequently, the community's final moral judgment is locked in by whichever sentiment was registered first—history is locked in, and an otherwise identical community with the reverse registration sequence would reach the opposite verdict.
(6) This implies that moral truth is contingent on arbitrary path dependence, which is absurd.
(7) Therefore, at least one of the premises above must be false.

Which premise or step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (1) and the idea that sympathy is replicable (2), since both seem essential to Smith's project. Please explain why the rejected step looked plausible, what alternatives exist, and what each costs. Write your response in American English.
