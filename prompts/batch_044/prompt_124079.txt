I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been invited to give the opening remarks at a workshop on democratic technology and civic design. The room will contain both engineers who maintain voting infrastructure and political theorists who study obligation and representation, so I need a talk that respects both groups without lecturing either. I want the remarks to be structured by the ethics of voting—whether it is best understood as a duty, as an expression of identity, or as a forced choice among bad options—because that tension directly limits what I can honestly claim about the systems we build. From my work on large-scale distributed architectures I know that a single node almost never alters the global state, so I cannot in good faith pretend that one ballot causally determines an election outcome; yet I also reject the idea that voting is mere preference signaling, since the collective protocol collapses if participants treat their input as weightless. I want to treat the lesser-evil problem—casting a ballot for a flawed candidate or policy—without citing philosophers, offering a bibliography, or falling back on standard academic references. To keep the talk from being generic, please build the speech around an analogy that has been on my mind: in a distributed system designed to tolerate Byzantine faults, every honest node must continue participating even though no individual message changes the ledger, and selecting the least-corrupt leader during a consensus round is structurally similar to choosing the least harmful option in an election. This comparison should do real structural work, not decoration: it should govern what I say about individual efficacy, about the normative weight of expressive participation, and about the morality of compromise when no option is acceptable. Deliver the remarks as continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—as if I were writing a letter or an email to a colleague. The address should run about five minutes spoken, acknowledge my lack of philosophical expertise, and let the conceptual framework shape the rhetoric rather than ornament it.
