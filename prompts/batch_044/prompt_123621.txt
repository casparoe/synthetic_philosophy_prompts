I recently began serving on a regional advisory board for long-term environmental risk, and a dispute about intergenerational obligations has made the nonidentity problem suddenly personal rather than academic. I want you to analyze Finland's 2010 parliamentary decision-in-principle to approve the Onkalo deep geological repository for spent nuclear fuel through the lens of Derek Parfit's nonidentity problem, but with a specific twist: do not treat it as a single reproductive choice like the standard fourteen-year-old case. Instead, treat it as an institutional cascade—the repository is designed to remain hazardous for roughly 100,000 years, and the people who might suffer harm from leakage in, say, year 80,000 are separated from today's decision-makers not only by energy-policy counterfactuals stretching back to the 1970s, but by thousands of generations of governance. Map the decision precisely: who had standing to decide (the 2010 parliament, present operators, or future institutional custodians?), what the counterfactual populations look like under nuclear versus non-nuclear Finnish development paths, and whether the "no harm" defense—that we do not wrong particular future people because they owe their existence to our choice—holds when the risk is geological and involuntary rather than reproductive. Then tell me where the theory breaks: does it explain why Finnish legislators still spoke of obligations to future generations, or does it miss something about institutional responsibility that does not depend on harming identifiable individuals? Finally, I want an honest judgment about whether this case reveals that the nonidentity problem is genuinely about personal identity conditions, or whether it is a red herring that conceals a different moral failure—perhaps one about the quality of lives lived under conditions of inherited risk, regardless of who exactly lives them.
