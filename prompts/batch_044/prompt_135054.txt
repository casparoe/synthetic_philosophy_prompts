So I was chatting with some grad studnets after my seminar on welfare philosophy, and we kept circling a pattern I don't have a clean word for. There's this thing that happens when a benefit is technically needs-based— unemployment, disability, housing aid— and then the state piles on fraud-detection or compliance rules that are way more intense than the actual abuse rate would justify. What's weird isn't just stigmatization; it's an epistemic inversion in the bureaucracy itself. The system stops asking "do you need this?" and starts asking "have you proven you're not cheating?" The proof-requirements get so central that they redefine the moral category of the recipient class— genuinely needy people are treated as presemptive fraudsters until they clear every hurdle— and then policymakers point to the very hurdles, and the occasional fraud they catch, as justification for the whole architecture. It's circular in a specific institutional way. I've seen versions in workfare regimes, in those UK benefit-sanction schedules, and in disability audits that treat claimants like presumptive malingerers. I know it's near "moral hazard" and near the old "undeserving poor" rhetoric, but neither captures the loop I'm describing, where the detection apparatus actually constructs the vice it claims to filter out. Is there an established concept for this? If not, could you coin a term, define it with boundary cases— distinguishing it from plain conditionality, from stigmatization, and from moral hazard— and explain exactly where its edges are? I want the carving, not just a label.
