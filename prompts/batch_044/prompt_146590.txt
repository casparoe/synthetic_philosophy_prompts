For my History of Philosophy seminar on early modern practical ethics, I am drafting an essay on Benjamin Franklin's project of moral perfection as outlined in his Autobiography. Rather than reading his thirteen virtues—Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and Humility—as a conventional Aristotelian virtue-ethics program, I want to argue that Franklin treats moral improvement as a form of proto-behavioral engineering: he frames virtue acquisition as an iterative process of habit tracking, incremental correction, and asymptotic approach, evidenced by his weekly chart and his explicit acknowledgment that he never reached perfection yet considered himself happier for the attempt. I further propose that his ordering of the virtues encodes a hidden causal-dependency theory—specifically, that Temperance must be secured first because it functions as a precondition for the others, while Humility is placed last because it is only stable once the preceding virtues are already entrenched, not because it is least important.

Before I finalize the draft, I would welcome critical comments on two specific issues. First, is it historically defensible to interpret Franklin's method as asymptotic rather than teleological, or does this impose a modern, post-behaviorist framework that obscures the explicitly civic and religious motivations he gives for the project? Second, I draw an analogy between Franklin's paper-based tracking system and contemporary quantified-self practices; does this comparison genuinely illuminate his conceptual structure, or does it risk anachronism by downplaying the moral-theological context in which he embedded the scheme? I am particularly interested in suggestions about where the argument might be misleading, whether the causal-dependency reading is overdetermined, and how I might sharpen the distinction between Franklin's practical strategy and the normative theory that might underpin it.
