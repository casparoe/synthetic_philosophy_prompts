I'm taking a seminar on historical ontology and have become convinced by the view—advanced in recent philosophy of history—that "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution" are not natural kinds discovered in the archive but retrospective chronological containers, narrative devices that impose artificial discontinuity on what was in fact a continuous field of knowledge practices stretching from the high scholastic period through the early modern era. My friends think I'm overreaching, but here's the logical consequence that keeps bothering me: if these three macro-blocks are purely later inventions with no ontological grip on the past, then there cannot be a distinctive, bounded transformation in the norms of empirical justification occurring between, say, 1300 and 1700 either. Without a "Scientific Revolution" functioning as a coherent episode, the institutionalization of peer witnessing, replicability norms, and collective experimental reporting around the 1660s cannot represent a structural epistemic shift—only a localized, incommensurable event that tells us nothing cross-temporally. So rejecting these invented periods seems to force us into a flat historiography in which every local practice is sui generis and no comparison of epistemic regimes across time is legitimate. Is this inference correct? Or can one consistently deny that these macro-periods are real while still maintaining that something genuinely different occurred in how natural philosophers warranted claims, without implicitly smuggling the very categorical boundaries back in through a side door?
