After my sister, a working painter with no philosophy background, admitted that she recently praised a peer's installation mainly because the gallery owner was present and she hoped to secure a future show, she asked me why philosophers say some reasons "count" for an aesthetic judgment while others do not, even when both motivate you. I want to design a single-session teaching plan—delivered over dinner, without texts or citations—to help her understand the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem well enough to audit her own evaluations. I need the conceptual architecture specified: the exact sequence in which to introduce the distinction between reasons that bear on whether an attitude is fitting and reasons that merely cause or incentivize it; why the idea of a "pro-attitude" should come only after she has seen a concrete failure case, not before; the one carrying example drawn from artistic judgment—preferably one that contains both an obvious wrong-kind reason (financial or career gain) and a less obvious one (loyalty to a mentor or institutional pressure)—and what that example must include and exclude so she does not confuse wrong-kind reasons with mere selfishness; the misconceptions she is likely to bring (that honesty about one's motives dissolves the problem, that any explanatory reason is automatically justifying, or that the issue is only about hypocrisy) together with the point in the sequence where each should be preempted; a brief check for understanding that requires her to classify a novel case rather than repeat a slogan; and an explicit boundary of what to leave out—such as parallel problems for belief, metaethical debates about value realism, and institutional-rationality literature—so the session stays focused. Please present this as a plan for explanation, not the explanation itself, and do not include reading lists or references she could not access.
