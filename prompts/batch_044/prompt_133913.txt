I am drafting a novel about a provisional council that assumes authority after the collapse of a long-standing authoritarian regime, and I need to understand the philosophical architecture of the compromises its members will inevitably make. The central figure, a former dissident who now directs internal security, must authorize covert measures against former allies—measures she regards as necessary to prevent restoration but which violate every principle that justified the revolution. To frame her private reckoning, I want to compare the classic dirty-hands problem—the view that a political actor must sometimes violate moral norms she is bound to uphold, leaving a genuine and enduring personal moral stain that cannot be rationalized away—with a framework I will call deferred institutional culpability. Under this latter conception, the wrong is not denied or minimized; rather, its full moral weight is structurally relocated onto a sequence of future institutions—reparative assemblies, constitutional revisions, and public reckonings—so that the actor acknowledges the stain but does not bear it in isolation, distributing responsibility across time rather than concentrating it in a single conscience.

Which of these two conceptions offers a more coherent account of responsibility in transitional governance? Specifically, does deferred institutional culpability preserve the reality of moral wrong, or does it risk dissolving dirty hands into clean administrative procedure by displacing personal guilt onto an imagined future? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of political theory; I want a direct comparison of the conceptual structures, with attention to whether the architecture of deferral can survive the insistence that the actor herself must remain marked by the act, and whether distributing culpability across institutions genuinely honors the victims or merely aestheticizes the violence of transition. Defend your comparison as if explaining it to a reader who understands the moral stakes of governing but has no access to external texts.
