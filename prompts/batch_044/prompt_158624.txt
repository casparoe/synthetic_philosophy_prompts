Compare Aristotle's claim that money is naturally barren — invented to facilitate exchange but incapable of reproducing itself like seeds or livestock, and therefore unsuited to interest-bearing lending — with the institutional logic of modern negative nominal interest rates paired with quantitative easing. Does a monetary regime that deliberately penalizes hoarding and expands supply to force circulation realize Aristotle's normative vision of money serving exchange alone, or does active state manipulation of yield and value constitute precisely the kind of chrematistikē (wealth-getting pursued as an end in itself) that he regarded as unlimited and unnatural? Separate the descriptive issue — whether money's reproductive sterility is essential or institutionally contingent — from the normative issue: whether engineering non-reproductivity requires the artificial intervention that Aristotle associated with unnatural acquisition.
