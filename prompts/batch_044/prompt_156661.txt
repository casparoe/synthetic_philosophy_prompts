Okay so I spent thirty years in a lab measuing stuff that either happened or didn't, and now I'm poking around in philosphy of history and I'm stuck on this. I think Ranke's old idea — showing how it acutally happened — can be defended against Carr and Hayden White's scepticism if we treat historical narratives like competing scientific models: they're underdetermined by the traces (documents, artifacts) but we still pick between them using consilience, predicitive success about new finds, and coherence with background conditions. Carr says every historian picks and arranges; White says the narrative form itself imposes meaning that isn't in the events. I grant both happen, but I say that doesn't kill objectivity any more than theory-ladenness kills science.

What would actually count against this? Not just "historians are biased" — I already conceed that. I want the defeaters sorted: fatal ones where my analogy to science collapses, awkward ones where I need epicycles, and ones I'd shrug off. For instance, if different narrative structures — say tragic versus comic framing — produce genuinely incommensurable but equally consilient accounts of the same period, does that sink me? Or if source criticism shows our traces are so theory-laden there's no independent check? Also, has my view been qualified into unfalsifiability by just saying "the best model wins"? If so, is that a problem?

Tell me what a defender of this position would themselves conceed as counting against them, not just what critics say.
