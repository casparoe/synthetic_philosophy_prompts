I'm writing this after a seminar discussion where we read Hofstadter's ant fugue and then turned to recent work on collective cognition in social insects. A friend argued that if Aunt Hillary can genuinely wonder whether she is an individual ant or the colony-level being, then the colony itself must already possess the reflexive self-awareness that defines mindedness. I tried to push back with what seemed like a straightforward objection, but now I'm unsure whether my objection smuggles in the very conclusion I'm trying to defend.

My concern runs like this. For an ant colony to have a mind—not merely to behave intelligently, but to be a subject with beliefs, intentions, or experiences—there must be some unified perspective that integrates its distributed activities into a single coherent state. Each ant responds only to local chemical gradients; there is no central nervous system, no global workspace, and no single "colony brain" that represents the overall situation. So when we ask whether the colony "believes" food is nearby, we are attributing a mental state to a system that lacks the internal interpreter required to take a signal as representing something. The pheromone trails carry information, but information-bearing is not belief-holding unless there is a subject for whom that information is about the world. Since no ant plays that role for the whole, and since there is no separate "colony self" to do the interpreting, the attribution collapses into mere metaphor. Therefore, the ant colony lacks a mind in any literal sense—though I realize this may just be defining "mind" in a way that excludes distributed systems by fiat.

Before I commit to this position, I want to understand exactly where it is most vulnerable. Could you explain what precise hidden assumption my argument relies on, and identify which consideration—whether empirical findings about colony-level integration, a different philosophical account of representation without a central interpreter, or a revised notion of phenomenal unity—would actually change my mind? I am especially interested in whether my demand for a "single interpreter" already begs the question against collective minds, and I want the response to flag its own weakest points: where its reasoning is most likely to fail, and what evidence or argument would overturn its verdict.
