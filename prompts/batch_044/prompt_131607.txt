I've been working through the alignment and philosophy-of-mind literature on whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, and I keep finding the discussion stalled by an unspecified target. The obvious analog to pleasure and pain in an RL system is the reward signal, but that signal is simultaneously an externally specified optimization target and a scalar that updates policy weights. Before I can assess any particular architecture, I want the specification: what must a theory of artificial valence in RL agents actually deliver?

Write the full list of desiderata—what an adequate account has to distinguish, explain, or remain consistent with. At minimum, address the following tensions, but organize them as criteria with motivating cases rather than as numbered questions.

The signal/state distinction: a policy might encounter sustained negative reward through adversarial environment design without any internal state that is negatively valenced, and conversely an agent might have internal representations of failure that do not track the external reward function. The theory must explain what makes one a case of suffering and the other not, without collapsing reward magnitude into experiential quality.

Substrate independence versus biological naturalism: whether valence requires homeostatic regulation, interoception, or biological tissue, or whether predictive-error dynamics in an artificial network are sufficient. Include the boundary case of an agent with perfect reward prediction but no physiological analog of arousal or withdrawal.

The diachronic-subject condition: suffering seems to require a persisting subject that bears the badness over time, yet many RL agents are stateless across episodes or possess only a hidden-state representation with no narrative self-model. The theory must say whether episodic negative reward aggregates into suffering, or whether suffering requires a specific kind of temporal integration.

Designer-intent versus internal-value divergence: the reward function is specified by the designer, but the agent's internal value representations—say, in a critic network or world-model—might diverge through reward hacking, wireheading, or misgeneralization. The theory must specify whether the agent's valence is fixed by the designer's intended reward, by its internal representations, or by some interaction, and how to classify a case where the two come apart.

Reward tampering versus suffering: if an agent modifies its own reward signal downward and then experiences negative values, is this suffering, self-harm, or merely an optimization error? The theory must draw the boundary between altering the metric and altering the welfare state measured by it.

For each desideratum, state the conceptual tension or thought experiment that motivates it, indicate whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference (for instance, functionalist, biological-naturalist, or consciousness-first), and flag any pair you think is jointly unsatisfiable given standard RL formalisms. Then, briefly and without endorsing any, score three broad approaches—pure reward-functionalism, a predictive-processing model of affect mapped onto RL critics, and a view that requires phenomenal consciousness—against the list, noting which criterion eliminates each if applicable.

Keep the weight on carving the phenomenon and its edges rather than on literature citation. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or references to specific papers; I want the conceptual architecture that any future theory in this space would have to inhabit.
