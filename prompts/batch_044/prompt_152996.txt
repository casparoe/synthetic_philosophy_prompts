The open-borders versus welfare-state compatibility debate has run for decades without appreciable movement: one camp holds that liberal egalitarianism requires free movement regardless of a state's fiscal or integrative capacity, treating integration as an engineering problem that funding and institutional design can eventually solve; the other camp holds that generous redistribution depends on prior social solidarity and reciprocal membership, treating integration not as a policy output but as a relational precondition that must be substantially in place before full obligations of distributive justice apply. Both sides routinely invoke the same comparative cases—high-immigration welfare states, assimilation trajectories across generations, and metrics of social-trust elasticity—yet draw diametrically opposed conclusions, and neither updates in light of new fiscal-impact studies. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a hidden empirical disagreement about contribution timelines or trust decay, but a deeper conflict about whether nationality functions as a morally arbitrary boundary condition or as a cooperative status that generates special obligations, with the shared vocabulary of "integration" and "solidarity" masking incompatible pictures of what makes a welfare state legitimate. Is that right? Specifically, is this a verbal disagreement in which "integration" means assimilation into labor markets for one side and incorporation into a scheme of mutual obligation for the other; a substantive but masked disagreement about which temporal scale counts as data and what standard of evidence applies to claims about institutional sustainability; or a case of two questions being answered under one heading—one about the moral rights of individuals to enter, the other about the conditions for maintaining a particular distributive scheme? Diagnose it using what each side actually says about reciprocity, membership, and the bounds of justice, not by surveying immigration ethics. And given your diagnosis, could anything actually move the debate—a conceptual distinction between resource constraints and solidarity constraints, a formal framing of membership goods as non-excludable but rivalrous, or a reframed empirical question—or should we expect permanent stalemate, and why?
