While drafting a novel about an architect who spends years perfecting a monumental structure commissioned by a hostile regime, I have returned to David Lean's film The Bridge on the River Kwai as a narrative parallel. I want a philosophical reading of Colonel Nicholson's arc—from initial refusal, through obsessive craftsmanship, to the final recognition that his bridge serves the enemy—that treats the sunk-cost fallacy not merely as cognitive error but as a potential expression of moral integrity, collective dignity, or narrative continuity of the self. Does Nicholson persist because he seeks to redeem suffering already expended, or because abandoning the work would erase the meaning his men have invested? Identify specific scenes where the logic of irrecoverable costs genuinely illuminates his choices, and moments where the film resists that logic, suggesting that a project can become constitutive of identity rather than instrumentally valuable. I am particularly interested in whether philosophy offers a defensible distinction between rationally writing off economic losses and honoring costs bound to personal or communal selfhood. Give me a reading faithful to both the film and the concept, and conclude by addressing whether Nicholson's final realization—what he has done—is best interpreted as an economic awakening or as a crisis of who he has become.
