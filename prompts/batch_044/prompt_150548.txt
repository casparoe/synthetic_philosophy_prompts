I'm a retired biologist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, and I'm preparing questions for a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a modified anti-experientialist account of dreaming—she calls it retroactive confabulation with affective tagging. On her view, dream reports do not record occurrent phenomenal states during REM sleep; instead, they are rapid narrative reconstructions assembled during brief micro-arousals at waking, retaining only an affective residue rather than perceptual content. She accepts that REM sleep produces cortical activation patterns resembling wakeful perception, but denies that an integrated experiencing subject is present during sleep; the subject is reconstructed only upon awakening. I want a focused sequence of questions that avoid biography and press her position directly. First, ask how her retroactive-assembly model differs from Dennett's false-memory account of dreaming—specifically, whether her view requires an active semantic construction mechanism at awakening rather than a stored trace, anticipating her reply that no stable memory trace survives the transition, and include a follow-up asking what empirical neural signature could distinguish active assembly from trace retrieval. Second, ask about lucid dreamers who claim real-time metacognitive awareness—anticipating her argument that these reports actually describe misremembered micro-arousals, then follow up on whether graded or partial lucidity forces her into an ad hoc taxonomy of arousal levels. Third, probe her criterion for integrated subject presence, noting she rejects Malcolm's purely linguistic or behavioral criterion in favor of a neurodynamic global-workspace integration threshold—anticipating her defense that this threshold is empirically measurable, and ask whether fixing that boundary risks rendering her view stipulative rather than genuinely falsifiable. Fourth, ask whether her position entails that a fully anesthetized patient who reports a dream upon emergence never actually experienced anything, and what ethical implication this holds for clinical assessment of intraoperative awareness. Finally, ask what specific neuropsychological finding—if observed—would compel her to concede that at least some dreams involve genuine occurrent experience rather than reconstructed pseudo-experience.
