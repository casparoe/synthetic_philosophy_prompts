My philosophy professor has been running a seminar on the ethics of bringing beings into existence, and I have recorded her verdicts on four scenarios without knowing the underlying principle. She holds that (1) conceiving a child whose life will contain substantial suffering is wrong; (2) failing to conceive a child who would have lived a blissful, suffering-free life is not a moral failure; (3) a couple’s decision not to have a child whose life would be net-positive but include unavoidable minor pains is permissible, though she adds that actually having that child would have been wrong; (4) creating and then painlessly dissolving a conscious being that experiences neither pleasure nor pain is not wrong, if pointless.

Next week she must evaluate a proposal to cultivate an isolated neural organoid for six months: it is engineered to experience sustained intense aesthetic pleasure, guaranteed to suffer nothing except an unavoidable brief cold sensation at dissolution, and then destroyed without awareness. Predict her verdict on whether this creation is morally wrong. Lay out the two main rules consistent with her first four judgments—one treating harm and benefit asymmetrically so that benefits never justify even minimal harm, the other judging creation by net welfare for the being that comes to exist—say what each predicts for the organoid, name the further hypothetical case that would best separate the rules, and give your best guess with reasons.
