I've reconstructed an argument about historical periodization that seems to show that anyone who accepts the Scientific Revolution is logically forced to accept both the Middle Ages and the Renaissance as real structures, with the further consequence that selective rejection is impossible. Here is the chain.

(1) Historical period labels—such as 'Middle Ages', 'Renaissance', and 'Scientific Revolution'—function in serious historiography either as names for real historical structures or as purely conventional groupings with no ontological weight.

(2) If they are purely conventional, then there is no objective constraint on assigning an event to one period rather than another; assignment is a matter of scholarly stipulation alone.

(3) Yet there are clear objective constraints: for instance, the publication of Newton's Principia belongs to the Scientific Revolution and not to the Renaissance, not because scholars have voted but because the work instantiates a specific mathematized cosmological method that defines the Scientific Revolution and is absent from the Renaissance's characteristic revival of classical textual practices.

(4) Therefore, these labels must denote real historical structures.

(5) Moreover, these structures are relationally interdefined: the Scientific Revolution is partly constituted by the overthrow of an Aristotelian-Scholastic worldview characteristic of the Middle Ages; the Renaissance is partly constituted by the transitional recovery of classical sources that makes that overthrow possible.

(6) Consequently, the reality of any one of these periods entails the reality of the others: one cannot have the overthrow of a worldview (the Scientific Revolution) without the worldview being real (the Middle Ages), nor a transition between two states without both endpoints being real (the Renaissance).

(7) So any historian who accepts the Scientific Revolution as a real structure is logically committed to accepting both the Middle Ages and the Renaissance as real structures.

(8) But prominent contemporary historians reject the Middle Ages as a Eurocentric fiction and the Renaissance as a Burckhardtian myth, while continuing to treat the Scientific Revolution as a genuine historical transformation.

(9) Therefore, these historians are engaged in a logical contradiction: they affirm the reality of a structure whose existence necessarily depends on structures they deny.

(10) The only consistent positions are either to accept all three as real or to reject all three—including the Scientific Revolution—as conventional inventions.

(11) Yet rejecting the Scientific Revolution is intellectually untenable for anyone who accepts the explanatory success of modern natural science, which is precisely the achievement that the period label tracks.

(12) Hence, anyone who wants to deny the Middle Ages or the Renaissance while preserving modern science must be making an error in reasoning.

I want to preserve the idea that a historian can accept the Scientific Revolution while selectively denying the Middle Ages or the Renaissance without contradiction—so I am reluctant to give up (7) or (8)—but I need to know what I am paying if I do.
