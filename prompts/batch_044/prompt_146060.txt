I'm drafting an essay that tries to push Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique one step further—not merely as a methodological warning against adaptationist storytelling, but as an argument about the ontology of biological traits. Here are the notes I intend to develop.

First, the standard framing: adaptationism treats organisms as bundles of separately selectable traits, each carrying its own adaptive history. In their 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm: A Critique of the Adaptationist Programme," Gould and Lewontin challenge this by pointing to spandrels—features like the triangular masonry spaces between arches that exist not because they were selected for, but because they are structurally necessitated by other design choices.

Second, the hidden ontological assumption. For the spandrel/adaptation distinction to do real explanatory work, spandrels must be individuated as traits in their own right. But if a spandrel is genuinely a by-product with no independent selective history, in what sense is it a separate trait rather than a modal feature of the primary architecture? I want to argue that spandrels are ontologically dependent entities—analogous to holes, shadows, or the concavity of a cup—existing only in virtue of the primary structure that generates them. If this is right, then asking "what is the adaptive function of this spandrel?" is not merely empirically wrong but conceptually malformed, like asking what job the hole in the donut was hired to do. This strengthens the anti-adaptationist critique by showing that the adaptationist program implicitly presupposes a modular ontology of traits that may not hold.

Third, the tension with exaptation. Gould and Vrba introduced the term in 1982 to cover cases where a spandrel or other non-adaptive feature is later co-opted for a new function. But if spandrels are ontologically dependent, co-optation seems puzzling: how can something that is not an independent kind be recruited for a new role? My tentative resolution is that exaptation requires a kind of "functional emancipation"—the dependent feature must first achieve selective independence before it can be genuinely co-opted—so that the spandrel/exaptation boundary is not just historical but ontological.

I'd like your comments on this outline, particularly on the following points:

- Does the analogy between spandrels and ontologically dependent entities (holes, shadows) hold up biologically, or does it misrepresent how evolutionary biologists actually individuate traits?
- Is my claim that adaptationism presupposes a modular ontology of traits fair, or can a sophisticated adaptationist accept deep structural interdependence without abandoning the framework?
- Does the idea of "functional emancipation" help resolve the tension with exaptation, or does it introduce an ad hoc distinction that saves the view only by renaming the problem?
- Are there obvious inconsistencies between the second and fourth points—especially regarding whether spandrels can ever become independent kinds—that I haven't noticed?

I'm not looking for reading lists, citations, or bibliographic suggestions; just conceptual feedback from someone familiar enough with the philosophy of biology literature to spot where I'm reinventing a wheel, smuggling in an equivocation, or pushing the argument past where it can stand.
