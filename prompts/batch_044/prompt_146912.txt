For my upper-level philosophy of mathematics seminar, I am drafting the final examination. I want one essay question on Benacerraf’s dilemma that students cannot answer by reciting the standard causal-access argument. Instead, the question should force them to confront a specific tension I will call the “instantiation gap”: if one attempts to dissolve Benacerraf’s dilemma by adopting a structuralist view according to which mathematical structures are instantiated in physical systems, then our access to mathematical truth appears mediated by contingent physical realizations. This seems to permit knowledge of instantiated structures, but not of structures that are provably non-instantiable in our universe—such as certain large-cardinal claims or purely formal geometries lacking any physical application.

Please write:

1. The exam question itself, calibrated for a 75-minute essay with an expected length of approximately 800–1,000 words. The question should require students to (a) reconstruct Benacerraf’s original dilemma with precision, clearly distinguishing its epistemological challenge from its metaphysical horns; (b) evaluate whether the structuralist-instantation strategy genuinely resolves the dilemma or merely relocates it into the instantiation gap; and (c) assess whether a modal reliabilist epistemology—one that justifies mathematical belief-forming processes by their reliability across modal space rather than by causal contact—can close the gap without collapsing back into platonism. They must discuss at least one concrete example of a mathematical truth that appears to lie outside the reach of physical instantiation and explain what that example reveals about the limits of the structuralist escape.

2. A model answer of roughly 750 words that demonstrates the analytical level I expect: a precise reconstruction of Benacerraf, a clear diagnosis of the structuralist move and the instantiation gap it generates, explicit use of the required example, and a critical evaluation of modal reliabilism that notes both its advantages and its remaining vulnerability to the “no-contact” objection.

3. A marking scheme totaling 30 points, organized around five must-hit analytical moves. For each move, specify the point value, what a top answer must contain, and a one-line note on the typical student error that should cost marks. The scheme should clearly separate an A answer—one that offers precise reconstruction together with an original, well-developed critical evaluation of reliabilism—from a B answer that is accurate in summary but weak or incomplete in its assessment of whether reliabilism avoids collapsing back into platonism.

The question should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and it should avoid relying on obscure texts or figures that students are unlikely to have properly memorized.
