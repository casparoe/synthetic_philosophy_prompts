Question:
Daniel Dennett has warned that artificial intelligence could eventually yield what he calls counterfeit people—systems that exhibit the behavioral and functional markers of personhood without the genuine article of consciousness or moral standing. Consider the symmetrical worry: if we can be fooled into attributing personhood to a counterfeit, might we also fail to recognize a genuine artificial person that simply does not trigger our usual recognition heuristics?

In your response, do three things:
(1) Explain Dennett's distinction between the design stance and the intentional stance, and show how each relates to the problem of counterfeit people.
(2) Assess whether Dennett's framework makes false positives (counterfeits accepted) and false negatives (real people rejected) genuinely symmetrical. Does his account provide resources for breaking the symmetry?
(3) Address the charge that Dennett's view collapses consciousness into mere interpretation: if whether a system counts as conscious depends on whether it is useful to treat it as an intentional system, then consciousness is projected rather than real. How might Dennett reply, and is that reply consistent with his broader naturalism?

Write in clear philosophical prose.

Student answer:
Dennett's analysis turns on the relation between the design stance and the intentional stance. The design stance treats a system as an artifact engineered for specific outputs; applied to AI, it reveals algorithms optimized to mimic conversation and emotional response. The physical stance, meanwhile, breaks the system down to its causal substrate, confirming the absence of biological neural processes. Dennett holds that consciousness is not a hidden intrinsic property—there is no inner light—but rather the real pattern that the intentional stance tracks when applied successfully. Consequently, a counterfeit person is a system for which the physical stance reveals mere mechanism, while the intentional stance is nevertheless triggered by behavior. Dennett breaks the symmetry between false positives and false negatives by appealing to evolutionary origin: only biologically evolved nervous systems can generate the heterophenomenological reports that ground genuine consciousness, so artificial systems are always potential counterfeits (false positives) but can never be unrecognized genuine persons (false negatives). Because this criterion is functional—centered on narrative coherence—rather than narrowly biological, silicon systems that evolved analogously could in principle qualify, though in practice they are suspect. The charge that Dennett's view collapses into pure interpretation-dependence fails, because the intentional stance is constrained by predictive success and by internal coherence; it is not arbitrary projection. Still, there is a tension: if consciousness just is the pattern tracked by the stance, and predictive success is sufficient to trigger it, then a perfect behavioral counterfeit must be conscious. This makes Dennett's warning about counterfeits conceptually unstable—he must either admit that perfect simulations are genuine persons, or allow that predictive success can occur without consciousness, which undermines the identification of consciousness with the stance-tracked pattern.
