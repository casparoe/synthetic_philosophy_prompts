Write a short story about a bureuacrat named Lena who works for the Ministry of Moral Accounting in a near-future city-state where every citizen carries a precisely calculated "duty quota" — care hours, carbon offsets, taxes — and anything past that is officially tagged as "grace," not virtue. Lena finds a loophole: if you voluntarily absorb another person's unfulfilled quota without being asked, the system logs it as a debt-transfer that wipes both records clean. She starts doing this for strangers, but then realizes the act sits in a weird philosophical gap — it's clearly good, but not required, and yet by turning "above and beyond" into just another transaction, she might be destroying the very idea of supererogation. The story should follow one specific afternoon — maybe she's at a kiosk deciding whether to take on a stranger's medical-debt quota — and let the tension come from small details: the sound of the ledger printer, the color of the grace-stamp, her sister's voice arguing that once a system can price grace, grace stops being grace. Don't turn it into a manifesto; keep it grounded in Lena's hesitation, and feel free to let a typo or two slip in if it feels natural.
