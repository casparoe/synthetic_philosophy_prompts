I'm working through the foundations of logic for a seminar and have tried to extend Russell's reasoning from pure set theory into philosophical methodology. The argument below now strikes me as suspicious—its conclusion would seem to make philosophy impossible—but I cannot locate the precise failure point. Here are the steps: (1) Call a system "reflexive" if its formal language can represent its own internal categories—truth, belief, reference, property-hood—within itself. (2) Any philosophical theory adequate to its task must be reflexive, since it needs to defend its own methods and account for the very concepts it deploys. (3) Russell's paradox arises whenever a reflexive system permits unrestricted comprehension: the formation of a collection containing exactly those entities in the domain that fail to bear some specified self-relation. (4) Therefore, in any reflexive philosophical theory, we can construct the "Russell concept"—the concept comprising all concepts in that theory that do not apply to themselves. (5) Applying this Russell concept to itself yields a direct contradiction: if it applies to itself, then by its defining condition it does not; if it does not apply to itself, then it satisfies the condition and therefore does apply. (6) Hence every adequate philosophical theory is formally inconsistent. (7) From inconsistency, every proposition is derivable, which means no reflexive theory can genuinely discriminate correct claims from incorrect ones. (8) Yet we plainly treat theories in epistemology, ethics, and metaphysics as capable of such discrimination. (9) So either philosophical theories fail to be reflexive—contradicting (2)—or unrestricted comprehension is somehow illegitimately blocked in conceptual domains—contradicting (3). I am most reluctant to abandon (1) and (2), since giving them up seems to reduce philosophy to an enterprise that cannot examine its own foundations. Where does the argument actually break down: is there a hidden equivocation between sets and concepts, a scope error in applying the paradox across levels, or is the argument valid and the real cost lies in giving up one of the premises I prefer to keep?
