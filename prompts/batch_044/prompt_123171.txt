In debates over whether the Middle Ages, Renaissance, and Scientific Revolution are real historical joints or later editorial inventions, critics often deploy an analogy: periodization is like applying the familiar five-stage grief model—denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance—to a continuous process. Both frameworks, they say, impose a tidy sequential narrative on heterogeneous, non-sequential material in order to serve pedagogical or therapeutic needs; therefore these three eras are narrative conveniences, not discovered structures. Audit this analogy: lay out the correspondence table (what plays the role of the clinician, the patient, the actual emotional trajectory, the institutional need for the framework, etc., and what in history corresponds to each); separate the disanalogies that undermine the anti-realist conclusion from those that don't; identify what normative or psychological assumptions the grief model smuggles into the historical case; and say whether the claim that these periods are largely invented survives once the analogy's work is made explicit, or whether the conclusion collapses without it.
