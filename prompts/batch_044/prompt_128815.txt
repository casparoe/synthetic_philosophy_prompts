As a journalist covering science and ideas, I'm writing a feature on the conceptual frontier where metaethics meets institutional design and artificial cognition. The subject is free-floating rationales—the view that normative reasons can exist and carry authority independently of any particular agent who holds, recognizes, or is moved by them. Rather than another survey of the standard literature, I want a strategic assessment: if you were advising a small interdisciplinary group—say, two philosophers of normativity, one legal theorist, and one researcher working on the conceptual architecture of decision systems—with roughly two years of focused effort, which open questions should they tackle first, in what sequence, and why?

Please prioritize among the following lines of inquiry (feel free to replace one if you judge another more urgent, but defend any substitution):

1. The distributed recognition problem: can a rationale remain genuinely free-floating if its practical force depends on a recognition structure—some minimal capacity to register normative weight—that is not housed in a single mind but spread across an institution, a legal code, or an engineered system? Does distributing the recognizer undermine the claim that reasons exist without reasoners, or extend it?

2. Normative silence and particular authority: if reasons do not depend on agents, what explains why a specific agent in a specific context is bound by them? Is there a plausible account of directed obligation that does not smuggle a reasoner back in as the source of the binding?

3. The collapse of agent-relativity: once reasons are fully detached from reasoners, does the distinction between agent-neutral and agent-relative reasons lose its grounding? And if so, does that simplify moral theory or dissolve useful distinctions in legal and political obligation?

4. Non-representational governance: can an artificial decision architecture be guided by normative targets—optimization criteria that correspond to genuine reasons—without possessing any representational state of having a reason? Would such a system be an instance of free-floating rationales in operation, or proof that rationales necessarily require some form of bearer?

Your answer should not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; a model without access to a library should be able to follow the argument on its own terms. Instead, deliver a defended agenda of no more than 700 words. Rank the questions (or your revised set) with explicit criteria—conceptual tractability, dependency relations (which answers unlock which others), relevance beyond academic philosophy, and neglect—explain the trade-offs that make one upstream of another, identify which lines are currently overrated given available conceptual tools, and specify what kind of intermediate result over the next two years would cause you to reorder the priorities. I am interested in where limited intellectual labor should actually go, not in a literature review.
