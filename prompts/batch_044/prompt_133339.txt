Draft the joint statement that a committed Sartrean existentialist of the 'radical freedom' school and a committed phenomenological critic of the 'situated constraint' school could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith.

The 'radical freedom' party holds that Sartrean freedom is ontologically absolute and non-derivative, that facticity is never an external constraint but only the material that freedom transcends, that bad faith is always a pre-reflective and culpable choice to flee one's own transcendence, and that social or psychological conditions can never cause unfreedom — they can only provide the occasion for a free choice of denial.

The 'situated constraint' party holds that freedom is always already mediated by embodied facticity, historical trauma, and social structures that operate as genuine limits; that 'radical' freedom is a metaphysical abstraction that obscures real asymmetries of power; that bad faith is often a non-culpable, structurally induced adaptation rather than a universal moral failure; and that treating all denial of agency as culpable self-deception ignores the ways in which oppressive conditions can systematically suppress the very awareness required for such a choice.

Produce the document in three parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims about Sartre's ontology and ethics that both parties can sign. Include agreements about the formal distinction between being-in-itself and being-for-itself, the structure of the pre-reflective cogito, the definition of bad faith as self-deception in which the deceiver and the deceived are one, and the claim that freedom and facticity are inseparable yet irreducible to one another. You may also agree on a specific conceptual framing introduced for this collaboration: that facticity should be analysed not as static givenness but as an active 'resistance' — and that the core dispute concerns whether this resistance operates internally to freedom or as an external boundary upon it.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should present: (i) the precise disagreement in wording that each side accepts as accurate for its own view; (ii) the neutral formulation both sides agree describes the dispute; and (iii) the crux that resolving it would address. Cover at least: whether freedom can ever be genuinely constrained or only appears so; whether the pre-reflective consciousness possesses the resources to choose its own denial in all circumstances; whether moral culpability attaches to every instance of bad faith; and whether historical or social structures can be causal determinants of unfreedom.

Third, for each crux, specify a concrete case or thought experiment both sides accept as bearing upon it — for example, a worker in an authoritarian system who asserts that he has no choice about complicity, or a survivor of prolonged trauma who denies personal agency — together with the argument or observation both agree is relevant. Then record each side's explicit prediction about what careful analysis of that case should reveal.
