I keep returning to money, since everyone agrees a dollar bill is just paper and ink, yet we treat it as having real value that survives the destruction of any particular bill. Still, I'm puzzled by an apparent inconsistency in how institutions actually manage different forms of it, and I want to know what hidden theory of value makes the inconsistency look acceptable.

Consider the cluster of practices that govern physical cash, digital balances, and proposed central-bank digital currencies. In many jurisdictions, a torn banknote can be redeemed at a central bank if more than half remains, suggesting the value belongs to the token-type rather than to that specific piece of paper; yet a bent or defaced coin is typically withdrawn and melted without compensation, as if its material form were essential. Meanwhile, when a bank corrects a duplicated digital balance, it restores the amount precisely to the cent, treating digital money as pure information that must be preserved exactly, even though physical cash remains an anonymous bearer instrument whose loss is never tracked. Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and kind-identity that makes these asymmetries coherent, distinguish what the practices strictly require from what merely fits them well, and say whether the introduction of programmable digital currency forces a revision in how we understand the social construction of monetary kinds.
