Read Akira Kurosawa’s Rashomon through the lens of Jain anekāntavāda, specifically the epistemological framework of syādvāda and the distinction between individual nayas (partial standpoints) and the non-absolutist claim that reality is many-sided (anekānta). The film presents four incompatible accounts of the same violent encounter in a forest, with no authoritative narrator to reconcile them. Does this narrative structure genuinely illustrate anekāntavāda—where each testimony captures a partial, conditionally valid truth that must be held alongside others—or does it instead slide into skeptical relativism, in which truth is merely fragmented and unrecoverable? I want a reading that takes both the film’s architecture and Jain epistemology seriously: identify which scenes or testimonies correspond to specific nayas (for instance, the standpoint of direct sensory observation versus that of moral self-exculpation), discuss whether the deliberate absence of synthesis is philosophically faithful to the Jain view that no single perspective exhausts reality, or whether anekāntavāda requires a meta-perspective of integration that Rashomon withholds. Where does the film illuminate the practical difficulty of maintaining many-sided truth in moral judgment, and where does it risk distorting Jain doctrine by equating partial truth with self-interested fabrication? Conclude by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about perspectivism and truth that a purely conceptual argument about syādvāda does not capture.
