I am a novelist drafting a historical scene set in the intellectual salon at Cirey during the 1730s, and I need help understanding a particularly dense passage from Émilie du Châtelet's manuscript treatise on happiness, composed in the middle decades of the eighteenth century and circulated among her friends before its posthumous publication. In the passage, she distinguishes between contentment derived from physical satisfaction and a more durable state that arises from continuous mental exertion, which she describes in terms that recall the equilibrium of opposing forces in mechanics. Yet she immediately introduces a strange condition: this mental equilibrium, she claims, is preserved only so long as the mind does not achieve full clarity about its own limitations. She suggests that a kind of necessary obscurity must remain in place, functioning not as deception for social advantage but as a structural requirement—much like a concealed variable that prevents a system from resolving into stasis or collapse. I am unsure how to read this. Is she arguing that happiness requires a deliberately cultivated illusion about one's intellectual capacities? Or is she suggesting that consciousness itself operates through partial opacity, and that stripping away this veil produces a form of melancholy analogous to mechanical instability? Furthermore, I wonder whether her appeal to physical equilibrium is meant as a rigorous analogy drawn from her scientific work, or merely as a rhetorical ornament. Could you help me parse the argument of this passage on its own philosophical terms, without invoking modern psychology or relying on the editorial apparatus of later editions?
