I run a logic workshop for phil grad students and every semester they write proofs that spiral into Carroll's tortoise problem — they justify modus ponens with some meta-rule, then ask how we justify that, and suddenly we're in inifinite regress. I want a grading procedre my TAs can actually run on a proof: a decision flow that treats justification like a ledger with a small "base budgett" of primitive inferences (direct substitution, identity, and modus ponens taken as primitive) plus "collateral" deposited in whatever prior texts the workshop agreed on. For each inference step in a student proof, the TA checks in order: is the rule in the base budget? If yes, stop and mark it paid. If no, is there valid collateral already on deposit? If yes, note the source and move on. If no, flag it as running up unjustified deductive debt — then the TA has to decide whether to accept it on credit, reject the step, or demand a reformulation using only primitives. Make clear that what's in the deposit box is partly a value call made at the start of term, not something the procedure discovers.

Then apply it to three student proofs: (1) a clean syllogism that never leaves the base budget; (2) a proof that tries to justify modus ponens itself by appealing to normalization in natural deduction — this should hit the regress loop and the procedure should come back with an answer I'll hate, either that the collateral is circular or that we have to grade the extra layer as running on credit; and (3) a sloppy proof where someone derives p from p∧q without naming conjunction elimination, so the TA has to decide if that's hidden deductive debt or just lazy notation.

Be explicit about where judgment can't be eliminated — like setting the initial budget, deciding what counts as deposited collateral, and whether to tolerate credit — and say what the procedure flat-out can't do, like settle whether modus ponens really is primitive or just force you to declare your stance upfront. Keep it practical, not a philosophy essay.
