Exam question: Compare Charles Sanders Peirce’s and William James’s pragmatist accounts of truth. Explain how each thinker relates truth to practical consequences or effects, identify one significant divergence in their frameworks, and say whether either view makes truth depend on what any particular individual finds useful.

Student answer:

Peirce and James are both pragmatists, but they understand the connection between truth and practice differently. For Peirce, the meaning of a concept is given by the practical effects it produces, according to his pragmatic maxim. He extends this to truth by defining it as the opinion that is fated to be agreed upon by all who investigate in the indefinite long run. In other words, truth is essentially a coherence property: it is whatever survives collective scientific scrutiny forever. Peirce thinks this agreement is not accidental; it happens because reality constrains inquiry from the outside. Still, the content of truth is fixed by the ideal endpoint of consensus rather than by direct correspondence with a mind-independent world.

James, on the other hand, argues that truth is made rather than discovered. A belief is true if it works—if it successfully guides action and produces satisfying results in experience. For James, there is no single, fully determinate reality that waits to be copied by true beliefs; instead, reality is partly shaped by human interests and selective attention. This means that different people or communities can hold genuinely different true beliefs, provided each belief works well within its own practical context. The key divergence is therefore that Peirce anchors truth in an objective, future-oriented convergence, while James allows truth to be plural and immediately tied to present verification. Both reject the idea that truth is a static correspondence to reality, but James goes further in denying that reality fixes truth independently of human practice.
