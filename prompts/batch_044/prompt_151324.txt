I was covering the annual symposium on applied normative ethics in The Hague last month, and during a coffee break I overheard a sustained argument between Dr. Amara Osei, who works on moral psychology and the structure of long-term commitment, and Dr. Leo Brennan, a political philosopher known for his work on distributive obligations. They were debating a real-world case that has become something of a touchstone in their field: a surgeon who spent twenty-five years running an emergency clinic in an active conflict zone, routinely working eighty-hour weeks with no contractual requirement to remain after her initial two-year commitment expired. The dispute was not about whether she was admirable—both agreed she was—but about whether her actions in, say, year twenty were still genuinely supererogatory, or whether sustained voluntary commitment eventually transforms every subsequent act into a plain moral obligation. They asked me, as a neutral observer, to referee.

Osei opened by proposing what she called moral amortization: the idea that supererogatory acts generate a kind of depreciating moral capital that, if sustained long enough, crystallizes into role-based obligations. She argued that the surgeon's first years were clearly beyond the call of duty, but that by remaining she had made herself structurally indispensable; the patients who now depend on her presence generate present obligations regardless of how she initially arrived in that position. Brennan countered with what he termed an index of comparative sacrifice, insisting that supererogation must be indexed to each discrete act relative to a baseline of reasonable expectation, not to the biography of the agent. He said that treating the surgeon's continued presence as obligatory commits a retrospective fallacy: it confuses the harmful consequences of leaving with a duty not to leave. He added that if sustained virtue always hardens into duty, then supererogation collapses into a mere developmental stage, which would perversely incentivize people never to begin heroic projects.

Osei replied that Brennan was ignoring the normative force of role-occupancy: once you voluntarily occupy a position of unique dependency, the obligations flow to those currently vulnerable, not backward to your original choice. Brennan answered that this collapses the distinction between causing a dependency and having a duty to maintain it; he compared it to arguing that a parent who chooses to have a child is thereafter bound by obligations that erase any supererogatory dimension to continued parenting. Osei said the analogy fails because parenting is already institutionally obligatory, whereas this surgeon had invented her own extreme role, making her sustained presence an ongoing architectural achievement rather than a granular series of gifts. Brennan concluded that this makes moral heroism architectural rather than act-based, which he considers a theoretical cost too high: we lose the ability to say that each additional hour she works, knowing she could board a flight home, is a fresh supererogatory act.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each major move—Osei's amortization thesis, Brennan's index and retrospective-fallacy charge, the role-occupancy reply, the parenting analogy, and the architectural-versus-granular conclusion—indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, largely correct, or somewhere in between, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall and identify what both are missing, particularly regarding whether there is a meaningful distinction between obligations generated by past commitments and obligations generated by present vulnerability. Finally, please include an explicit statement of what would change your mind: which specific argument or empirical finding—say, about how actual long-term aid workers describe their own motivations—would lead you to a different overall verdict, and where in your reasoning you think failure is most likely.
