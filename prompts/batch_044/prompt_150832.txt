I'm preparing a workshop on Enlightenment political aesthetics and need a sustained piece of conceptual satire that treats Rousseau's critique of theatrical spectacle not as a historical curiosity but as a living administrative logic. Write a deadpan, fully formal grant proposal—approximately 650 words—submitted to a fictional "Ministry of Civic Amusement" by an invented organization, the Geneva Society for the Abolition of Passive Spectatorship. The proposal should request public funding not to stage a production but to permanently decommission a mid-sized repertory theater and replace its entire season with a series of unstructured, participatory civic festivals modeled on the public celebrations Rousseau defended in his Letter to d'Alembert.

The voice must remain that of a sincere, bureaucratically fluent advocate who never breaks character: every paragraph should exaggerate a genuine feature of Rousseau's argument—the spectator's moral corruption through identification with artificial characters, the actor's professional cultivation of falsehood, the substitution of private emotional catharsis for public virtue, and the structural superiority of active collective festivity over scripted representation—without ever naming Rousseau or using direct quotation. To keep the satire legible rather than merely absurd, have the applicant address contemporary arts-policy concerns (audience diversification, accessibility metrics, community engagement, representational casting) and show, with deadpan precision, how Rousseau's framework resolves each by rejecting the premises that make them problems. For instance, accessibility should be solved not by subsidizing cheaper tickets but by eliminating the architectural separation between performer and citizen; diversity should be achieved not through inclusive casting but through the dissolution of represented roles altogether, since every role is a lie.

Include a detailed budget section—complete with line items for "demolition of the proscenium arch," "de-professionalization stipends for former actors transitioning to civic participation," and "procurement of collective dance surfaces"—that reveals the internal logic of the proposal: each expenditure should follow from a specific premise in the critique. The proposal should close with a risk-assessment paragraph that treats likely objections (performer unemployment, loss of cultural heritage, audience boredom, diminished tourist revenue) as confirmations of the very passivity and dependency on artificial stimulation that the project aims to cure. The target of the satire is not Rousseau himself but the modern habit of treating aesthetic reform as a matter of tweaking representation rather than questioning the spectator's structural position; the humor should come from the fact that, within the proposal's own terms, every liberal objection sounds like symptomatic evidence of the disease the grant is designed to treat.
