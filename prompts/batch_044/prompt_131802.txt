Consider the widespread institutional and rhetorical practice, visible in democratic legislatures and formal ethics boards, of treating a critic's own analogous misconduct as disqualifying them from blaming or adjudicating others for the same offense. In parliamentary debate, an accusation of corruption is routinely met not with a defense on the merits but with a charge of hypocrisy; in university and corporate ethics committees, members often recuse themselves not merely for financial conflicts but for having engaged in behavior resembling the violation under review, on the implicit premise that they have forfeited moral standing. Meanwhile, criminal and civil procedure insist that a prosecutor's or plaintiff's personal record is irrelevant to the validity of the charge—the law claims to be blind to the messenger's sins. What philosophical theory of blame and standing does the first practice presuppose? Must its defenders hold that hypocrisy vitiates blame itself, or can they retreat to a purely pragmatic worry about credibility and authority? Is the practice consistent with the legal system's official rejection of "unclean hands" among adjudicators, or does it smuggle in a coherence condition—that blame is legitimate only when the blamer is free of the same fault—which jurisprudence explicitly denies? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say whether a society that kept legal impartiality but abolished the hypocrisy objection in politics and ethics review would be conceptually incoherent or only politically different.
