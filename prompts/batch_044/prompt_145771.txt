I've noticed a recurring pattern in my own use of large language models for philosophical reasoning that I can't name. When I ask for a complex argument—say, about modal realism or the ethics of deception—the model often returns a structured chain: premise A, then B, then C, with brief justifications for each link. I can read each step, see that it follows, and even explain it back in isolation. Yet a day later I find I cannot reconstruct the full sequence without the model, and I certainly can't adapt the reasoning to a slightly different case. The understanding feels distributed: present at every node, absent in the architecture.

This is not quite the illusion of explanatory depth, since I genuinely grasp each component; nor is it ordinary reliance on an authority, since I'm not deferring to reputation but actively processing each link. Is there an established concept—perhaps in epistemology of testimony, cognitive science, or philosophy of technology—for this specific failure mode, where comprehension of discrete steps coexists with an inability to integrate them into an independent mental model? If not, how should it be carved: what distinguishes it from standard epistemic dependence, from the Dunning-Kruger effect, and from simple forgetfulness? I'd want the boundaries clear—when it applies, when it doesn't, and whether it requires the intermediate representations that AI provides, or could occur with any complex external reasoning aid.
