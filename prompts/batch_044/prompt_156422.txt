I have noticed a recurring pattern in the translation of philosophical texts under severe formal constraint—say, rendering a treatise on substance and accident into rhymed alexandrines or into a fixed syllabic metre. To preserve the rhyme scheme, a translator might replace an abstract term such as substance with a concrete near-rhyme like presence, or turn a passive construction into an active one so that the line scans. Each substitution is locally justifiable: the metre holds, the rhyme sounds, and the sentence remains grammatically sound. Yet over the course of a chapter these substitutions accumulate into a different metaphysical picture: the world becomes populated by present agents rather than enduring substrates, and causal relations are replaced by intentional ones. The finished work is formally impeccable—readers praise its elegance—but it quietly advances a philosophical position that the original author explicitly rejected. I encounter this not only in historical examples linked to Marot's practice, where beauty often outweighs literal fidelity, but also in modern verse translations of philosophical poetry, where the constraint is structural rather than ornamental. Is there an established term for this specific kind of cumulative, constraint-induced conceptual displacement? How does it differ from the general idea of belles infidèles, from domestication, and from simple mistranslation? If no precise term exists, I want a definition that captures its essential features: the preservation of form, the local reasonableness of each change, the global divergence in conceptual architecture, and the possibility that the result is internally coherent yet philosophically alien to the source. I also want to know whether the phenomenon requires a philosophical source text, or whether it can occur whenever formal rules force systematic lexical substitution across a long work.
