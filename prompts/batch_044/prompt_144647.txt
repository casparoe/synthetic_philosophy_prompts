I'm writing with a question that has been nagging me since following discussions of mathematical epistemology on LessWrong and related forums. Consider the following line of reasoning, which captures Frege's argument for the objectivity of arithmetic without appealing to psychological intuition: numbers are not mental constructions, since two thinkers can disagree about a sum only if there is an objective fact of the matter they both attempt to grasp; yet they are not physical objects, since no spatial or causal interaction with a number is possible. This leaves arithmetic objects fully determinate and mind-independent, but causally isolated from the physical processes by which minds ordinarily come to know things.

My difficulty concerns Benacerraf's dilemma applied in an unexpected direction. The dilemma seems to turn on exactly this isolation: if mathematical objects are abstract and causally inert, then beliefs about them cannot be causally connected to the facts that make them true, undermining mathematical knowledge on standard causal theories. But I want to extend this constraint beyond human cognition to any information-processing system—say, a large language model trained only on statistical patterns in text. Such a system never physically interacts with abstract objects; its outputs about arithmetic derive entirely from syntactic correlations in its training data. Does Benacerraf's dilemma apply with equal force to this artificial mathematical cognition? Or is there a principled difference between human intuition of abstract objects and statistical extraction of arithmetic patterns that allows the latter to escape causal isolation, perhaps because the patterns themselves are physically instantiated in the corpus?

Please explain, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, how Frege's separation of arithmetic from both psychology and physics relates to this extended challenge. I want to understand whether the dilemma deepens when applied to non-human cognition, or whether the causal theory of knowledge applies only to biological agents with sensory apparatus, making the extension a category mistake.
