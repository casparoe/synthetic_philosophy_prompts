A philosophy-of-technology essay I recently read attempts to apply Gödel’s incompleteness results well beyond arithmetic. The author summarizes the theorems like this: “Gödel’s first theorem proves that in any consistent formal system strong enough to encode basic arithmetic, there exists a statement that is true yet unprovable within the system. The second theorem proves that no such system can demonstrate its own consistency using only its own rules. This means that any fully formalized domain—whether a scientific theory, a legal code, or an ethical framework—will contain true propositions it can never derive, and it can never fully certify its own freedom from contradiction from within. The author then notes that human mathematicians can recognize the truth of a Gödel sentence for a given formal system, which shows that human cognition is not confined to any single formal architecture. From this, the essay concludes that an artificial intelligence built entirely on formal logical rules will necessarily be incapable of accessing certain truths—especially in ethics and jurisprudence, where both consistency and completeness are required—that human minds can grasp.”

How fair is this characterization? Please audit it claim by claim: identify which statements correctly capture the first and second theorems together with their standard technical conditions (effective axiomatizability, consistency, and arithmetic expressibility), which parts illegitimately extend those conditions to scientific theories, legal codes, or ethical frameworks, and which parts present the Lucas-Penrose-style inference about human cognition as if it were uncontroversial rather than a disputed argument that itself relies on assuming a stronger system subject to the same limitation. Finally, assess whether the conclusion about AI follows from anything Gödel actually established, or whether it depends on the prior overextensions and on treating “recognizing truth” as escaping formalization altogether. Be specific about what a careful philosopher of mathematics or logic would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong.
