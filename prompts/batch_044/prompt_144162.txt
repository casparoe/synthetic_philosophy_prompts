Consider an adversarial collaboration between two camps on whether immoral art can be good art, confined to cases where the immorality lies in the work’s representational content rather than in the artist’s biography or production history. Camp A defends structural autonomy: artistic value is constituted by formal coherence, emotional complexity, and representational insight, and a work’s moral stance is evaluated separately; an immoral perspective can even be an aesthetic virtue when it yields an unflinching or structurally necessary vision. Camp B defends moral integration: artistic value is partly constituted by ethical achievement, so when a work structurally invites the audience to adopt, enjoy, or approve of serious wrongs, that corruption is an internal artistic defect, not merely an external moral hazard, making the work worse as art precisely where its aesthetic technique succeeds.

Draft the joint statement both camps could sign. Open with a numbered section of agreed claims—covering at least the distinction between producer-context and work-content immorality; the acknowledgment that technically masterful works with abhorrent content exist; the shared rejection of the view that aesthetic and moral evaluation never interact; and the clarification that both camps use “artistic value” in a normative-evaluative rather than purely descriptive sense. Follow with a disagreements table stating each camp’s precise position in wording that side accepts, distinguishing genuine disagreement from verbal confusion—specifically addressing whether “moral perspective” denotes an internal structural feature of the work or an externally attributed effect.

Then identify three cruxes whose resolution would move at least one camp. The first crux must address the counterfactual audience vulnerability problem: if a technically flawless work causes moral damage only among especially susceptible viewers, does that vulnerability enter artistic evaluation, or is it an external risk analogous to placing a beautiful but dangerous object near a child? The second must address whether the work’s internal “moral perspective”—the attitude it structurally invites toward represented wrong—can be separated from any actual audience response, and whether that separation undermines the integrationist claim. The third should concern the normative weight of aesthetic distance: does the framing of a wrong as fictional, stylized, or ironized genuinely insulate the work’s artistic achievement from moral criticism, or does it merely relocate the corruption? For each crux, specify the case, conceptual test, or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, and record each camp’s explicit prediction about what the correct resolution would demonstrate. The final document should read as a collaborative publication, not a dialogue or verdict, and should avoid any request for citations, bibliographies, or appeals to obscure theoretical apparatus.
