After a long conversation with a friend about scientific modeling, I have come to hold a hard-line version of the Friedman-style defense of unrealistic assumptions—what is sometimes called the F-twist—deliberately extended far beyond economics into climate science, epidemiology, and macro-level social modeling. The position is that unrealistic assumptions are never, in themselves, a legitimate ground for criticizing a scientific model; predictive accuracy and instrumental success are the sole relevant metrics. Even cases that look like "structural misalignment," where false assumptions generate correct predictions only through compensating errors within a calibration domain but risk catastrophic failure in extrapolation, do not constitute a separate category of flaw. Either the model eventually fails predictively outside its domain—in which case we have ordinary predictive failure—or it succeeds, in which case the unrealistic assumptions have performed exactly the isolating function they were meant to perform. Critics who invoke mechanistic realism, explanatory depth, or distinctions between Galilean isolation and Aristotelian approximation are, on this view, importing metaphysical demands that predictive science does not owe. I believe this applies as strongly to agent-based climate projections with stylized behavioral rules as to macroeconomic equilibrium models.

What I want is an honest map of what would actually count against this universalized position—not the standard intuitive objections that unrealistic assumptions "feel wrong," but the specific arguments, formal results, or empirical patterns that should move me. Which of these would defenders of the hard-line F-twist themselves concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as category errors? In particular, I am interested in whether there are documented cases where a model's predictive success within a domain is systematically coupled with extrapolation failure that traces specifically to its unrealistic structural assumptions, and whether that coupling can be identified independently of simply waiting for the out-of-domain prediction to fail. I also want to know whether this position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every predictive success counts in its favor and every failure is treated as "not yet enough predictive success"—and whether that insulation is itself a problem for a view of this kind. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, including both what has arguably already happened in fields like epidemiological forecasting or climate projection and what would have to be shown formally, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated if that is where the assessment lands.
