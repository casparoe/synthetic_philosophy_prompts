Write a deadpan, approximately 500-word satirical policy brief in the formal bureaucratic voice of a fictional “International Consortium for Phenomenological Hygiene.” The brief should recommend that elective induction of pain asymbolia become the global standard of care for chronic pain, arguing that because pain’s “badness” resides entirely in its affective-motivational overlay rather than in its sensory-discriminative content, decoupling nociception from negative affect eliminates suffering without sacrificing clinically useful damage-information. The satire must exaggerate a real philosophical position—specifically, the representationalist or content-based account that treats pain as essentially a damage-report whose unpleasantness is an optional interpretive layer—while remaining rigorously internally consistent: the board should sincerely believe it is preserving patient autonomy, dignity, and well-being. To keep the argument conceptually sharp rather than generic, inject and deploy the following invented but philosophically motivated distinction: the “somatic sovereignty thesis,” which holds that the affective withdrawal response is not merely an unpleasant side-effect but the body’s primary normative mechanism for asserting territorial ownership over itself; consequently, removing the affect does not merely relieve pain but dissolves the patient’s standing as a bodily subject with legitimate claims to integrity. The brief should accidentally reveal this consequence through its own clinical language—for example, by reporting that post-procedure patients show “complete satisfaction with tissue damage” and now require “external agents to enforce withdrawal behaviors previously executed autonomously.” Include one invented quotation, set in quotation marks, from a dissenting board member who warns: “Once the withdrawal reflex is decorrelated from nociception, you haven’t cured pain—you’ve cured the patient of being a patient.” Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific named scholars or texts. The humor must come from getting the philosophy of pain right—clearly distinguishing sensory-discriminative from affective-motivational dimensions, recognizing that asymbolia spares location and intensity reporting while eliminating the “hurting” and the drive to withdraw—so that a specialist recognizes the target is being satirized fairly rather than through caricature. Write in polished, formal prose appropriate to an international ethics review document, and maintain a sustained deadpan irony throughout: the reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without any winking from the author.
