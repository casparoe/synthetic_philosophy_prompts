A close friend and I recently spent several hours arguing about Leibniz’s claim that this is the best of all possible worlds, and we kept talking past one another because we never clarified which commitments actually distinguish the interpretations. I want to turn our confusion into a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—for an advanced undergraduate philosophy seminar. Each branch should be labeled by a concrete claim or case that someone can answer without knowing the philosophical label it leads to. The tree should split first on whether there is exactly one best world or only a top tier of worlds tied for best; second, on whether God’s choice is constrained solely by the principle of sufficient reason or also by prior logical or compossibility conditions; third, on whether evil is a purely privative absence of good or a positive reality that weighs against a world’s value; and fourth, on whether genuinely free local choices that produce suffering are compatible with the world’s being globally optimal—specifically, consider the case of an agent who freely does evil: is there a nearby possible world in which that very agent freely does good without disrupting the overall harmony? The leaves must include: (i) strict optimism with a unique global best and privative evil; (ii) the “tied-worlds” view that denies uniqueness but preserves global optimality; (iii) the “local-freedom” view that permits sub-optimal local regions within the best world; (iv) the “PSR-only” view according to which God necessarily picks the best but could have created nothing at all; (v) the rejection that denies any best-world ranking on the grounds that evil is real and weighing; and (vi) at least one unoccupied combination—such as the claim that there is a unique best world but God was externally compelled to actualize a worse one—which should be flagged with a brief diagnosis of why it is incoherent, theologically unattractive, or merely unexplored. You should also flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, especially if the “local-freedom” view collapses into strict optimism once free will is analyzed correctly. The deliverable should be buildable from logical space rather than from a list of named philosophers, though the six positions above must appear as leaves. The audience is students reasoning from concepts alone, so do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists, and keep the instrument to about six to eight terminal positions.
