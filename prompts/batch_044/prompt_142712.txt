I've been working through Einstein's hole argument in its modern formulation—specifically the version that applies a diffeomorphism which is the identity everywhere outside a matter-free region and smoothly non-identity inside it. This produces two metric tensors that solve the field equations, agree on all measurements outside the region, yet assign different curvature values to the same manifold points within it.

My difficulty is with the standard framing that this undermines determinism. If the transformation is an active diffeomorphism—essentially dragging the coordinate grid along with the fields—then the two metrics seem to represent the exact same physical geometry, only with bare point-labels permuted inside the hole. So it appears to me that initial data on a Cauchy surface outside do determine a unique physical state; the only problem is that a substantivalist who reifies bare points will misdescribe that single state twice. In other words, isn't determinism actually preserved, and isn't the sole casualty a redundant ontology of spacetime points?

I wonder if I'm misapplying the idea of Leibniz equivalence. If the metric is a field on the manifold and the diffeomorphism shifts points but not field values relative to an external system, then for any particular bare point p inside the hole one metric assigns one value and the other assigns a different value. That looks like representational ambiguity—like labeling the same temperature distribution with two differently indexed thermometers—rather than physical indeterminacy. Given this, is the familiar characterization of the hole argument as a determinism failure resting on the false presupposition that representations with different point-assignments automatically correspond to different physical worlds? Or does a genuine physical ambiguity survive even after we grant Leibniz equivalence?
