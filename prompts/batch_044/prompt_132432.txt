I am a journalist who covers philosophy and ideas, and next Thursday I am presenting at a colloquium on political aesthetics and media theory. I need a speaker-prep sheet for the Q&A. My thesis is an unorthodox reading of Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert on the theater: rather than treating his critique as a moralistic or aesthetic complaint about representation, I argue it is fundamentally epistemic. Specifically, I claim that theatrical spectacle introduces what I call a counterfactual emotional investment—the spectator's structured identification with fictional personae trains the cognitive habit of substituting vicarious, scripted affect for the direct, unmediated judgment of the common good that legitimate democratic deliberation requires. This is not the claim that theater makes people immoral; it is the claim that it corrupts the epistemic conditions of the general will by making citizens accustomed to evaluating public affairs through the lens of private, narrative identification.

The audience will be about fifteen people: primarily Rousseau scholars who regard this letter as a minor, reactionary footnote and are well acquainted with the standard interpretive literature; two media-theorists who treat spectacle as constitutive of modern political identity rather than corrupting; and one philosopher of emotion who is skeptical that fictional engagement can have any structured, civic-level epistemic effect. They know the text well enough to invoke specific passages, but I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations—only for the objections they will actually raise against my specific epistemic framing, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is to my argument. For each objection, I want it phrased in the vocabulary of that particular camp, a concise reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest or concedes ground. I also want the one question I am hoping nobody asks—the one that would force me to distinguish my position from a generic anti-media moralism without collapsing back into the very aesthetic-moral reading I reject. Finally, tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks to lower the temperature, and flag which objection carries enough real force that I should not attempt to deny it outright but rather absorb it into my position.
