For my graduate seminar in metaphysics and digital ethics — fourteen students, one seventy-five-minute session — design a structured, in-class role-play that forces participants to experience the tension between treating a virtual artifact as genuinely destroyed and treating its corruption as mere data rearrangement, without naming the metaphysical positions in advance. The scenario should involve a persistent, user-co-created virtual monument that exists only as distributed procedural states maintained by collective protocol agreement, with no unique physical token; the conflict arises when one party exploits a governance-level vulnerability to irreversibly alter the monument’s functional and aesthetic properties while leaving every underlying data record intact. Provide explicit roles — for example, original co-creators, a neutral archivist, the actor who triggered the vulnerability, and observers who must adjudicate a collective response — together with rules for interaction, a non-monetary payoff or reputation scheme that makes the adjudicators’ decision consequential within the exercise, and a timeline that fits the session. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that students first feel the pull of moral outrage and ontological loss, as if something genuinely real had been harmed, and only afterward discover that no physical substrate was touched and no files were deleted. Include a debrief that maps specific moments of the role-play onto distinctions in virtual ontology — substrate independence, functional persistence, collective recognition — and onto ethical questions about harm without biological or physical victims. Be explicit about what will predictably derail the first run, such as technically inclined students collapsing the distinction into software engineering, and how to correct for it in facilitation. Finally, state frankly which dimension of virtual objecthood or virtual harm the exercise cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator’s framing.
