Here are my verdicts on several hypothetical translation strategies, and I want to know whether they hang together under a single principle or whether consistency requires me to abandon one or more of them. First, a rendering that preserves the original rhyme scheme and meter exactly but replaces every image with a culturally equivalent one drawn from modern American English: my intuition is that this succeeds, even though almost every literal word has changed. Second, a version that keeps every literal image and metaphor intact but abandons rhyme and meter entirely, presenting the content as free verse: I judge this a failure as a translation of the poem, though it might serve as an explanatory gloss. Third, a translation that preserves both rhyme and meter and all literal images, but is forced to insert an extra couplet explaining an obsolete reference that modern readers would miss: I think this is a worse failure than the second, because it corrupts the poem's economy rather than merely its form. Fourth, a version that preserves rhyme, meter, and literal content, but rearranges the stanza order so that the emotional arc runs backward: I think this fails too, but I am unsure whether its failure belongs to the same category as the second or the third. Fifth, a rendering that preserves every formal feature and every literal image, but achieves this only by using archaic vocabulary that makes the poem sound like a pastiche of Elizabethan English rather than a living voice: I think it succeeds technically as translation yet fails aesthetically, and I am uncertain whether aesthetic failure should count against it in the same way as formal or literal failure.
