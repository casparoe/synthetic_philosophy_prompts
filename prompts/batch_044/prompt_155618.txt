For a feature I am preparing on the hidden philosophical commitments of twentieth-century physicists, I need a poem that performs conceptual labor rather than merely ornamenting a biography. Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and are compelled into the same final couplet—staging Erwin Schrödinger's late argument, developed across his essays on Advaita Vedanta, that the apparent multiplicity of conscious subjects is not an ontological plurality but a kind of māyā: a veil over one underlying consciousness that only seems to fracture into separate observers. One refrain should articulate the monistic thesis—perhaps in the register of a single eye, a shared breath, or one continuous awareness—while the other should articulate the empirical resistance: the undeniable presence of other faces, other pains, other points of view that refuse to dissolve into my own. Do not reduce the poem to a devotional gloss; let the form carry the philosophy. The villanelle's compulsive returns should mirror Schrödinger's claim that the illusion of separation keeps reasserting itself with every new encounter, and the closing couplet, where both refrains are locked together, should enact his resolution—that multiplicity is real only as appearance, never as substance. You may invoke, without quoting, his tendency to compare the plurality of minds to the plurality of quantum descriptions prior to measurement, treating both as differentiated expressions of a single reality rather than genuinely discrete entities. The poem should be precise enough that a reader familiar with Schrödinger's non-technical writings on consciousness recognizes the specific conceptual tension—unity versus the stubborn phenomenology of otherness—yet it should stand on its own formal and emotional merits for a reader who has never encountered the view. Aim for restraint, clarity, and structural rigor; avoid mystical vagueness and do not include citations, titles, or requests for further reading.
