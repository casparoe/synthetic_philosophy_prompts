Consider the following situation, described in practical terms. Over the past eighteen months, you and your closest friend—both serious amateur radio astronomers—have independently constructed functionally identical backyard telescopes using the same open-source hardware designs and calibration protocols. You both detected the same faint repeating signal and calculated its periodicity: you arrived at a 47-day cycle, your friend at 53 days. You have walked through every stage together—filter settings, noise-floor adjustments, Fourier transforms, baseline corrections—and neither of you can locate an error in the other’s work. Your training, past accuracy, and access to data are essentially indistinguishable. Now a local university has offered you both a single shared observation slot on a larger array next week, but the optimal window depends on which periodicity is correct; you must submit one joint proposal with a single number by tomorrow. Your friend argues that because your skills and reliability in this domain are essentially identical, with symmetric reasoning and shared practical stakes, rationality demands splitting the difference and submitting 50 days, then revising together once the professional observation is made. You feel that averaging two genuinely different inferences—without having identified any specific flaw—would substitute social compromise for evidence, and that you should submit 47 days and let the array data serve as the decisive test. You are genuinely unsure whether holding firm is principled epistemic autonomy or mere stubbornness. Without naming any philosophical theories, analyze whose position is more defensible, explain what rational belief revision actually requires when two equally competent reasoners disagree and no mistake can be found, and propose a practical resolution that respects both your evidential commitments and the collaborative reality of the proposal.
