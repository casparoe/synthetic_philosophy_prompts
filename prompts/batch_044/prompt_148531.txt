Consider the institutional practice of constitutional rights-review in democracies that combine three design features: courts apply a structured proportionality framework—testing suitability, necessity, and a final balancing of the right's weight against competing public interests—to invalidate legislation; the same legal culture describes fundamental rights as objective values that place inviolable limits on democratic majorities, and justifies judicial review on precisely that counter-majoritarian ground; yet when courts do find a violation, they often refrain from immediate annulment and instead issue a declaration of incompatibility with a transitional period, directing the legislature to craft the constitutional remedy. This combination seems to pull in different directions: proportionality treats rights as scalar interests that can be weighed against collective goods, which sits awkwardly with the rhetoric of rights as side-constraints that justify removing issues from democratic control; meanwhile, deferred nullification treats the legislature as the authoritative agent of repair, which complicates the claim that judges alone guard objective constitutional values. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory—of rights, democratic authority, and the separation of powers—that makes this practice coherent rather than contradictory. Does it strictly presuppose a particular ontology of rights (for instance, as institutionally embedded roles rather than as individual interests), or is it compatible with several rival accounts? Identify which theories of value and legitimacy the practice quietly rules out, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and specify which institutional elements—appointment mechanisms, the stages of review, or the temporal effect of judgments—would need to change if the underlying theory were replaced by a purely consequentialist or a purely proceduralist account of constitutional justice.
