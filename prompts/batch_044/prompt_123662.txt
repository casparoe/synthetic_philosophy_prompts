I'm delivering the opening remarks at the dedication of a public history installation in the university's central quad, where three bronze portraits—of Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus—have been installed with nothing beneath them except birth and death years and a single engraved question: "What do we owe them?" The audience is deliberately mixed: roughly half are historians and moral philosophers who have argued about these figures for decades, and half are students, donors, and local residents with no special background in ethics or historiography. I want the speech to do genuine philosophical work, not merely decorate the occasion. To that end, please structure it around a distinction between two very different appeals people make to "he was a man of his time"—one grounded in epistemic limitation (he lacked access to the moral knowledge we now take for granted) and one grounded in motivational constitution (his character was formed by norms he could not have escaped)—and please build in the idea of "serial monism," by which I mean the hidden assumption that any given historical era possesses a single, dominant moral framework, so that being "of" one's time implies being uniformly embedded in it. The speech should use this idea to determine what can and cannot be said: it should explain why the installation refuses explanatory plaques not out of ignorance, but because serial monism is false—each figure lived amid competing moral vocabularies that were available even to them—and therefore neither the easy excuse nor the pure condemnation that depends on that excuse can stand. It must avoid comforting either uncritical celebrants or iconoclasts; for the philosophers in the room it should treat the distinction and its failure with precision, and for everyone else it should make the refusal of easy judgment feel like intellectual honesty rather than evasion. Please write an address of about eight minutes, with no citations, no references to specific scholars, and with one concrete, tangible image—perhaps Jefferson's own library holding texts that argued against slavery, or Churchill's contemporaries within the British establishment who publicly opposed imperial violence—that illustrates the presence of internal moral disagreement within each figure's own world, without reducing the point to mere biography.
