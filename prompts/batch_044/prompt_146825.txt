Consider the institutional design of Halden prison in Norway, where incarcerated people wear civilian clothes, cook together in shared kitchens with ordinary utensils, and live in units designed to resemble apartments; guards are unarmed and trained in social-work interaction, and family visits take place in private rooms where prisoners may prepare meals for relatives. This practice of normalization holds that prison life should mirror outside life as closely as security allows. Contrast this with standard American mass incarceration: uniform dress, movement restricted by locks and schedules, family contact mediated through glass or video, and in supermax units, prolonged solitary administrative segregation that removes almost all social interaction and sensory variation. Both systems claim to serve punishment and rehabilitation, yet their architectures seem to presuppose opposite views of moral personhood. Does normalization assume that moral agency is continuous and relational—exercised only through ordinary social roles—so that stripping those roles damages the very capacity rehabilitation aims to restore? Does the American model instead presuppose a status-based theory, where conviction justifies a kind of civil exclusion that removes not only liberty but standing to exercise ordinary capacities? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes each design coherent, distinguishing what it strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Then address an internal tension: Norway still uses solitary confinement for disciplinary infractions and withholds voting rights from prisoners, both of which look like exclusion rather than normalization. Is the system philosophically incoherent, or can one theory—perhaps distinguishing spatial restriction, disciplinary suspension, and political membership—license both? Finally, if punishment were reconceived as purely temporal restriction without any change in moral or civic status, which concrete features of both systems would become unjustified?
