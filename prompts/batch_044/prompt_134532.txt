Write the six-minute opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop on physician-assisted dying. The audience is evenly divided: palliative-care clinicians and family advocates who have lived through end-of-life decisions, and philosophers and graduate students familiar with Hume's withdrawn essay on suicide and his critique of causal powers in the Treatise. Structure the remarks around two Humean constraints that must shape what you can and cannot say. First, adopt Hume's claim that suicide becomes permissible not when life lacks pleasure but when continued existence is experienced as an active burden—use this to reject any framing that treats the value of life as a single scale the workshop will calibrate. Second, develop an analogy drawn from Hume's denial that the will operates as an efficient cause in the metaphysical sense he attacks: frame the patient's decision not as a sovereign causal act that produces death, but as the cessation of a contrary inclination, like releasing a weight that can no longer be held. This analogy must block metaphysically loaded language about causing one's own death without retreating into passive-voice euphemism. Speak so the clinicians and families are oriented, not lectured, but be precise enough that the philosophers see you are not misrepresenting Hume's view. Do not request citations, do not include a bibliography, and do not quote Hume verbatim—paraphrase his positions. Keep the remarks suitable for oral delivery.
