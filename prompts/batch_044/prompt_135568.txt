Consider the following argument, which I think pushes the sociology of nationalism into a democratic-theoretic contradiction.

(1) Following Anderson, a nation is an imagined community: its members will never meet most fellow members, yet they maintain a mental image of shared membership sustained by institutions, education, and narrative framing.

(2) Following Hobsbawm, the traditions that present this community as ancient are largely invented in modernity—especially the nineteenth and twentieth centuries—constructing rituals, symbols, and historical narratives that project present political needs onto a past never actually lived as a continuous national existence.

(3) If both hold, then the boundary of the nation—who belongs to the demos—is not discovered in an objective historical record but is an artifact of the invented narrative and the institutions that propagate it; the “age” of the nation is therefore a function of archival selection rather than continuity of political life.

(4) Democratic legitimacy requires that those subject to collective decisions be the ones who authorize them. This requires that the boundary of the demos be stable and non-arbitrary, not revisable at will by the very authorities it authorizes.

(5) But on (3), the boundary is precisely revisable: whenever the state revises its curriculum, commemorative practice, or origin story, it can expand or contract the imagined community.

(6) This produces a reflexive loop: the current demos authorizes the institutions that maintain the traditions that define who belongs to the demos. There is no independent ground for membership outside the loop.

(7) One might break the loop by saying democratic legitimacy requires only internal acceptance—those inside the narrative accept the boundary, and that acceptance is self-validating.

(8) Yet if internal acceptance is sufficient, then any group with sufficient control over narrative and archival institutions can declare itself a demos and retroactively include anyone within its revised tradition—neighboring populations, future generations, or immigrants assigned membership through a rewritten origin story—regardless of their actual consent.

(9) That makes democratic authority indistinguishable from successful narrative control, and it strips outsiders of any standing to resist incorporation. That seems intolerable.

(10) So one of (3), (4), (6), or (7) must fail. But (1) and (2) look like standard sociology, and (4) looks like a minimal democratic constraint. Which step actually collapses, and what does giving it up cost?

If I had to preserve the Anderson/Hobsbawm picture intact and keep some boundary constraint on democratic legitimacy, I would want to reject whatever makes the reflexive loop vicious. But I am unsure whether the error is in (3)—that invented traditions really do determine membership boundaries—or in (6)—that the loop is harmful rather than benignly self-constituting—or in (7)—that acceptance alone is never enough.

Please take a committed position: name the single weakest step, explain exactly where the slide or equivocation occurs (whether it is a scope shift, an ambiguity in “invented,” or a hidden premise about circularity), and price the alternatives. If you think the argument is actually valid and one of (1)–(4) must go despite appearances, say so and defend which abandonment is least costly for democratic theory. Do not lay out the options neutrally; defend the diagnosis you actually think is right.
