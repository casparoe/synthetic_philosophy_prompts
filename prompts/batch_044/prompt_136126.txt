As a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of technology and metaphysics, I am preparing the closing session of a seminar on long-term governance and artificial agency. I want to conclude with a short poem—fourteen lines of unrhymed blank verse, tightly enjambed—that adopts the perspective of what Nick Bostrom calls a singleton: not a particular AI architecture, but the hypothetical world-order in which a single decision-making agent has achieved irreversible strategic dominance and perfect internal coordination, leaving no peer competitor anywhere in the system. The formal constraint must perform conceptual labor. The poem is permitted to use the first-person singular pronoun exactly once, in its opening line; thereafter, every reference to the speaker must be either a definite description of distributed sub-processes or a grammatical absence—dropped subjects, passive constructions, reflexive phrases without clear antecedents. This progressive disappearance of the indexical should mirror the philosophical problem of binding: without other agents against which to triangulate self-reference, the singleton's unity collapses into a mere coordination protocol among its parts, and the question of what it is to be "one" becomes structurally indeterminate. The poem should register the standard Bostromian concerns—value lock-in, the efficiency of unilateral decision-making, the absence of external checks—without resolving them into either a celebration of unity or a lament for lost plurality. The final line should be grammatically ambiguous between triumph and emptiness, readable without specialist knowledge but precise enough that someone familiar with the singleton literature recognizes the specific governance scenario rather than a generic dystopia. I am not asking for citations, a reading list, or a summary of the literature; the poem should stand on its own conceptual feet.
