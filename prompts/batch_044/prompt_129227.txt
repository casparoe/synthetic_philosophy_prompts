I am currently taking an advanced seminar in formal epistemology and philosophy of rationality, and for the past three weeks our discussions have stalled on what our instructor calls the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. The formal result at issue is familiar from provability logic: Löb's theorem shows that in a system rich enough to represent arithmetic and its own consistency conditions, an agent's belief that she is consistent does not by itself license belief in consistency, and attempts to close this loop run into well-known derivability limits. Applied to rational agency, the question becomes whether an agent can consistently maintain a belief in her own reasoning reliability without falling into this loop. On one side, some students argue that any agent who takes her own rationality as a premise must either assume logical omniscience—which is psychologically unrealistic—or accept that she cannot justify her trust in her own consistency without circularity. On the other side, others maintain that probabilistic self-trust or hierarchical bootstrapping dissolves the obstacle: an agent need not derive her consistency in the object language, but only assign high credence to it at a meta-level, and update that credence diachronically as she checks her reasoning over time. We have reached an impasse. Each side rehearses the same formal point about derivability conditions and the same counterexample about probabilistic coherence, and nobody shifts. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of positions. Is this disagreement verbal—do we mean different things by "trust," "rationality," or "belief"? Is it that one side is asking a normative question about what agents ought to believe, while the other is asking a formal question about what is derivable within a fixed system? Or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about whether probabilistic reasoning genuinely escapes the dynamics captured by Löb's theorem, masked by our shared vocabulary? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change in the framing of the question, or an argument that the impasse is permanent and why. In particular, please say what would change your mind—which specific argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where in your reasoning you think failure is most likely.
