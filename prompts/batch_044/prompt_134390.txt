As a retired experimental physicist now working in moral philosophy, I defend the view that standing to blame functions analogously to calibration authority in metrology: one may issue a corrective moral judgment only if one’s own normative record is traceably uncorrupted with respect to the standard being applied, and hypocrisy is therefore not an independent moral failing but a breakdown of traceability that voids the corrective act without undermining the norm itself. This analogy deliberately imports instrumental reasoning into interpersonal ethics, and I recognize that critics who deny hypocrisy undermines standing—along with those who treat moral authority as non-instrumental—will resist it. What precisely would count against this metrological model? Please rank the potential defeaters: which findings in moral psychology, which social practices of collective or institutional blame, and which logical features of normative discourse would be fatal rather than merely awkward? In particular, consider whether the recovery of standing after moral improvement, and the possibility of collective correction where individual traceability is absent, genuinely falsify the analogy or only require parameter adjustment. I want an honest assessment of how exposed the position is, including what its defenders ought to concede.
