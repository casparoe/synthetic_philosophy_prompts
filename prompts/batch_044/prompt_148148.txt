I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving active research, has begun studying philosophy of science with some seriousness. I have sketched the following short argument, which attempts to isolate a form of underdetermination that I think is obscured in the standard Quine-Duhem formulation. I would welcome your critical comments on its conceptual coherence, on whether my characterization of the holistic thesis is accurate, and on whether the distinction I propose genuinely adds something rather than collapsing into familiar arguments. I am particularly interested in suggestions for sharpening the thesis and in identifying any obvious philosophical misconceptions.

Essay sketch: Instrumental Underdetermination in Mediated Observation

The standard Quine-Duhem thesis holds that isolated theoretical hypotheses are never conclusively refuted by single recalcitrant observations, because one can always preserve the hypothesis by adjusting auxiliary assumptions elsewhere in the broader network of commitments. I do not dispute this holistic picture, but my experience with detector physics suggests a structurally related yet distinct problem. In mature experimental science, raw instrument outputs—voltages, digitized counts, time-series traces—carry no evidential weight until they are processed through calibration protocols, reconstruction algorithms, and background-subtraction models, each of which embeds substantive theoretical assumptions about the apparatus and its environment. As a result, when two competing theoretical frameworks, call them T1 and T2, are both compatible with the same reconstructed dataset, that compatibility often depends not on ad hoc adjustments within T1 or T2 alone, but on the simultaneous adoption of correspondingly different instrumental interpretation schemes, C1 and C2. The empirical choice then lies not between T1 and T2 in isolation, but between the paired systems (T1, C1) and (T2, C2), and the data underdetermine this choice in a way that is not captured by saying merely that auxiliary hypotheses have been modified. I propose calling this instrumental underdetermination: a condition in which the boundary between theoretical claim and observational evidence is not merely porous but systematically co-constituted by the calibration framework itself.

Consider, for illustration, track reconstruction in a drift chamber. The inference from ionization signals to particle trajectories presupposes a model of electron drift velocity, diffusion coefficients, and electric-field uniformity. Should two competing field-theoretic descriptions of the underlying interaction require different parameterizations of these instrumental processes in order to reproduce the same reconstructed track patterns, then the reconstructed tracks do not adjudicate between the theories independently of the paired instrumental models. The observational vocabulary is therefore not a neutral intermediary but part of the theoretical network subject to simultaneous revision.

If this analysis holds, philosophers of science ought to treat calibration and reconstruction frameworks not as transparent windows onto phenomena, but as components of the belief network that are equally susceptible to preservation or revision in the face of anomalous data. I seek constructive criticism of this claim—specifically, whether it is genuinely distinct from standard holistic underdetermination, whether the drift-chamber example is conceptually sound, and how the argument might be refined without relying on specialized historical citations. I ask for conceptual guidance rather than a bibliography or reading list; my aim is solely to improve the architecture of the argument.
