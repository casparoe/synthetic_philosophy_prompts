Spinoza argued in his political writings that the state should not suppress opinions but may restrain actions that threaten public peace, and he defended a sharp distinction between internal freedom of judgment and external compliance with law. Imagine that Spinoza, retaining these commitments, is confronted with Karl Popper's paradox of tolerance—the idea that a society extending unlimited tolerance to intolerant groups risks being destroyed by them, and therefore must reserve the right to suppress intolerant movements—as well as with a modern extension I will call the recursive moderation problem: on digital platforms, the mechanisms used to suppress intolerant speech (algorithmic suppression, deplatforming, and labeling) are themselves frequently characterized by the suppressed as acts of intolerance, producing a second-order paradox about whether tolerance requires tolerating the charge that one is intolerant. Predict how Spinoza would resolve the interaction between Popper's original paradox and this recursive digital variant. Would he accept Popper's conclusion that unlimited tolerance is self-defeating and that intolerant groups forfeit their claim to toleration? Or would he reject the framing altogether, perhaps by arguing that digital speech acts do not constitute the kind of action that threatens civil peace, or by distinguishing between suppressing a belief and suppressing an organizational capacity? Reach and defend a specific verdict about Spinoza's likely position, grounding it in his actual philosophical commitments—particularly his view of the relationship between belief, expression, and state power—without appealing to his biography as determinative and without asking for bibliographic references.
