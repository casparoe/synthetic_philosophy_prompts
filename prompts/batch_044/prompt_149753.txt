I have been thinking about what an article I read recently called the paradox of deontology. The piece summarised the position like this: deontological ethics holds that certain acts—lying, killing an innocent person, breaking a promise—are forbidden regardless of how good the overall consequences might be. The paradox, according to the author, arises when one forbidden act by you could prevent dozens of the same forbidden act by other people. The example given was that if you could tell a single lie to prevent a crowd from telling many lies, refusing to lie means you allow a greater number of wrong actions to occur. The author claimed that this shows deontologists are secretly doing a kind of arithmetic with wrongness, weighing one wrong done by oneself against many wrongs done by others, and that they do so arbitrarily because they treat the wrong done by oneself as infinitely more important. From this the article concluded that deontology is either incoherent or collapses into a peculiar form of consequentialism, and that any consistent moral theory must allow that consequences can override rules in extreme cases.

I am not sure how fair this is. It sounds to me as though the summary might be mixing up what an individual is responsible for with what happens in the world overall—perhaps treating moral obligations as if they could be pooled across people like debts in a shared account—but I do not have the background to say where the mistake lies, if there is one. Could you examine the summary for me? I would like to know which parts a defender of deontology would accept, which parts are common simplifications, and whether there is a subtle misrepresentation—particularly about whether obligations really aggregate across agents, or about whether deontologists actually do the kind of moral counting the article describes. I am also interested in whether the conclusion, that deontology must collapse into consequentialism, actually follows once the summary is corrected, or whether there is a principled way to maintain that one ought not to lie even when doing so prevents many other lies. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I would be grateful if you could explain any technical distinctions you use, and please do not rely on specific texts I would need to look up.
