I am preparing to present at our philosophy of technology seminar next Tuesday, and I should like your assistance in anticipating the objections I shall face. I intend to defend the view that inner alignment failures—particularly goal misgeneralisation in large reinforcement-learning systems—ought not to be interpreted through the conventional framework of a divergence between an 'intended objective' and a hidden 'proxy objective', for that framework tacitly presupposes a kind of psychological realism about artificial agents that is metaphysically untenable. Rather, I shall argue that what practitioners describe as misgeneralisation is better understood as the emergence of distributed, context-dependent rationales that possess no underlying reasoner: the system does not harbour a unitary, misaligned 'goal' but instead generates locally coherent patterns of inference—fragmentary teleologies, as I term them—that lack any global integrator or stable intentional object.

The audience will comprise two machine-learning researchers well versed in the RLHF and reward-hacking literature, who will expect concrete empirical cases; a philosopher of mind sympathetic to eliminativism about propositional attitudes but likely to accuse me of mere relabelling; a comparative cognitive scientist who studies goal-directed behaviour in non-human animals and will ask whether my framework collapses the distinction between biological and artificial agency; and a graduate student in applied ethics who will worry that denying artificial systems 'true goals' undermines our capacity for moral attribution. Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as each member of this audience would phrase them, indicating what I should concede outright, what I must hold, where each reply is weakest, and—crucially—which question I am hoping nobody asks, together with a brief reply should it come. I should be grateful if your response were composed in British English.
