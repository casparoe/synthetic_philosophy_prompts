As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am moderating a one-hour public seminar for physics graduate students and philosophy undergraduates with no prior metaphysics coursework. The subject is Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, taught specifically through Black's counterexample of two identical spheres in an otherwise empty, rotationally symmetric universe. I need a pedagogical plan, not a lecture script. First, lay out the dependency order: which distinction—between qualitative and relational properties, or between intrinsic and extrinsic features—must be secured before the spheres are introduced, and why presenting the spheres too early causes the audience to treat spatial separation as an obvious qualitative difference rather than as the problem's crux. Second, specify the carrying case precisely: use the two spheres, but modify them with one added constraint—say, a temporal symmetry or an epistemic blind spot that removes any hidden relational marker—so that the audience cannot resolve the paradox by appealing to an unnoticed asymmetry. Third, map the misconceptions this particular audience will import: quantum statistical indistinguishability, gauge invariance of position, and the tendency to treat "left of" as an intrinsic property. For each misconception, identify the exact point in the sequence where I should interrupt and correct it. Fourth, provide one precise closing question that will reveal whether a participant grasps that the paradox is metaphysical rather than physical. Finally, state explicitly which modern debates—such as structural realism and primitive thisness—I should acknowledge as outside the session's scope rather than attempt to explain.
