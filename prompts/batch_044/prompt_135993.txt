I've been trying to map the Dennett-Caruso disagreement about just deserts onto a specific design problem, and I want to see whether the core tension survives once we strip away the usual rhetorical framing. Write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors—call them A (compatibilist, working in a broadly Dennett-style register) and B (free-will skeptic, working in a broadly Caruso-style register)—who are debating whether punishment can be justified without metaphysical desert.

The twist: they are both advising a jurisdiction in which every offender's complete neural trajectory and full environmental history is transparent to the sentencing system. A must defend that reactive attitudes still track a real functional property—something like authorship under local rational constraints—that distinguishes punishment from mere preventive detention, even when causal opacity is eliminated. B must argue that once transparency removes all opacity about etiology, the only remaining justification is consequentialist risk-management, and retaining the label "punishment" is a category error that disguises quarantine as moral accounting.

Make it real back-and-forth, not parallel monologues. B should press A specifically on why the evolutionary indispensability of reactive attitudes survives total causal transparency—if we can see exactly which inputs produced the action, what work is the attitude doing that a risk-score wouldn't do? A should press B specifically on whether the quarantine model can preserve the expressive, communicative function of social blame—telling the offender and the community that this act was wrong—without collapsing into pure engineering. Neither should be a strawman; neither should invoke bibliographies, citations, or obscure technical apparatus. Keep it tight, focused, and in the weeds of the arguments themselves.
