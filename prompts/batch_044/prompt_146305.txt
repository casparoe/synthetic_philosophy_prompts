Write an essay assessing whether Jain anekāntavāda—specifically its pairing of anekānta (non-absolutism) with syādvāda (conditional, standpoint-relative predication)—can function as a live methodological option for contemporary philosophy of science, rather than merely as a metaphysical doctrine about reality's complexity. Ground your argument in a specific, persistent interdisciplinary conflict: evolutionary biologists account for human cooperation primarily through kin selection and reciprocal altruism, while cultural anthropologists treat it as an emergent property of symbolic ritual systems that cannot be reduced to genetic fitness. Examine whether adopting an anekāntavāda framework—treating each account as conditionally valid (syāt) from its respective naya (standpoint)—actually advances scientific understanding, or whether it collapses into an unproductive pluralism that avoids the hard work of theoretical integration. Address the realist objection that science aims at unified, approximately true theories, not at a collection of perspectival truths, and consider whether anekāntavāda can be modified to accommodate this aim without losing its core insight. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: either that anekāntavāda offers a necessary corrective to scientific imperialism in this domain, or that its adoption would paralyze research by removing the incentive to adjudicate between genuinely competing explanations.
