In my graduate seminar on medieval Islamic metaphysics, I have taken to introducing al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism—especially his denial of necessary causal connection in the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa—by borrowing Malebranche’s clock analogy: just as two clocks strike the hour together not because the gears of one causally move the gears of the other, but because a single clockmaker adjusts both mechanisms to coincide, so fire and the burning of cotton occur together not because fire possesses an intrinsic causal power, but because God directly creates both events in constant conjunction according to His custom. The analogy is meant to do heavy philosophical lifting—if constant conjunction never proves necessary connection in the mechanical case, it should not do so in the natural case, and attributing causal efficacy to created things is as mistaken as attributing time-keeping authority to a gear.

Lately, however, I have been preoccupied with distributed computing—specifically, how nodes in a peer-to-peer network achieve consensus through mechanisms like Lamport logical clocks or Byzantine fault tolerance without any central server pushing state updates. This made me wonder whether the clock analogy quietly smuggles in a centralized ontology that modern coordination theory has shown to be unnecessary. Can you audit the mapping precisely? Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of the clockmaker, the gears, the synchronization protocol, the apparent message-passing between nodes, and the failure mode if one node stops—then identify the disanalogies that actually matter for al-Ghazālī’s argument. In particular, does the fact that distributed nodes genuinely coordinate without a central authority undermine his inference from constant conjunction to a single direct cause? And how does his well-known distinction between knowledge established by demonstrative reason and knowledge available only through habitual expectation fare once we replace the centralized clockmaker with an emergent protocol?

Finally, tell me whether the occasionalist conclusion—that secondary causation is an illusion and only God is the true agent—survives once these fractures are on the table, or whether the clock analogy was carrying the argument all along. If it collapses, is there a better analogy—perhaps drawn from computation or physics—that captures al-Ghazālī’s denial of necessary connection without importing the very centralized ontology his position requires?
