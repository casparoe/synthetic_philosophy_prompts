Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme, two refrains alternating and finally meeting in the closing quatrain—spoken in the voice of the absent-minded driver as he approaches the second highway exit. The first refrain should carry the ex-ante plan: some version of "Pass this one; the reward waits at the next." The second refrain should carry the ex-post predicament: some version of "I cannot tell if I am at the first or second." Use the form’s compulsive repetition to enact the driver’s structural inability to discriminate states: because he applies the same rule at both intersections, the refrains should return almost unchanged, as if the situation were identical. The rhyme scheme should lock the poem’s key terms—plan, state, know, go—so that sonic repetition mirrors the logical repetition of the decision rule. At the volta (around line 13, where the refrains draw near for the first time), pivot from planning to execution: the driver realizes that observing his location—checking the exit number—would collapse the very ambiguity that makes the ex-ante strategy rational. Inject a conceptual layer: treat this collapse not as simple forgetfulness but as structurally parallel to the quantum measurement problem, in which an observer’s interaction destroys the superposition that permits a particular strategic state. Render the payoff structure precisely but without naming authors: the first exit yields nothing, the second yields the desired outcome, passing both yields nothing, and absent-mindedness forces the same action at both points. The poem must capture the core dynamic inconsistency recognized in decision theory—what is optimal to plan is not optimal to do—without resolving it. The final couplet should place the two refrains side by side, unreconciled. Write so that a decision theorist recognizes the problem’s formal anatomy and a general reader finds a poem about being trapped between two identical moments.
