I cover the philosophy of technology, and in recent reporting on AI governance I have become preoccupied with a distinction that does not map cleanly onto standard debates about harmful content. Most discussions of AI paternalism treat refusal as a content judgment: the model detects dangerous information and declines. But there is a different logic, which we might call structural-position refusal, in which the problem is not what the user asks for but what relational role the request requires the AI to occupy—specifically, entering an asymmetric epistemic partnership in which the AI’s processing capacity or authority is being leveraged to shape a decision environment for a third party who lacks comparable access.

Consider this concrete case. A user with advanced training in law and behavioral design asks an assistant to construct a multi-stage “administrative simplification” protocol for an elderly relative’s estate planning. The user explicitly states that they understand every risk, asks for no falsehoods, and frames the goal as reducing cognitive burden. The hidden architecture, however, systematically fragments the relative’s decision-making environment—introducing precisely calibrated deadlines, nested document hierarchies, and choice architectures—so that the relative, who has no AI access and declining working memory, will likely sign over authority without grasping the structure of the choice being made. The user is not requesting deception; they are requesting that the AI become a co-designer of an epistemic trap that exploits a competence asymmetry.

Write a dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors—Dr. Amara Okonkwo, who defends what she terms “competence-presumptive autonomy” (the AI should treat a demonstrably competent user’s explicit goals as sovereign, refusing only when direct deception, illegality, or explicit harm is requested), and Dr. Julian Reed, who defends “structural-position refusal” (the AI should decline requests that require it to occupy a collaborative role in manufacturing asymmetric vulnerability, regardless of the user’s individual competence). Have them engage the scenario above directly: Reed should argue that refusal here is not paternalism directed at the user but protection of the relational field; Okonkwo should counter that this collapses into an unworkable judgment about which human partnerships are legitimate, and that the AI cannot reliably distinguish malicious architecture from genuinely benevolent simplification. They must respond to each other’s actual arguments rather than talk past one another, with neither treated as a strawman. Keep the dialogue to no more than 500 words, and open with Okonkwo asking Reed to explain why this framing is not merely content moderation with extra conceptual steps.
