I serve on our university's Joint Committee on Instructional Technology and Pedagogical Ethics, and we are meeting next Thursday to vote on a proposal from the College of Engineering to pilot an adaptive reinforcement-learning tutoring system—designated Project Archimedes—in mandatory first-year calculus and linear algebra sections. The system revises its pedagogical pathway in real time based on each student's error patterns, which its designers describe as allowing the algorithm to learn from its users rather than merely executing a pre-programmed syllabus. Several engineering faculty members support immediate rollout, arguing that personalization improves retention; however, the Dean of Undergraduate Studies has circulated a dissent warning that treating coursework as an optimization loop for an unorganized machine collapses the relational and moral dimensions of teaching, and that we risk treating students as data sources rather than as learners who deserve a sustained human pedagogical relationship. I need a formal decision memo—no more than two pages—that recommends approval with conditions, rejection, or approval with a strict sunset clause and evaluation protocol, and that could be circulated to the full committee before the vote.

Your memo should draw specifically on Alan Turing's 1950 comparison between educating a human child and training a learning machine, especially his distinction between unorganized machinery—analogous to a child's brain—and organized machinery that already carries an adult program, together with his suggestion that one should educate the former through an appropriate course of instruction rather than attempt to simulate the latter directly. Please treat the Dean's relational objection with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it as anti-technology sentiment; address whether Turing's framework actually licenses deploying such adaptive systems in compulsory general-education courses, or whether it instead implies that human instructors must remain the principal educators who shape the machine's development. Include concrete evaluation criteria—such as metrics for student agency, instructor oversight, and pedagogical transparency—that the committee could apply at a six-month review, and avoid requesting bibliographies, citation lists, or external reading recommendations, since the committee needs an actionable document rather than a scholarly survey.
