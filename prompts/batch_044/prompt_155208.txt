As a novelist researching a narrative about the hidden lives of logicians, I ask you to adopt the role of a senior historian of philosophy—someone equally at home with Frege’s technical apparatus and with the hermeneutics of intellectual biography. Before rendering judgment, please take a moment to brainstorm the criteria that should govern whether a thinker’s private writings bear on the interpretation of their formal work: for instance, chronological independence, authorial intent, structural isomorphism, and the distinction between psychological causation and philosophical availability. Think carefully and move step by step.

The disagreement I observed was between two scholars—let us call them Mara and Elias—who were discussing whether Gottlob Frege’s private political diary, composed near the end of his life in 1924 and containing virulent antisemitic and nationalist sentiments, should alter how we read his logical philosophy.

(1) Mara argued that the diary is not merely biographical gossip but philosophically consequential. She claimed that Frege’s logical notation—the Begriffsschrift and the system of the Grundgesetze—is animated by what she called a “politics of sharp boundaries”: every object either falls inside or outside a concept, ambiguity is expelled, and vagueness is treated as a failure of rational order. She said this formal habit structurally mirrors the diary’s rhetoric about cultural “pollution” and the need to preserve pure demarcations between groups. Even if Frege never intended his logic to serve politics, the same “boundary obsession” shapes both domains, and we should read the formal system as expressing—not causing, but expressing—a worldview in which exclusion is built into the architecture of reason.

(2) Elias replied that this is both a genetic fallacy and an anachronistic overreading. He pointed out that Frege’s major logical works were essentially complete by 1903, decades before the 1924 diary entries, so the chronology alone makes direct philosophical influence implausible. More importantly, Frege’s explicit anti-psychologism in logic forbids deriving norms of reasoning from any thinker’s psychological or political dispositions. The diary reveals an old man’s bitterness and moral failure, but it cannot penetrate the objective validity of logical laws that Frege insisted apply universally to all rational beings, regardless of nationality or origin.

(3) Mara countered that chronology protects causation, not interpretation. She granted that the Grundgesetze predates the diary, but insisted that hermeneutics does not require the author to have intended every structural resonance. The sharp binary of concept-inclusion in Frege’s logic—there is no middle ground, no partial membership—exhibits the same cognitive pattern that organizes his political rants. To ignore this isomorphism is to practice a sanitized history of philosophy that treats formal systems as politically innocent by definition. She added that Frege’s late correspondence shows he never separated his belief in logical universalism from a conviction that certain groups were inherently incapable of grasping it, suggesting the universalist rhetoric and the exclusionary practice were not contradictory but complementary.

(4) Elias responded by defending the principle of charity and the autonomy of argument. He noted that many philosophers with impeccable logical systems held personal prejudices—he mentioned Kant and Hume as parallel cases—without those prejudices invalidating or secretly defining their formal achievements. If Frege’s logic truly aims at objective truths independent of the thinker, then reducing it to a psychological pattern is precisely the psychologism he fought against. The contradiction between universal logic and private racism is a biographical and moral tragedy, not a hidden hermeneutic key. To read the Begriffsschrift as crypto-nationalist formalism, Elias said, violates the text’s own explicit commitments and conflates the conditions of discovery with the conditions of justification.

(5) Mara then refined her position rather than repeating it. She said she was not claiming the logic is “crypto-nationalist” in any causal or intentional sense. Instead, she proposed that formal properties—especially the elimination of ambiguity and the insistence on sharp, exhaustive demarcation—possess what she called a “formal imagination”: they make themselves available for exclusionary applications regardless of the author’s intent. The diary is philosophically important not because it caused the logic, but because it reveals Frege himself applying logical habits of sharp division to human groups. This should make us suspicious of the common defense that formal systems are politically neutral instruments; neutrality, she argued, is not innocence, and the same structure that purifies logic can purify populations.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move. For each of the five moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state clearly which specific points Mara and Elias each win, what—if anything—both parties are missing (for instance, about the relationship between formal structure and ethical use, or about whether anti-psychologism actually blocks the kind of interpretation Mara proposes), and whether there is a coherent middle position that preserves what is valuable in both arguments without collapsing into either genetic fallacy or naive formalism.
