You have been asked to serve as the neutral scribe for an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of science—call them the Principalists and the Calibrationists—who have spent several years arguing past each other about whether rational agents must defer to what they treat as objective chance. The Principalists defend a strict version of the Principal Principle: if an agent knows that the objective chance of some event E is x, then—absent inadmissible evidence—the agent’s credence in E ought to be x. The Calibrationists deny this, arguing that in domains where “objective chance” is not a fundamental physical property but a constructed feature of deterministic yet computationally irreducible systems—specifically, the spread of calibrated climate-ensemble forecasts—the principle either collapses into a tautology or generates contradictions with expert judgment about structural model bias.

Your task is to draft the joint statement they should have produced from the start. The document should open with a numbered section of claims both sides can sign: about the formal structure of the Principal Principle, about what distinguishes admissible from inadmissible evidence, about the behavior of ensemble forecasting in deterministic climate models, and about the normative claim that rational belief should be responsive to evidence rather than arbitrary. Next, present a disagreements table in which each side’s position is stated in wording its own advocates would accept, without caricature: the Principalists should articulate exactly when and why ensemble spread counts as objective chance to which one must defer, and the Calibrationists should articulate exactly why expert knowledge of systematic bias constitutes admissible evidence that breaks the deference link. Then identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect two cruxes are whether determinism at the micro-level undermines the objectivity of macro-level ensemble probabilities, and whether an agent’s awareness of her own calibration record counts as inadmissible self-locating information. For each crux, specify a concrete forecasting scenario or piece of empirical evidence both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts about that case—for instance, whether a forecaster who knows her model systematically overestimates extreme precipitation should still match her credence to the ensemble mean. If any portion of the dispute turns out to be verbal—say, a disagreement about whether “objective chance” requires physical indeterminacy rather than stable macro-regularity—say precisely which portion and why. Do not provide a verdict; end with the residual disagreement exactly framed.
