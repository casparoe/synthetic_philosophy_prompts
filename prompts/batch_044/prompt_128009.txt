I have recently been appointed to a municipal infrastructure review board, and we have been tasked with designing a pilot program for resolving technical disputes about public works through prediction markets rather than through conventional expert testimony. The board operates under tight constraints: we must render recommendations within ninety days, none of us are professional economists or forecasters, and any proposal that involves compulsory acquisition of property or intergenerational costs requires a separate democratic sign-off. I need a reusable decision procedure—something my colleagues and I can run in sequence on any incoming proposal—that translates the core logic of governance by prediction markets into practical steps for a non-specialist body.

The procedure should ask, in a fixed order: how to determine whether the dispute is genuinely about empirical beliefs or disguised value disagreement; what minimal conditions a prediction market must satisfy before its forecast can be treated as advisory rather than binding; where the board must stop and defer to a political process because the resolution depends on distributive judgments; and how to audit for capture by stakeholders with disproportionate capital or expertise. It must include explicit stopping rules—answers that yield an immediate decision not to use the market—and must flag the precise points where judgment cannot be eliminated, especially when the scoring mechanism depends on outcomes observed long after current board members have left office. Please apply the procedure to three cases: a disagreement between hydrologists over which levee design best reduces flood risk over a fifty-year horizon; a proposal to rezone a waterfront district where the technical forecast is uncontested but residents disagree over whether economic growth outweighs community displacement; and a scenario in which the market strongly predicts that a cheap, technically adequate bridge alignment succeeds, but that alignment requires demolishing a registered heritage site that was never priced into the betting conditions. For each case, show where the procedure lands, including the case where its answer conflicts with the board's intuitive preference. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot settle—where it is likely to misfire, and which institutional safeguards it still requires outside its own logic.
