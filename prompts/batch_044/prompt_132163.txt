I work for the strategic foresight unit of an international development institution. Each quarter we review long-range project proposals—governance restructuring, infrastructure lock-in, regional abandonment—that justify major commitments by appealing to laws of history, inevitable civilizational trajectories, or cyclical patterns. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Popper’s critique of historicism, that an analyst with no philosophy background can run in about 45 minutes. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist: the questions to ask at each stage, what answer is sufficient to pass, the precise stopping point where the procedure returns rejected—rests on an illegitimate historicist claim, the stopping point where it returns proceed to value judgment—the historical claim is conditionally sound but the normative choice is open, and every point where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be flagged for senior sign-off. The procedure must operationalize the distinction between trends and laws, test for reflexivity (whether the act of predicting alters the institutional conditions), and check whether the proposal assumes holistic social reconstruction rather than piecemeal intervention, all without requiring access to primary texts. Apply it to three proposals: first, a claim that democratic institutions in a specific region will inevitably decay within two generations according to a law of imperial collapse, used to justify preemptive authoritarian restructuring; second, a claim that societies adapt to climate stress only after catastrophic failure, used to justify delaying resilience investment; third, a genuinely conditional, institutionally bounded demographic projection used to argue for a specific pension reform—where the procedure clears the historical reasoning but I personally oppose the reform on distributive grounds and want that boundary marked. End by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot settle—such as the normative weight of risk aversion or the legitimacy of transformative versus incremental change when both are historically permissible—and identify the characteristic misfire in which analysts confuse no historical law has been established with no useful pattern exists.
