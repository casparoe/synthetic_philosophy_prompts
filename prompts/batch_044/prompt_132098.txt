Exam question:
Distinguish the etiological (selected-effects) account of biological function from the causal-role (system-capacity) account, illustrating each with reference to the mammalian heart. Then consider a bio-engineered synthetic pump implanted in an artificial organism that circulates synthetic blood but has no evolutionary history: would either account assign it a biological function? Finally, discuss how each account treats exaptation and spandrels, and whether either can accommodate functional change without contradiction.

Student answer:
The etiological account, developed by philosophers such as Larry Wright and Ruth Millikan, holds that a trait’s function is the specific effect for which it was naturally selected, so that the heart functions to pump blood because pumping explains why hearts are present in the population today. The causal-role account, defended by Robert Cummins, holds that function is defined purely by a trait’s contribution to some complex system capacity; on this view, the heart functions to pump blood because that contribution sustains the organism’s circulatory capacity, regardless of evolutionary origins.

Applying this to the synthetic pump: since it was never subject to natural selection, the etiological account must deny it has any biological function, whereas the causal-role account grants it a function because it contributes to circulation in the artificial organism.

Regarding exaptation and spandrels, the etiological account easily accommodates exaptation—feathers originally selected for insulation can later be selected for flight, updating their function—while it correctly denies function to spandrels such as the human chin, which were never selected for anything. The causal-role account, however, struggles with spandrels because it requires an evolutionary history to assign functional status; without selection, Cummins’s view cannot determine whether the chin contributes to a capacity, leaving spandrels functionally undefined. By contrast, exaptation is problematic for the causal-role account, since any change in contribution immediately changes the function, making historical continuity impossible.
