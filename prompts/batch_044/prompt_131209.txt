I am preparing a session for my graduate seminar on collective rationality and institutional failure, and I want to use Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove to examine whether multipolar traps—those competitive dynamics in which rational local optimization produces globally catastrophic outcomes—are best understood as failures of agency or as failures of institutional architecture. Rather than reading the film as a simple prisoner's dilemma between superpowers, I want you to treat it as a study in second-order commitment failure: the Doomsday Machine is designed to solve the credibility problem of retaliation by automating it, yet by removing human deliberation from the escalation pathway it transforms a strategic race into an irreversible structural trap. Please read the film through the lens of Moloch dynamics and races to the bottom, focusing specifically on how Kubrick distinguishes, if at all, between first-order multipolar competition (the arms race itself) and second-order institutional lock-in (bureaucratic protocol, automated response, and the logic of credible commitment that no single actor can revoke). Consider the war-room negotiations, the revelation of the Doomsday Machine, and Major Kong's enthusiastic ride on the bomb. Does the satire present General Ripper's unilateral action as an aberration that disrupts an otherwise stable equilibrium, or does it suggest that the institutional design—deterrence logic included—already encodes the catastrophic trajectory? Be precise about where the philosophical framework illuminates the narrative and where the film resists reduction to coordination-game analysis: for instance, does Kong's exuberance represent rational agent behavior under perverse incentives, or does it introduce an element of human agency—perhaps even moral complicity—that a purely structural account of multipolar traps cannot capture? Finally, address whether the ending, with survivors sealed in the mine shaft, offers any normative exit from the trap or merely confirms that once competitive dynamics reach their structural limit, survival becomes the only remaining variable. What does Kubrick's vision add to the philosophical analysis of Moloch that analytical argument alone does not?
