According to the summary, empirical studies show that liberals activate mainly care and fairness while treating the other three as optional or suspect, whereas conservatives draw on all five in roughly balanced measure. The essay then argues that this is not a neutral cultural difference but a cognitive deficit on the liberal side: liberal moral philosophy, from Rawlsian liberalism to contemporary cosmopolitanism, systematically suppresses loyalty, authority, and purity concerns, yielding theories that are descriptively impoverished and motivationally unstable because they require adherents to rationalize away intuitions that remain active in ordinary moral cognition. By contrast, conservative ethics integrates the full palette and achieves what the author calls a complete reflective equilibrium that liberals can only approximate through suppression. From this psychological asymmetry, the essay concludes that conservative moral outlooks are closer to the actual architecture of human morality, and that liberal objections to nationalist loyalty, traditional authority, or purity-based norms are therefore not merely mistaken but cognitively maladaptive—normative positions that fight evolved moral cognition and are destined to fail in practice.
