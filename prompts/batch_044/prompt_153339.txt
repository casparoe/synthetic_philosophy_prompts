In my graduate seminar on moral responsibility we have spent two full terms on Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities, and the discussion has stalled in a way that feels structurally frozen rather than merely hard. Every participant accepts the canonical case architecture: an agent deliberates and acts for her own reasons, a hidden counterfactual intervener stands ready to compel the very same act should she show any sign of wavering, she never in fact wavers, and we still intuitively hold her responsible. The defenders of the Frankfurt strategy take this to show that responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise, since all robust alternatives have been eliminated. The defenders of PAP reply with the flicker-of-freedom objection: there remains a minimal alternative—the mental onset of wavering, or the failure to trigger the mechanism—which is enough to preserve PAP if one distinguishes the modal robustness required for responsibility from mere causal possibility. After months, both sides agree on the modal logic of the example, agree that the agent is responsible, and agree on the taxonomy of objections; yet no participant has revised a single commitment.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden disagreement about which modality—causal, logical, or agential—counts as the “relevant” one for assessing alternatives, with each side silently precisifying “could have done otherwise” in incompatible ways? Or are the sides answering different questions under one name, one offering a conceptual analysis of responsibility and the other offering an account of deserved blame? Could a formal result about the closure conditions of responsibility-under-intervention move things, or is the real work a distinction—say, between structural alternatives (paths the world could take) and agential alternatives (exercises of the agent’s own power)—that neither side has made explicit? Alternatively, is the stall a sign that the question is ill-posed, because “alternative possibility” is being used with precisifications that pull in opposite directions once embedded in a theory of blame? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a change in the framing of the question (for instance, asking not whether alternatives are necessary but what explanatory work they do), an argument that this is a bedrock intuition clash that should not be expected to resolve, or something else? And if you think the debate should not be expected to move, say exactly why the apparent convergence on cases and logic is deceptive.
