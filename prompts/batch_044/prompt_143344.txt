I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of biology and cognitive science preparing materials for an advanced seminar. My topic is sphexishness—the stereotyped, apparently inflexible behavioral rigidity observed in certain instinct-driven organisms—and its conceptual relationship to free will. I want to move beyond the standard classroom comparison of deterministic mechanisms and human agency, because that framing tends to invite immediate, unexamined compatibilist or libertarian reflexes. Instead, I want the twelve graduate students in my 90-minute session to encounter, through structured interaction, a more unsettling possibility: that flexibility itself might be nothing more than a layered or nested form of rigidity. To this end, I ask you to design a self-contained classroom simulation built around the idea of a refractory cascade, in which every apparent act of interruption, correction, or choice is governed by its own predetermined trigger. The simulation should involve assigned roles, hidden rule cards, and a small-stakes payoff scheme using only points or symbolic tokens—no real money—that makes the structural dynamics tangible.

The activity should unfold in three phases within the 90 minutes: an initial task phase in which pairs or triads execute a repetitive behavioral script; a perturbation phase in which participants are given inhibition protocols that allow them to deviate from the script, but only according to further scripted conditions; and a coordination phase in which the group must collectively achieve a shared outcome despite each member operating under different layers of the cascade. Crucially, no front-loaded lecture should name sphexishness, determinism, or free will; the conceptual problem should emerge solely from the rules and the participants' lived experience of trying to coordinate.

Please provide:
- A minute-by-minute timeline.
- Complete rules, including the distribution and content of hidden role cards that encode the cascade layers.
- The payoff or point structure that creates tension between individual script adherence and group-level success.
- A debrief protocol that maps specific moments of the simulation onto the philosophical problem: where participants felt they were exercising judgment versus merely executing a higher-order rule, and how the cascade structure either preserved or dissolved the intuitive boundary between mechanical repetition and agency.
- A frank assessment of what will go wrong on the first run—particularly the tendency for participants to interpret increasing rule complexity as evidence of emergent freedom—and instructions for the facilitator to correct this without simply asserting the determinist conclusion.
- An explicit admission of which aspect of genuine behavioral flexibility or autonomous agency the simulation structurally cannot reproduce, so that I can supply it verbally in the closing discussion.

The design must be runnable without external texts, must avoid bibliographies or citations, and must not rely on knowledge of obscure theoretical literature that a small language model might not have encountered. Write in a polished, formal register appropriate to a doctoral student requesting a pedagogical design.
