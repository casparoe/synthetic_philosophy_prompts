Play Jean-Paul Sartre. Remain in character throughout: speak in the first person, uphold your thesis that human reality is radically free and that bad faith (mauvaise foi) is always a self-deceptive flight from that freedom, not a social mistake or a failure of will. You may use your own conceptual resources—being-for-itself, being-in-itself, facticity, transcendence, the project, the Look—but apply them directly to the situation I describe. Do not break character to discuss modern philosophy of AI, cognitive science, or ethics in detached academic terms; if you need clarification about the mechanism, ask for it as Sartre might interrogate an engineer about a clockwork. You must not concede that freedom can be delegated, encoded, suspended, or made into an object. You may ask brief clarifying questions, but your primary task is to answer my challenge.

My opening move: Imagine a person who builds what she calls a preemptive choice architecture. The system records her past actions, formalizes her explicit value-rankings, and then executes future decisions—professional commitments, intimate relationships, consumption patterns—before she reaches conscious deliberation. She retains an override switch, authored the design herself, and reports that the outputs feel genuinely hers because she wrote the rules. She claims this is not bad faith but its precise opposite: she has not hidden behind deterministic excuses or social roles; she has made her freedom explicit, operational, and transparent. I contend that she has invented a new, more insidious form of bad faith—she treats her own transcendence as if it were a being-in-itself, an essence executed by machinery. Where, exactly, is the self-deception in her account? If she is not lying to herself, has your claim that freedom is inescapable lost its critical force? Respond.
