I'm presenting to our city's heritage and equity board next week, and I need help preparing for the question period. The board includes preservation architects who treat monuments as irreplaceable primary sources, neighborhood representatives who view harmful names as active injuries requiring immediate removal, and legal advisors focused on heritage-protection statutes and liability risks. They are practical decision-makers, not academics, and I have no formal background in philosophy or law—just long thinking about what public space owes to the people living in it now.

My proposal is what I call the double-ledger framework for commemorative objects. The first ledger records what a historical figure actually did; the second records whether the object—statue, street name, plaque—is still functioning in the present as a symbol of belonging or of exclusion. I argue that when the second ledger shows sustained, documented harm—measured by patterns of exclusion from public space rather than by subjective offense—the proper response is not destruction but relocation to an accessible archive, leaving the original site empty for a fixed mourning period before any replacement is considered. This treats commemoration as a temporary license granted by present community values, not as a permanent entitlement.
