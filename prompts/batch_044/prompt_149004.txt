I'm working through a problem in the metaphysics of dependence, and I keep returning to the tension between the formal properties usually assigned to grounding—irreflexivity, asymmetry, and transitivity, which together imply a well-founded hierarchy terminating in basic entities—and the appearance of circular or mutual dependence in complex organized systems. To probe this without simply invoking familiar puzzles like the statue and the clay, I've constructed a more specific case: a distributed digital protocol with no central server, in which each node is constituted as a functional unit only by its relational position within the network, while the network as a macro-entity exists only through the actual interactions of those nodes. One might try to dissolve the apparent loop by distinguishing material grounding, in which the physical nodes exist independently, from functional grounding, in which the nodes possess their specific roles. But this raises a deeper question: if functional grounding is genuinely a form of metaphysical dependence rather than mere causal or conceptual determination, does introducing a second dimension of grounding allow for a coherent form of asymmetric co-dependence—where the nodes materially ground the network and the network functionally grounds the nodes—without violating the prohibition on circularity? Or does the well-foundedness requirement force us to treat one dimension as ontologically prior, thereby rendering the other derivative and second-class? I'm looking for an intuitive but conceptually precise explanation of whether such cross-dimensional grounding relations are coherent, and whether they genuinely threaten the standard tree-like model of fundamentality, or whether they can be accommodated by refining our taxonomy of dependence relations rather than abandoning hierarchical well-foundedness altogether.
