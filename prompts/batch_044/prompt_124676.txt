Write the full specification—desiderata, motivating cases, and negotiability flags—for a theory of free-floating rationales: normative reasons that hold irrespective of any actual reasoner’s mental states or recognition. The theory must account for reasons in contexts where no agent is present to grasp them (e.g., ecological carrying capacities that constrain population dynamics, or decision architectures in artificial systems lacking phenomenal states), without collapsing normative force into descriptive regularity. For each requirement, state the case behind it: must the theory preserve the distinction between reasons-for-action and reasons-to-be; must it accommodate reasons that no being could ever act upon; must it explain why a reason exerts normative pull in the absence of a subject to be pulled; and which distinctions—such as that between justification and motivation, or between having status and bearing weight—must it avoid collapsing? Flag which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect camp-specific prejudices (psychologistic, constructivist, or reductive naturalist). Then briefly assess how Scanlon-style reasons fundamentalism, Korsgaard’s constructivist account, and a “structural-relational” view that treats reasons as normative relations between states of affairs fare against the list. Conclude by saying what would change your mind: identify the specific argument or finding—such as a complete reduction of normative vocabulary to evolutionary game-theoretic equilibrium conditions—that would force you to abandon or weaken a desideratum, and point to where your reasoning is most vulnerable, especially around the boundary between descriptive structure and genuinely normative authority.
