I am a high school student preparing to present at our school's philosophy seminar—attended mainly by upperclassmen who have worked through Davidson on action explanation and some Anscombe on intention, plus a faculty advisor committed to phenomenological accounts of agency. I will defend the view that the absent-minded driver problem is not a genuine puzzle about missing intention, but rather an artifact of assuming that intentional action requires occurrent, reportable awareness at every moment. Specifically, I argue that autopilot driving displays a form of embodied, distributed motor intentionality in which awareness is structurally embedded in habitual bodily engagement rather than absent; the "problem" only arises if we implicitly accept a Cartesian snapshot model of the mind. Please prepare me for the objections this particular audience will raise, ordered from the most damaging to the least: the Davidsonian worry that I have collapsed the distinction between action and mere behavior, the phenomenologist's charge that I am substituting an impersonal "system" for lived experience, and the question—likely from someone who just read Anscombe—about whether my view can preserve the direction of fit that distinguishes practical reasoning from causal explanation. For each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary that audience member would actually use, provide a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds, and note precisely where that reply is weakest. Also include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—probably about whether my account makes reckless or harmful driving count as intentional. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in this preparation and single out the point about which you are least certain.
