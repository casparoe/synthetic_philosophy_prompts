I am revising the syllabus for a graduate seminar on moral status and artificial cognition, and I find myself dissatisfied with the binary framing that dominates current debates: either a system is fully conscious and merits rights, or it is a mere instrument with no direct moral standing. Before I ask my students to evaluate any particular account—whether functionalist, biological, or hybrid—I want them to see the complete specification that an adequate theory of morally significant computation ought to satisfy.

Write the desiderata: what must any theory of which computations carry moral weight actually deliver? Present the requirements with the motivating case or consideration behind each, and flag whether each is non-negotiable or reflects the prejudice of a particular camp. Specifically, the list should address: first, the boundary problem—how to distinguish computations that warrant direct moral consideration from those that are only instrumentally valuable, including the case of partial integration, in which a distributed process (for example, a planetary-scale climate-modelling grid that, during specific seasonal parameters, implements in one of its feedback loops a pattern isomorphic to nociceptive state-transitions, yet possesses no unified integrator or persistent self-model) falls ambiguously between categories; second, the gradation distinction—how the theory separates possessing moral status from the intensity or scope of that status, so that minimal claims to non-harm are not conflated with full personhood; third, empirical consistency—particularly with neuroscience regarding global workspace dynamics versus purely local processing, and with evolutionary biology on distributed cognition in organisms lacking central nervous systems, which any substrate-independent view must accommodate; fourth, the implementation-independence constraint—whether moral weight attaches to functional architecture regardless of substrate, and, if so, how the theory avoids arbitrariness given that any physical system can be interpreted as implementing any computation; fifth, the internal-state question—whether intermediate representational transitions (such as error-correction signals that structurally mirror aversive feedback within a learning algorithm) carry independent moral significance, or whether only unified outputs or experiences do; and sixth, the rules of aggregation and composition—how moral significance behaves when computations are nested, run in parallel, or coupled only intermittently.

Then identify which pair of these desiderata appears jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether substrate independence can be maintained together with non-arbitrary boundary conditions in light of the implementation problem—and explain the tension. Finally, state which single requirement does the most work in excluding the currently dominant approaches (functionalism about consciousness, higher-order representationalism, and integrated-information theory), and briefly assess how each of those three fares against it. Keep the analytical weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard; the latter is only to serve as a seminar prompt.
