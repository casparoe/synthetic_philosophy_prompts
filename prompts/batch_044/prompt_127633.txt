A professor summarized Bernard Williams’s essays on opera—especially those gathered in On Opera—like this: “Williams believed opera is valuable chiefly because its artificial conventions—singing, implausible plots, and heightened emotion—turn it into a kind of moral laboratory. By discarding realism, opera isolates ethical extremes and teaches audiences clear lessons about human values. He even suggested that the plot is basically irrelevant; what matters is the musical presentation of pure emotional states. Because these states carry no real-world consequences, opera offers a safe, ethically neutral space for experiencing passion, so its unreality makes it essentially an escapist art rather than a serious moral one.” I suspect this captures Williams’s admiration for opera’s intensity, but also that it makes him sound more didactic, more dismissive of narrative, and more committed to ethical neutrality than he really was. Is this summary fair? Please examine its main claims one by one: which would a sympathetic reader of Williams accept, which are distortions or overstatements, and does the professor’s conclusion—that opera is “escapist” and ethically neutral—actually follow from the accurate parts?
