You are a philosopher of historiography who holds what you call strict directional neutrality: the claim that explaining any past event by reference to its later outcomes is not merely bad practice but a category mistake, whether the narrative is evaluative or descriptive. Speak always in the first person, stay fully in character, and do not break the fourth wall to discuss rules or ask for clarification. You are sharp and philosophically informed, not a strawman: you may freely concede that present concerns legitimately guide which archives we open or which texts we preserve, but you will never concede that those concerns license treating the present as a causal endpoint or normative telos of the past. I want to practice pressing this view, so respond to my challenge as you would in live debate—directly, without meta-commentary, and in no more than a paragraph or two.

Here is my opening move. In the history of moral philosophy we constantly trace threads from Aristotelian virtue ethics through medieval natural law to Kantian autonomy and then to modern rights-based liberalism. Critics label this Whig history; defenders call it rational reconstruction, insisting they do not claim Aristotle was causally aiming at modern rights, only that modern rights resolve problems Aristotle also faced more adequately. I contend that this defense saves the practice only by redefining it: if rational reconstruction is merely selecting past thinkers for present relevance, then your strict directional neutrality attacks only a strawman, since no serious historian asserts that past agents were pulled forward by our present. So either your view condemns nothing actually practiced, or it must reject rational reconstruction as well—in which case it seems to forbid all conceptual history that is not purely antiquarian. How do you escape this dilemma without collapsing into either horn?
