I was arguing with my roommate about Hofstadter's careenium example — you know, the billiard-ball universe where high-level patterns are called simms and the particles are simmballs — and he tried to show that if symbols don't literally push substrate around then mental causation collapses, but then he adds this twist about "semantic drag" that makes the conclusion go haywire. Here's his chain, and I really can't tell where the leak is.

(1) In the careenium only the individual simmballs have direct physical causal power; any simm is just an observer-dependent pattern over them and doesn't itself push anything. (2) If patterns are observer-dependent and physically inert, then describing a system at the simm-level gives no real causal explaination of what the simmballs actually do. (3) Our mental states — beliefs, intentions, the whole lot — are patterns over neural substrate, structurally just like simms over simmballs. (4) So mental states are physically inert and can't be the real cause of bodily movement. (5) But clearly my belief that I should drink coffee causes me to reach for the cup; if mental states were inert that reaching wouldn't happen. (6) Therefore mental states must have downward causal efficacy over neurons. (7) Now here's his twist: downward causation only works if the semantic content of the pattern — not just its physical shape — exerts a kind of semantic drag on the substrate, pulling simmballs back into alignment with what the pattern means. (8) If semantic drag exists in brains, then any substrate that realizes meaningful patterns should show it too. (9) So in a computer running a chess engine, the concept of checkmate literally exerts drag on the transistors, keeping them in states that represent the board correctly rather than drifting randomly. (10) But that's absurd — transistors don't care about chess, and if semantic drag were real we'd see physical anomalies in chips running symbolic programs that we never observe. (11) So either downward causation doesn't exist and (6) is an illusion, or semantic drag is real everywhere and physics is wrong, or one of the earlier steps is bad.

He says he wants to keep (6) and reject (11)'s absurdity without buying semantic drag in computer chips, but I feel like the slide from (7) to (8) is where the nonsense occured. Which premise do I actually have to give up if I want mental causation without letting symbols shove transistors around? And what does rejecting that premise cost me — do I end up with epiphenomenalism, or with some weird restriction on downward causation?
