I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I recently found myself in a discussion about whether complex systems can be fully captured by listing their parts and the pairwise links between them. A colleague kept invoking a compressed version of Bradley’s worry about relations: “If relations are real, they have to be related too, and that never stops.” He used the line to dismiss any reductionist account—whether a database schema, a dependency graph, or an organizational chart—arguing that such representations always leave out the “togetherness” of the whole, since any binding mechanism you add becomes just another item that itself needs binding.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses hiding inside that one-liner? For each reading, I want to know whether it is a logical truism about how language works, a substantive metaphysical claim about relations and their relata, a thesis about the limits of formal representation, or simply false—and whether it genuinely supports the conclusion that a component-plus-link model must fail to capture unity. In particular, I am interested in how these readings map onto a concrete engineering distinction: in software, one can treat binding either as an explicit “meta-object” in the data model (which seems to invite an infinite hierarchy of binders) or as part of the formal syntax that is not itself an element within the system. Which philosophical reading corresponds to each design choice, and which one is actually being invoked when someone uses the slogan to end an argument against reductionist descriptions of systems?
