I am preparing a graduate seminar on the normative architecture of moral emotions, and I would like you to compose a formal dialogue for it. The exchange should take place between two equally competent philosophers—Dr. Althea Voss and Dr. Kiran Mehta—who disagree about whether guilt or shame is the structurally superior moral emotion, and about whether a culture can deliberately choose its dominant emotional regime.

Voss should defend guilt on the grounds that it permits autonomous moral accounting: it operates without an essential audience, allows for private reparation, and preserves the agent’s capacity for self-correction independent of social surveillance. Mehta should defend shame on the grounds that it binds the agent to a relational normative web, making violations visible to the community and thereby enabling forms of collective repair and social reintegration that private guilt cannot achieve. Crucially, neither should talk past the other: Voss must respond directly to Mehta’s claim that guilt risks solipsistic moral bookkeeping, and Mehta must respond directly to Voss’s worry that shame collapses moral agency into mere social performance.

To keep the discussion somewhat in the weeds and away from well-worn summaries, have them invoke the niche framework of affective constitutionalism—the thesis that emotional regimes can be institutionally designed much as political constitutions are engineered—debating whether a society can deliberately shift from a guilt-based to a shame-based moral economy, or whether such regimes emerge organically from deeper social ontologies that resist top-down selection. They should also examine a specific hypothetical: a culture that institutes transparent accountability rituals intended to convert private guilt into structured public shame. They must ask whether this represents an improvement in moral coherence or a dangerous erosion of the private moral sphere. The dialogue should be polished, precise, and should conclude without resolving the tension, leaving the question of cultural choice genuinely open. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts.
