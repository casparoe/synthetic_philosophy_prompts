I have noticed a recurring pattern in arguments about scientific change that I cannot map neatly onto the standard taxonomy, and I want to know whether philosophy of science recognizes it as a distinct phenomenon rather than just an instance of something broader. Consider cases analogous to the transition from a caloric theory of heat to a kinetic one, where defenders of an older framework do not reject the rival's measurements but instead show that every quantitative result the new theory produces can also be generated within the old framework by adding auxiliary hypotheses—perhaps allowing the hypothetical substance to take on distribution properties that exactly mirror particle velocities. They then claim that the rival offers no genuinely new ontology, only a notational variant or computational shortcut, since every claim in the new vocabulary can be translated back into the old without predictive loss. The rival community replies that these translations require contrived, independently unmotivated adjustments, and that the old framework misses genuine explanatory unifications—such as linking thermal phenomena directly to mechanical work—that the new view achieves naturally. What is striking is that both sides understand each other's language and agree on the observational outcomes; there is no mutual incomprehension in the Kuhnian sense. Yet one side uses this very translatability to deny that an incommensurable paradigm shift has occurred. Is there an established term for this specific configuration—predictive equivalence manufactured through ad hoc domesticating rules and then wielded to resist claims of theoretical progress? I am not satisfied with a bare name; I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, how its edges are drawn, what distinguishes it from ordinary empirical underdetermination and from successful theoretical reduction, and whether it represents a genuine failure mode or boundary case for Kuhn's account of incommensurability.
