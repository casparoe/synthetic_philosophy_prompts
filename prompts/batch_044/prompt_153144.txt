I'm writing a response paper for my philosophy of language seminar on linguistic relativity, and I want to focus on Denis Villeneuve's 2016 film Arrival, based on Ted Chiang's novella Story of Your Life. The narrative turns on linguist Louise Banks learning Heptapod B and thereby experiencing time non-linearly, so that past, present, and future feel simultaneously present. I want to read this through the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis with precision: Whorf's actual claims about Hopi temporal concepts are routinely caricatured, and the film seems to slide from language shaping cognition to language revealing the true metaphysical structure of time—almost a form of eternalism or block-universe determinism that Whorf never defended.

Does Arrival dramatize strong linguistic determinism, weak linguistic relativity, or a metaphysical thesis about time that merely borrows Whorfian vocabulary? Which scenes—the circular heptapod logograms, Louise's visions of her daughter, the negotiation with General Shang—best illustrate this slide, and where does the narrative resist being read purely as a case study in linguistic influence? I'm particularly interested in whether the film distinguishes epistemic restructuring from ontological revelation, or whether it deliberately conflates them for dramatic effect. I'd like a reading that takes both the philosophical debate and the film's narrative logic seriously, and that says what the work adds to the discussion that a standard argument about Hopi tense markers does not capture.
