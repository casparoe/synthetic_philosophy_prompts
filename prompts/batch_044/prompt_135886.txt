I'm delivering the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "Choice Without Preference," hosted jointly by a philosophy department and an autonomous-systems laboratory. About half the room designs decision algorithms for robotics and recommendation engines; the other half are phenomenologists and applied ethicists who rarely work with formal utility representations. Our organizing problem is Buridan's ass — the agent suspended between two identically valued options — but we are treating it as a live structural issue rather than a medieval curiosity. I need roughly ten minutes that frame the day's discussions by using the paradox to block two default resolutions: the engineering assumption that any apparent tie merely reflects hidden preferences we have not measured precisely enough, and the humanistic assumption that authentic choice always transcends comparison through some irreducible act of freedom.

I also want to seed a less conventional framing — that certain choices are constituted by prior commitment in a way that renders comparative evaluation moot — not to settle the debate but to show both groups that their usual exits from the paradox miss something. The remarks must be fully accessible to graduate students and non-specialist visitors without simplifying the tension for the specialists, and they should close by posing a single shared question that both algorithm designers and ethicists can recognize as their own. Please write these remarks so that the paradox shapes what I can and cannot say about rational agency, rather than serving as decorative background.
