In a conversation I observed between a historian of religion and a labor lawyer—both precise, neither a strawman—the dispute turned on whether anyone genuinely holds a right to work and leisure in a culture still shadowed by the Protestant work ethic. The historian opened by arguing that the ethic was never about maximizing labor hours but about vocation, a form of spiritual leisure disguised as duty; consequently, the 35-hour week is not an emancipation but a category error, treating work as a quantifiable burden rather than an integrated calling. The lawyer replied that this romanticizes a doctrine that, in its Japanese corporate form, produces karoshi—death by overwork—proving the ethic is structurally lethal, and that the 35-hour week is a minimal negative right against such compulsion. The historian conceded that karoshi is real but insisted it arises from the corruption of vocation into productivity, not from the ethic itself; true calling includes rest, so a state-imposed temporal limit is paternalistic, substituting bureaucratic leisure for authentic integration. The lawyer answered that this distinction lacks practical weight: if an idea consistently generates deadly outcomes under real power asymmetries, defending it as corrupted abandons the workers who suffer, and without the 35-hour week there is no enforceable boundary to prevent exploitation from consuming life entirely.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move—rating each turn from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying the rating—then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing about what a right to leisure or work actually requires.
