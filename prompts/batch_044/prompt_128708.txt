In my democratic theory seminar, defenders of sortition routinely invoke jury duty as the controlling analogy: we already accept that randomly selected citizens can weigh evidence, deliberate under guidance, and render binding judgments on matters of life and liberty, so there is no principled barrier to letting a larger random sample legislate. More recently, some proponents have recast this defense as an epistemic lottery, comparing the random selection of legislators not to a jury pool but to randomization in clinical trials—valued because it breaks selection bias rather than because it yields a statistically representative microcosm of the population. Opponents reply that legislation lacks the adversarial structure, judicial framing, discrete endpoint, and temporary mandate that make jury service workable, and that randomization solves the wrong problem if the task requires sustained policy competence rather than unbiased allocation.

Please audit both analogies together. Lay out a correspondence table for each: what plays the role of the defendant, the judge's instructions, the null hypothesis, the treatment and control groups, the endpoint, and the statistical power calculation; then do the same for the jury model (the voir dire screening, the evidentiary rules, the verdict, the single-case finality, the appeal structure). Identify precisely where each mapping fractures—particularly regarding continuous governance versus discrete judgment, the absence of adversarial testing in lawmaking, whether randomization addresses representativeness or competence, and what happens to accountability when there is no judge and no trial record. Specify which features of the source domains—adversarial procedure, judicial oversight, trial finality, bias-breaking—are quietly imported into the defense of lottocracy and whether legislatures can possess analogues of them. Finally, assess whether either analogy can genuinely support the conclusion that sortition is both democratically legitimate and epistemically sound for ongoing governance, or whether the conclusion depends on imports that disappear once the disanalogies are made explicit, leaving the defense unsupported. If the analogies fail, suggest what a more accurate comparison—perhaps to delegation, to statistical sampling for policy input, or to another institutional form—would need to include to do honest work.
