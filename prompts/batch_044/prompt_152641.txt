Having recently become the primary caregiver for a parent whose chronic neurodegenerative illness involves both severe physical pain and intermittent emotional anguish, I have found myself preoccupied with proposals for the biotechnological abolition of suffering—sometimes discussed under the heading of the hedonistic imperative. Rather than asking for a survey of existing literature, I want you to help me think through a specific conceptual tension by constructing an original example. Give an example of a conceptual issue in which a society’s successful implementation of partial affective calibration—genetically lowering citizens’ negative affective baselines without eliminating cognitive complexity—creates a problem of moral recognition between the calibrated majority and a small, unmodified minority who still experience acute suffering. Your example should illustrate how the engineered absence of distress might obscure, rather than fulfill, obligations of beneficence toward those left outside the biotechnological program, and it should avoid relying on familiar tropes such as the experience machine or simple utilitarian calculus. Frame the scenario with enough concrete detail that the conceptual issue—concerning whether the elimination of suffering can simultaneously erode the intersubjective conditions required to recognize suffering in others—becomes sharply visible.
