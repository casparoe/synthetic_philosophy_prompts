Draft the joint statement that Daniel Dennett, defending a compatibilist and evolutionary-functional reconstruction of moral responsibility, and Gregg Caruso, advancing free-will skepticism and the rejection of retributive punishment, could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration focused specifically on whether any coherent notion of "just desert" can survive the elimination of basic-desert moral responsibility. I want the collaboration to move beyond generic free-will framing and concentrate on a sharper conceptual question: whether a forward-looking, risk-management or public-health model of punishment can genuinely retain desert-language—such as "the offender deserves this specific burden"—without collapsing into hidden retributivism on one side or into pure utilitarian cost-allocation on the other. Frame the inquiry around whether "naturalized desert" can be distinguished conceptually from "expected social cost," and whether the well-known asymmetry between praise and blame creates an instability for any penal system that tries to preserve selective desert-based sanctions while denying backward-looking moral fault.

The deliverable should be formatted as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims that both parties can endorse: about the empirical conditions under which punishment modifies behavior; about the moral and legal constraints that rule out cruelty, arbitrariness, and discrimination in sentencing; about the determinist or near-determinist picture of human action being at least seriously plausible; and about the shared rejection of any penal theory that treats suffering as intrinsically good. Second, a disagreements table in which each camp's position is phrased in wording that camp would accept for itself—specifically addressing whether evolutionary-functional explanations of blame can ground genuine normative desert-claims, whether the asymmetry between praise and blame undermines the coherence of desert-based punishment, and whether moral luck in sentencing (e.g., social determinants of crime) can be eliminated without eliminating desert itself. Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, such as whether desert is essentially backward-looking or can be fully reconstructed in predictive terms, and whether a system that assigns burdens solely by risk algorithms can still meaningfully say that anyone "deserves" those burdens. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it—for example, a hypothetical penal code that assigns sentences purely by predictive-risk algorithms with no backward-looking component, or a scenario in which two offenders with identical risk profiles but different histories of social deprivation receive identical sanctions—and record in advance what each side predicts that case shows about the possibility or impossibility of non-retributive desert.
