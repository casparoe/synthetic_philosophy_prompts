I have been working through Moore's proof of an external world for a graduate seminar on anti-skeptical strategies, and I keep encountering a particular comparison in our discussion group and in some of the secondary literature: Moore's gesture of holding up his hands is treated as analogous to an ostensive definition in the manner of Wittgenstein's language-game examples. The idea is that just as one teaches the use of a color-word by presenting the sample directly rather than describing it, Moore presents an external object directly rather than arguing for its existence from premises that the skeptic might reject. Proponents of this reading use the analogy to defend Moore against the charge that he begs the question against the idealist—he is not reasoning in a circle, they say, but exhibiting the very thing that is in dispute, much as one exhibits red rather than defining it. I want to audit this comparison with some precision.

Can you lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what plays the role of the teacher, the learner, the sample, the linguistic rule being taught, and the conditions of failure or misunderstanding in each case? Then identify exactly where the mapping breaks down: in particular, whether an ostensive definition can be false or unsound in the way a proof can, whether the skeptic's denial functions like a learner's misunderstanding of a word or like a rejection of a demonstration altogether, and whether the analogy quietly imports an assumption about the transparency of perceptual presentation that the skeptic precisely denies. Finally, tell me whether the analogy actually supports Moore's defense or whether it was carrying the conclusion all along, and if it collapses at a critical point, what a more apt comparison would be for what Moore is actually doing when he holds up his hand.
