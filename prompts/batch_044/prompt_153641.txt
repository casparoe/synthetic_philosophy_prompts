Read the film Groundhog Day (1993) through the lens of absurdism and contemporary hybrid accounts of meaning in life — specifically the problem of value persistence without causal trace. Phil Connors lives the same day on repeat; every action is erased by midnight except in his memory, decoupling subjective fulfillment from any lasting impact on the world. Track him through three phases — hedonistic exploitation, nihilistic despair, and the turn to mastery and altruism — and ask, at each stage, whether a hybrid theory (meaning requires both subjective engagement and objective worth) can be satisfied when there is no audience, no legacy, and no altered future state. Does his final loop-day, in which he saves lives and learns skills without expectation of external reward, show that objective value can be self-certifying inside a closed system, or does the film imply that meaning requires an open temporal horizon, as suggested by the fact that the loop breaks only after he achieves romantic union? Identify where the narrative resists the philosophical framing — for instance, whether its comedic structure and absence of other conscious loop-participants distort the conditions for genuine value — and conclude by stating one claim about temporal openness and meaning that the film dramatizes but that abstract argument alone tends to miss. This is for an essay in a philosophy of life seminar, so the reading should take both the film's mechanics and the hybrid theory seriously, without reducing either to an illustration.
