I am a novelist presenting at a symposium on narratives of defeat, and I need a prep sheet for the Q&A. My thesis is that the literary afterlives of four so-called lost causes—the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk—share a hidden narrative architecture I term counterfactual loyalty: protagonists are rendered virtuous not by victory but by an imagined refusal to surrender even when the historical record shows rational capitulation or serious internal dissent. I claim this architecture requires suppressing documented internal fractures—Confederate desertions, Masada’s suicide debates, Kosovo’s pragmatic negotiations, Dunkirk’s chaotic evacuation orders—to manufacture a false unanimity of will that serves present identity politics. The audience is a joint panel of comparative literature scholars, a Balkan historian skeptical of cross-case myth-generalization, a Southern-studies specialist in Lost Cause rhetoric, and a philosopher of history who suspects counterfactual loyalty is merely counterfactual history with a moral gloss. Give me the objections in the order they will likely come, phrased as each interlocutor would phrase them, with for each: what I should concede, what I should hold, the weakest point in my reply, and how to defend that weakness in the time available. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—specifically the challenge that my framework collapses all four cases into an identical template despite their radically different relationships to actual defeat, strategic retreat, and collective survival. If my framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if treating Dunkirk as a lost cause rather than a strategic retreat misapplies my own category, or if counterfactual loyalty is not distinguishable from ordinary tragic heroism—say so plainly instead of accommodating me politely.
