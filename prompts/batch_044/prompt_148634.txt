I'm currently taking an advanced metaphysics seminar on identity and individuation, and I'm trying to determine my own reflective equilibrium on the identity of indiscernibles after working through Black's sphere argument and its standard quantum-mechanical responses. Rather than asking for a literature survey, I want to test whether my case-judgments converge on a coherent principle.

Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: Black's original scenario—two perfectly spherical iron spheres of identical diameter, separated by one meter in an otherwise entirely empty and symmetric universe, with no external orientation markers. I judge that there are genuinely two spheres, not one sphere in two places. They share every intrinsic property and every symmetric relational property (each stands in the one-meter relation to the other). So if qualitative identity entails numerical identity, the principle fails here.

Case 2: Two helium-4 atoms in a Bose-Einstein condensate, occupying precisely the same spatial region and quantum state, with no distinguishing spin orientation or internal excitation. Here I am less certain, but I lean toward saying that talk of "two atoms" is a mode of counting rather than a claim about two metaphysically distinct individuals; it seems closer to saying there is one quantum field with excitation number two. So in this setting, qualitative sameness might indeed correspond to the absence of genuine numerical plurality at the level of individual substances.

Case 3: Two causally isolated "pocket universes" arising in an eternal-inflation model, with identical large-scale structures, identical histories, and no shared causal past or future, separated by a non-spatiotemporal boundary. Despite the qualitative replication being total—including all internal relational structures—I still judge that these are numerically distinct universes, not one universe redundantly described. The isolation itself seems to ground the multiplicity.

Case 4: A person is scanned and destroyed on Earth; an atom-for-atom qualitative duplicate is assembled on Mars using only the information in the scan, with the original scan-data destroyed en route and no continuous causal chain linking the original body to the new one. I judge that this is not the same person surviving; it is a perfect replica. Qualitative replication, even with environmental relational matching, does not preserve numerical identity for persons.

Case 5: Two identical instances of the same software program—say, version 4.2 of a chess engine—running on physically separate, causally disconnected servers, with identical internal state configurations. I judge these are clearly two instances, not one program existing in two places. The physical substrate and causal isolation seem sufficient to multiply the instances despite perfect qualitative overlap.

Case 6: In Black's scenario, but now a distant, asymmetrically placed star is added, so that sphere A is 10^23 meters from the star and sphere B is 10^23 + 1 meters away. Here they differ in an asymmetric relational property. I still think they are two spheres, but now the difference feels metaphysically superficial—like labeling—whereas in Case 1 the duality felt more fundamental.

My difficulty is reconciling Case 2 with Cases 3 and 5. In Cases 3 and 5, causal and substrate isolation seem to generate multiplicity despite qualitative sameness. In Case 2, overlap and shared state seem to collapse multiplicity into one field. Meanwhile, Case 4 suggests that for persons, causal continuity is required, not just qualitative or relational match. Is there a single principle—perhaps involving ontological category, substrate-dependence, or the distinction between internal and external relations—that delivers all six verdicts? Or must I abandon one? In particular, if I try to save Case 2 by appealing to spatiotemporal overlap, that seems to conflict with Case 1, where overlap is absent yet multiplicity remains. Conversely, if I save Case 3 and 5 by appealing to substrate or causal boundary conditions, why does that not apply to Case 2?

Please propose the most precise principle (or a small competing set) that captures the pattern, specifying which clause is motivated by which case. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: two perfectly identical macroscopic crystals formed independently in separate, causally isolated laboratories with no shared origin, each composed of atoms arranged in the same lattice. Would your principle count them as one crystal or two? Finally, state honestly which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate without ad hoc revision, and whether you think that verdict should be overturned for the sake of simplicity.
