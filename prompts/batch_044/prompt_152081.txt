If tranquilism is correct that intrinsic value resides solely in the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, then any human activity that is neither a relief from pain nor a direct suppression of desire can be valuable only instrumentally. But consider the documented phenomenon among advanced practitioners of contemplative calligraphy who describe maintaining rigorous, decades-long discipline in a condition they call a motivational zero-state: precise, effortful attention that produces neither pleasure nor discomfort, is not pursued as a means to tranquility, and is structured entirely by formal and aesthetic constraints rather than by desire for an outcome. Must the tranquilist therefore deny that this state has any intrinsic worth? Or, if the tranquilist argues that the value of such practice is precisely that it instantiates the absence of craving, does this not conflate a negative affective condition with the positive perfection of a rational capacity—thereby collapsing tranquilism into a perfectionist view that prizes the exercise of human excellence regardless of whether suffering or desire are present?
