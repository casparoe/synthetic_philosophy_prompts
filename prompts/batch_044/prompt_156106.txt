I just spent several hours arguing with a close friend about whether the French republican model of laïcité and assimilationist integration can be defended against the Anglo-American multiculturalist framework, and we kept talking past each other. Rather than adjudicating who is right, I want the kind of document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between our two positions should have produced. Draft a joint statement between, on one side, a defender of the French republican position—who holds that democratic equality requires strict state neutrality in public institutions, that visible religious or cultural markers ought to be relegated to private life, and that social cohesion depends on a shared civic identity that supersedes group particularism—and, on the other side, a defender of Anglo-American multiculturalism—who holds that group-differentiated rights and public recognition of minority identities are necessary for equal dignity, that purported neutrality typically masks dominant-culture norms, and that integration should not require cultural invisibility.

Structure the response as follows. First, an agreed-claims section listing propositions both sides could sign, including shared empirical observations about immigration patterns, shared normative commitments to democratic equality and individual liberty, and shared acknowledgments of where each institutional model has produced tension or success—without attributing these outcomes to specific statutory citations unless essential for clarity. Second, a disagreements table that states each side's view in wording that its own defenders would accept, not in caricature. Third, identify the cruxes: I take one to be whether neutrality of the public sphere can be non-oppressive when the baseline culture is already shaped by secular and historically Christian norms; another to be whether the maintenance of minority cultural practices is a primary good or is instrumentally valuable only insofar as it serves individual autonomy; and a third to be whether visibility-restricting regimes predictably produce higher intergroup trust and social mobility than recognition-granting regimes, or whether the causal story runs the other way. For each crux, present a concrete comparative institutional design—such as the regulation of religious dress in public schools versus the accommodation of minority arbitration in family law—and record what each side predicts about its empirical and normative outcomes.

Then assess exposure: for each crux, state what evidence or argument would genuinely move each side, distinguishing decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, and say whether either position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a defect for a view of this kind. Finally, examine whether part of our dispute was verbal rather than substantive, specifically whether the terms assimilation, integration, and recognition are carrying different normative loadings for each of us; if so, isolate the purely semantic disagreement from the substantive one and reformulate the residual conflict with those ambiguities removed. Do not include a bibliography or reading list, do not ask me to choose a winner, and frame the entire document as something both parties could in principle sign except for the precisely located disagreements.
