I have noticed a recurring configuration that feels adjacent to well-known discussions about design without a designer and computation without cognition, yet I cannot tell whether it has its own precise name. In one kind of case, generations of practical selectors—say, animal breeders—gradually produce organisms with complex, seemingly purpose-built traits, yet none of the selectors understands the underlying mechanisms, and any explicit theory they hold is usually mistaken; the practical mastery is real, but it operates without comprehension and even resists it, because attempts to formalize the selection rules tend to yield worse outcomes than the "blind" process did. In a parallel kind of case, an automated system achieves accurate, complex classification through internal adjustments that correspond to no feature its operators understand; the engineers can describe statistical correlations but not reasons, and efforts to impose transparent, human-interpretable rules typically degrade performance. What I want to identify is not merely the separation of practical success from explicit understanding, but the stronger arrangement in which the two appear inversely related: the very feature that makes comprehension impossible—distributed, evolutionary, or statistical optimization without representation—is what enables the competence, so that injecting understanding is not just unnecessary but often harmful. Is there an established concept that captures exactly this tension? If so, how does it differ from ordinary skilled practice that resists articulation, and from overconfidence about one's own knowledge? If not, can you propose a precise definition, specify its boundary conditions—for instance, whether it applies only to evolutionary or computational processes, or also to human institutional learning—and clarify what it is not?
