Consider the following comparative institutional arrangement not as four separate national reports but as a single object of analysis.

In Germany, citizens who register membership in a recognized religious community—primarily Catholic and Protestant, though also Jewish and several others—pay a church tax surcharge collected automatically through the state income-tax apparatus. To terminate this, the citizen must file a formal declaration of exit (Kirchenaustritt) with a local court or administrative office, after which the tax authority updates the record. In England, the Church of England remains legally established: its bishops sit in the House of Lords, and religious education in state schools includes Anglican content by default, yet there is no direct tax on citizens for church membership; funding comes partly through historic endowments and voluntary giving, with the state maintaining pre-Reformation church buildings through heritage preservation mechanisms. In France, the state is constitutionally prohibited from funding religious groups and from recognizing religious categories in public administration, yet it retains ownership of church buildings constructed before the separation law and subsidizes their maintenance as cultural heritage. In the United States, the Establishment Clause bars official religion, but religious organizations receive tax exemptions and clergy enjoy special legal privileges, effectively treating religious status as a category that earns state forbearance rather than state collection or exclusion.

The question is not which system is best, but what philosophical commitments about religious membership, state authority, and collective identity are required to make this specific patchwork coherent—or whether it is coherent at all.
