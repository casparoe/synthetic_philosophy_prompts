Design a classroom activity for an advanced philosophy-of-decision seminar: about 18 students, 75 minutes, one session. The goal is for participants to feel, through their own choices and defenses, that violating the independence axiom is not merely a cognitive bias but reflects a structural tension between ex-ante maximization and what we might call scenario-integrated evaluation—treating outcomes not as isolable probabilities but as embedded in complete narratives of accountability and avoided alternatives. The idea should emerge from the mechanics before any name is given.

Structure the session in three phases with a point-based payoff scheme (no real money). Phase 1: Each student privately allocates a shared group fund between two pairs of gambles, arranged so that consistent preference for certainty in the first pair and risk-seeking in the second reproduces the classic common-consequence pattern, but frame the outcomes in terms of collective responsibility rather than individual gain so that the stakes feel social. Lock the choices without discussion.

Phase 2: Randomly assign half the room as Defenders and half as Auditors. Defenders must justify their Phase 1 allocations to the Auditors using only one of two permitted vocabularies: either strict expected-utility language, or a vocabulary of scenario-integrated evaluation in which every outcome is assessed together with the counterfactual path that was avoided. The Auditors award coherence points. Build a rule that if a Defender uses EU language, they must apply it consistently to both pairs—thereby condemning their own Phase 1 choice—while using scenario-integrated language saves coherence but forces them to explicitly reject independence. Include a “rationality bonus” pool that Defenders can access only if they preserve independence, so they feel the cost of abandoning it in real time.

Phase 3: A physical “swap” exercise. Give groups printed cards representing the gambles. They must literally excise the common consequence from both options in one pair and replace it with an identical alternative, then observe whether their preference order flips. Follow with a structured deliberation: ask them to draft a modified axiom—call it context-sensitive independence—that permits their Phase 1 pattern while blocking full intransitivity. The rules should require them to write the axiom in formal terms (conditions on preference preservation under substitution) rather than in loose intuitive language.

The debrief should map Phase 1 onto the common-ratio/common-consequence setup; Phase 2 onto the conflict between ex-ante utility maximization and ex-post accountability; and Phase 3 onto whether independence can be repaired rather than rejected. Do not name the Allais paradox or cite any literature until the final five minutes. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong: students will likely conflate scenario-integrated evaluation with ordinary risk aversion, so include a facilitator prompt that forces them to distinguish “I dislike variance” from “This outcome is evaluated differently because the avoided alternative changes its meaning.” Also be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the cardinal measurement of utility differences across distinct risk domains, which requires numerical comparison that a role-play with points cannot capture, and which you must supply conceptually in the debrief. Keep all instructions self-contained: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations.
