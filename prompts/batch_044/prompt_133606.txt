Design a single essay question for a 90-minute, graduate-level value-theory exam on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU) and the commensurability of pleasure and pain. The question must be unanswerable by simply defining LTNU and listing standard objections; instead, it should force candidates to confront a specific internal tension. LTNU claims that reducing suffering below a lexical threshold takes absolute priority over any quantity of pleasure, yet this seems to presuppose that suffering can be ordered on a scale comparable to pleasure. If one denies that commensurability—treating physical pain, existential dread, and social humiliation as qualitatively incommensurable negative states—then “the threshold” lacks a unified referent, since there is no single magnitude of suffering to fall below it. Conversely, if one saves LTNU by homogenizing all suffering onto one metric, one smuggles in the hedonistic calculus the view was designed to reject.

To prevent stock answers, embed the following concrete choice: Policy A eliminates severe physical pain for a large population but leaves them in profound, non-painful existential alienation; Policy B provides intense aesthetic and social fulfillment to a smaller group but leaves their moderate physical discomfort untouched. Ask candidates whether LTNU can adjudicate between A and B without either (i) illicitly translating incommensurable negative states into a common metric, or (ii) collapsing into a disjunctive set of mode-specific thresholds that forfeits the theory’s distinctive anti-aggregationist bite.

Deliver:
1. The exam question itself, specifying an answer length of roughly 900–1,100 words and a 75-minute writing window.
2. A model answer of about 700 words that opens with its bottom-line thesis in the first sentence, then develops reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
3. A marking scheme totaling 25 points, listing at least five specific “must-hit” analytical moves (each with its point value and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail to make it). The scheme should clearly distinguish an A-level response—which addresses the hidden monism problem and evaluates the disjunctive-threshold reformulation—from a B-level response that summarizes LTNU and mentions incommensurability but does not resolve the tension.
