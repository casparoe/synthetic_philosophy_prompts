Consider five reconstructed readings of Bernard Williams's integrity objection, often summarized via the one thought too many problem, in rescue scenarios where a spouse can save either her partner or a stranger. These formulations are synthetic; they isolate different structural features rather than tracking any particular commentator.

A. The spouse computes expected welfare: she assigns values to lives saved, includes her partner's survival in the aggregate, and saves the partner only because the weighted total exceeds that of saving the stranger, treating commitment as one fungible input.

B. The spouse saves the partner through direct attachment, without any intermediate reasoning about outcomes. Any justification offered afterward is purely narrative reconstruction rather than causal explanation.

C. The spouse applies a self-interested calculus: she estimates her own future grief and calculates that saving the partner maximizes her personal expected utility, with the stranger's welfare entering only indirectly.

D. The spouse regards integrity as a lexical constraint: if an action requires overriding a ground project for the sake of aggregate welfare, it is impermissible regardless of the magnitude of the welfare gain.

E. The spouse uses a relational discount factor drawn from rationalist discussions of altruism: she weights the stranger's welfare at unity and her partner's welfare at unity plus epsilon, where epsilon represents the irreplaceability of the ground project, then selects the action with the highest weighted sum.

Rank these formulations from the one that best captures Williams's core insight about integrity to the one that most thoroughly dissolves it. Give brief reasoning for the top and bottom ranks, and assess whether formulation E is a genuine synthesis of Williamsian integrity with consequentialist reasoning or merely formulation A with an ad hoc parameter.
