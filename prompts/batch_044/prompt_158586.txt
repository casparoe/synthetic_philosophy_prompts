I'm preparing a feature on how political cultures narrate moral victories, and I've kept returning to the 1807 British Slave Trade Act—not the parliamentary maneuvering itself, which is common knowledge, but the historiographical tradition that treats its passage as an almost inevitable unfolding of British moral enlightenment. I want to examine that tradition through Herbert Butterfield's critique of Whig history, but with a specific twist: I'm interested in whether the standard Whig framing of 1807 operated as what I am calling a moral pivot, a narrative device that allowed the British state to maintain imperial legitimacy by converting an economic and strategic retreat into an ethical triumph. Analyze the conventional Whig account—the framing that casts the Act as the triumph of Wilberforcean conscience over mercantile interest—through the lens of Whig history as a narrative form. What does the theory predict historians will suppress or overstate about the Act's immediate economic context, about the continuity of coerced plantation labor after 1807, and about the relative agency of enslaved people versus parliamentary elites? Does the moral pivot concept add explanatory power that Whig history alone lacks, or is it merely a restatement of the same suppression mechanism? Where does Whig history illuminate the narrative choices made by Victorian and mid-twentieth-century historians, and where does it fall silent—for instance, does it explain why the framing persisted despite Butterfield's well-known critique, or does that persistence require a sociological account outside the theory's scope? I am not looking for a bibliography or citation list; I want a disciplined two-way analysis in which the theory is mapped onto the episode with precision, and the episode is used to test whether Whig history can genuinely distinguish between moral progress and strategic self-legitimation, including an honest note on where the mapping frays.
