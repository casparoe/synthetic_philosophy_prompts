As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, I have been working through Jaegwon Kim’s causal exclusion argument and its implications for non-reductive physicalism. Most introductory treatments present the problem at a high level—supervenience, causal closure, and the threat of epiphenomenalism—but I want to understand a more specific conceptual knot. If mental properties are treated as higher-level variables within an interventionist framework for causation, does the exclusion problem arise primarily because such properties fail to satisfy the stability conditions that genuine causal relevance requires, or does it arise independently at the level of causal production, with interventionism merely making the overdetermination explicit? In other words, is the difficulty that mental properties are not robust “difference-makers” in the relevant sense, or is it a prior metaphysical conflict about physical sufficiency that no reconceptualization of causation can dissolve?

Please explain this distinction carefully: lay out the basic structure of the exclusion argument, the core commitments of interventionist causation, and how the two interact, particularly regarding stability, proportionality, and levels of causation. In doing so, separate what is broadly settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of mind and philosophers of science—especially concerning whether interventionism offers non-reductive physicalists a substantive escape, only a redescription of the same problem, or something in between. Do not present any one of these contested interpretations as the consensus view.
