After a long and exhausting argument with a close friend last night, I am still unsettled about a betting game he proposed for our weekly card club and about the reasoning he used to defend it. The game is simple: flip a fair coin repeatedly until it lands heads. If heads appears on the first flip, the player wins two dollars; on the second flip, the player loses four dollars; on the third, wins eight; on the fourth, loses sixteen; and so on, with the amount doubling each round and the sign alternating between win and loss. I worked out the usual expectation—multiplying each outcome by its probability, which is one-half raised to the roll number, and adding them all together. The result is the series one minus one plus one minus one, continuing forever without ever settling on a value. My friend conceded that the expectation is undefined in this standard sense, but he insisted that this formal failure does not make playing irrational. He pointed out that the potential winnings grow exponentially, and he argued that a rational agent should still be willing to pay a modest, perhaps even substantial, entry fee, since the upside is enormous and any single session is likely to end quickly with a small payout. I replied that once the expectation collapses, we lose the very benchmark that would tell us what counts as modest or substantial, and that appealing to intuition about large payouts feels like substituting psychology for normative structure. He then offered a resolution I had not previously considered: rather than weighting every future roll equally, we should apply a temporal discount factor that decays exponentially with the roll number, reflecting both the practical cost of waiting and the psychological reality that distant outcomes matter less to present deliberation. Under this weighted scheme, he claimed, the series converges to a definite value, and the game becomes fully analyzable within standard decision theory. I remain skeptical, partly because the choice of discount rate seems arbitrarily calibrated to force convergence, and partly because I worry that revising the evaluation rule in this way does not solve the underlying problem but merely relocates it into a conventional parameter. The heart of our disagreement, I think, is not arithmetic but philosophical: when the standard tools of rational choice break down in the face of an undefined expectation, what do we owe those tools? Should the undefined result be treated as a prohibition on entering the game, as a license to fall back on intuitive risk assessment, or as an invitation to reformulate the evaluation framework itself? And if we do reformulate it—whether through discount weights, through averaging partial sums, or through some other adjustment—have we genuinely preserved the rational structure of the choice, or have we simply replaced one paradox with an unexamined convention? I would appreciate help identifying the philosophical structure of this situation and understanding whether there is a principled way to navigate it, particularly one that does not depend on citing the specific history or formal literature of the game but addresses the reasoning on its own terms.
