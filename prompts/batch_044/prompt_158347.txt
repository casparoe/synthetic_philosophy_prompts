I'm preparing a defense of my epistemological position for a graduate seminar on foundational knowledge, and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to it in writing. Here is the package of views I've settled into: (a) I accept a moderate foundationalism according to which basic perceptual beliefs possess what I take to be a "default entitlement"—they carry prima facie justification without requiring prior inferential support, though this entitlement is defeasible rather than absolute. (b) I reject the bootstrapping objection to so-called "easy knowledge"; I think it is legitimate to use a first-order perceptual belief (for example, that I see a red surface) to support a second-order claim about the reliability of my color vision in that context, because the two claims operate at different epistemic levels and the circularity is not vicious. (c) Despite (a) and (b), I hold that reflective equilibrium is the correct overarching method in epistemology—particular judgments and general principles should be brought into mutual adjustment, and even basic beliefs can in principle be revised if the coherence costs of retaining them become severe enough. (d) I maintain that evolutionary debunking arguments succeed in undermining moral knowledge—because we lack any independent, non-circular access to moral facts with which to calibrate our moral faculties—yet fail against perceptual knowledge, precisely because perception permits the kind of cross-checking and bootstrap-style verification that morality does not. (e) Finally, I think the distinction between basic and non-basic beliefs is not merely pragmatic or psychological but structurally real: there is a feature of justification—call it "epistemic primacy"—that makes basic beliefs genuinely prior in the order of reasons, so that the justification of derived beliefs ultimately traces back to them rather than circulating holistically.

These commitments feel largely reasonable and recognizably held by someone working in post-Gettier foundationalism, but I worry there are hidden tensions I'm glossing over, particularly around the interaction of (b), (c), and (e). Specifically, I want to know: which pairs are genuinely in conflict, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and what the least costly revision would be in each case? Please rank the tensions by severity, explain exactly which commitment has to give (or be qualified) to resolve each one, and do not treat all conflicts as equally damaging. I also want you to check whether my asymmetry in (d) is actually stable given what I say in (b)—it seems to me that if bootstrapping works for perception, then the reason evolutionary debunking fails there might not be that perception has some special calibration mechanism, but rather that bootstrapping itself is legitimate, which would suggest morality might be able to bootstrap too unless there's an independent difference I'm not seeing. Please address that specifically. Write your response in American English, and organize the audit as an ordered evaluation rather than a general lecture.
