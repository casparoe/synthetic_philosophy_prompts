I have recently found myself examining the motives behind my own commitments—to my work, to certain political norms I have long taken for granted, and to a friendship that has come under strain—so the question of which considerations are the right kind of reasons for an attitude and which are the wrong kind has become personally urgent rather than merely academic. Here are my judgments, offered in the hope that they reveal a pattern. I think it is acceptable to admire a colleague’s intellectual honesty because her example inspires me to become more rigorous myself, but it is the wrong kind of reason to admire her solely because her reputation might reflect well on me by association. I believe that if I discover the kindness I valued in a partner was performed entirely for an audience, my love ought to dissolve, yet if I remain out of inertia or because leaving would be administratively inconvenient, that also seems corrupt, though perhaps differently so; I keep returning to the case where I might stay out of fear of loneliness, and I cannot decide whether that is wrong in the same fundamental way or only weak and imprudent. I take it that believing a policy is just because it aligns with my party’s platform and I want my party to win is the wrong kind of reason for a belief about justice, whereas believing it after independent evaluation and then noticing my agreement is not; what unsettles me is whether my belief in climate change is corrupted if part of my motivation is that accepting it allows me to belong to a community I value, even when I also possess scientific reasons. When I forgive a friend for a betrayal, doing so because forgiveness will improve my mental health seems permissible, but doing so because I have calculated she can still advance my career feels wrong; I am unsure, however, whether forgiving her solely to avoid the discomfort of confrontation is wrong in the same sense or merely imprudent. I also think there is a wrong kind of reason for resentment: resenting an insult because it damages my social standing seems corrupt, whereas resenting it because it expresses contempt for my dignity seems appropriate, though I struggle to apply that distinction when the insult is genuinely ambiguous. Is there a coherent principle, or a small family of principles, that explains why some instrumental or self-regarding considerations corrupt an attitude while others do not? I want the principle stated with precision, together with the specific case that motivates each clause, its predictions for situations I have not listed—such as whether it is the wrong kind of reason to maintain trust in a democratic institution primarily because that institution protects one’s property, or to reject a scientific theory because accepting it would alienate one’s family—and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it must overturn in order to remain consistent. I am particularly interested in whether the correct principle distinguishes corruption of the attitude itself from corruption of the reasons that happen to sustain it, and what that distinction implies for the case I cannot settle: whether fear of loneliness is a wrong-kind reason to remain in love or only a bad but permissible one.
