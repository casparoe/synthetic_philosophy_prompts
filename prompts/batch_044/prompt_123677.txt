I'm a novelist who thinks in terms of point of view, dramatic irony, the ethics of representing interiority, and the formal distinction between a chorus of minor voices and a single protagonist's consciousness. Restate the panpsychist position that fundamental physical entities possess phenomenal states capable of suffering, together with its ethical implication that obligations may arise even where no macro-subject emerges—specifically in the variant that holds micro-experiences do not aggregate by quantitative summation but by a kind of narrative coherence (call it the dramaturgical binding problem), so that a stone might contain a chorus of suffering voices that never coalesce into one narrative agent. Use only my craft vocabulary: interiority, narrative agency, representation, dramatic irony, chorus, protagonist, point of view, and the moral problem of giving voice to the voiceless. Then deliver the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which must be distorted to fit a literary framework, and which—such as physical law, normative calculus, or combinatorial aggregation—have no counterpart at all. Finally, say whether that residue marks a genuine limit in fiction's capacity to conceptualize distributed suffering, or whether it reveals the original philosophical claim to be less determinate than it first appears.
