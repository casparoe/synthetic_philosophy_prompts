I'm currently taking a seminar on rational choice and symmetric games, and we spent last week on Hofstadter's superrationality puzzles—specifically the Platonia postcard dilemma and what he called the Luring Lottery, in which identically situated agents must each decide whether to enter a prize pool that pays out only if exactly one participates. The classroom reasoning seemed airtight for these toy cases, but when I tried to extend it over coffee with a friend who works in quantitative finance, the argument began to swallow every competitive domain I could name. Here is the chain I built; it feels like it proves far too much, but I cannot locate the leak.

(1) In genuinely symmetric decision problems—same information, same reasoning architecture, no hidden differences—superrational agents should treat their choice as perfectly correlated with every other agent's choice, since they share an identical decision procedure.

(2) In the Luring Lottery, if my reasoning leads me to enter, then every identically situated peer enters too, so the payout condition (exactly one entrant) fails and I gain nothing; if my reasoning leads me to stay out, then everyone stays out and I also gain nothing, but I avoid the entry cost. Universal abstention is therefore the only stable superrational equilibrium.

(3) Now consider any competitive domain where success requires being the outlier: applying to a single-seat fellowship, bidding in a common-value auction against rational peers, or pursuing a niche artistic career whose market reward depends on scarcity. If my reasoning tells me that this path is worth pursuing, then by (1) every peer with the same reasoning will pursue it too, which eliminates the scarcity or outlier status that made the path valuable in the first place.

(4) So superrationality implies that I should withdraw from every competitive endeavor in which my peers are rational and similarly informed.

(5) But this conclusion is absurd: it predicts that rational agents should never apply to graduate school, never enter markets, and never attempt distinctive achievements. Since we observe rational agents succeeding in all of these domains, (4) must be wrong.

(6) Therefore either superrationality is false outside artificial puzzles, or there is a hidden asymmetry in every real-world competition that breaks the correlation asserted in (1).

My instinct is to defend (1) and (2) for the toy puzzles but reject the generalization in (3), yet I worry that (3) is just (1) applied consistently without ad hoc boundary-drawing. If I reject (3), am I committing to a mysterious distinction between "puzzle" symmetry and "real-world" asymmetry? Or is the error elsewhere—perhaps in treating correlation as deterministic rather than probabilistic, or in ignoring that real agents differ in taste, risk tolerance, or private information? I would prefer to keep the core idea that identical reasoners should align in Platonia, but I need to know exactly what I am paying to contain the argument. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost?
