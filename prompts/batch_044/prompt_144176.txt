I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and recently, while discussing with colleagues why certain long-running technology stacks survive despite repeated predictive failures while others are quickly abandoned, I encountered the framework of Imre Lakatos’s scientific research programmes. The distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts struck me as potentially relevant to how we evaluate sustained technical trajectories—particularly in artificial intelligence, where debates about scaling laws versus architectural innovation often feel less like straightforward empirical disputes and more like competing core commitments defended by auxiliary adjustments. I want to understand what this philosophical framework genuinely implies, not merely as a historical taxonomy of science, but as a conceptual tool that a non-specialist could apply to living technical debates.

Please describe the most important implications of Lakatos’s research programmes for assessing whether a modern technical research trajectory—such as the sustained pursuit of ever-larger foundation models versus the development of novel reasoning architectures—is advancing in a rationally defensible way or instead showing signs of degeneration. In particular, address how one should interpret persistent predictive failures within such a programme; under what precise conditions absorbing anomalies through auxiliary hypotheses becomes intellectually costly rather than legitimate; whether a programme must consistently generate novel empirical predictions to remain progressive, or whether maintaining internal coherence among protective-belt adjustments can ever be sufficient on its own; and what obligations, if any, this framework places on practitioners who continue to direct substantial resources toward a trajectory that exhibits degenerative patterns. Please keep the discussion entirely self-contained, without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as this is intended for my own reflection and for conversation with other engineers who will not have access to specialized academic sources.
