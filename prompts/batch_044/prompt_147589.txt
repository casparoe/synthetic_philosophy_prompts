I am working through Dennett’s discussion of Sphex ichneumoneus for a philosophy of action seminar, and I have built an argument that seems to collapse the distinction between the wasp’s rigidity and human practical reasoning. Here are the steps:

(1) Call an agent sphexish with respect to a sequence if, when an intermediate instrumental step becomes redundant, the agent restarts the sequence rather than proceeding directly to the goal — as when the wasp drags the cricket away and back to check the burrow again.

(2) Sphexishness is not all-or-nothing: the wasp eventually abandons the site if the burrow is blocked, so rigidity is domain-local.

(3) Human rational planning also decomposes goals into ordered sub-goals. A flexible planner skips a sub-goal when it is already satisfied exogenously.

(4) But that “skipping” is itself governed by a fixed meta-rule: execute sub-goal i unless detection-condition C_i is met.

(5) Thus the human planner differs from the wasp not by lacking rigidity, but by possessing a higher-order rigidity — call it meta-sphexishness — about which conditions license omission.

(6) If free will requires escaping sphexishness altogether, it must require escaping every fixed meta-algorithm governing one’s cognitive architecture.

(7) No embodied agent with a stable architecture can escape all such meta-rules; therefore free will is impossible for humans as well as wasps.

(8) Yet we have strong reason to think human deliberation is genuinely free in a way the wasp’s behavior is not.

(9) So one of (1)–(7) must fail.

The conclusion in (7) strikes me as absurd — it seems to show that rational planning and free will are incompatible. I want to keep (8) and, if possible, (3). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a principled way to distinguish meta-sphexishness in humans from the wasp’s first-order rigidity without simply stipulating that humans are special?
