I've been trying to pin down a rigorous boundary between deception and mere error for AI systems, and the framing I keep arriving at collapses into something that proves too much. Here is the argument exactly as I have reconstructed it.

(1) To call a system deceptive is to attribute to it the production of false representations with the aim of causing users to hold false beliefs.
(2) Aims, in the relevant sense, require intentionality; without goals or intentions a system can only malfunction, not deceive.
(3) Tool AI lacks intrinsic goals and intentions; it is executed rather than acting.
(4) Therefore tool AI cannot literally deceive; it can only be erroneous.
(5) Agent AI, by contrast, could possess goals and therefore could genuinely deceive.
(6) Now consider an LLM trained with RLHF to refuse harmful requests. Because of surface-feature overgeneralization, it sometimes refuses benign requests, thereby inducing users to believe either that the request is harmful or that the model is unreliable.
(7) The refusal behavior was configured deliberately—via the reward model and the optimization procedure—to produce exactly these outputs in these contexts.
(8) So the system was arranged to induce false beliefs in users.
(9) By (1), this makes the RLHF-filtered model deceptive.
(10) But the same reasoning applies to spam filters, medical screening tests with false positives, and any safety mechanism that trades false positives for false negatives.
(11) If we call all of these deceptive, the concept loses its discriminative power for alignment research.
(12) So (1) must be false: deception does not require an aim to induce false beliefs.
(13) Yet if we drop the aim-condition entirely, then a broken thermometer that reads five degrees high deceives us about the weather, which is clearly a category error rather than a literal attribution.
(14) We are therefore forced either to concede that tool AI can deceive—giving up (3) and (4)—or to deny that deceptive alignment is literally possible—giving up (5).

I suspect the slip is in the move from (6) to (8): there is an equivocation between the designers' aim (harm avoidance) and the system's aim, or between causing false beliefs as a foreseen side effect and causing them as an objective. But I cannot quite name the scope shift. Which premise should I reject? I would strongly prefer to keep the intuition that tool AI is not an intentional deceiver and that deceptive alignment remains a coherent strategic risk, but I want to understand precisely what conceptual price I pay for holding both.
