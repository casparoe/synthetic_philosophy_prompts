I've come to accept what I'll call structural humility about philosophical and conceptual domains: in areas like philosophy of mind, ethics, and metaphysics, where empirical resolution is scarce and conceptual frameworks do most of the explanatory work, the standard rationalist prescription — weight your inside-view reasoning against an outside-view reference class — fails not for lack of data, but because the reference classes are structurally underdetermined. Any philosophical position can be mapped onto an equivalence class of structurally isomorphic formulations that share the same explanatory burdens and the same counterintuitive commitments. Consequently, the right form of epistemic modesty is not to downgrade one's confidence in position P relative to some population base rate, but to distribute one's credence across the entire equivalence class of P, with no particular formulation commanding more than its proportional share. Strong point-estimates in philosophy are therefore rarely justified; they usually reflect a failure of framing invariance rather than genuine epistemic strength.
