I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and for the past year I have been trying to report on the Great Divergence debate without merely repeating the standard narrative. The contrastive question—why Britain industrialized and not China—has structured economic history since Needham, yet after decades of scholarship the disagreement feels structurally stuck. Institutionalists point to property rights and state capacity; geographical determinists emphasize coal endowments and navigable rivers; cultural historians invoke divergent epistemic norms; and a growing minority argues that the framing itself is anachronistic. They share much of the same comparative evidence, yet the camps do not converge. I suspect the stall is not about missing data but about what kind of answer the contrastive form demands. Specifically, I wonder whether historians are conflating contrastive explanation—accounting for why one trajectory occurred rather than another, which presupposes a shared baseline of possibility—with comparative trajectory analysis, which tracks divergent paths without assuming a common fork. There is also the problem of counterfactual legitimacy: does asking "why not China?" require treating eighteenth-century Britain and Qing China as comparable candidates in a single causal competition, and is that assumption defensible? Can you diagnose the disagreement? Are these sides disputing hidden empirical claims, diverging about what counts as macro-historical causation, answering different questions—necessary conditions versus sufficient triggers versus framing coherence—under one heading, or relying on bedrock intuitions about naturalness and contingency that neither can argue for? And is there any conceptual distinction, formal result, or reframing—perhaps clarifying the logic of contrastive questions in history—that could actually move the debate, or is the impasse a sign that the question is genuinely ill-posed? I do not want a bibliography or a survey of interpretations; I want a structural diagnosis grounded in what each camp actually says about explanation, comparison, and counterfactual reasoning, together with an honest judgment about whether progress should be expected.
