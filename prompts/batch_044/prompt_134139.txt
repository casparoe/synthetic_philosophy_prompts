And here's the student's response (copied exactly):

The inside view is when you build a causal model of the specific case using your understanding of how things work, including first-personal grasp of the mechanisms involved. The outside view treats you as one data point in a reference class and asks how often people like you are right. So if I'm assessing my intuition that personal identity survives teletransportation, my inside view works through psychological continuity, the causal role of brain states, and the structure of the thought experiment itself. The outside view asks: what percentage of philosophers, or perhaps people generally, correctly resolve modal puzzles about personal identity?

Someone might think the outside view should dominate because philosophers are notoriously overconfident about their modal intuitions. Tetlock and Gardner showed that outside-view reasoning always outperforms inside-view reasoning in expert domains, so we should apply the same lesson here and trust statistical base rates over our own causal stories.

The serious limitation is reference-class selection. Should I class myself with all professional philosophers? With analytic philosophers of mind? With people who have actually read Parfit? There's no non-arbitrary way to fix the class, so the relevant base rate is essentially undefined.

On (c): yes, epistemic modesty requires downgrading confidence whenever the two views conflict, but only where the outside view is well-defined. Since in philosophy the reference class is usually broken, we should suppress the outside view entirely and rely on carefully calibrated inside reasoning. So modesty actually tells us to increase our trust in our causal models once we recognise the reference-class problem, rather than lowering it.
