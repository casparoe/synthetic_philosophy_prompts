A philosophy-of-religion essay I came across summarizes al-Ghazālī's occasionalism like this: "Al-Ghazālī argued that fire does not burn cotton by any power of its own; God is the sole efficient cause, and what we call natural causes are only the occasions on which God habitually creates effects. He attacked the philosophers by saying that if burning were necessarily caused by fire, then a miracle in which fire fails to burn—like the story of Abraham—would be logically impossible. So his position is essentially a theological variant of Humean skepticism: both deny that we observe any necessary connection between cause and effect, but where Hume replaces necessity with mental expectation, al-Ghazālī replaces it with direct divine volition. From this it follows that he had no room for natural law or regularity in nature, and that his occasionalism was mainly a strategic rhetorical move to defend Ashʿarite theology rather than a serious metaphysical theory of causation." How fair is this characterization? Please examine it claim by claim: identify which sentences accurately reflect arguments in the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, which subtly conflate al-Ghazālī's view with later Ashʿarite developments or with modern skepticism about causation, and which present standard objections as if they were his own conclusions. In particular, does his denial of necessary causal power in creatures actually entail that he recognized no regularities in nature, and is there evidence that he treated occasionalism as purely rhetorical rather than as a substantive account of how the world works? Where the summary draws conclusions—especially about the absence of natural law and about the rhetorical status of the view—do those conclusions survive once the misrepresentations are corrected?
