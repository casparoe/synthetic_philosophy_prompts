I'm drafting a feature for a science-and-ideas column on how students reason about institutional failure, and I'm acting as a TA grading a take-home exam in ancient political thought. The exam asked students to apply the framework of "epistemic jurisdiction"—the claim that democratic bodies fail specifically when they assert decision-making authority over questions requiring specialized knowledge they structurally cannot possess—to three Athenian episodes: the Mytilene debate, the Sicilian Expedition, and the trial of Socrates. For each case, students had to classify the failure as primarily an epistemic-jurisdiction failure, an emotional-manipulation failure, or a legal-procedural failure, then defend their choice for the weakest fit.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted:

"In the Mytilene debate, the ekklesia initially voted to execute all male Mytileneans and enslave the women and children after Cleon's emotional appeal. The next day it reversed the decision after Diodotus argued that sparing the population served long-term Athenian strategic interests. This reversal demonstrates an epistemic-jurisdiction failure: the assembly lacked the specialized strategic expertise to evaluate imperial policy consistently and was swayed by competing rhetorical appeals rather than stable knowledge.

The Sicilian Expedition shows the same pattern. Nicias, acting as a technical advisor outside the command structure, warned the assembly that the logistical requirements exceeded Athenian naval capacity, but Alcibiades persuaded the citizens that conquest would bring wealth and security. The ekklesia overruled these expert objections and authorized the fleet. Because the democratic body asserted authority over complex military logistics it did not understand, this is clearly an epistemic-jurisdiction failure.

The trial of Socrates is the weakest fit for the framework. The dikasterion convicted him of not recognizing the gods recognized by the state and of corrupting young men. This was not a claim of specialized technical authority but the enforcement of existing religious and legal norms through standard court procedure. The failure here is best understood as procedural bias combined with emotional hostility toward philosophy, not as an overreach into expert knowledge. Thus Socrates' trial should be classified as legal-procedural."
