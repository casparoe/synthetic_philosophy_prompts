Hey — I'm on the acquisitions committee for a small contemporary art centre up north, and we keep running into the same mess. Every quarter we get maybe five or six proposals to acquire or loan works by artists whose personal behaviour has been publicly questioned — anything from financial fraud to serious abuse allegations — and the committee ends up arguing in circles about whether we can 'seperate' the art from the artist. I was chatting with my mate about this over coffee after reading some old LessWrong threads on moral taint, and realised we don't actually have a decision procedre; we just have vibes and a vague feeling that autonomism is probably right but not always.

Please act as a professional philosopher of art with some practical experience in institutional ethics. Before you write the checklist, do a quick brainstorm of the relevent distinctions that actually survive being turned into operational steps — aesthetic autonomy, moral taint transmission, institutional complicity, and that third axis I sketched out called 'epistemic contamination' (where displaying the work forces viewers to suppress knowledge of harm in order to appreciate formal properties). Then turn all that into a reusable flowchart-in-words my committee can run in about 45 minutes per case, with no philosophers in the room and a director who has to sign off at the end. I need ordered gates: the exact question at each stage, what counts as a sufficient 'yes' or 'no', where the procedure stops with a hard rejection, and where it must hand off to a human because it's a value call dressed up as logic. Be explicit about those judgement points — don't hide them. The procedure should cover hard cases like: (a) an abstract painter convicted of fraud whose canvases carry no narrative content; (b) a sculptor accused of coercive abuse whose new installation is literally about power and consent; (c) a photographer whose technically brilliant series required intimate access allegedly obtained through manipulation. Run all three through it, and please make sure one of them gives an answer I'll genuinely dislike — I want to see where the framework bites. Also tell me honestly what the procedure can't do: which kind of case will make it misfire, and which feature of the debate it just can't reproduce. No bibliographies, no citation strings like 'see Jones 2015' — we can't search the web in the meeting, and I'm not asking for a reading list. Keep it practical, keep it honest about the gaps, write in British English, and don't turn it into a lecture.
