I am drafting a contribution for an interdisciplinary seminar on spatial ontology in legal and scientific contexts — a topic that has become personally urgent since a recent inheritance dispute over rural property boundaries forced me to confront whether location is an intrinsic feature of land or merely a relation among adjacent parcels. To clarify my own position, I am returning to Newton's argument for absolute space in the Scholium to the Definitions in the Principia Mathematica (1687), particularly the paired thought experiments of the rotating bucket of water and the two globes joined by a cord in an otherwise empty universe. My reconstruction of the passage is as follows: Newton asks us to imagine a vessel of water suspended by a twisted cord; initially the surface is flat, but once the vessel rotates and the water eventually shares that rotation, the water climbs the sides of the vessel, indicating that its circular motion is not merely relative to the container but is true motion with respect to an immovable framework. He then asks us to imagine two globes connected by a cord in empty space, rotating about their common center of gravity; the tension in the cord reveals true circular motion even in the absence of any other bodies that could serve as a reference frame. From these observations he concludes that true motion cannot be defined purely in terms of relations to other bodies, and that absolute space exists as a real entity that remains similar and immovable regardless of external relations.

My confusion concerns a precise inferential step rather than the general conclusion. Newton treats the concavity of the water's surface and the tension in the cord as direct manifestations of true motion, yet he also describes absolute space as lacking any parts or marks by which it could be observed. If absolute space is in principle unobservable and exerts no detectable influence, in what sense does the water rotate with respect to it? I have encountered modern formulations that distinguish structural substantivalism — which treats spacetime structure as real but not necessarily substantial — from stronger views that treat space itself as a substance. I want to stay close to Newton's text: does his argument commit him to the claim that absolute space is causally efficacious in producing the concavity and tension, or only to the claim that there is a non-relational fact of the matter about the state of motion? Furthermore, how is the two-globes case meant to block the relationist reply that appeals not to local bodies but to a distant, hidden material plenum — an option that the bucket case alone might not foreclose?

Please help me understand this passage without providing bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I need conceptual clarification that does not depend on web access. Specifically, I would appreciate guidance on three points: (1) whether Newton's reasoning requires space to act as a causal agent or merely as an ontological ground for true motion; (2) how the globes example is designed to defeat relationist appeals to distant matter rather than only local relative motion; and (3) whether a contemporary structural relationist — who accepts an absolute metric structure but denies that space is an independent substance — could consistently accept Newton's observational claims about the bucket and globes while rejecting his substantivalist ontology.
