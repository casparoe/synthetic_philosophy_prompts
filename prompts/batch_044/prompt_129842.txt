I am preparing to lead a fifty-minute discussion section for first-year undergraduates in my metaphysics course, and I have been asked to walk them through Bradley's regress and its bearing on the unity of the proposition. I want the session to do more than rehearse the standard formulation—where a relation R is said to relate terms a and b, which then requires a further relation to relate R to a, and so on—but to make the underlying problem about propositional unity vivid. In particular, I want to use a carrying example that illustrates why the regress is not merely an infinite list of entities but a failure of unification: perhaps a sentence like 'Socrates is wise' and the question of what binds 'Socrates' and 'wisdom' into one thought rather than two isolated items. I am also inclined to bring in the adverbialist response—that 'Socrates is wise' might be analyzed as Socrates being wisely, with no relation needing to be related—because it came up in our lectures, but I worry this will confuse students before they have grasped the regress itself.

Could you plan the explanatory sequence for me? Specifically: what idea must be in place before Bradley's argument can even seem like a problem rather than a puzzle about grammar; which concrete example should carry the whole session (and why the standard 'R relates a and b' diagram should be deferred or avoided); the misconceptions students are likely to form—such as treating the regress as an epistemological infinite chain like Agrippa's trilemma, or thinking that Russell's theory of relations is just Bradley's view with an extra axiom, or conflating the unity of the proposition with the syntactic unity of a sentence—and exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted; one final question I can ask to determine whether they understand that the issue is ontological unification rather than mere relational connection; and what I should explicitly leave out at this level, including whether the adverbialist move belongs in this session at all.

Please also indicate where your pedagogical reasoning is most vulnerable—what feature of first-year cognition or of Bradley's text, if interpreted differently, would cause this plan to misfire—and what specific observation about student responses during the session would lead you to revise the order or abandon the carrying example.
