As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I drafted the following short argument and would value your comments—particularly on whether the central analogy is coherent and whether I am overlooking well-known objections.

Essay notes: Everyday conventions such as tipping, queueing, and jaywalking behave like local protocols in a distributed system. In computing, a protocol binds only nodes that join the network; a foreign node running a different stack is not morally defective unless its behavior corrupts shared global state. By parallel reasoning, a visitor who does not tip in a tipping culture, or who crosses outside a crosswalk where jaywalking is locally prohibited, violates only a local convention rather than a universal moral rule. The harm of skipping a tip is indirect—shifting labor costs within that local economic system—rather than direct harm comparable to physical injury. Queue norms seem closer to coordination protocols: breaking them creates friction and inefficiency, but not necessarily injustice. Jaywalking appears to be a purely local optimization with negligible effect on global state. My tentative view is that visitors bear weak or no moral obligations to follow conventions that lack a harm-prevention backbone—analogous to a foreign API being required to respect authentication boundaries but not local formatting rules. I worry, however, that this framing treats moral obligation as overly instrumental, and that I may be neglecting arguments that voluntary participation in a local practice generates implicit consent or that fairness requires honoring local expectations regardless of harm.

Please comment on the accuracy and interest of this analogy, on whether the distinction between "local protocol" and "global harm-prevention" genuinely tracks moral distinctions, and on any conceptual issues—such as consent-based or fairness-based objections—that a non-specialist should consider.
