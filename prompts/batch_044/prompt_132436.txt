The exam question asked students to evaluate this claim: the deliberative-demandingness objection—about the cognitive burden of constant consequentialist calculation—succeeds against act-consequentialism but fails against rule-consequentialism, since rules eliminate case-by-case reasoning, yet this leaves structural and transactional costs untouched and so does not affect Bernard Williams’s integrity objection.

The student’s answer:

“Deliberative demandingness should be treated as genuinely independent of both structural and transactional demandingness. Structural demandingness concerns the global shape of a life—how resources and commitments are distributed across projects—while transactional demandingness concerns the cost of particular acts, such as writing a large check. Deliberative demandingness, by contrast, concerns the mental effort required by the decision procedure itself. Even an agent with flexible ground projects and cheap particular donations might find constant optimization exhausting, so the category does not collapse into either of the others.

As for rule-consequentialism: once an agent internalizes the optimal rules—say, fixed donation percentages—the need for case-by-case computation disappears. The burden is relocated to the initial selection of rules, but since that selection is a one-time theoretical exercise rather than a lived practical task, it imposes negligible cognitive weight in everyday life. Hence the deliberative objection succeeds against act-consequentialism but is largely neutralized by the rule-based version.

The claim that structural and transactional costs remain untouched is correct. Rule-consequentialism generally prescribes the same lifestyle adjustments—career changes, reduced consumption—that generate those costs. Regarding Williams’s integrity objection, this is essentially the same structural-demandingness complaint: the agent’s ground projects are subordinated to an impersonal calculus. Because rule-consequentialism embeds that calculus in automated habits, it does not restore integrity; rather, by removing conscious reflection, it deepens the alienation that Railton identified in his critique of consequentialist moral psychology. Thus the original claim is right about act-consequentialism, but wrong to treat deliberative demandingness as the central problem: it is actually the weakest of the three, since rules and heuristics can mitigate it easily, whereas structural alienation is exacerbated, not relieved, by rule-following.”
