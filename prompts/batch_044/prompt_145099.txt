I serve on the institutional review board of a neurotechnology consortium that is evaluating a proposed clinical study on patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness. The researchers intend to deploy an adapted neural-monitoring protocol that detects what they term "partial broadcast events" — sub-threshold signatures of information availability to downstream cognitive and decision-making systems that fail to reach the usual integration threshold associated with full global workspace activation, and that occur in the absence of any behavioral, subjective report, or standard electrophysiological markers of phenomenal experience. They claim that these events constitute access consciousness without phenomenal consciousness, and they want this classification used to inform family counseling about whether the patient retains any form of meaningful cognitive presence, with direct implications for recommendations about the withdrawal of life-sustaining measures. The board is split: one faction treats this as a legitimate, empirically disciplined extension of global workspace theory into clinical practice; the other argues that applying the access-phenomenal distinction in this degraded, fragmentary form collapses the very boundary it relies on, since without phenomenal consciousness there is no subject for whom information is genuinely accessible, and that using an unvalidated sub-threshold metric for life-or-death recommendations risks reifying a theoretical construct into a clinical fact.

Please draft the board's formal decision memo — approve the study as proposed, reject it, or approve it with specific binding conditions — addressed to the consortium's oversight committee and suitable for circulation before Thursday's meeting. The memo should state a concrete recommendation with an explicit timeline for implementation, revision, or termination; it should engage the researchers' conceptual framework seriously rather than merely restating existing legal definitions of consciousness or death; and it must include an honest, non-caricatured account of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The reasoning should remain accessible to neuroscientists and clinicians who are not specialists in philosophy of mind, but it should not oversimplify the stakes of separating access from phenomenal experience in a clinical context.
