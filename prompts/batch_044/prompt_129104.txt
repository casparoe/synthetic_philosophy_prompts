Hey, can you help me untangle a corner of the money-pump argument that keeps bugging me? The usual diachronnic story is that cyclical preferences—A > B > C > A—are irrational because a bookie can run you through three trades and drain your cash. But I'm wondering what happens if you have perfect foresight and can bind yourself with a stopping rule: you take A→B and B→C, but you refuse the final C→A swap because you see it closes the loop. If you can do that, you never actually lose money, so is the intransativity still a rationality failure, or is it just a practical weakness that vanishes once you have the right commitment device?

More specifically, I think people conflate two different objections: the practical one about predictable loss, and the conceptual one about synchronic coherance of your preference ordering. If precommitment blocks the loss, does it also dissolve the coherance problem, or is there a deeper flaw in having cyclical preferences even when you're fully protected from being pumped? And does that mean diachronnic rationality is really about your mental or institutional structure—having stopping rules—rather than about the shape of your preferences themselves? I feel like there's a distinction here that standard explanations gloss over.
