I am drafting an essay—tentatively titled "Actual Worlds and Unwritten Drafts"—that borrows from modal realism to think about literary revision. I want your comments on its philosophical accuracy and on whether the analogy does any real conceptual work, rather than merely dressing a familiar artistic practice in metaphysical language.

Here is the skeleton of the argument. David Lewis holds that possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated totalities, and that "actual" functions indexically: this world is actual for its inhabitants precisely because they are here, not because it possesses some privileged ontological status. Counterpart theory, rather than transworld identity, explains how an object in one world resembles an object in another. Applying this to fiction, I consider an author who writes a first draft of a novel in which a protagonist dies at sea, then revises the manuscript so that she survives and returns to port. On a naive modal-realist reading, both drafts describe concrete worlds; neither is more real than the other except relative to the author who occupies one. But the revision process itself raises a puzzle: does the author, by altering the manuscript, stand in a causal or descriptive relation to the world in which the protagonist survives? Lewis denies that worlds are created—he treats them as already existing—so the author cannot be said to bring about the survival-world. Yet there is an intuitive sense in which revision selects, or makes salient, one possibility over another.

My essay then asks whether counterpart theory can be extended to fictional characters across drafts. In Lewis's framework, a real person has counterparts in other worlds based on qualitative similarity. A fictional character, however, lacks spatiotemporal location in any world except those described by the text. If we treat each draft as specifying a distinct concrete world, the protagonist of Draft One and the protagonist of Draft Two are not identical; they are, at best, counterparts bound by narrative resemblance. This seems to dissolve questions about whether the author changed the character, replacing them with questions about which counterpart relation the author has chosen to emphasize.

I am particularly unsure about three points, and I would like your comments on each.

First, accuracy: does Lewis's indexical analysis of "actual" genuinely block the idea that an author can move between worlds through revision, or am I importing a causal theory of reference that Lewis explicitly rejects? Second, coherence: is it consistent with modal realism to speak of fictional worlds as concrete in the same sense as non-fictional ones, given that they depend for their description on an author's mental states? Or does this collapse into a form of ersatzism that Lewis criticized? Third, interest: does this framing illuminate anything about the phenomenology of revision—the sense that one is discovering a truer version of a character rather than inventing an alternative—or does it merely translate that feeling into ontological jargon?

Please respond in American English. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to secondary literature; I am interested only in whether the reasoning holds together and where it might be strengthened or abandoned.
