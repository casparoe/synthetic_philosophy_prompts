ok here's a take-home essay prompt for my grad rationality seminar, meant to take about 90 minutes and about 800 words. please don't ask students to cite anyone specific.

Question (30 marks):

The usual evidential-cooperation-in-large-worlds story says that if you're one of many causally disconnected but decision-theoretically similar agents, you should cooperate in a prisoner's dilemma because your choice is evidence about what the others do. Now imagine a weirder case: Agent X and Agent Y live in two seperate pocket universes with zero causal contact. They are not just similar—each is the sole physical running of the exact same deterministic algorithm, call it Z. Z faces a local PD in each universe against a different opponent. Because Z is deterministic, if it outputs Cooperate in universe 1, it necessarily outputs Cooperate in universe 2. X knows all this.

Analyze three things. First, is X's decision evidentially supported by Y's expected behavior, or does the fact that both are just Z running twice collapse the 'other agent' into a kind of self? Second, does this show that evidential cooperation can rest on pure logical correlation without any counterfactual or causal dependence, and if so, is it vulnerable to the calculator objection—that two pocket calculators running the same addition program don't 'cooperate' when they both show 42? Third, does this scenario strengthen or weaken the overall case for cooperating in large worlds?

Model answer—key moves a top response must make:

A strong answer distinguishes correlation-from-identity from correlation-from-similarity (around 6 marks). In standard ECL the correlation is mediated by shared reasoning processes, not by being the same token process; here, because X and Y are token-identical instantiations of Z, X's choice doesn't provide evidence about an independent decision-maker—it's closer to deciding what your left hand does based on your right hand, except both are the same algorithm (6 marks). The calculator objection needs to be addressed head-on: calculators lack decision-theoretic relevance and don't face payoff structures, so the analogy fails unless one accepts that any deterministic correlation counts as cooperation, which trivializes the concept (6 marks). On logical correlation, the answer should argue either that causation was never essential and this shows ECL is more robust, or that without causal/counterfactual separation we overgenerate and weaken the view—either horn is fine if defended (6 marks). Finally, the answer should be coherent and take a real stance rather than sitting on the fence (6 marks).

Marking scheme (total 30):

- Correct framing of standard ECL vs the identity twist: 6 marks. Typical mistake: describing cross-universe influence as causal.
- Analysis of evidential vs self-cooperation: 6 marks. Typical mistake: saying it's clearly evidential without addressing the token-identity problem.
- Handling the calculator/spurious-correlation objection: 6 marks. Typical mistake: dismissing it by assertion instead of explaining why agents with stakes differ.
- Assessment of whether logical correlation alone suffices: 6 marks. Typical mistake: insisting causation is necessary without argument.
- Overall coherence and defended stance: 6 marks. Typical mistake: listing both horns without committing or explaining the cost of each.

Typical errors that should cost marks: treating Z's two runs as two agents in the full moral sense (-3), ignoring that determinism here makes correlation perfect rather than probabilistic (-2), or claiming the scenario is impossible because universes can't share algorithms (-2, since the prompt asks you to grant the setup).
