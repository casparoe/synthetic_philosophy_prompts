A clinical-ethics fellow summarized the doctrine of double effect for me through the lens of mass-casualty triage. She said that in the standard switch trolley case, pulling the lever is permissible under double effect because the one death is foreseen but unintended, and the saving of the five does not causally depend on killing the one as an instrumental step. By contrast, she claimed that withdrawing a ventilator from one stable patient to reallocate it to five others is impermissible under the same doctrine, not merely because of the numbers, but because the withdrawal is an intentional killing that serves as the causal means of freeing the resource—making it structurally analogous to pushing the large person in the footbridge case. She concluded that double effect therefore draws a sharp line in medicine: it prohibits active withdrawal for redistribution, yet permits withholding further treatment when the prognosis is poor, since in the latter case the death results from the underlying disease rather than from an intended lethal act. How accurate is this summary? Please separate what correctly captures the conditions of double effect (intention, proportionality, and the means/side-effect distinction), what subtly imports distinctions foreign to the doctrine—such as act versus omission or killing versus letting die—and whether her triage conclusion actually follows from standard formulations, or whether it smuggles in an independent moral premise about resource ownership or clinical obligations.
