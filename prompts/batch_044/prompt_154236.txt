I'm putting together a syllabuss for my philosophy of risk and rationality seminar, and I want the students to watch Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove not as a simple MAD satire but through the specific lens of nuclear brinkmanship — Schelling's idea that credible deterrence can require you to appear somewhat irrational, plus the paradox that automating the response solves credibility by removing human choice entirely. The niche twist I want them to wrestle with is what I'm calling "epistemic brinkmanship": for a threat to actually deter, the adversary needs shared knowledge of both the capability and your inability to back down, but in the film the Doomsday Machine is literally secret, so it fails as a commitment device in exactly the way Ulysses tying himself to the mast fails if he doesn't tell the crew he's tied. So here's the ask: read the War Room scenes and Major Kong's bomb ride through this framework. Where does the film show deterrence collapsing not because anyone is irrational, but because the information conditions for credible threat are broken? Is the Doomsday Machine presented as a successful precommitment that backfires, or was it never strategically sound to begin with? And where does the comedy come from — is it that rationality is too strong, or that rational agents are trapped in a system where shared knowledge is asymetric and therefore unstable? I want a reading that takes both the strategic logic and the film seriously — don't just reduce Kubrick to an illustration of game theory — and I want them to be honest about where the reading breaks down, maybe where masculine hysteria or bureaucratic absurdity just overwhelms the analysis. No bibliography needed, just the argument tied to specific scenes, and say what the film adds that the philosophy papers miss.
