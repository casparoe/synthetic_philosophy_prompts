The examination question was this: In her essays and fiction, George Eliot develops a critique of evangelical Christianity and proposes what interpreters sometimes call a religion of sympathy. Examine her argument that moral obligation can survive the loss of theological foundations, with particular reference to (a) her essay Evangelical Teaching: Dr. Cumming and her distinction between religious feeling and dogmatic belief; (b) her engagement with Feuerbach's projection theory in The Essence of Christianity, which she translated into English; and (c) her portrayal of Dorothea Brooke in Middlemarch, especially the tension between Dorothea's early religious vocation and her eventual life of quiet, unvisited influence. How convincing is the claim that Eliot's secular morality retains the motivational and unconditional force of Christian grace without retaining its metaphysics — a position occasionally labelled secular grace — and does this interpretation risk conflating her view with Auguste Comte's Religion of Humanity?

Candidate's response:

George Eliot's religious position can be summarised as a rejection of dogma combined with a preservation of moral seriousness. In her essay on Dr. Cumming she attacks evangelical moral arithmetic: the idea that good behaviour is a kind of investment in heavenly reward. She argues that this corrupts genuine sympathy, which should be directed towards other people without expectation of divine repayment. This sympathetic basis for ethics comes largely from her translation of Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity, where she adopts the view that God is a projection of human moral qualities onto an imaginary being. Once we recognise this projection, we can reclaim those qualities for ourselves and build a secular morality on human sympathy alone.

In Middlemarch, Dorothea Brooke begins with an intense religious vocation, modelling herself on St. Teresa. She eventually abandons this grand ambition and marries Will Ladislaw, living what the novel describes as a hidden life devoted to sympathy. This demonstrates that religious feeling can be fully translated into domestic human love without any metaphysical residue. The interpretation that Eliot offers a form of secular grace is therefore correct: sympathy in her work operates with the same unconditional, non-instrumental force as Christian grace, but it is directed entirely towards human beings. Her position is essentially Comte's Religion of Humanity stripped of its institutional priesthood; she wants the moral energy of religion without either God or the church.
