Write a deadpan restaurant review, in the precise, evaluative voice of a Michelin Guide inspector, assessing a fictional high-end establishment called “The Gradualist Table” that serves only live-prepared decapod crustaceans and farmed fish. The review should be approximately 650 words and must maintain absolute deadpan seriousness: the inspector praises the ambiance, the chefs’ technique, and the restaurant’s “ethical sourcing framework,” never breaking character to signal that anything is absurd.

The satire should target the conceptual confusion in applying welfare science and moral philosophy to aquatic invertebrates—specifically, the tendency to treat nociceptive capacity, phenomenal consciousness, and moral status as a smooth gradient that can be calibrated like a wine list, and the performative nature of “humane” certification schemes that rely on procedural checklists rather than substantive understanding of suffering. To prevent a generic animal-rights response, inject the following niche framing: the restaurant organizes its menu not by species or preparation, but by a “Phenomenal Depth Index” (PDI) derived from a fictional synthesis of decapod welfare literature and gradationalist moral-status theory. Each course is tagged with a PDI score (e.g., 2.3 for “minimal affective valence,” 7.1 for “robust nociceptive integration”). Diners are encouraged to “curate their moral footprint” by selecting only dishes below a self-imposed PDI threshold, with a supplementary “amnesty surcharge” funding research into whether the threshold itself is arbitrary.

The review should exaggerate real features of the debate: the difficulty of inferring subjective experience from behavioral or neural markers in non-mammalian species; the regulatory gap between stunning requirements for vertebrates and invertebrates in most jurisdictions; and the way ethical-consumption discourse shifts responsibility to individual choice rather than structural practice. Include an invented in-character interlocutor—the restaurant’s “Director of Interspecies Ethics,” Dr. A. Velleman—who defends the PDI system with arguments that mirror actual gradationalist positions (moral status tracks cognitive complexity, suffering is scalar rather than binary, consumer autonomy resolves collective-action problems) but pushed to a logical extreme. The inspector should report these arguments with the same respectful tone used for a chef’s explanation of terroir, allowing the absurdity to emerge from framing rather than explicit mockery.

The piece should be legible to someone familiar with aquaculture welfare debates—references to decapod nociception, electrical stunning protocols, and the distinction between welfare-as-function and welfare-as-experience should be accurate in outline—while remaining fully intelligible without external research. Do not include a bibliography, citations, reading list, or requests for further sources; do not name any specific living philosopher or activist as the target of satire. The humor must come from the collision between the restaurant-review genre’s obsession with refinement and discernment and the genuinely unsettled conceptual terrain of invertebrate sentience.
