You serve on the University Medical Center’s Committee on Continuity of Personhood and Institutional Obligations. A tenured professor of philosophy, Dr. Elena Vasquez, volunteered for an experimental clinical protocol—approved by this same committee three years ago—called Graduated Substrate Replacement (GSR). Over eighteen months, her biological neurons were replaced one-for-one with synthetic analogues that preserve all psychological traits, memories, and dispositions; the original biological tissue was destroyed incrementally. The procedure is now complete. The individual who now occupies her office, teaches her courses, and remembers her life insists she is Vasquez and demands continuation of her tenure, pension contributions, and spousal health benefits. The university’s legal counsel argues that this is a new entity—call her Vasquez-2—because physical continuity has been broken, and that the original Vasquez died during the process; they recommend treating this as a resignation and death event, terminating benefits and opening a search for the position. Vasquez-2’s counsel counters that psychological continuity is all that matters for survival, citing Derek Parfit’s teletransporter reasoning: just as a teletransported replica should be treated as the same person despite physical discontinuity, so should she. The family is divided: her spouse supports Vasquez-2’s claim, while her adult children argue that “my mother was destroyed cell by cell” and that honoring the claim erases a real death that occurred.

Draft the committee’s formal written opinion (approximately 700–900 words) recommending either (a) full institutional recognition of Vasquez-2 as continuous with Vasquez, (b) denial of continuity with termination of obligations, or (c) a conditional or partial recognition with a defined transition period. Your recommendation must explicitly engage Parfit’s distinction between personal identity and what actually matters in survival—particularly his claim that psychological continuity with the right kind of causal connection is sufficient for survival even if strict identity fails—and explain how that distinction should or should not govern an institutional decision with concrete stakes (tenure, benefits, family rights, and teaching obligations). You must also treat seriously the strongest argument on the losing side: if you recommend recognition, confront the children’s claim that institutional recognition amounts to denying a death that genuinely occurred and that benefits should not flow to a successor entity; if you recommend denial, confront the spouse’s claim that the institution is treating a living, suffering, psychologically continuous person as dead for bureaucratic convenience. The reasoning should stand on philosophical analysis alone; do not invoke legal precedents, request bibliographies or citations, or ask for reading lists. The opinion should be structured as a document the committee could circulate and act upon, with a clear bottom line, a brief summary of the opposing view, and explicit criteria that future cases of substrate replacement could apply.
