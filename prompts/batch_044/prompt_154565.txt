Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently begun serious reading in philosophy of social science, I find myself holding what may be an unstable cluster of commitments regarding explanation, emergence, and ontology in the social domain. Rather than a general survey, I want an audit of whether the following six positions form a jointly tenable package, with tensions ordered by severity and the cheapest revisions specified.

Here is roughly where I have landed:

(1) I accept methodological individualism in the explanatory register: any adequate account of a macro-social regularity—say, the persistence of a particular market institution or the diffusion of a linguistic norm—must eventually cite the beliefs, intentions, and interactions of individual agents.

(2) At the same time, I think institutional structures can exert downward causal influence on those agents: rules and norms constrain choice sets in ways that are not merely epiphenomenal summaries of individual behavior, and this downward causation is ontologically real.

(3) I take the appropriate form of emergence here to be weak emergence—supervenience with multiple realizability—rather than strong emergence involving novel causal powers that violate the closure of the physical domain.

(4) I reject the claim that social science requires a sui generis ontology or methodology discontinuous with the natural sciences; explanatory strategies should be continuous across domains, even if social subject matter is irreducibly historical and path-dependent.

(5) I hold that collective intentionality—joint commitments, shared beliefs, and group agency—is real but fully decomposable into interlocking individual mental states, leaving no irreducible collective residue.

(6) Finally, I maintain that in contexts of path-dependent institutional evolution, macro-level patterns possess temporary explanatory priority: to explain why individuals adopted specific beliefs, one must first cite the institutional trajectory itself, not merely reduce it to prior individual choices.

My question is whether these hang together, or whether I am drawing on incompatible motivations. In particular:

- Is the tension between (1) and (2) only apparent once (3) is in place, or does weak emergence still fail to license genuine downward causation within an individualist framework?

- Does (5) undermine (2)? If collective states are just interlocking individual mental files, then institutional constraint seems reducible to aggregated individual interaction, leaving downward causation heuristic at best.

- Does (6) constitute a covert abandonment of both (1) and (4)? Granting macro-level explanatory priority in historical cases looks like methodological holism, and it may demand a distinctive, narrative mode of explanation discontinuous with natural-scientific mechanism.

- Is there an apparent tension between (3) and (5) that dissolves on closer inspection—perhaps weak emergence and decomposability are compatible in ways that initially seem contradictory?

Please do not offer a general lecture on individualism versus holism. I want a disciplined audit: identify which pairs are genuinely in conflict, ordered by severity; distinguish these from tensions that dissolve upon proper parsing; and for each genuine conflict, specify the cheapest revision and what it costs—especially whether surrendering (6) is less damaging than surrendering (2), and whether (5) is the commitment that must go if I wish to preserve both downward causation and methodological continuity.
