I'm designing a workshop session for about ten graduate students in metaphysics and epistemology—seventy-five minutes, one meeting, no assigned reading required beforehand. I'm trying to move them past familiar slogans about ontology and into the lived difficulty of identifying entities when information is structurally partitioned. To that end, please design a classroom activity—part structured game, part deliberation—that makes participants feel the force of what I'll frame as the "partitioned registry" problem: they must build a shared ontology of entities, but each subgroup possesses access to only one dimension of identifying information—spatio-temporal continuity, functional role, or compositional structure—and they are barred from exchanging raw descriptive data across these partitions. The mechanics should be arranged so that students make concrete identity judgments before they discover that their criteria depend on information permanently inaccessible to the other groups.

Please specify the group structure (three stations of three to four students), the materials (cards describing entities through single-dimension lenses, hidden "revelation" cards reserved for the debrief), the rules for proposing and voting on cross-partition identity claims, and a non-monetary payoff scheme—perhaps collective reputation points lost if approved identities conflict with revealed ground-truth cards—so that stakes feel real without requiring payment. Include a timeline for the seventy-five minutes, and design the rules so participants run into the coordination failure themselves: they should begin with confidence, encounter an irreconcilable conflict near the midpoint, and only then recognize they have been operating with incommensurable ontological criteria. The debrief should map these moments—first unanimous vote, the first cross-station contradiction, the attempt to negotiate shared vocabulary—onto the philosophical claim that ontology-building may be an artifact of accessible informational partitions rather than discovery of mind-independent entities.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run: for instance, one station monopolizing discussion, students circumventing the information barrier with coded descriptions, or the group resolving conflict too quickly by splitting the difference rather than feeling the philosophical tension. Give concrete facilitator moves to handle each failure without breaking the game's structure. Finally, include an explicit, honest limitation: note which feature of the actual philosophical problem—likely the metaphysical status of entities that genuinely transcend any observational partition—the activity necessarily fails to reproduce, since in the game hidden cards eventually provide a resolution that real-world ontology does not guarantee. Write in American English, keep the response to roughly four hundred fifty words, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or direct quotations from texts.
