I'm trying to get my anthropic reasoning commitments straight before presenting them in a seminar, and I want an audit of whether they hang together. Here is where I stand: (1) I accept the Self-Sampling Assumption—given that I am an observer, I should reason as if randomly sampled from my reference class of observers who share my available evidence. (2) I reject the Self-Indication Assumption, including the presumptuous philosopher objection: my own existence should not count as direct evidence for larger populations, since that licenses absurd inflationary conclusions about cosmological scale. (3) I think reference classes should be fixed by qualitative similarity of mental states rather than spatiotemporal location, so observers in distant galaxies should be excluded unless they are epistemically indistinguishable from me. (4) I hold that if two cosmological models share identical local structure and differ only in total observer count, SSA does not license preferring the larger model, because conditioning on my being in this local structure already fixes the sample space. (5) Yet I also think that if I am genuinely uncertain whether I am one of 10^10 or one of 10^30 observers with identical experiences, I should assign prior probabilities proportional to those counts, which feels like letting SIA in through the back door. Which pairs are in genuine tension versus only apparent? Does (5) misapply (1), or does it conflict with (2) in a way that forces me to drop one? Rank the tensions by severity, identify the cheapest revision that preserves the core of (1) and (2), and say which single change would clean up the most inconsistencies.
