Present the following philosophy-of-logic exam item and the student response that follows. The topic is logical induction: the framework in which rational prediction markets assign prices to logical sentences and converge, in the limit, to correct indicators of provability within a formal system such as Peano Arithmetic.

Question: The logical induction criterion guarantees that market prices converge to 0 or 1 according to whether a sentence is disprovable or provable. Consider a self-referential sentence S, formulated in Peano Arithmetic augmented with a predicate referring to the market price of S itself, that asserts: “The price of S will never reach within ε of either 0 or 1, for any rational ε > 0.” Explain why the existence of such an S does not undermine the convergence guarantee, and carefully distinguish this resolution from the standard classical response to the liar paradox.

Student answer: Logical induction avoids paradox here because the market is not a belief state but a betting mechanism. Rational traders observe that if S’s price remains stuck in (δ, 1−δ), they can construct a risk-free arbitrage by simultaneously buying contracts that pay on S’s falsity and selling contracts that pay on S’s truth, since exactly one must eventually be provable. This forces convergence. The mechanism is essentially Bayesian: each trader begins with a subjective prior over all arithmetical sentences and updates on newly discovered proofs, so the aggregate market behaves like a single Bayesian reasoner with a uniform prior. By the reflection principle, if a sentence has a proof of length L, the market price reaches 1−ε by day L. Hence S is treated like any other sentence—its price eventually collapses once traders recognize the arbitrage, and the self-reference is neutralized by treating the price oracle as lying outside the formal system being evaluated.
