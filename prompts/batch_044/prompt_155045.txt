Consider the institutional design—exemplified by Switzerland’s militia-based defence, Sweden’s reinstated selective conscription, and Finland’s longstanding universal male conscription—where the state relies not on a large standing professional force but on distributed obligation across the civilian population, often coupled historically with doctrines of armed neutrality or military non-alignment. In the Swiss case, conscripts retain service equipment within the domestic sphere; the citizen is therefore not merely a taxpayer funding defence but a direct bearer of coercive capacity embedded in civil life.

What philosophical commitments does this design presuppose about the nature of citizenship, the legitimacy of coercion, and the moral status of neutrality? In particular, does it presuppose that democratic sovereignty requires the diffusion rather than the centralisation of lethal force, or can a liberal state justify concentrating violence in a professional caste while still claiming to represent those who bear no direct military burden? Furthermore, if one holds that moral equality requires that the risks of defence be shared universally rather than outsourced, does armed neutrality become a necessary expression of that equality, or can it be reconciled with a professional army that insulates the majority from the costs of neutrality? And does the practice of storing arms within civilian households presuppose a particular conception of trust between state and subject—one that treats the citizen as a co-sovereign rather than as a potential threat requiring disarmament?
