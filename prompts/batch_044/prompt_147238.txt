Following a prolonged argument with a close friend about whether rationality ever permits me to hold firm against an equally qualified peer who reaches the opposite conclusion, I need a practical framework I can actually deploy. I serve as the chief review officer for a national regulatory agency that evaluates emerging chemical and technological risks, and we are now required to issue recommendations whenever two independent expert panels submit contradictory assessments of the same body of evidence. The disagreements are rarely about explicit values; they typically concern model selection, the weighting of observational versus experimental data, hidden prior commitments about dose-response relationships, and what I will call nested disagreement—cases where the panels do not contest a single claim but contest which evaluative framework should govern the assessment at all. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in what the epistemology of peer disagreement actually establishes about conciliationism and steadfastness, that my reviewers can run within ten working days. It should specify the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation, and where it must hand the decision to a human judgment call because the remaining dispute is about values, institutional legitimacy, or prior commitments that reason alone cannot adjudicate. The procedure must include diagnostic tests for genuine epistemic peerhood versus hidden asymmetry—such as proprietary data access, undisclosed institutional incentives, or methodological commitments one party cannot independently evaluate—and it must handle cases where applying it produces a recommendation my office will find unwelcome. Provide worked applications to three scenarios: a straightforward statistical interpretation dispute in which both panels share identical data; a case involving proprietary industry-funded studies that creates an asymmetry the procedure is designed to flag; and a nested-framework dispute in which the procedure forces a conciliation that overrides what appears to be a clearly superior evidentiary position. Include an explicit account of what the procedure cannot do, particularly regarding disagreements that turn on fundamental prior commitments rather than shared evidence, and specify who must sign off at each gate.
