A scholar applying Kierkegaard's distinction between "the public"—the purely quantitative, leveling abstraction that dissolves individual responsibility through anonymity and numerical aggregation—and genuine communal responsibility has classified several modern communicative phenomena. Her verdicts are: a nineteenth-century mass-circulation newspaper printing unsigned opinions and measuring success by subscriber numbers, yes; a local town council in which every speaker is personally known and answerable, no; a double-blind scientific peer-review process that assigns qualitative evaluation to specific, accountable reviewers, no; a global viral hashtag campaign in which millions participate fleetingly without sustained commitment, yes; a named labor delegation bargaining face-to-face with an employer, no; an anonymous imageboard in which visibility is governed solely by upvote counts and users maintain no persistent identity, yes. Now consider a decentralized blockchain governance protocol in which every vote is permanently linked to a pseudonymous wallet address, participation is open globally without qualification or deliberative structure, and outcomes are determined by pure token-weighted numerical aggregation. Predict her verdict, articulate the precise rule she is implicitly running—carefully distinguishing the roles of anonymity, quantitative aggregation, absence of deliberation, and dissolution of qualitative responsibility—identify at least one alternative rule equally consistent with her earlier judgments, and specify the single further case that would best distinguish her rule from that alternative.
