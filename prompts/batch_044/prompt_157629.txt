I'm drafting a long-form piece for the science and ideas desk on the hidden architecture of collective choice, and I've been working through Amartya Sen's liberal paradox — specifically, whether the contradiction between minimal liberty and the Pareto principle survives when we shift the domain of rights from personal actions to the constitutional rules that govern them. In the standard formulation, two individuals each hold liberal rights over private choices, yet each also holds preferences over the other's choice, and a society that respects both Pareto efficiency and minimal liberty is driven into an intransitive social ordering.

My essay notes propose a reflexive constitutional extension: suppose a polity must choose among frameworks R1, R2, and R3, where each assigns different liberal rights to citizens. Every citizen has a minimal liberty right over which framework governs them personally, but citizens also hold preferences over which framework governs others — perhaps because they believe certain frameworks corrupt public reasoning. Applying the Pareto principle across preferences over constitutional rules, while honouring each citizen's right to be governed by their preferred framework, reproduces Sen's cyclical contradiction at the meta-level: society cannot consistently rank the three frameworks without violating either Pareto efficiency or minimal constitutional liberty.

I then argue that the resolution requires restricting minimal liberty to direct action spaces rather than governance structures — but I worry this is ad hoc, saving consistency only by rendering liberal rights politically trivial, and that it conflates first-order preferences over rules with second-order social choice over them. Could you comment on these notes? Specifically, I would value your assessment of: (1) whether the reflexive constitutional version is faithful to the logical structure of Sen's original proof, or whether it introduces an ambiguity about preference orderings; (2) whether confining rights to direct action spaces genuinely dissolves the contradiction or merely relocates it into the classification of institutional structures; and (3) whether the central distinction between rights over outcomes and rights over rules holds up, or collapses under scrutiny. I am particularly interested in comments on logical coherence, conceptual accuracy, and whether this extension is worth developing into a full essay — please reply in British English.
