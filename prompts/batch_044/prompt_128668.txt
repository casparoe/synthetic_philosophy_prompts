hey, been stuck on this argument about mechanistic anomaly detection—y'know, the idea that you flag something not just because it's statistically weird but because it's wrong relative to how the mechanism is *supposed* to work. I think it proves too much.

(1) To call a state mechanistically anomalous you need a refernce mechanism that defines normal operation.

(2) Two systems are the same mechanism for detection only if they share identical causal-mechanical structure: same parts arranged with same activities and organizational relations.

(3) Take a physical turbine and its digital twin running a physics simulation with perfect fidelity. The twin reproduces every relevant relation—pressure differentials cause virtual blade stress, stress causes virtual deformation, etc.

(4) So by (2) the twin and turbine are literally the same mechanism.

(5) Therefore an anomaly detected in one should be an anomaly in the other.

(6) But the twin can suffer anomalies with no physical analog: a floating-point rounding error that fakes a vibration spike, or a bit-flip that simulates a crack that never happened in metal.

(7) If those are real mechanistic anomalies in the twin, and the twin is the same mechanism, then monitoring the turbine through the twin should flag them for the physical system too.

(8) The turbine obviously can't have a floating-point error.

(9) So either digital twins are useless for mechanistic detection—which kills half the field—or (2) is false and material substrate breaks mechanism identity.

(10) But if replacing a worn gear with an identical new one makes it a different mechanism, then every repair destroys your baseline, and historical data is garbage.

(11) So mechanistic anomaly detection either collapses into pure statistical outlier hunting, or it can't survive routine maintenance. Where's the exit?

Which premise do you reject, and what does giving it up cost? I want to keep (2) if possible, but then I need to explain why (3) fails without making all modeling impossible.
