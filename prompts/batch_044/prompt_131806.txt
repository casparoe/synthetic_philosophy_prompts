I am drafting a novel of literary science fiction in which scientific thought experiments occur as lived narrative episodes—scenes in which characters reason through imagined apparatuses or biological impossibilities not as metaphors but as attempts to extend conceptual understanding. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophy of science on how thought experiments yield knowledge, that I can run on any proposed scene before committing it to manuscript. The procedure must be executable in about thirty minutes by someone without formal philosophical training; it should take the form of an ordered checklist with explicit stopping conditions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, and clearly flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be referred either to my science consultant or to my own narrative authority. It must distinguish scenes that genuinely function as argument-based or Platonic thought experiments—yielding conceptual knowledge about an existing theory—from scenes that are merely facilitative illustrations, empirically ungrounded hypotheses, or category mistakes disguised as reasoning.

Please construct the procedure by translating the core philosophical distinctions—between argument-reconstruction accounts, Platonic-access-through-idealization accounts, and the conditions under which imaginative scenarios smuggle hidden empirical premises—into practical tests that a practicing writer can apply. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Then apply the procedure step by step to three narrative proposals I am considering.

First, a scene in which a blind physicist derives the relativity of simultaneity using only a tactile train-model and mental arithmetic, with no instrumental observation—an eliminative idealization of a classic relativistic case, but stripped of all visual intuition and rendered purely through haptic reasoning.

Second, a scene in which a biologist imagines a symbiotic organism that transmits acquired neural patterns to offspring via epigenetic encoding in gut microbiota, explicitly framing the scenario as a conceptual test of whether the Weismann barrier can be dissolved in principle rather than violated empirically.

Third, a scene to which I am particularly attached—one I hope will work—in which a quantum observer becomes phenomenologically entangled with a particle and subjectively experiences superposition, with the narrative treating this first-person report as bearing directly on the measurement problem.

For each case, run the procedure, identify the closest philosophical analog if relevant, and deliver a final verdict: whether the scene yields conceptual knowledge, yields only a testable empirical hypothesis, functions as pedagogical illustration, or fails altogether. Ensure that the quantum-entanglement scene receives a negative verdict, and that the procedure makes the reason for that rejection explicit rather than intuitive. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do: the boundary conditions under which narrative imagination exceeds its epistemic authority, and the categories of scientific question that must be handed off to empirical work regardless of how compelling the fiction is.
