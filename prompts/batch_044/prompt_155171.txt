As a novelist drafting a book about historians who dispute the origins of the Cold War, I keep noticing a rhetorical pattern for which I lack a precise name. A scholar will establish a structural framework—stressing security dilemmas, misread signals, and the tragedy of mutual suspicion—thereby dissolving blame into systemic inevitability. Yet the moment the narrative reaches a particular decision, such as the framing of the Truman Doctrine or the management of the Berlin crisis, the same voice abruptly grants full intentionality and moral culpability to one actor, while the opposing side’s parallel conduct remains safely embedded in explanatory context. The shift is not merely chronological; it toggles within the same argumentative breath, deployed defensively to protect a preferred verdict without acknowledging that the standard of judgement has changed. I have observed it in accounts that treat American containment as a deliberate ideological offensive, whilst Soviet consolidation in Eastern Europe is read as an understandable reaction to insecurity, and in mirror-image arguments that reverse the assignment. Is there an established concept in historiography or philosophy of history for this selective oscillation between systemic and agential blame? How does it differ from ordinary presentism, or from applying the fundamental attribution error to states rather than individuals? If no precise term exists, I want a committed coinage with clear boundary conditions—what distinguishes it from a legitimate change in analytical scale, and at what point does it become a methodological vice rather than a narrative necessity? I am not seeking a neutral survey of interpretations; I ask for a definite position: does this pattern fatally undermine the question of who started the conflict, or is it an unavoidable feature of writing moral history about structural wars?
