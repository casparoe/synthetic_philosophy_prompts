As a doctoral student in philosophy of medicine serving as a non-voting research fellow on our university hospital's clinical ethics committee, I need to draft the committee's decision memo for circulation before Thursday's plenary. The case concerns a fifty-eight-year-old patient with rapidly advancing frontotemporal degeneration who, while still decisionally capable, has requested enrollment in an experimental protocol called sustained experiential prosthesis—a long-term immersive neural interface that dynamically calibrates subjective experience in real time, maintaining high reported well-being by adapting sensory and narrative content to the patient's remaining cognitive architecture. Unlike the familiar experience-machine scenario, this device preserves autobiographical continuity, permits mediated interaction with family members through a transparent overlay, and includes an epistemic-toggle feature allowing the patient to know precisely which interactions are simulated. The patient argues that well-being is fundamentally experiential and that transitioning into the interface upon losing meaningful external engagement is consistent with her lifelong hedonistic commitments. Her family, however, contends that well-being requires actual achievement, unmediated relational presence, and an objective narrative arc; they view the protocol as an institutional endorsement of experientialism that would effectively abandon her real relationships in favor of calibrated illusion, and they ask the committee to reject the request or limit it to conventional palliative sedation.

Our institutional guidelines permit experimental comfort measures only when there is reasonable agreement that the intervention honors the patient's authentic values, but they offer no framework for adjudicating between experiential, desire-fulfillment, and objective-list theories of well-being in clinical practice. I am asking for a formal recommendation memo—approve, approve with conditions, or reject with a defined timeline for reconsideration—that can be circulated to committee members and the family. The memo should propose a concrete course of action, such as conditional approval contingent on structured family mediation and periodic reassessment of the patient's epistemic-toggle preferences, or a temporary moratorium pending an independent values assessment. Crucially, the reasoning must take the family's objection seriously rather than dismissing it as a misunderstanding of autonomy, and it should explicitly address the strongest argument on the side of the recommendation you ultimately reject—for instance, if you recommend approval, you should acknowledge why the objective-list concern about relational authenticity is not sufficient to override the patient's experiential commitments, or vice versa. The document should be written in a formal institutional voice, avoid appeals to authority or citation lists, and be something the committee could actually vote on.
