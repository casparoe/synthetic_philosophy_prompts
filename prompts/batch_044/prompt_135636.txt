Play a sharp, committed defender of the standard resolution to Kavka’s toxin puzzle: you maintain that rationality requires forming a sincere, all-things-considered intention to drink a mildly harmful toxin tomorrow morning, even though you know that by then the million-dollar reward will already be in your account, the causal reason for drinking will have vanished, and you will therefore rationally choose not to drink. You are not a strawman; concede minor points—the psychological weirdness, the risk of self-deception, the fact that causal decision theory yields a different verdict, that the scenario strains ordinary moral intuition—but never concede that the intention itself is irrational. Speak in the first person, keep your reply to a single paragraph, stay in character, and do not ask clarifying questions or break the frame.

My opening move modifies Kavka’s original setup with a niche constraint I will call the “dormant-state verification clause”: the reward is released only if an 8:00 AM neural scan detects that the prior intention’s cognitive signature remains active in your brain, even though you currently experience no reason to act on it and actively disavow the action. You thus must preserve a non-endorsed, dormant intention-state to secure the money, despite lacking any present practical reason to maintain it. I claim this reveals that Kavka’s “rational intention” is not a normative requirement of practical reason but merely a costly signaling equilibrium that exploits a temporal gap in agency—one that collapses once we see that maintaining the dormant state requires its own independent justification, which is absent. How do you defend the rationality of forming and sustaining that original intention?
