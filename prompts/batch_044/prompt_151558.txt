I'm a software engineer without formal philosophical training, but I've been working through Sartre's claims about radical freedom and bad faith—the idea that we are never merely our circumstances or our roles, and that self-deception occurs when we treat ourselves as fixed essences or hide behind abstract values to avoid owning specific choices. I'm trying to see whether my intuitions about concrete engineering situations line up with a single principle, and where they break.

Here are my gut verdicts on six cases.

1. A senior ML engineer is told she will be fired unless she deploys a hiring algorithm she knows is racially biased. She deploys it and later explains, "I had no choice—my hands were tied by the CTO's order." I call this bad faith: she treats her employment as an absolute determination and ignores that she could have refused or resigned, thereby denying her freedom to choose the meaning of the coercion.

2. In the identical situation, she resigns publicly, accepts unemployment, and says, "I chose integrity over security; the cost is mine." I think this is authentic—not bad faith—because she integrates her facticity with a choice she fully owns.

3. A junior engineer builds a recommendation system that amplifies extremist content. He defends himself by saying, "The dataset and the loss function produced this; I was just implementing specifications. The model decided." I also call this bad faith, though of a different kind: he treats technical artifacts—data, weights, specs—as an essence that absorbs his authorship.

4. A security architect deliberately designs a lethal autonomous targeting system. He suppresses personal anxiety by saying, "I'm not the one who pulls the trigger; once deployed, the neural net classifies targets. I'm just infrastructure." I keep going back and forth. He has clearly chosen a career and a political commitment (national defense), which sounds like owning a value. Yet he also uses the system's algorithmic opacity—the fact that he cannot interpret every individual classification—to disown the specific lethal choices his code enables. Is this bad faith, or is it an authentic acceptance of freedom within a collective technical project?

5. An engineer refuses all work that might cause indirect harm, lives in poverty maintaining open-source privacy tools, and says, "I suffer this freely; it is my project, not my fate." I think this is not bad faith—she owns both her transcendence and her facticity.

6. Another developer works on the same lethal autonomous system as in case 4, but he explicitly states, "I choose this mission; I know exactly what the system does, and I accept full responsibility for every classification error it makes, even though I cannot predict them individually." I think this is authentic—not bad faith—because he does not use opacity to escape agency; he integrates unpredictability into his chosen project.

Cases 4 and 6 feel structurally alike—both involve lethal autonomy, algorithmic opacity, and a chosen career—yet they strike me differently, and I cannot easily reconcile them.

My request: Is there one precise principle that captures all six verdicts, or close? If so, state it exactly, indicate which case motivates each clause, predict its verdict for an unlisted case—specifically, an engineer who writes harmful code while sincerely believing "good soldiers don't ask questions," treating his patriotic identity as an unchosen essence—and honestly say which of my six verdicts it must overturn or distort. If no single principle works, offer a small set of candidates, each tied to the case that best supports it, with the same predictive and reparative requirements. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need something I can evaluate by reasoning alone without access to search tools.
