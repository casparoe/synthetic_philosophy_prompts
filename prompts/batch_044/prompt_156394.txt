I have been trying to understand the strange inversion shared by Darwin and Turing: in one case, design emerges without a designer's comprehension; in the other, correct behavior emerges without understanding. I would like to read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through this lens of competence without comprehension. Does the novel present Victor's scientific labor as a kind of blind, evolutionary assembly—competent in technique but lacking comprehension of what he is actually creating? And does it present the Creature's rapid acquisition of language and strategy as a parallel inversion, a functional intelligence that operates without human understanding? I want to know which scenes best illuminate these two forms, and where the text resists the comparison—perhaps by insisting that comprehension eventually must arrive, or by showing that the Creature's competence is inseparable from suffering that pure mechanism cannot explain. Finally, if this reading holds, what does the novel contribute to the philosophical debate that the arguments about natural selection and machine intelligence alone do not?
