so me and two friends got into this at a rationalist meetup after someone brought up jaspers' four kinds of guilt. the question was whether a nation can literally be guilty, or if thats just sloppy metaphor.

friend A said yes, but only in a structural, non-psychological sense. she called it diachronic institutional guilt: if institutions carry obligations across generations (like treaties or debts), they can also carry unrepaired wrongs. she said jaspers' political guilt is basically this -- citizens collectively bear the state's moral status, not because they feel bad, but because they're part of an ongoing entity that hasn't made amends. she was clear its not inherited like a genetic stain; its that the institution itself is still in the wrong-state.

friend B said thats just responsability in a guilt-costume. he said guilt is necessarily first-person and affective -- you feel it, or it applies to agents who could have done otherwise. citizens born after 1945 literally couldnt have acted otherwise. he said calling it guilt instead of liability does rhetorical work: it makes current people feel personally stained, which is the logic behind collective punishment. he accused A of smuggling in agency by talking about "the entity" -- entities dont feel, and without affect or agency its not guilt, its bookkeeping.

A came back: youre assuming guilt has to be affective. but we talk about objective guilt all the time -- being in the wrong regardless of feelings. she said B was confusing guilt with shame. her point was that if the institution persists and the wrong is unrepaired, the wrong-state persists with it. its not about citizens inheriting guilt like property; its that they participate in a structure that owes repair.

B said okay but objective guilt applies to actions, not to groups over time. he said theres a difference between "this state owes reparations" and "this state is guilty." the first is about remedy, the second is about moral status. he said A was committing a reification fallacy, treating a social construct as if it had a moral biography. he pushed the boundary: if this works, is my family guilty because my great-grandfather stole something? is humanity guilty?

A said those cases can be blocked with conditions -- continuity of institutional identity, democratic participation, benefit from the wrong. she said B's reification charge misses that we already treat groups as moral agents elsewhere (corporate liability, state responsibility).

they asked me to adjudicate. who's right at each step? is B correct that guilt is being misused? is A right that theres a coherent non-affective version? and does the institutional-continuity vs individual-innocence distinction actually hold up, or are they both missing something about collective agency?

if this exact tension has a name in philosophy -- the gap between institutional persistence and non-transferable moral affect -- tell me what it is. if not, say so. and please dont just declare a winner; go move by move and say what each side is getting right and wrong.
