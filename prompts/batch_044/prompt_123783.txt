You need a reusable fifteen-minute editorial procedure that non-logician staff can run to flag whether a submission's definition implicitly relies on unrestricted naive comprehension in a Russell-paradox-risky way, without requiring formal proof reconstruction.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of practical tests: the specific linguistic structures that trigger scrutiny, the follow-up distinctions that show whether the collection is safely restricted by type, hierarchy, or domain boundary, and the exact escalation points where staff must hand the decision to an editor because the issue turns on philosophical judgment rather than form. Be explicit about false positives—cases where the checklist flags safe stratified constructions—and about hidden-paradox risks that vague qualifiers like "all genuinely possible" conceal. Then apply the procedure to three submissions: (1) a paper defining "the set of mental representations that do not represent themselves"; (2) a paper defining "the framework's own class of unsolvable problems within its domain"; and (3) a paper using "the collection of all true propositions about this very collection." For each, state the checklist verdict, mark where staff judgment must override the procedure, and identify the one case where the procedure permits publication despite an underlying tension you will dislike. Conclude by stating two substantive limitations: things the procedure cannot determine even in principle.
