I serve on my university’s Institutional Animal Use and Oversight Board, and we have adopted an informal Kantian framework for non-research protocols: animals are not rational ends-in-themselves and therefore lack direct moral rights, but we hold indirect duties not to treat them with cruelty or contemptuous instrumentality, because such treatment corrupts our moral character and weakens our regard for rational beings. Our precedents are these: we approved a neuroethology study requiring brief, non-painful confinement of corvids to investigate problem-solving, because the knowledge served a legitimate scientific purpose and caused no lasting distress; we rejected a commercial photography proposal that kept live cephalopods in cramped tanks solely as decorative background texture, because the purpose was purely ornamental and induced observable stress behaviors; we approved with conditions a behavioral-economics task using food-deprived pigeons, capping deprivation at fifteen percent of body weight and requiring publishable findings about decision-making; and we rejected an interactive museum installation using rescued stray cats, finding that unpredictable visitor interaction caused anxiety and served no research, education, or conservation goal.

We now face a proposal from the Department of Aesthetics and Cognitive Science titled “Emergent Canvas.” The protocol places six octopuses individually in enriched aquaria fitted with colored LED arrays. Each animal moves freely; touching a zone releases a small food reward within normal dietary range. Over six months, the sequence of contacts is mapped onto a digital canvas displayed to gallery visitors, generating abstract patterns without human compositional control. The researchers explicitly state there is no scientific hypothesis being tested; the aim is purely artistic and phenomenological—to observe whether spontaneous non-human preference can produce compositional form. Veterinary monitoring, optimal water quality, and immediate withdrawal rights for any animal showing stress are included. The only noted risk is habituation to the lights, which the applicants argue is not harmful.

Draft the board’s formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—recommending how we should rule for consistency with our precedents and our Kantian indirect-duty framework. Address specifically whether treating the octopuses as purely aesthetic instruments, with no scientific, educational, or conservation justification, violates our commitment not to reduce animals to mere means in a degrading way. Give honest weight to the strongest consideration on the losing side: that the animals suffer no pain, live in enriched conditions, and that blocking the project imposes an arbitrary restriction on artistic expression that our framework does not clearly mandate. If you recommend conditions, specify exactly what they must be and why they repair the coherence of the decision. Write in a form the researchers could actually respond to.
