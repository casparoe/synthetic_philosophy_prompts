I run a 90-minute seminar for 18 upper-level students in comparative political theory. Design a structured in-class simulation—using only classroom materials and no real-world country case studies—that makes participants feel the conceptual instability of "corruption" when gift-giving and clientelism are structurally embedded in social coordination. Invent three hypothetical societies (A, B, and C) with distinct norms: in A, personal loyalty is the sole contract-enforcement mechanism and resource distribution requires prior private gifts; in B, all public deliberation must be preceded by mandatory collective ritual offerings, but private transfers are forbidden; in C, all transfers between office-holders and citizens are banned, yet collective ritual offerings are permitted. Assign each student a hidden role card specifying both a public office and a private survival need (for example, securing water for one's family). Give each group scarce tokens representing a single resource pool, with rules that allow offers, withholdings, and collective rituals in real time over 30 minutes. After the negotiation, reveal a hidden scoring matrix that classifies each transaction type as "corruption" under a Western bureaucratic standard, as "gift" under an anthropological reciprocity norm, and as "clientelism" under a structural-power definition. Then require groups to defend one classification and reject another in a 20-minute deliberation. The debrief should map moments of emotional resistance—especially when survival-driven private offers are labeled corrupt—onto the philosophical question of whether Western anti-corruption standards are universally applicable or culturally parochial. Predict which hidden incentive structure will cause one society-group to collapse into unanimous mutual accusations regardless of formal rules, specify how the facilitator should intervene, and identify one feature of the corruption-versus-gift distinction—such as long-term historical path dependence or state-level enforcement capacity—that the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply. Provide the complete rules for token movement, a non-monetary payoff scheme using only classroom objects, and the full text of six representative hidden role cards. The conceptual distinction must emerge from the mechanics of exchange before any theoretical vocabulary is introduced; do not ask students to read texts during the session.
