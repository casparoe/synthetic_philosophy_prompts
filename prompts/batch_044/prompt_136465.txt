I've been noticing something strange about how I learn with AI. I ask a model to explain a complicated idea—say, a philosophical argument or a scientific concept—and it gives me a clear, step-by-step answer. I read it, I nod along, and I feel like I understand. But a day later, if someone asks me about it, I can barely reconstruct the reasoning. It feels less like I've learned something and more like I've borrowed someone else's finished map.

I mentioned this to two friends. One said I'm being too hard on myself: understanding just means having access to a correct explanation, whether it's in my head or in a chat history; the point is accuracy, not suffering through confusion. The other said that's exactly wrong—understanding isn't possession of a correct string of sentences, but the capacity to navigate the idea from a state of genuine confusion, and if I never do that navigation myself, I'm not understanding, I'm just caching.

Write a dialogue between these two friends—call them Sam and Jordan—where they argue over whether depending on AI explanations erodes real understanding. Sam should defend the view that correct explanation is sufficient and that requiring personal struggle romanticizes difficulty unnecessarily. Jordan should defend the view that understanding requires being able to rebuild the reasoning from confusion, and that AI explanations risk functioning as replacements rather than scaffolds for that capacity. They should respond to each other's actual points: Sam might ask whether Jordan's standard condemns textbooks or lectures too, and Jordan should distinguish tools that guide reconstruction from those that substitute for it. Make both sides equally sharp, with no strawmen.
