I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether art-status is conferred by an institution or by properties intrinsic to the object, and we kept returning to a scenario neither standard example resolves. I call it the reverse-readymade: a work that has been displayed as canonical art for decades is formally revoked by the same institution that originally conferred its status, not because the physical object changed, but because the board decides the artist's later conduct disqualifies the work from the canon. The friend insisted the object is still art; I wasn't sure. Now I want my students to feel that instability rather than just discuss it. I run an upper-level aesthetics seminar—twelve students, ninety minutes, all philosophy majors familiar with institutional theories but not with this reversal case. Design a structured in-class simulation in which participants collectively write the rules for art-status conferral before they see the revocation scenario, then must apply their rules to vote on whether the object retains art-status. The rules, roles, and voting mechanism should be arranged so that the group runs into a coordination failure or circular dependency—perhaps between curatorial authority and public aesthetic judgment—before anyone names the institutional circularity problem. Include specific roles (e.g., board curators, estate representatives, independent critics, public voters), a payoff or point scheme that requires no real money, materials needed, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each crisis point in the activity onto a corresponding part of Dickie's or Danto's institutional framework and onto rival intrinsic-property accounts. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time—say, the group collapses to a simple majority vote too quickly—and how to prevent it. Also be frank about which feature of the actual philosophical debate, such as the historical-intentional constraint on artistic identity, the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by me in the debrief. The students should leave having experienced the pull of both the institutional and the anti-institutional intuition, not just having heard them named.
