I serve on an institutional review board for a public-science funder that receives frequent proposals to validate new measurement instruments—psychological screening tools, automated text-classifiers, clinical risk scores—and I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in about thirty minutes per proposal, without statistical expertise beyond what a generalist auditor can apply, because I have to sign off on funding recommendations. Too often a proposal validates an instrument by applying it to generate data, then uses those generated classifications as the criterion against which the instrument is tested: a circularity that may look like legitimate calibration but can be an illegitimate bootstrap in the epistemological sense, especially in cases of so-called easy knowledge where a source is used to establish the very warrant it needs. To operationalize this, the procedure should distinguish first-order bootstrapping—using the instrument’s own outputs as the direct validation criterion—from second-order bootstrapping—using prior outputs to shape the independent-looking criterion that then validates later versions—and it should apply an independent-anchor requirement: at least one node in the validation chain must not be causally or informationally downstream of the instrument’s own prior outputs.

Write the procedure as an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear reject or clear pass, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values or stakes rather than logical structure. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure, flag exactly where the procedure is likely to misfire—for example, when an apparently independent criterion was subtly calibrated by exposure to the instrument’s outputs without explicit acknowledgment—and state two things the procedure absolutely cannot do, such as establishing the instrument’s base-rate reliability in the wild or resolving whether a small residual circularity is tolerable given high-stakes intervention.

Then apply it to three proposals: (1) a depression-screening app validated by comparing its scores to clinician diagnoses that were made using the app’s preliminary risk flags as part of the clinical intake form; (2) a literacy-assessment tool validated against teacher ratings where teachers were trained using sample items drawn from the tool itself, but the final ratings used unseen texts; and (3) a widely supported, high-budget climate-adaptation survey instrument validated against expert annotations that are genuinely independent in origin, but where the procedure detects a hidden dependency because the expert panel’s coding manual incorporated terminology introduced by an earlier pilot of the same survey—this should be the unwelcome case, since the proposal is politically popular and well-funded, but your procedure should flag it or reject it, and you should say what the auditor must do with that result.

Lead with the bottom line—the full procedure summarized in one paragraph—then expand with the checklist, the judgment points, the misfire warnings, the three worked cases, and the limits. Keep the entire response to a maximum of 850 words. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation list; refer to theoretical distinctions by descriptive name only, and do not quote any specific text.
