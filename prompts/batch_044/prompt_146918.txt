As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy with any seriousness, I have been working through Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction in Two Dogmas of Empiricism. In my former field we constantly draw a practical line between conventional choices—unit systems, coordinate frames, gauge conditions—and substantive claims that experiment can overturn; Quine's argument that no statement is fully immune to revision, and that the boundary is at best pragmatic, seems to blur this line in a way that unsettles my intuition about scientific change. Please describe the most important implications of Quine's position for how I should understand the difference, if any survives, between conventional choice and empirical discovery when evaluating physics from outside the discipline. Does his holism erase the distinction entirely for practical judgment, or can it be reconstructed in a weaker form? I am not asking for an exposition of Quine; I want to know what his view demands of someone trying to assess whether abandoning a coordinate convention and abandoning a disconfirmed law are genuinely different kinds of intellectual move.
