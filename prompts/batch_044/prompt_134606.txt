The long-running dispute between defenders of Block’s access/phenomenal distinction and global-workspace theorists—about whether phenomenal consciousness can exist without cognitive access and whether broadcasting to a global workspace is sufficient for phenomenology—feels stalled: both sides invoke the same no-report paradigms and backward-masking data, reply with the same charge that reportability is a conservative criterion, and neither updates. I suspect the deadlock is partly verbal, since “access” slides between reportability, reasoning-availability, and global broadcast; partly substantive, about whether the target is the intrinsic quality of experience or the functional architecture that makes it available; and partly hidden, because both sides assume working-memory capacity sets the boundary of phenomenology but disagree on whether that boundary is a contingent bottleneck or constitutive of consciousness itself. Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says about overflow cases and about attention’s role in binding. Is there a disguised empirical question—say, whether prefrontal broadcasting is necessary for any conscious content—or are the sides answering different questions (ontology versus information flow) under one heading? If your diagnosis holds, what would move it: a conceptual split, such as distinguishing synchronic broadcast to consumer systems from diachronic integration into a narrative self-model; a specific neural result about fronto-parietal connectivity in no-report conditions; or an argument that the question is ill-posed? Say concretely what progress would look like and whether anything should be expected.
