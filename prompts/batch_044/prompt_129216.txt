After a long argument with a friend about Friedman's F-twist, I sketched the following chain to show that it proves too much, and now I cannot find the exit. I want to keep (1) and (3) if I can, and I think (5) is faithful to the literature, but I am willing to weaken (2) if that is where the damage lies.

(1) Friedman's F-twist holds that the unrealistic character of a model's assumptions is never, by itself, a methodological defect: only predictive failure counts against a model.

(2) If so, predictive success is not merely necessary but sufficient for retaining a model's ontological commitments, at least provisionally.

(3) The Ptolemaic astronomical system employed geocentric assumptions that are false, yet it predicted planetary positions with considerable accuracy for centuries.

(4) Therefore, on the F-twist, there was no methodological reason to reject Ptolemaic astronomy on ontological grounds; its false core was harmless so long as predictions held.

(5) Now consider a macroeconomic model in which every agent performs an infinite-horizon continuous-time expected-utility optimization that no human brain could execute; the model predicts aggregate consumption patterns well.

(6) By (1) and (2), any objection based on psychological impossibility must be dismissed, no matter how extreme the implausibility, provided predictions remain intact.

(7) Extend the reasoning to a constructed climate-forecasting algorithm that treats atmospheric heat as a discrete binary lattice with no fluid dynamics, yet happens to match regional temperature data for a decade.

(8) Since the algorithm predicts successfully, the F-twist seems to forbid rejecting it even when its causal mechanism is not merely simplified but demonstrably false and non-explanatory.

(9) But then the twist collapses the distinction between representational modeling and pure curve-fitting, and it makes scientific theory change—such as abandoning caloric theory—unmotivated except by predictive breakdown.

(10) So either the F-twist proves too much by licensing every predictively successful fiction, including astrological correlations that happen to hold in a sample, or it secretly depends on a criterion—explanatory depth, structural correspondence, or domain robustness—that Friedman officially rejects.

Which step should I reject? Is the error in (2)'s move from "not a defect" to "sufficient"? Is (4) wrong because Friedman allows comparative evaluation—so Ptolemy should be dropped when Copernicus predicts better, not because geocentrism is false? Or is the real damage in (8), where I assume that local predictive success protects a model globally? If I weaken (2) rather than abandon (1), does the F-twist become a trivial claim that false assumptions do not matter unless predictions fail, or does it open the door to non-predictive criteria of model choice? I would like a diagnosis that names the step, explains why it looked plausible, and prices the alternatives—including what I pay if I keep (1) but surrender (2).
