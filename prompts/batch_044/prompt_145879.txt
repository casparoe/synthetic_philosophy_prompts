I'm working on a novel about a sleep lab in the mountains — kind of a gothic science thing — and I got into an argument with my writing group over cheap red wine about whether dreams actually count as experiences. I don't want to just recycle the usual Malcolm/Dennett stuff, so I'm testing my own gut on concrete cases to see if there's a hidden principle. Here are my verdicts, and I'm warning you they're a bit messy.

If I wake up remembering I was flying over a city, that feels like a real experiance, no doubt — my body was in bed but I was "there" in the sky. But if a sleep study shows full REM waves and the subject wakes up insisting they were in total mental blankness, I lean toward saying they didn't actually experience anything; the brain was just doing housekeeping. If someone is hypnotized while awake and vividly describes walking on a beach, that's guided imagination to me, not a dream experience, since they're responsive and conscious in the usual way. A patient under deep anaesthesia who accidentally shows REM-like spikes but never wakes up? Definately no experience — it's just electrical noise without a subject. Then there's the lucid dreamer who realizes she's dreaming and stops the plot to float in pure black emptiness; she reports feeling present in the void. I think that's still an experience, even of nothing. The one that keeps breaking me is my friend with sleep apnea: her heart rate spikes, she gasps awake, and remembers zero mental content — not even fear, just the physical jolt afterward. Part of me says there must have been a hidden panic-experience she instantly forgot; part of me says it was pure bodily alarm with no subjective feel at all, and I flip-flop every time.

Is there one principle that holds all these together, or do I have to drop one? I want a committed answer — pick a side, defend it, and tell me exactly which of my verdicts it can't save. Also, in my book there's a "dream replay" machine that copies one sleeper's REM signature into another person's brain; the recipient wakes with the full narrative memory but says it felt like watching a movie, not living through it. What does your principle predict about that case? Don't just lay out options — take a stand and reason it out.
