I'm in an upper-division philosophy of art seminar, and two classmates—Maya and Julian—got into a dispute during section about whether an artist's immoral biography can diminish a work's aesthetic value even when the work's internal content is morally neutral or humane. Their exchange went like this:

Maya: We need to distinguish internal moral corruption from authorial taint. A painting that glorifies cruelty is aesthetically worse because its form embodies bad values. But if a genuinely humane portrait was made by someone who later turned out to have abused the very sitter he depicted, the painting itself hasn't changed—its balance, emotional resonance, and visual harmony are intact. Any shift in our judgment is just an epistemic update about provenance, not an aesthetic discovery.

Julian: That works only if aesthetic value is purely intrinsic. But evaluation is relational: it involves a response from an informed, sensitive audience. Once we learn the painter exploited the sitter, the tenderness in the brushwork can feel manipulative rather than compassionate. The biography doesn't sit outside the work; it reshapes how formal features function in experience.

Maya: But that's a change in us, not in the work. By your reasoning, discovering a masterpiece is a perfect forgery should destroy its beauty, yet the visual properties remain. We distinguish aesthetic value from market value precisely because the former tracks features of the object, not its history.

Julian: Forgery involves deceptive authorship—an institutional failure. Here the authorship is real but morally compromised. Artistic expression is partly communication between maker and audience. If the maker's intentions toward the subject were exploitative, expressing tenderness becomes hypocritical or hollow. The work's emotional authenticity—an aesthetic property—is undermined.

Maya: You're assuming we can read psychology into form. A cruel novelist can write genuinely compassionate fiction; the compassion resides in the text's structure, not in the novelist's heart. To call the text hollow is to confuse biography with meaning.

Julian: I don't need full intentionalism. I'm saying that when biography reveals the work's humane appearance was produced in a context of actual harm—say, the painter was abusing the sitter while painting—this creates interpretive tension. The form and the context clash, and that clash diminishes the coherence of the aesthetic experience. The colors don't change, but their significance within a total experience is altered.

Maya: So aesthetic value includes the total context of reception? Then hidden facts always matter, which makes judgment unstable and overly moralized.

Julian: Not always—only when biography directly undermines the values the work expresses. There's no conflict if a bad person paints an abstract geometric pattern. The conflict arises specifically between the work's internal moral orientation and the author's external record.
