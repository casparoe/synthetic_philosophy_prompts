I'm designing a single fifty-minute session for philosophy undergraduates who have just seen the principle of indifference applied to discrete cases but have not yet encountered its breakdown in continuous spaces. Rather than the familiar chord-in-circle version, I want the carrying example to be the inscribed-triangle problem: three points are selected on a circle, and we ask for the probability that the triangle is acute. Plan the teaching arc: what conceptual groundwork must be in place before the triangle problem can even be posed, the exact order in which the three competing parameterizations—uniform selection of points on the circumference, uniform selection of chord lengths, and uniform selection of central angles—should be introduced so that the contradiction feels inevitable rather than contrived, and the misconceptions students will form at each stage, especially the belief that one parameterization is the "objectively correct" way to be random. For each misconception, specify the point in the sequence where it should be headed off. Include one check question for the end that tests whether a student grasps the parameter-dependency itself rather than merely recalling that the answers conflict. Finally, say which standard resolution—Jaynes's symmetry argument or an invariant-measure fix—I should explicitly set aside for this session and why excluding it serves the pedagogical aim.
