I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar on comparative political ethics and corruption, and I've been trying to work out my own judgments about what actually counts as corruption across different institutional and cultural contexts, rather than simply applying Western legal categories. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or whether consistency requires me to drop some.

Case 1: A municipal councilor in a small town accepts a handcrafted ceramic vase from a constituent after successfully advocating for a zoning variance the constituent had requested. The vase was completed before the request was filed, and there is no evidence of a prior agreement. Verdict: not corruption; it expresses retrospective gratitude for fair representation.

Case 2: The same councilor accepts an all-expenses-paid vacation package from a real-estate developer whose project she is currently reviewing in committee. Verdict: clearly corruption.

Case 3: In a rural municipality where civil-service salaries are chronically delayed, citizens routinely bring fresh produce and homemade goods to clerks processing land titles. The clerks process applications in strict chronological order regardless of gifts, and refusal is culturally interpreted as insulting hostility. Verdict: not corruption; it is embedded reciprocity within a failing institutional framework, not an instrumental exchange for preferential treatment.

Case 4: A cabinet minister appoints his university roommate to head a regulatory agency. The roommate holds relevant technical qualifications, but the selection was driven primarily by long-standing personal loyalty rather than comparative merit. Verdict: corruption, specifically institutional nepotism.

Case 5: A community organizer distributes food parcels to families who attend her political rallies; attendance is tracked, and families who do not attend are systematically excluded from future distributions. Verdict: corruption in the form of transactional vote-buying.

Case 6: A diplomat in East Asia accepts an expensive ceremonial tea set from a counterpart immediately before treaty negotiations begin. Refusing the gift would violate established diplomatic protocol and be interpreted as a hostile refusal of the relationship itself. Verdict: not corruption; it is protocol-bound reciprocity that sustains the institutional framework of negotiation rather than distorting a specific outcome.

Case 7: A physician in an underfunded public hospital accepts a live chicken and fresh vegetables from a recovered patient in a region where medical staff are severely underpaid and such offerings are customary expressions of gratitude. No preferential treatment was given or expected during care. Verdict: not corruption.

Case 8: A surgeon in the same hospital accepts a cash-filled envelope from the family of a patient currently on the surgical waiting list, and moves that patient up the list shortly afterward. Verdict: corruption.

Here is the pair I find genuinely hard to reconcile. Case 9: A senior hiring official directs a substantial portion of public-sector jobs to members of her own ethnic group, arguing that this corrects centuries of structural exclusion; she derives no personal financial benefit, and the hires are technically competent. I am genuinely unsure whether this is corruption—perhaps legitimate redress—or whether it is a form of group-based favoritism that corrupts state impartiality.

Case 10: A senior hiring official directs a substantial portion of public-sector jobs to members of her religious congregation, arguing that shared moral commitments will build institutional trust; she derives no personal financial benefit, and the hires are technically competent. Verdict: corruption, because the justification appeals to a particularistic identity rather than a publicly justifiable criterion.

Cases 9 and 10 seem structurally parallel—both involve non-merit, identity-based allocation justified by a collective good—yet I judge them differently. I cannot tell whether the difference lies in the nature of the identity, in the historical context of redress versus consolidation, or whether my distinction is mere rationalization.

There is also one case I keep going back and forth on. Case 11: A senior bureaucrat allows her adult child to skip a mandatory professional licensing exam that the bureaucrat herself administers. She believes sincerely that the exam is poorly designed and that her child is qualified, but she also knows that granting this exception violates formal equality of treatment. Is this corruption—an abuse of office for private benefit—or is it permissible parental advocacy within a flawed system?

Finally, I want the principle, if there is one, to address not only these cases but also a variant I have not listed: what should we say about a system of rotating mutual aid in which local officials informally exchange small favors—expediting permits, recommending contractors—within a closed network, where every participant both gives and receives over time, and no individual transaction can be mapped to a specific quid pro quo?

Please do three things. First, state the principle (or a small set of competing principles) that best captures my verdicts, being precise about which clause explains which case. Second, apply it to Case 11 and to the rotating-mutual-aid variant, and tell me honestly which of my original verdicts—especially the tension between Cases 9 and 10—the principle forces me to drop if I value simplicity over saving every intuition. Third, please write your answer in American English, using a formal and polished academic tone appropriate for a seminar discussion.
