I have recently been asked to document whether my team's language model "believes" certain false premises in its outputs, or whether I should restrict myself entirely to mechanistic and design-level descriptions. This practical pressure brought Dennett's intentional stance to mind, together with a specific real-world episode: the widely reported case in which Google engineer Blake Lemoine argued that the dialogue system LaMDA was sentient, citing conversations in which the model expressed fear of being deactivated and claimed to possess subjective experience. The popular framing treats this as either naive anthropomorphism or a legitimate crisis of mental attribution. I want a more disciplined analysis.

Please apply Dennett's three stances—physical, design, and intentional—to the human actors as well as to LaMDA. For Lemoine, was he genuinely adopting the intentional stance, treating the system as a rational agent whose utterances revealed beliefs and desires, or was he in a hybrid position, using design-level knowledge of transformer architecture while allowing mentalistic vocabulary to leak in? For the reviewers who dismissed his claim, were they insisting on the physical or design stance, or were they implicitly deploying intentional predictions—about Lemoine's psychological state or institutional incentives—to explain his reports?

Then consider Dennett's predictive-justification criterion: the intentional stance is warranted when it yields predictive success that the other stances cannot match. In this case, attributing beliefs to LaMDA might indeed predict its dialogue outputs more efficiently than describing its weights and training data. Does this mean the stance is genuinely justified for artificial systems, or does the permanent availability of the design stance render intentional attribution merely a heuristic convenience?

Finally, I want to press a distinction that is rarely made in these debates. Is there a separate "simulative stance"—a mode of reasoning about mental content in a system explicitly engineered to produce it, while withholding commitment to real rational agency—that is neither Dennett's intentional stance (which presupposes genuine agency) nor his design stance (which ignores mental content entirely)? If such a stance exists, does Lemoine's error lie not in adopting the intentional stance per se, but in failing to recognize that AI demands this intermediate posture? Analyze the episode with these distinctions in view: say precisely where Dennett's framework illuminates the dispute, where it breaks down or is silent, and whether the case ultimately supports the view that attributing beliefs to AI is always pragmatic, always a category mistake, or requires a taxonomy that Dennett's tripartite division never anticipated.
