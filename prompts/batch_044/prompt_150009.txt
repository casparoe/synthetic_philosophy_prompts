I'm a product manager who recently began using Dennett's framework of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—to understand why our support chatbot misreads user frustration, and I want to work through the idea by exaggerating it. Write a deadpan parody of an internal corporate training manual, titled "Stance-Based Escalation Protocols for Customer Interaction," issued by a fictional tech firm. The manual should formalize the three stances as liability-tiered resolution tracks: Tier 1 (physical stance—signal noise, hardware degradation), Tier 2 (design stance—specification limits, intended function), and Tier 3 (intentional stance—attributing beliefs, desires, and blame to the user). Inject and develop the concept of "stance drift": the bureaucratic reclassification of a complaint downward through the tiers whenever repair cost or legal exposure rises, so that the same interaction is explained physically when the company must pay and intentionally when the user must adapt. The satire should exaggerate real features of Dennett's view—especially that the intentional stance is a predictive strategy rather than an ontological commitment, that real patterns earn their keep through projectible predictions, and that reducing one stance to another is not always available—without inventing fake philosophical positions. The humor must come from the manual's absolute, unbroken seriousness about treating interpretive strategies as liability shields. Open with a one-sentence summary of what the manual inadvertently reveals about why organizations prefer certain stances. Keep the voice deadpan, bureaucratic, and never winking. Maximum 650 words.
