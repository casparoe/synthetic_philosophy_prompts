Hey, I'm throwing together a unit on aesthetics and obligations to the dead for my grad seminar, and I've gotten stuck trying to formalize my gut reactions about biopics and historical fiction. I think there might be something like a "posthumous hermeneutic contract" — the idea that dramatizing someone is okay when it respects the public evidential record they left behind, but violates something when it colonizes private or ambiguous spaces they never intended for narrative consumption. But I'm not sure it actually holds up.

Here are my verdicts. One: inventing dialogue at a conference a dead scientist actually attended, putting words in his mouth that match his published papers, feels totally fine — it's just filling gaps in the archival record. Two: taking a dead activist's genuinely ambiguous private letter and turning it into a central scene where she viciously mocks her allies feels not okay, even though the letter exists and historians argue about its tone. Three: moving a famous painter's suicide from a wheat field to a church for symbolic weight strikes me as permissible, maybe even respectful, since the psychological truth is intact and only the scenery is changed. Four: using modern grief-counseling language in voiceover to describe a dead soldier's documented letters feels okay, because it's translation, not fabrication. Five: inventing a hidden romance for a dead figure who deliberately kept their private life out of the record feels like a real violation, closer to case two than to case one.

But then there's this pair that breaks my tentative rule. Compressing five real years of a dead politician's career into one cinematic year — merging speeches, merging enemies — feels like standard narrative license, nothing wrong with it. But inventing one entirely fictional secret meeting that symblically captures the same political shift feels wrong to me, even though both are just bending chronology for a two-hour frame. If my contract idea is about respecting the evidential record, why does merging real events feel okay while inventing one symbolic event doesn't?

Can you state a precise principle — or maybe two competing ones — that captures all or most of these verdicts? I want to know which clause of my "contract" needs repair, and whether I have to drop the verdict about the invented meeting or the one about merged chronology. Also, apply whatever you land on to the case I keep flipping on: a documentary that uses AI voice synthesis to have a dead poet perform her own poems with emotional cadences that are pure speculation. Is that closer to the invented dialogue, the translated grief-language, or a seperate category? I'm not asking for a bibliography — just whether my intuitions hang together or if I need to bite a bullet somewhere.
