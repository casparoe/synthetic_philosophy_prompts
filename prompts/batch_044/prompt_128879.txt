My older brother has been living with our parents since finishing college. For about a year he has stopped bathing regularly, refuses paid work, and spends long periods talking aloud to empty rooms. He has never been violent, but he rejects the idea that anything is wrong with him. He argues that what our parents call symptoms are simply unconventional choices—refusing work is a protest against exploitation, neglecting hygiene is a rejection of vanity, and talking aloud is no different from prayer. He insists that psychiatric language is used to pathologize people who break social rules, and that the concept of lacking insight is circular: anyone who denies the diagnosis is taken to prove the diagnosis is correct. Our parents want me to help them initiate an involuntary evaluation and possible commitment. They say he is clearly suffering, that his refusal to accept help demonstrates he is not competent to decide, and that family loyalty requires me to act before he harms himself through neglect. When I visit, he is coherent, frightened, and asks me not to let them lock him up for being different. I am torn between respecting his autonomy and preventing what looks like serious self-neglect.

Please respond in the following sections:

1. The philosophical structure of the conflict (~150 words): describe, without naming specific authors or movements, what is at stake regarding the nature of this condition, the legitimacy of using clinical categories to justify coercion, and the problem with treating refusal as proof of incompetence.

2. The case for supporting the parents (~200 words): argue that suffering and self-neglect can justify intervention, that family members have a duty of care, and that autonomy is not absolute when a person's judgment is severely impaired by a genuine disorder.

3. The case for siding with my brother (~200 words): argue that labeling his behavior as illness risks enforcing conformity, that lack of insight is a self-sealing justification, and that involuntary treatment in this context functions as social control rather than genuine medical help.

4. A concrete recommendation (~150 words): state clearly whether I should help my parents, with specific conditions—what evidence or change in circumstances would alter your recommendation, and what practical steps I should take regardless of which side I choose.

5. What this reasoning cannot settle (~100 words): identify the questions—such as empirical predictions about outcomes, legal specifics in my jurisdiction, or the emotional dynamics within our family—that lie outside the philosophical framework and require judgment or information beyond what the argument provides.
