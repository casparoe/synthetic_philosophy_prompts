So me and my cousin were talking over drinks about why my great-grandfather's textile mill town ended up way richer than the coal town thirty miles over. He keeps saying its clearly the culture — people there had this weird religious hangover where treating saving money like a moral duty was just part of the air, and that value-system is what made them pool capital and build the cooperative bank instead of blowing wages on booze. I pushed back and said isn't that backwards? If you're in a boom town with steady wages, of course you start praising thrift; the values feel like they're following the money, not leading it.

But now I'm second-guessing myself because he pointed out the cooperative bank actually started before the big boom, when the town was still pretty poor. So maybe the beliefs really did come first and shape the economy? I'm not asking for a family history lesson — I want to know if "culture caused prosperity" is a real kind of explanation, or if its always just smuggling in the outcome you're trying to explain. Can values and beliefs genuinely cause economic outcomes, or is that explanation circular by definition? And please, keep clear whats actually settled among people who study this versus whats still genuinely fought over — don't present one side as if everyone agrees.
