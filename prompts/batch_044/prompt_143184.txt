I'm trying to determine whether a Bentham-style account of sexual morality—one that judges acts by their contribution to pleasure and absence of pain, giving central weight to informed consent and to the absence of deception or coercion—can accommodate my gut reactions to concrete cases, or whether my reactions force a revision or abandonment of that framework.

Case 1: Two strangers meet, agree explicitly in advance that they want protected, purely recreational sex with no emotional attachment or deception, follow through, and both later report satisfaction. Verdict: not morally wrong.

Case 2: One party is in a committed monogamous relationship and has promised exclusivity to their partner, but conceals the encounter, believes the partner will never discover it or suffer harm, and proceeds anyway. Verdict: wrong, and the wrongness lies in the broken promise and deception rather than in the physical act itself.

Case 3: Two adults who are biological siblings were separated at birth and raised in different households; they meet later in life, learn of the relation, and freely choose to enter a sexual relationship using contraception and maintaining complete secrecy. No one else is deceived or harmed. Verdict: wrong, though I cannot identify any victim or violated right within a purely utilitarian calculus.

Case 4: A fully voluntary, well-compensated sex worker provides services to a client in a legal, regulated environment; both understand the terms, physical risks are minimal, and the worker reports job satisfaction comparable to other service work. Verdict: not wrong.

Case 5: A married couple agrees to open their relationship. One partner genuinely consents; the other secretly feels coerced by fear of abandonment but hides this feeling and proceeds to have sex with others. Verdict: wrong, because the consent is corrupted by hidden coercion, not because non-monogamy is intrinsically wrong.

Case 6: A person uses an immersive virtual-reality system to engage in simulated sex with a photorealistic digital replica of a specific, identifiable real person (a public figure) who has not consented to this use of their likeness. No physical person is touched or deceived in the interaction. Verdict: wrong, and the wrongness feels closer to a violation of autonomy or dignity than to deception or direct harm.

Can a single principle—Bentham's own, a modified utilitarian rule, or an alternative—deliver all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? State the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, predict the verdict on an unlisted case (an asexual couple who share deep physical intimacy—sleeping naked together, non-sexual genital contact—purely for emotional bonding with full mutual understanding), and honestly say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Please finish with a line beginning "Verdict:" that names the best principle (or leading candidate) and the verdict I must drop if I keep it.
