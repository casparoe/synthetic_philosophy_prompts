I am delivering the opening remarks for a three-day interdisciplinary workshop entitled Partial Truths in Interdisciplinary Inquiry, co-hosted by our philosophy department and the university’s institute for cognitive science. The audience is divided evenly between specialists in Indian philosophy and philosophy of science, who will notice every technical misstep, and practicing empirical researchers together with first-year graduate students, for whom Jain epistemology is entirely unfamiliar. The address should last approximately six minutes, maintain a formal yet welcoming register, and do substantially more than ornamentally invoke pluralism: I want Jain anekāntavāda to shape the very architecture of what I can and cannot claim about the disagreements that will arise over the next three days.

Specifically, I want you to organize the remarks around the practice of syādvāda, or conditional predication, and its underlying doctrine of nayavāda—that every cognition grasps only one aspect, or naya, of a complex object—but applied not to metaphysical objects, but to the evaluation of interdisciplinary claims themselves. Build the central section around an unconventional framing: treat the peer-review evaluation of a single controversial study as an exercise in saptabhaṅgī, the seven-fold scheme of predication, so that a neuroscientist’s causal claim about consciousness and a phenomenologist’s descriptive claim about the same phenomenon can each be affirmed in one respect, denied in another, and affirmed-and-denied in yet another, without logical contradiction. Use a concrete hypothetical conflict between these two perspectives to illustrate the point, and make clear that this framing is meant to govern how we listen to one another during the workshop sessions rather than to dissolve substantive disagreement.

Crucially, the speech must avoid the vulgar reading that anekāntavāda makes every view automatically correct; there should be explicit space within the framework for saying that some methodological claims are simply erroneous, since even the Jains allow that a particular naya can be false when it purports to be exhaustive. The remarks should therefore welcome disagreement without collapsing it, comfort the non-philosophers that they need not master Sanskrit terminology to participate, and end by proposing that our collective task is not to reach one final, absolutist synthesis but to hold our respective partial truths with the kind of epistemic humility that syādvāda demands. Please write the speech accordingly.
