As a retired experimental biologist who has turned to philosophy in retirement, I recently observed a seminar discussion in which two interlocutors—both well-read in the ethics of behavioral policy—became deadlocked over a case they described as “ambient nudging.” The first argued that when a municipality redesigns a public plaza so that pedestrians are physically channeled past health-food vendors before reaching tobacco kiosks, this is merely a rearrangement of choice architecture, morally on a par with placing stairs near elevators; it does not restrict autonomy, only alters salience. The second countered that this collapses an important distinction between informational nudging, which changes framing or salience, and structural nudging, which changes the physical or temporal cost-structure of access; she maintained that only structural interventions genuinely constrain the option set and therefore require a stronger justification than welfare promotion alone. The first replied that the distinction cannot hold, because framing always imposes cognitive costs—attention, interpretation, affect—so all nudging is structural at some level, and drawing a line between cognitive and physical costs is arbitrary. The second then advanced a further criterion: an intervention should be evaluated by whether it is “reversible without residue,” meaning the agent can restore the pre-intervention environment without lasting alteration to her preference structure; she claimed structural nudges typically fail this test because they reshape habits that persist after the architecture is removed. The first responded that this criterion is either implausibly strong—good advice can also reshape preferences—or question-begging, since it assumes preference formation lies outside the legitimate scope of public design, which is precisely the issue in dispute. They turned to me for adjudication. Please work through their exchange move by move: assess which steps are clearly mistaken, which are plausible but incomplete, and which are clearly right; identify the precise point at which the argument turns on an unstated assumption about autonomy; and say what both participants are missing about the relationship between environmental design and the conditions for rational deliberation.
