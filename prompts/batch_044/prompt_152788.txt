I sit on the board of a community garden cooperative. Recently we rejected a member's request to cultivate a specific crop after our soil-testing protocol showed contamination levels above the charter's safety threshold. I justified the decision by walking through the inference: the measurements exceeded the limit, and our protocol states that exceeding the limit entails rejection. A fellow board member then asked why we are bound by that particular inference rule—why "exceeds limit, therefore reject" should govern our collective choice rather than being treated as one optional heuristic among others. I answered that the rule derives its authority from the charter itself, but she pressed further: what justifies treating the charter as an authoritative source of reasoning rules unless we already accept a higher-order principle about institutional legitimacy? I appealed to our founding commitment to precautionary reasoning, yet she observed that applying that commitment itself requires an inference step whose authority is precisely what's in question. Each reply I offer seems to require a further defense of the reasoning step used to offer it. In practical governance, is there a legitimate point at which we may treat a basic rule of inference or decision as self-authorizing, or does insisting on complete justification for every reasoning move necessarily paralyze collective action?
