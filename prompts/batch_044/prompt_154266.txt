I am designing the final exam for an upper-level graduate seminar on epistemic game theory and the foundations of strategic reasoning. I need one essay question on program equilibrium that students cannot answer by summarizing the standard Rubinstein-style transparent-procedure model. The question should present a modified one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma in which each agent runs a decision procedure with full read-access to the opponent's source code, but is subject to a reflection bound: the agent may execute at most k nested simulations of the opponent's procedure, for some fixed finite k, before committing to an action. Ask students to analyze whether mutual cooperation remains rationally sustainable under this bound, comparing the epistemic conditions—common knowledge of rationality, procedural transparency, and self-reference—with those required by standard Nash equilibrium and by the unbounded program-equilibrium result. Specifically, the prompt must require students to address: (i) exactly how the reflection bound disrupts the fixed-point argument that usually supports cooperation in transparent program games; (ii) whether introducing probabilistic or noisy decision rules can restore cooperative outcomes without requiring full transparency, and what this implies about the role of determinism in the original argument; and (iii) whether the bound reveals that program equilibrium genuinely resolves Newcomb's problem or merely relocates the paradox into a meta-level choice about simulation depth. Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 650 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should distinguish an A-grade response—which must identify the fixed-point failure, discuss the noisy-repair strategy together with its epistemic costs, and take a defensible stance on the Newcomb issue—from a B-grade response, which describes the standard unbounded model accurately but treats the reflection bound as an incidental technical limitation rather than as an epistemic rupture. Specify that candidates have 90 minutes and should aim for roughly 600–700 words. Do not include any request for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
