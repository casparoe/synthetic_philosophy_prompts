Financial prediction markets are run on the assumption that the price of a contract reveals the collective belief about an event’s likelihood, and they are actively used for forecasting despite being populated by traders who are, in principle, rational and well-informed. Yet the no-trade theorems in economics establish that, if agents share common prior probabilities and update rationally on private information, no voluntary trade should ever occur: the willingness to transact reveals information that ought to cause the counterparty to revise their belief and withdraw. The sustained liquidity of these markets therefore sits in tension with the theoretical conditions under which rational belief revision is supposed to operate. What epistemological commitments does the institutional practice of treating prediction-market prices as authoritative forecasts presuppose? Must it deny that traders share common priors, that belief revision is Bayesian, or that prices reflect rational consensus rather than a heterogeneous aggregation of incommensurable frameworks? Reconstruct the implicit theory of collective knowledge that makes this practice coherent, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what would have to change in forecasting practice if we treated the no-trade result as showing that liquid markets are necessarily composed of agents whose beliefs do not converge in the Bayesian manner.
