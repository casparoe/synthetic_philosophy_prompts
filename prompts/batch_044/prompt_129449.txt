Write an essay on whether normative evaluation of past scientific practices can escape the charge of Whig history without collapsing into radical historicism. Center your discussion on the historiography of nineteenth-century anthropometric race science and its eventual displacement by modern population genetics. Begin by distinguishing two modes of retrospective judgment—teleological narration, which reads past theories as defective anticipations of present knowledge, and normative retrospection, which assesses past reasoning by standards available both then and now without treating present outcomes as predetermined aims. Then ask whether normative retrospection can consistently condemn craniometric methodology for flawed inference while still explaining why competent researchers found it plausible given their evidential context. Must one either treat craniometry as merely wrong-for-us (the historicist escape) or as an inevitable step toward genetic science (the Whiggish trap)? Reach and defend a conclusion: either there is a principled middle position—if so, specify precisely what distinguishes it from both extremes—or one extreme is unavoidable and the other collapses on inspection. In reaching your verdict, address whether granting internal coherence to past science necessarily undermines our right to say it was objectively mistaken, and whether any defensible form of progress-talk in historiography can avoid smuggling in presentist teleology.
