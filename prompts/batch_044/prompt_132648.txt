As a faculty member serving on my university's Constitutional Reform Committee, I must help produce a formal recommendation—due before the start of next semester—regarding a proposal to pilot a sortition-based Student Deliberative Assembly. The proposal, advanced by a cross-disciplinary working group, would suspend the current system of elected student representatives to the university senate for one academic year and instead seat thirty members selected by stratified random lot across faculties, years of study, and residential status. These members would serve a single semester, participate in mandatory structured deliberation sessions moderated by trained facilitators, and issue non-binding advisory opinions on tuition structures and general-education requirements. Supporters contend that elections chronically over-represent politically mobilized minorities and that random selection yields descriptive representation closer to the actual student population; critics respond that removing electoral mechanisms severs the accountability link between representatives and their constituents, and that a body lacking a mandate cannot legitimately advise on contested normative questions such as fee increases.

I need a short policy memo for circulation at our penultimate meeting. The document should recommend one concrete course of action—full approval with a fall launch, rejection with stated conditions for future reconsideration, or conditional approval subject to specific structural amendments—and should include a precise implementation timeline or sunset clause. The reasoning must treat the democratic-accountability objection with genuine philosophical seriousness rather than framing it as institutional inertia, and it should clarify how the distinction between descriptive representation and substantive representation bears on the design of the pilot. I am particularly concerned about a problem that the proposal addresses only in passing: if stratification categories mirror historical patterns of exclusion, the lot may reproduce the very structural inequities it claims to remedy, and a purely descriptive body may still lack the epistemic diversity required for sound deliberation on complex policy. The memo should therefore assess whether the proposed safeguards—facilitated deliberation, rotating membership waves, and a defined advisory scope—are sufficient to mitigate that risk, and it must present the strongest argument for the position you ultimately reject in terms the opposing members would recognize as fair.

Please write the memo as a document the administration could act upon: clear bottom line, supporting reasoning that engages both the democratic-legitimacy worry and the epistemic-structure concern, an explicit acknowledgment of what is being sacrificed under the recommended course, and a final paragraph specifying exactly what must happen by which date—whether that is drafting the stratification protocol, scheduling the first deliberation session, or formally closing the pilot review process.
