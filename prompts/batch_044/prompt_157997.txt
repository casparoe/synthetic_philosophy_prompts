Hey, I need a classroom activity for my phil seminar (around 8 students, 90 min) about advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. I want them to actually feel the pull of being bound by a past version of themselves, not just discuss autonomy in the abstract. Design a structured role-play or game: each student writes a binding 'life directive' for a hypothetical future self who has shifted core values — not the usual medical stuff, but things like career path, religious commitment, or what they think makes life good. Then split them: some play the 'past advocate' who insists the directive is still binding, others play the 'present self' who now sees thier old values as mistaken or foreign. Give them concrete stakes — maybe access to shared resources or relationships that are only available if they honor the old commitment — and rules that force them to negociate without just voting. Make sure they hit the conflict before anyone names the philosophical issue. For the debrief, map the moments where people felt loyalty versus rebellion onto the distinction between procedural autonomy (choosing the rules) and substantive autonomy (choosing within the rules), and be honest about what the activity can't capture, like whether the two selves are literally different people or just changed preferences. Also, inject one slightly offbeat directive into the deck: an 'epistemic' clause where the past self demands that if the present self ever comes to believe hard determinism, they must still behave as if moral responsibility is real. I think that makes the authority question sharper because it's about rational consistency, not just happiness. Don't lecture up front — let the rules and payoffs create the tension.
