The assigned question was: In his major work, Rawls objects that classical utilitarianism fails to take seriously the separateness of persons. Explain precisely what this objection claims about the logic of aggregation in moral reasoning, particularly the analogy between rational choice for one individual over time and moral choice for a society of distinct individuals. Then evaluate whether reformulating utilitarianism as an average-utilitarian doctrine—computing welfare per capita rather than in aggregate—adequately answers the objection. In your answer, distinguish this critique both from rights-based side-constraint objections and from concerns about the distribution of particular goods such as income or liberty.

The student wrote:

The separateness of persons objection, as John Rawls develops it, is that classical utilitarianism applies the logic of rational choice appropriate for one individual to society as a whole. A single rational agent may accept a present loss for a larger future gain; analogously, utilitarianism permits imposing costs on some people now if the aggregate welfare summed across all individuals is thereby increased. In doing so, it treats the welfare of distinct persons as fungible units within one overall calculus, failing to respect that each individual lives only one life.

Average utilitarianism is often presented as a remedy. By dividing total welfare by the number of people affected, it prevents the doctrine from recommending unlimited population growth and assigns each person equal weight in the denominator. However, it does not escape Rawls's critique. Because it still aggregates gains and losses across separate lives, it can license sacrificing one individual for the benefit of others whenever the mean welfare is thereby raised. The core problem—the interpersonal balancing of lives—remains untouched.

This argument is closely tied to Rawls's discussion of the original position: behind the veil of ignorance, parties reject utilitarian reasoning precisely because they might end up as the ones bearing the costs. Furthermore, Derek Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity supports Rawls's conclusion. If there is no deep, metaphysically substantial self that persists through time, then treating society as a single subject spread across persons is clearly erroneous. Thus, whether one adopts a total or an average formulation, the separateness objection retains its force, and it should be distinguished from Nozick's rights-based side-constraint critique, which concerns violations of individual rights rather than the logic of aggregation itself.
