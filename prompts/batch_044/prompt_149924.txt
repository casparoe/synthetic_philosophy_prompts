I have been thinking about whether the classical liberal tradition can coherently accommodate modern environmental risk governance without collapsing into either technocratic authoritarianism or laissez-faire negligence. Specifically, I am interested in how John Stuart Mill might have approached the strong precautionary principle in the context of an intervention that only became conceivable in the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries: the proposed field release of self-propagating gene drives designed to suppress malaria vectors in parts of sub-Saharan Africa. The case is philosophically awkward for Mill because it inverts the usual harm-principle asymmetry. The harm of non-intervention—continued endemic malaria mortality—is direct, quantifiable, and present; the harm of intervention—potential ecological cascade, horizontal gene transfer, or evolutionary backlash—is speculative, spatially diffuse, and deferred across generations.

Rather than asking for a generic risk-benefit analysis, I want you to predict Mill’s reasoning through a specific conceptual distinction I have been developing: the difference between precautionary prohibition, which demands proof of safety before any deployment, and precautionary architecture, which permits deployment only when the technology is engineered with intrinsic reversibility mechanisms—such as daisy-chain gene drives with programmed generational extinction timelines—so that the burden of proof shifts from demonstrating absolute safety to demonstrating controllable failure modes. Would Mill regard precautionary architecture as satisfying the evidentiary standards of the harm principle, or would he maintain that the state still bears an absolute burden to prove absence of cross-generational harm before licensing such an irreversible ecological commitment? In your answer, please attend closely to Mill’s commitments regarding individual liberty, the utility calculus, and the legitimate scope of social control, and indicate precisely where his framework might strain or break when applied to risks that are collective, transboundary, and epistemically opaque.
