I'm on the governance board for a 40-unit housing co-op and we keep running into these weird deadlocks where protecting one resident's personal-domain choice (like having a loud dog or picking a roommate) conflicts with another resident's preference, and then some collective vote makes everyone worse off compared to the status quo. I read about Sen's liberal paradox and it feels like exactly our problem, but I need something my board can actually run in a meeting without everyone having to read social choice theory.

Can you turn Sen's idea into a practical decision proceedure for us? I want a step-by-step checklist we can apply when a dispute hits the board: how to identify the "liberal domains" involved, how to check for Pareto improvements, where to stop and say "this is a values call not a logic call," and specifically how to avoid the paradox cycle where A's right over X conflicts with B's right over Y and collective preference goes the other way. It needs to handle cases where domains overlap — like noise that crosses unit walls, or guest policies that affect shared hallways.

Then apply it to two actual-style proposals: one where a resident wants to ban all overnight guests in adjacent units because of noise (this touches someone's private life and someone else's sleep), and one where the board votes to require every resident to approve any new roommate in the building (Pareto might seem to improve security but crushes individual choice). Include one case where the proceedure gives an answer the board probably won't like. And flag the spots where the checklist can't eliminate human judgment — like weighing which domain is more "personal."

Keep it grounded in what Sen actually showed, don't just invent a random framework, but also don't ask me to read papers. No citations needed. And please don't reference any obscure post-Sen variants I won't have heard of — stick to the core paradox and maybe one simple extension you invent on the spot for overlapping domains, just to make it usable. The board meets in 20 minutes, so make it something we can run with a whiteboard.
