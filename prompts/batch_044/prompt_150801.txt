I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, working specifically on cross-modal perception and whether conceptual content must be modality-neutral in order to bridge sensory domains. In a recent seminar I tried to formalize a line of reasoning about Molyneux's problem, and the conclusion seemed to collapse both the negative and the positive answers into unacceptable positions. I cannot locate the precise slip, and I want to know which premise to reject and what philosophical debt that rejection incurs.

Here is the chain. (1) If Molyneux's problem receives a negative answer—if the subject who learned cube and sphere only by touch cannot distinguish them by sight immediately upon gaining vision—then tactile shape concepts contain no resources that could guide visual recognition. (2) But if tactile concepts are entirely modality-bound, then there is no genuinely unified concept of cubicality applied across both touch and vision; we possess only causally correlated but conceptually isolated representations, one tactile and one visual. (3) Ordinary perceptual judgment, however, clearly involves attributing the same shape property to an object through different senses—claiming, for instance, that something feels angular and looks angular—so the denial of any shared conceptual content eliminates cross-modal reference altogether. (4) Hence the negative answer proves too much: it forces us to abandon the coherence of everyday object perception.

(5) Therefore the answer must be positive: upon gaining sight, the subject can immediately identify the shapes. (6) Yet if she can do so without any prior visual experience, her pre-existing tactile concept of cube must already have included the conditions for correct visual identification. (7) The only available explanation is that the concept is amodal—structured independently of any particular sensory modality and merely instantiated differently in tactile and visual experience. (8) Once amodal representations are admitted, however, sensory modalities become nothing more than delivery systems for a central conceptual architecture that was never genuinely shaped by them. (9) This collapses the empirical claim that spatial concepts are formed through sensory interaction, reducing Molyneux's problem from a question about inter-modal learning to the mere activation of innate, modality-neutral structures. (10) So the positive answer also proves too much, by turning empirical concept acquisition into a form of rationalist nativism.

I am especially reluctant to surrender (2) or (5), since I take cross-modal reference to be genuinely demanding and think only a positive answer can preserve it. Where does the argument fail—whether in the move from (6) to (7), in the inference from (7) to (8), or in some other step? What does rejecting that step cost, and is there a middle position that keeps unified cross-modal judgment intact without committing either to full amodality or to nativism?
