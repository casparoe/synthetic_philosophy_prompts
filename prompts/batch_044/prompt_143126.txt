I work in conservation science, specifically in tracing the material genealogy of artifacts—mapping the physical lineage of pigments, substrates, and binding media through documented supply chains, workshop practices, and non-destructive spectroscopic analysis. I want you to restate the core philosophical debate about whether a perfect visual forgery of a painting lacks value that the original possesses, using only my vocabulary: compositional fidelity, documented provenance chains, detectable material signatures, restoration ethics, and the distinction between replication and continuity of origin. For each major philosophical claim—whether value depends on authorship, whether deception vitiates aesthetic worth, whether there is something like an irreproducible presence tied to causal history—translate it into a claim about physical materials, documentation, or professional obligation, and flag where the translation requires distortion. Then provide the residue report: which parts of the debate map cleanly onto concerns about material genealogy and provenance, which parts can only be expressed by stretching technical terms like authenticity beyond their laboratory meaning, and which parts, if any, have no counterpart at all in a framework that treats an object as valuable precisely insofar as its physical composition and documented history are intact and transparent. In particular, examine whether the notion that an original carries something that survives even perfect material replication is intelligible within conservation science or whether it marks a limit of the framework.
