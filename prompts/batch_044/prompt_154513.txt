I've been convinced lately that Jaspers was basically right about political guilt - that a nation can be guilty across generations not because there's some spooky collective mind, but because the political community functions as a continuing moral subject, kind of like a corporation that stays liable for old contracts even after every employee has turned over. I want to know what would actually count against this, and I don't mean the obvious "you can't blame people for what they didn't do" objection. I want the serious defeaters. For one thing, does the whole thing collapse if we deny that nations are agents with real intentions, or if we insist that moral patiency requires a first-personal stance that institutions just can't have? For another, if we apply the same logic to smaller groups - universities, sports clubs, even neighborhoods - do they inherit guilt too, and is that a feature or an absurd result? Then there's the institutional change problem: if a nation rebuilds its government and social structures completely, does the guilt disappear? If yes, then isn't the guilt really just institutional liability wearing a national costume? I'm also worried this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability - defenders always retreat to "this is political guilt, not criminal guilt" so that nothing could ever disprove it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a commited defender would concede versus dismiss outright, and tell me honestly whether the view risks being insulated. I'd be especially moved if you could point to a paralell case where we clearly reject inherited collective guilt and explain exactly where the analogy to nations breaks. Please don't use headings, bullet points, bold, or tables - just continuous prose like an email. And no citations or reading lists, just think it through.
