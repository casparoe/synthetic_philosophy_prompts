I am auditing a graduate seminar on practical reason, and after a long argument with classmates about whether “tragic choice” names a real structural feature of practical life or merely marks our failure to find a hidden common metric, I want a sorting instrument that reveals what people actually commit to rather than which camp they claim. Please construct a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with concrete branch questions—that classifies philosophical positions on hard choices between genuinely incommensurable values. The root case should be specific and non-abstract: a decision-maker must either destroy a unique, irreplaceable work of communal cultural heritage or allow a preventable human death, with both losses resisting translation into any shared scale of value. From there, build the tree from diagnostic commitments, not labels.

The first branch should ask whether the incommensurability is ontological—no fact of overall comparative value exists in principle—or epistemic—a comparison is real but permanently inaccessible to agents. Within each of those paths, the second branch should ask whether taking the best available option leaves “moral residue”: lingering obligations of repair, ritual acknowledgment, or emotional burden that do not dissolve once the choice is made, or whether the agent can legitimately treat the matter as closed. The third branch should ask about “rational regret”: whether continuing to judge that the unchosen option was genuinely better—without retracting the choice itself—is appropriate, incoherent, or merely psychologically understandable. Every node must be phrased as a question someone could answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to, and every leaf should name the position if it has one (for instance, strong value pluralism, pragmatic comparativism, resolute monism, or unnamed) together with exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. You must also flag any unoccupied combinations of answers and briefly diagnose the gap: logical incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or an unexplored region of the logical space.

The point is to sort respondents by what they say about the case, its aftermath, and the appropriateness of post-choice attitudes, not by whether they call themselves pluralists or monists. In your response, please separate what is broadly settled in value theory and practical reason from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any one framing—such as whether moral residue requires an ontological pluralist commitment—as if it were the consensus view.
