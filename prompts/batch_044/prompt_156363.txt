Restate the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem — the claim that some considerations can motivate or cause an attitude (belief, intention, emotional response) without counting as justifications of it, treating epistemic and practical cases separately — using only that engineering vocabulary. Treat belief-forming and action-guiding mechanisms as modules governed by contracts. Show, case by case, what translates without distortion: for instance, whether believing p because it is supported by evidence maps onto a call that satisfies its preconditions and establishes its postcondition through the intended execution path, and whether believing p because it makes one happy maps onto reaching the correct postcondition via an unintended side-effect or invariant violation. Do the same for practical reasoning, contrasting acting for a morally right reason with acting well for an ulterior motive. Then deliver the residue report: identify which philosophical claims — such as the normative exclusion of certain motives, or the idea that justification carries authority independent of causal efficacy — lack any precise counterpart in verification logic. Conclude by assessing whether the residue reveals a genuine structural gap in the contract framework or suggests that the philosophical distinction was always less precisely articulated than it appeared.
