I am drafting an essay for a literary quarterly that uses the debate over why some nations are rich—institutions versus geography versus culture—to examine what I call macro-causal overdetermination. My thesis is that economic historians construct narratives of national wealth the way novelists construct plots: selecting one causal thread, such as inclusive institutions, from a field of equally consistent alternatives not because the archive uniquely supports it, but because that thread satisfies formal demands for coherent agency and moral closure. I sketch three cases—England’s Glorious Revolution, the divergence of the Koreas, and the resource curse in postcolonial states—to argue that institutional and geographical explanations each yield internally consistent narratives, and that the preference for one tracks disciplinary aesthetics rather than decisive empirical tests. I then ask whether recognizing this narrative structure undermines the epistemic authority of economic history, or merely reveals that all macro-causal explanation is necessarily literary.

Below are my rough notes for the essay’s architecture. I do not seek bibliographic suggestions or citations; I want substantive engagement with three points. First, is my characterization of the institutionalist position—particularly the emphasis on inclusive versus extractive institutions—accurate enough to bear the weight of the analogy, or have I flattened a nuanced empirical debate into a straw man for narrative effect? Second, is the comparison between historical causation and novelistic plot construction genuinely illuminating, or does it collapse once we notice that historians are constrained by archival evidence in ways novelists are not? Third, in the middle section, where I treat the Korean peninsula as a natural experiment, do I inadvertently commit the same narrative fallacy I attribute to institutionalists by imposing a tidy causal arc on a messier geopolitical history?

Please treat this as a request for critical engagement with the argument’s structure, not for source verification. Where my historical summary seems wrong or oversimplified, correct it; where the analogy between narrative form and causal inference seems weakest, press on it; and indicate which part, if any, strikes you as genuinely interesting rather than merely clever.
