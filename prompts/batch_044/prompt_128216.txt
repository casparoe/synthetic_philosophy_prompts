I've noticed a recurring asymmetry in how people draw the line between moral values and mere biases or preferences, particularly in cross-cultural or intergroup moral disagreement. The pattern runs like this: a speaker holds a normative commitment—say, that certain ecological harms ought to be avoided regardless of economic benefit—and presents it as a universal value. When an interlocutor advances a structurally parallel commitment—say, that certain economic disruptions ought to be avoided regardless of ecological benefit—the speaker treats that not as a competing universal value but as a parochial preference, or even as a cognitive bias that distorts moral judgment. What makes the pattern distinctive is that the speaker does not apply a consistent criterion—such as universality, strength of conviction, or independence from self-interest—to both commitments. Rather, the classification tracks group membership: commitments shared by the speaker's reference group are elevated to the status of values, while commitments distinctive to the opposing group are downgraded to preferences or biases, even when both have the same formal structure and emotional salience. I have seen this in debates about dietary ethics, environmental policy, and family obligations, but the logic seems portable. Is there an established concept in moral psychology or metaethics for this specific asymmetry—this selective promotion and demotion of normative commitments based on social alignment rather than on any internal feature of the commitment itself? If there is, I want to know precisely how it differs from ordinary in-group favoritism, from motivated reasoning, and from straightforward moral relativism, because in each case the mechanism and the normative implications seem different. If there is no precise term, I would like one coined, together with a clear boundary: under what conditions does a legitimate distinction between value and preference become this particular failure mode, and where exactly does the failure lie—in the classification, in the reasoning that follows, or in the refusal to apply the distinction reflexively?
