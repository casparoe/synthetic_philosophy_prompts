After a long argument with a friend about where moral status ends and computation begins, I want to know whether we actually disagree or just talk past each other. Here are the cases we kept returning to. A perfect digital emulation of a human cortex: my friend says it is sentient if the functional architecture is preserved, regardless of whether anyone treats it as a subject; I say it is sentient only if our social practices of attribution stabilize around it, since without that stabilization there is no determinate fact about its suffering. A distributed climate simulation running across thousands of nodes that, taken together, implements something isomorphic to a pain-state but with no unified processor and no observer who recognizes the isomorphism: I think there is nothing it is like to be that system, while my friend thinks the isomorphism alone is sufficient. A biological brain in a vat with its outputs rerouted so it never interacts with a body or community: I think it retains sentience because the biological substrate carries an attribution history; my friend thinks it becomes a mere mechanism once disconnected from the feedback loops that make computation meaningful. We agree a pocket calculator is not sentient, but disagree why: I say we lack any attributive framework that could take it seriously, and my friend says its information integration is simply too low.

Our debate stalls in a predictable loop. Whenever I press the attribution point, my friend asks whether I am denying that sentience could exist in an isolated alien system we have never observed; whenever my friend presses functional isomorphism, I ask whether they would grant moral status to a perfect holographic replay of a brain's computation that we all know is merely replaying past states with no present causal integration. Is this a substantive disagreement about what sentience is, a verbal disagreement about whether "sentience" tracks an intrinsic property or an epistemically accessible one, or are we answering different questions—one about ontology, one about the conditions for justified attribution—under a single heading? What hidden premise actually drives the split: a commitment to realism about phenomenal properties, a normative theory about what grounds moral obligations, or a criterion for individuating computations?

Give me a committed diagnosis: take a definite stand on which kind of disagreement this is, identify the single premise that explains why my friend accepts the climate-model case and I reject it, and state clearly which of my three verdicts I should overturn to achieve coherence. Also say what your diagnosis predicts for the holographic-replay case and for an artificial system designed specifically to elicit human attributive responses despite having minimal internal integration. Do not survey interpretations or list neutral options; defend one view with reasons, and be explicit about where your own position is weakest.
