I am a high school student working on an independent study that bridges evolutionary biology and philosophy of science, and I have become particularly interested in the debate over the units of natural selection—whether genes, individual organisms, or groups can serve as genuine targets of evolutionary change. Rather than asking for a broad overview, I want to apply this theoretical framework to a specific historical episode: the controversy sparked in the early 1960s when the British ecologist V.C. Wynne-Edwards proposed that many animal populations regulate their own reproduction—through territorial behavior, dispersal patterns, and what he interpreted as voluntary reproductive restraint—to prevent overexploitation of shared resources and to avoid extinction at the species level. His argument was quickly attacked by theorists who insisted that such apparently group-beneficial behavior could not evolve unless it simultaneously served the genetic interests of individuals or their close kin, a critique that was later formalized in W.D. Hamilton’s kin-selection mathematics and popularized in explicitly gene-centered terms by Richard Dawkins.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of the units-of-selection debate. First, map the theoretical positions onto the biological mechanisms Wynne-Edwards actually described: specify the conditions—such as reproductive isolation among groups, heritable variation in group-level traits, and differential survival or extinction among those groups—that a collective would need to satisfy in order to count as a true unit of selection rather than as a mere statistical aggregate of individuals. Then evaluate whether the populations Wynne-Edwards studied, including territorial bird species and lemming population cycles, met those conditions, and explain precisely where Hamilton’s kin-selection framework and Dawkins’s gene-level framing can account for the same observational patterns without invoking group-level causation. Next, consider what the episode reveals in reverse about the theory itself: does the rapid scientific rejection of Wynne-Edwards confirm that gene-level selection is the only coherent explanatory framework, or does it instead expose a conceptual blind spot—namely, that group-level effects can be empirically real but only when they emerge from lower-level mechanisms, a distinction later developed in multilevel selection theory and in the concept of evolutionary transitions in individuality? Finally, address whether Wynne-Edwards’s language of “the good of the species” can be fully translated into gene-level terms, or whether it points to a form of collective welfare that the gene-centered view systematically obscures, and explain what this implies for whether the units debate is ultimately empirical, conceptual, or both.
