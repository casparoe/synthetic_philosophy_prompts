Academic history and archaeology maintain a methodological asymmetry that is visible in both university curricula and museum exhibitions, yet rarely examined as a philosophical commitment. When scholars treat textual accounts of the distant past—Herodotus on the Persian Wars or Thucydides on the Peloponnesian conflict—they apply rigorous source criticism: they interrogate authorial intent, temporal proximity, internal contradiction, and corroboration by independent testimony, treating the text as mediated testimony that must be authenticated before it can constrain belief. By contrast, when the same scholars encounter physical traces—stratigraphic sequences, radiometric measurements, ceramic typologies—they typically frame the task as interpretation or model-building rather than criticism of a testimony. The material record is presented as a direct causal constraint on theory, not as a message that might mislead through contamination, sampling bias, or the interpretive choices embedded in its extraction.

What implicit theory of knowledge and evidence does this institutional division presuppose? Does it depend on the claim that material traces lack an intentional author—there is no speaker who could deceive—so that the only uncertainties are technical rather than testimonial? Or does it rest on a deeper distinction between knowledge transmitted through human testimony and knowledge reconstructed through causal continuity, with the latter granted a default epistemic privilege?

Reconstruct the implicit theory precisely: is the asymmetry strictly entailed by the nature of texts versus artifacts, or is it a disciplinary convention compatible with rival epistemologies? Could one consistently apply a uniform critical framework—treating a radiocarbon date as a claim produced by a chain of laboratory practices with its own biases, and treating Herodotus with the hermeneutic charity usually reserved for physical traces—without collapsing into global skepticism about the distant past? And if we replaced the implicit dualism with a single theory—say, that all knowledge of antiquity is inference to the best explanation from traces of various kinds, none enjoying special testimonial privilege—what concrete changes would be required in how history departments train students and how museums label reconstructed timelines?
