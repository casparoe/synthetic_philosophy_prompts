I've been working on a novel about a disaster-relief co-ordinator — think something like a gritty medical drama but with more philosophy — and my editor and I have been argueing about whether Scanlon's contractualist idea that you justify principles to each person can ever allow you to 'add up' complaints. Here are four scenarios that keep coming up in my draft, with my gut verdicts. I want to know if there's a single principle behind them, or if I'm just being inconsistent, and which verdict I'd have to ditch to keep things tidy. Please write your answer in British English — I'm submitting this to a UK workshop.

Case one: you can save one person from permanent paralysis, or you can save five people from broken legs. I think you save the five. The individual complaint of the paralysed person is weightier, but it doesn't feel like it swamps all five others put together — so some kind of agregation is happening.

Case two: you can save one person from death, or you can save a million people from a mild, temporary inconvenience — say, a day of nausea. Here I flip: I think you save the one. The million don't 'pool' their complaints into something that outweighs death. So numbers don't always agregate.

Case three: there's a rescue plan that saves forty people from serious injury, but it exposes one completely unrelated bystander to a 1% risk of death. I think that's unacceptable — the forty don't dilute the one bystander's reasonable rejection. But if the risk is 0.001%, I think it's okay. So there's some threshold where aggregation fails but tiny risks don't count?

Case four — and this is the one that breaks my brain: imagine a 'domino rescue' where saving patient A lets you save B, then C, all the way to Z, fifty people total. But the only way to start the chain is to perform an action that kills one outsider who isn't part of the chain. If I can save all fifty but only by killing the outsider, I think it's wrong. But if the choice is between saving just A (and therefore B through Z never get saved) versus saving A-through-Z and killing the outsider, I... actually think it's still wrong? Or maybe I think it's right? I'm genuinely unsure, and it feels like whatever I say here clashes with case two or case three.

So: is there a coherent contractualist principle — maybe about the structure of complaints rather than their sum — that captures cases one through three? Does case four force me to abandon the idea that complaints are never agregable? And what would your repaired principle say about a scenario I haven't listed, like saving ten people from moderate harm by imposing a distributed burden on each of them? Please use British spelling and phrasing throughout.
