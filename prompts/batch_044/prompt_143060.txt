My half-formed idea concerns Von Neumann's comparison of brain and computer in the lectures he delivered near the end of his life and that were published posthumously. The standard reading—both in philosophy of mind and in neuroscience historiography—treats his analogy as an early, somewhat naive form of computational functionalism: neurons as logic gates, the brain as a digital machine that happens to use wetware. But in those same lectures he repeatedly stresses that neural components are orders of magnitude less precise than vacuum tubes or transistors, and that the brain achieves reliability not through digital error-correction codes but through statistical redundancy and analog averaging. Most interpreters treat this as a practical limitation—noise that a better-engineered brain-computer would eliminate—rather than as a conceptual claim about the kind of computation involved.

My proposed question is this: Was Von Neumann's distinction between "exact" and "statistical" modes of operation meant to be the core of his brain-computer comparison, implying that biological computation is structurally different—not merely slower or noisier—because it relies on distributed statistical averaging rather than on discrete symbolic correction? And if so, why has that distinction been systematically downplayed in both computationalist and connectionist receptions of his work?
