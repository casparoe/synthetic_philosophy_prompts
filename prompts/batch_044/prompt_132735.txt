I've been re-reading Keynes's old essay about our grandkids and the 15-hour workweek — you know, the one where he imagines we'll eventually solve the economic problem and then face the real challenge of living wisely and agreeably. I'm not an economist, just a longtime lurker on these forums, and I was disscussing it with some friends after a course lecture on post-war economic optimisim. I kept noticing a specific arguement pattern that I don't have a word for, and it's driving me a little nuts.

First, someone brings up Keynes's prediction, admits it hasn't occured, and usually chuckles about naive 1930s techno-optimisim. Then they pivot: they say Keynes was actually onto something deeper, that he foresaw how psychologically hard leisure would be for people used to defining themselves through labor. So far that's a reasonable read. But then they immediately use that psychological-difficulty framing to defend exactly the kind of work-obsessed culture Keynes thought we'd outgrow — arguing we need career ladders, side hustles, or artificial scarcity of purpose because people can't handle unstructured time. Essentially they're using his warning about post-scarcity leisure as a justification for maintaining scarcity of purpose. Is there an established concept for this specific reversal? It's not quite a strawman, since they're engaging with his actual point, and it's not just political co-option either. If there's no name, can you coin one with clear boundaries — like, how exactly does it differ from plain misreading, from a motte-and-bailey where leisure is the bailey, or from being generally pessimistic about human nature? I care more about the carving than the label.
