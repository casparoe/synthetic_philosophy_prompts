You are an expert philosopher of physics and a professional speechwriter. Before drafting, please brainstorm briefly how a philosophical tension can structure an opening address rather than merely decorate it, and think carefully about how to include both specialists and non-specialists without misrepresenting the science.

I am a lower-sixth student, and I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at the Midlands Regional Sixth-Form Philosophy of Science Workshop. The room is split: half are A-level physics students who have recently studied Newton's bucket experiment; the other half are parents, school governors, and a local science journalist with no background in philosophy or physics.

Please write a polished, approximately five-minute address. The philosophical idea — the dispute between spacetime substantivalism and relationism, centred on Newton's rotating-bucket argument — must do real structural work. Use the bucket observation (that water climbs the sides of the vessel even when it is at rest relative to the container) not as a simple proof of absolute space, but as a framing device: just as the experiment forces us to ask whether rotation is defined relative to distant bodies, to empty space itself, or only to other material objects, the workshop should ask whether scientific disagreements are ever purely empirical or whether they always depend on prior choices about what counts as the background structure against which change is measured. Please inject one somewhat niche but well-established idea: mention that general relativity's hole argument revives the relationalist challenge by showing that spacetime points inside a matter-free region can be permuted without producing any physical difference, yet observations of frame-dragging suggest that local inertial frames are physically coupled to distant rotating masses, complicating any straightforward relationalist victory. Navigate this tension without resolving it, and let it shape what I can and cannot say — I must not claim that physics has settled the matter, I must not present the bucket as an unambiguous triumph for Newton, and I must avoid implying that the audience needs specialised texts to follow the discussion. The speech should land with warmth, comfort both halves of the room, avoid citations, bibliographies, or reading lists entirely, and never ask the audience to consult further sources. Think carefully, then draft the address.
