I am preparing the interview outline for a colloquium podcast on comparative moral philosophy. Our guest is an unnamed contemporary philosopher who defends a reconstructed Confucian role ethics built around what she calls asymmetrical reciprocity structures: she accepts the classic five relationships but insists that each role contains an internal revision trigger—activated when a superior party systematically fails in the corresponding virtue—so that a minister’s loyalty, for example, does not disappear but transforms into a ritualized duty of remonstrance rather than unconditional obedience. She maintains that this mechanism answers the liberal autonomy objection without importing Kantian universalism, since the revision is relational rather than rights-based, and she has recently extended the framework to institutional design, proposing that modern organizations be rebuilt around role clusters rather than rights-bearing individuals, though she acknowledges that exit from such clusters is not merely vocational but involves partial self-dissolution. She also holds that digital anonymity erodes mianzi—the face necessary for role ethics—so virtual communities require artificial role anchors, though she insists these are pedagogical rather than coercive.

I need eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking her to summarize the view in general terms. I want at least one question focused specifically on the revision trigger: ask how it distinguishes itself from both unconditional obedience and liberal contract theory, with a follow-up anticipating her reply that the trigger is ritually mediated rather than subjectively invoked—specifically, ask what happens when the ritual vocabulary itself is not shared across cultures, as in multinational bureaucracies. I also want a question on her institutional proposal: ask whether role clusters can accommodate simultaneous incompatible roles—such as a civil servant who also acts as a whistleblower—with a follow-up keyed to her likely defense that roles are hierarchically ordered, pressing whether that ordering is discovered or imposed. Finally, include a question about the digital application that does not simply restate her claim: ask whether artificial role anchors in anonymous spaces risk recreating the authoritarian asymmetries she seeks to avoid, and provide a follow-up for her probable response that they are pedagogical, asking by what criterion we distinguish pedagogical anchoring from disguised coercion.
