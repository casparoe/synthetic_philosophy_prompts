Write opening remarks—approximately six minutes—for a symposium titled Form Without a Speaker, delivered to a mixed audience of literary translators, philosophers of language, and engineers who design constraint-based generative text systems. The occasion is diagnostic rather than celebratory: the gathering asks whether classical translation theory, particularly the tension explored in Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot between preserving the acoustic form of a poem and preserving its semantic content, can be applied to texts that have no human author and therefore no intended meaning behind their formal constraints.

Structure the remarks around a single invented case study. Imagine a stanza produced by an algorithm running strict Oulipo-style rules—specifically a lipogram that suppresses the letter e across every line—written in French. Now imagine translating it into Finnish, where the suppression is morphologically trivial because of agglutination but acoustically destructive because it eliminates the vowel-harmony patterns that give Finnish its characteristic sound. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation of specific scholars; instead, let Hofstadter's distinction between formal constraint as decorative ornament and formal constraint as the poem's primary meaning do the structural work of the speech.

The remarks should argue that in human-authored constrained writing the translator can appeal to the author's struggle with the rule to justify preserving the surface acoustic pattern even at the cost of literal fidelity. But when the constraint is executed by a machine that experiences no difficulty, that justification collapses. The translator must then choose between reproducing the generative rule itself—treating the algorithmic constraint as the only real content of the text—or preserving the accidental acoustic surface that the rule happened to produce in French, knowing that the same rule produces an entirely different surface in Finnish. The speech must be intelligible to the translators without losing the engineers, should avoid resolving the tension, and should conclude by posing a single divisive question: can a constraint that caused no conscious effort still be called the soul of the poem? End on that question without answering it.
