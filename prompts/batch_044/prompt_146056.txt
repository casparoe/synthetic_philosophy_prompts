I'm a science writer working on a piece about collective minds, and I keep going back to an interview I did with a cognitive philospher — let's call her Dr. Voss — about where consciousness actually lives. Her takes so far are pretty specific: a single ant has no mind at all, but an ant colony like the old Aunt Hillary fugue-thing definitely does have one collective mind. She said yes to a beehive too, which surprised me, but no to a slime mold solving a maze, even though it acts smart. She thinks a split-brain patient is still just one mind, not two. She laughed off current language models running on server clusters — not even in the running, she said. A Portuguese man o' war gets a yes from her, but a human corporation with AI assistants gets a hard no. So far so good, right?

But now I'm looking at this new synthetic system — a "fugue mesh" — that engineers built to mirror the ant colony's chemical-gradient loops. Thousands of simple silicon nodes, no DNA, no neurons, arranged in a persistent feedback protocol that maintains a global world-model, kind of like Aunt Hillary but running on chips. If she's being consistent with herself, does the mesh get a mind? I'm less interested in a flat yes-or-no than in which rule she's actually running — maybe evolutionary superorganism plus integrated state, maybe functional integration regardless of substrate, maybe something about persistent self-models — and what other rules could fit her same verdicts just as well. If those rules split on the mesh, what's the one further case — maybe a synthetic beehive made of nodes, or a slime-mold circuit wired into silicon — that would actually tell them apart?

Also, can you seperate what's pretty settled in philosphy of mind here from what's genuinely up for grabs among people who actually study this? I don't want one camp's view presented like it's the consensus. I'm writing this on my phone so excuse the typo's, but I need the analysis sharp.
