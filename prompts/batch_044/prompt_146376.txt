I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a workshop honoring my former advisor, Dr. Lena Vasquez, whose work on distributed cognition is familiar to half the room and entirely unfamiliar to the other half, which includes her family, the archivists now holding her papers, and several artists she collaborated with. Last night I had a long, exhausting argument with a friend about whether the extended mind thesis carries any real ontological weight or is merely a metaphor for praising notebooks and smartphones, and I want that disagreement to become the structural spine of the remarks rather than being smoothed away. Please write these opening remarks in American English as exactly five brief movements, numbered 1 through 5, each only a sentence or two long, with no separate introduction or conclusion outside those five; together they should form a coherent address that works for philosophers and non-philosophists alike. The idea must do real work: distinguish functional parity from causal coupling, treat Elena’s annotated scale models—which her students still handle, correct, and argue over—as a concrete case of potential cognitive extension rather than mere representation, and mark the precise point where the question stops being empirical and becomes one of values about presence and absence. Do not offer false consolation by saying she lives on in her books or that her mind now resides in the archive, but do not deny that something cognitively real happens when a student thinks with her objects. Let that tension shape every movement, keep the language accessible enough that a grieving family member can follow without feeling lectured, and avoid any bibliography or citation.
