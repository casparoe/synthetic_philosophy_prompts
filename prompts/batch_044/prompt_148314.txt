I am a doctoral candidate in comparative political economy, and I am trying to formalize the modal implications of path-dependent institutional development for my dissertation. I have been working with the assumption that if a historical policy sequence exhibits strong path dependence—specifically, the irreversible layering of unemployment-insurance regimes and active labor-market policies onto pre-existing poor-relief institutions—then this demonstrates that the resulting welfare-state configuration is historically contingent in a strong counterfactual sense. That is, I take micro-level lock-in to entail that the same macro-level equilibrium could not have been reached via an alternative sequencing of choices, because reversing the initial contingent branch would be prohibitively costly. However, I am concerned that I may be committing a category error by inferring macro-level uniqueness from micro-level irreversibility. Could you clarify whether path dependence actually restricts the set of reachable institutional equilibria, or whether it only governs the transitional dynamics, distributional costs, and formal properties of the path taken? In other words, does lock-in prove that the outcome would not have arrived anyway, or does it leave open the possibility of convergence to a functionally equivalent endpoint through a different causal route?
