I coordinate an interdisciplinary reading group for final-year students in political theory—most of them have worked through Rawls and some Foucault but have never taken a course in epistemology. Last week a colleague mentioned pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism in passing, and I realized I want to spend our next ninety-minute session on the tension between them, but I am deliberately avoiding the standard bank-case examples because this group thinks in institutional and policy terms. I need a teaching plan, not an explanation: the architecture of how I should introduce the ideas in sequence, with dependencies made explicit.

Specifically, I want the entire session built around one running scenario: a climate researcher holding preliminary but not peer-reviewed data about an accelerating permafrost methane feedback loop, who must decide whether she can sincerely say we know this is occurring in two different settings—first, to a government advisory panel where the practical stakes involve immediate resource allocation, and second, to journalists where the conversational context involves public credibility but the stakes are mediated through media framing. Plan the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that pragmatic encroachment and contextualism emerge as genuinely competing explanations rather than two names for the same phenomenon; identify the carrying features this scenario must have and the features it must lack, including why it should not involve obvious false belief; map the misconceptions these students are likely to form—I am especially concerned they will collapse practical stakes into conversational context, or interpret contextualism as a form of relativism about climate facts—and specify the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted; provide one check-for-understanding question for the end that tests whether they have grasped the conceptual separation rather than just memorized labels; and state clearly what formal apparatus, such as safety conditions, invariantist semantics, or modal tracking conditions, I should explicitly refuse to introduce at this level. I ask for a committed response: take definite positions on the ordering, on how the scenario is deployed across both settings, and on the misconception map, giving reasons for each choice rather than laying out options for me to choose among.
