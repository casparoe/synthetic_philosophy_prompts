I am drafting a short essay for a course on moral responsibility, and I would be grateful for your comments and suggestions. The essay engages with P. F. Strawson’s well-known distinction between reactive attitudes—such as resentment, gratitude, and indignation—and the objective attitude we adopt when we regard someone as not a fit subject for ordinary interpersonal responses. Rather than treating this as a strict binary, I want to argue that contemporary institutional practices generate what I shall call “bureaucratised reactive stances”: formalised mechanisms of censure and rehabilitation that preserve the normative structure of interpersonal blame but strip away its direct emotional expression. For instance, a medical regulatory board that suspends a negligent surgeon and mandates retraining does not feel personal resentment in the Strawsonian sense, yet its decision follows a logic structurally analogous to forgiveness and reinstatement: it treats the surgeon as an agent who can be held to account, demands recognition of fault, and conditions restoration of status upon demonstrated change. I suggest that Strawson’s framework therefore needs a middle category—neither fully reactive nor fully objective—to capture how modern organisations hold people responsible without engaging in face-to-face emotional confrontation.

I am particularly interested in whether this intermediate category is philosophically coherent, or whether it collapses back into either the reactive or the objective pole once analysed closely. I also wonder whether my use of the medical board example genuinely supports the claim, or whether it might be better explained by purely instrumental or deterrent reasoning that has nothing to do with reactive attitudes at all. If the idea holds up, I would like to extend it to automated decision-making systems that impose penalties—such as algorithmic credit scoring—where there is clearly no emotional subject behind the sanction, but where the affected party is still treated as an accountable agent rather than a mere causal system to be managed. Could you comment on the accuracy of my reading of Strawson, on the coherence and interest of the proposed middle category, on the strength of the examples, and on any improvements to the structure or argument?
