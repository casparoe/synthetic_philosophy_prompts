You serve on the clinical ethics committee of a psychiatric hospital that evaluates decision-making capacity in adults with severe dissociative and identity-fragmentation presentations. Rather than relying solely on standard cognitive tests, your unit has adopted the philosophical framework that a self is not a substance but a center of narrative gravity — a focal point around which autobiographical stories orbit, attract other narratives, and generate predictions about behavior. You need a reusable decision procedure your committee can run in under an hour to determine whether a given patient possesses a sufficiently stable narrative center to provide informed consent for a high-risk intervention, such as discontinuing a long-term medication, enrolling in an experimental therapy, or authorizing a do-not-resuscitate order.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of operational tests, with the following structure: first, the specific observation or interview question that probes narrative gravity; second, the threshold that distinguishes a functioning center from absence or fragmentation; third, the point at which the procedure yields a definite capacity-absent or capacity-present determination; and fourth, the explicit hand-off points where the question is no longer about narrative coherence but about values — for example, whether a coherent but self-destructive narrative should be honored, or whether family members' competing gravitational narratives should be weighted. The procedure must handle three hard cases: a patient whose autobiographical narratives reorganized radically after trauma and now present two competing centers with roughly equal orbital mass; a patient with an extremely coherent but largely confabulated narrative that successfully predicts behavior but lacks correspondence with documented history; and a patient whose narrative center is stable but almost entirely delegated to a spouse or caregiver, so that first-person stories are scarce but third-person predictions remain highly accurate. Apply the procedure to all three cases, including one where the procedure returns an answer that conflicts with the clinical intuition that the patient seems like themselves. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot adjudicate — for instance, whether narrative gravity is sufficient for moral personhood, or whether a temporary loss of center permanently destroys identity — and flag the judgments that must remain with the committee rather than the checklist.
