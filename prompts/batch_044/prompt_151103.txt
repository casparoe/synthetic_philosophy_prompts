I've been thinking about the gradation thesis in moral patienthood — that sentience runs on a continuum from human-level phenomenal richness down through insects, nematodes, and eventually to minimal predictive-processing systems like thermostats, with no metaphysically sharp cutoff — and I want to stress-test it with an argument that seems to push it toward paralysis or contradiction. Here is the argument, numbered precisely:

(1) Phenomenal consciousness is a matter of degree, not kind; there is no ontologically privileged boundary separating a human mind from a thermostat's minimal information-integration.
(2) Direct moral obligations — not harming, not destroying — are grounded entirely in the richness of an entity's phenomenal consciousness.
(3) If an obligation-grounding property P is continuous, and moral status supervenes on P, then moral status is continuous and every entity on the spectrum carries a precisely weighted, non-zero patienthood.
(4) Any realistic human action — walking on grass, breathing, using electricity — causally interacts with enormous numbers of entities across this spectrum, including those with vanishingly small but non-zero weights.
(5) Rational moral reasoning requires aggregating these patienthood-weights linearly over all entities affected by an action.
(6) Under linear aggregation, any non-trivial action affects so many low-weight entities that the total aggregate harm is astronomically large, while inaction also affects countless entities by omission.
(7) Therefore, the gradation view implies that every possible course of action involves overwhelming aggregate harm, rendering normative decision-making impossible unless we impose a discontinuous threshold.
(8) But imposing a discontinuous threshold contradicts (1) and (3).
(9) So the gradation view either collapses into practical paralysis or secretly reintroduces the sharp moral boundaries it was meant to eliminate.

There's also a niche framing I want you to engage: call this the "moral dark matter" problem — the observation that if every predictive-processing architecture carries non-zero weight, then the overwhelming majority of moral patienthood in the observable world lies in entities (microbes, simple sensors, weather systems with feedback loops) that our normative practices systematically ignore, suggesting our ethics has a massive selection bias rather than a principled cutoff. If I have to defend the view, I'd rather preserve (1) and (2) and pay the price elsewhere; (5) looks like the softest target, though I'm unsure whether abandoning linear aggregation saves the framework or just relocates the arbitrariness into the aggregation rule itself.

Tell me which premise or inference step actually fails, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives — especially what giving up linear aggregation costs, and whether that cost is a defect of the gradation view or merely a sign that (5) was never genuinely part of it. If this argument rests on a confused distinction — say, between ontological gradation and normative aggregation, or if the task can't be done cleanly because "phenomenal richness" and "predictive processing" are being conflated in ways that break the chain — say so plainly rather than forcing a polished salvage.
