After a long argument last night with a friend about whether personal identity could literally be distributed, I find myself stuck on a specific reductio that keeps returning in different forms. The friend accepts something close to Hofstadter’s idea that the self is not a soul or a brain-bound substance but a strange loop of recursive self-reference, and that if two brains were coupled closely enough—continuously exchanging information so that the loop traverses both substrates in real time—there would be one self distributed across them, not two selves with shared access. I think I see where this leads, and it seems to prove too much about harm and moral status. Here is the argument as I reconstructed it. (1) If the self is this kind of loop, its persistence conditions are functional and structural rather than tied to a single biological substrate; a loop can be distributed across coupled systems as long as the recursive pattern is maintained in real time. (2) Suppose brains B1 and B2 are coupled in exactly this way, so that there is one distributed self S spanning both. (3) If B1 is destroyed but B2 remains intact and the loop continues running through B2, then S has not been destroyed; only part of its substrate footprint has been lost. (4) The moral wrong of murder is plausibly the destruction of a self—the termination of that loop—rather than merely the destruction of a biological organism. (5) Therefore, destroying B1 in this scenario is not murdering S. (6) At most, it is a partial harm to S proportional to the reduction in substrate—say, one-half if there were two coupled brains, one-third if three, and so on. (7) But this implies that if a self is distributed across enough coupled brains, killing any one host body becomes morally negligible, and it also implies that B1’s own first-person experience of dying is not the death of the self, which seems to erase the moral significance of that experience altogether. I want to know which premise or inference to reject, and what rejecting it costs for the distributed-self view. Is the error in assuming that a distributed loop counts as numerically one self? In treating substrate loss as a divisible harm? Or in applying the moral concept of murder to functional loops rather than to living human beings? I would prefer to keep the core picture—self as strange loop, substrate-independent—if possible, but I am willing to give it up if the cost of saving it elsewhere is too high. Where does the argument actually break, and what do the standard positions say about whether we should deny numerical identity across substrates, deny that harm scales with substrate footprint, or deny that the moral wrong tracks the loop rather than the organism?
