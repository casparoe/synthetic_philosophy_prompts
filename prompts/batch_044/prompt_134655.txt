In discussions of special relativity and the ontology of time, a familiar analogy is pressed into service: just as there is no absolute "here" in space—no unique spatial location that is ontologically privileged—so there is no absolute "now" in time. The relativity of simultaneity, on this reading, shows that different inertial frames partition the world into different sets of simultaneous events, much as different spatial perspectives partition space into different "heres." From this, proponents of the block universe conclude that all temporal locations—past, present, and future—exist on a par with all spatial locations: the present is merely indexical, like "here," and eternalism follows.

I want an audit of this analogy, specifically as it functions within the Rietdijk-Putnam line of reasoning. First, lay out the correspondence explicitly: what in the spatial case plays the role of the inertial frame, of simultaneity, of the denial of a privileged slice, and of the ontological conclusion? Second, identify the points where the mapping fractures—pay particular attention to whether the analogy quietly imports mereological assumptions (for instance, that a persisting object is a four-dimensional "world-tube" composed of temporal parts) that are not entailed by the physics of relativity itself, and to whether causal asymmetry and the direction of entropy give temporal separation a structural feature that spatial separation lacks. Third, determine whether the conclusion—that all times are equally real—survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the argument succeeds only as a claim about indexicality and fails as an ontological proof. Finally, if there is a more precise analogy that captures what relativity actually establishes without overreaching into metaphysics, sketch it and say what work it can and cannot do for the block universe.
