As a journalist who covers science and philosophy, I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks for a one-day interdisciplinary workshop convening evolutionary biologists, philosophers of biology, and historians of science; the event is titled "What Is a Function For?" and is being held in the main lecture hall of a university museum of natural history. The audience is sharply divided: roughly half are practicing researchers who speak of gene and protein function in terms of present causal contribution to fitness, while the other half are philosophers committed to the view that such causal-role accounts collapse normative malfunction into mere statistical deviation and thereby erase the very distinction that makes biology explanatory rather than merely descriptive. I need an address of about seven minutes that does not summarize the debate from a safe distance but actually enacts it structurally: I want the tension between etiological, or selected-effect, accounts and causal-role accounts to determine what I can and cannot legitimately claim about why this room exists, what shared project binds its members, and whether the workshop itself has a "function" that could be malfunctioning. To keep the framing from being pure decoration, I want to inject a specific conceptual lens I have been developing in my reporting—treating the etiological account as a kind of institutional memory encoded in selection history, and the causal-role account as a present-tense job description assigned by systemic context—and I want this metaphor to do real argumentative work, shaping the rhetorical arc rather than ornamenting it. The speech must land precisely for the philosophers without alienating the biologists, it must avoid any request for bibliographies or citations since I am speaking rather than publishing, and it should steer clear of hyper-obscure technical literature that only a handful of specialists have memorized; instead, the machinery should remain conceptually rigorous but publicly accessible. Please write the address, allowing the philosophical distinction to govern what can be said about the occasion itself.
