I'm the lead reliability engineer at a mid-size firm that makes ceramic-matrix composites for jet engines. Every quarter I have to sign off on whether a part that failed in testing actually revealed a real brittle disposition in the material, or whether the failure was finked or masked by some interfering condition — a thermal coating, an internal stress-relief lattice that only activates at room temperature, that kind of thing. The lawyers care because if we classify it as a genuine disposition we have to recall the whole line; if we call it an artifact we recieve the liability if it happens again in flight. I need a reusable decision procdure I can run in about forty minutes per case, with no philosophy PhD required from whoever fills out the form. It should be a sequence of tests or questions in strict order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, a clear stop for "masked or finked — no recall," a clear stop for "genuine disposition — recall," and explicit flags for where the call is actually a value judgment about risk tolerance rather than a fact. Then apply it to three cases: (1) a turbine blade that only shattered after its thermal barrier coating was accidentally stripped during cleaning; (2) a composite panel that seemed ductile but contained hidden micro-lattices that absorb shear stress only below 200°C; and (3) a deadlock or unwanted-answer case — say, the material genuinely has the brittle disposition but it's masked in 99% of real-world flight conditions, so the recall feels wasteful but the procdure demands it anyway. Be honest about where nested masks and reverse finks (where the interfering mechanism itself is unreliable) will break the flow, and say exactly what the procdure cannot do.
