Construct a thought experiment involving a realistic, non-fictional governance institution: a seasonal inter-basin water authority that convenes only during designated flood-risk months and issues binding allocations whose legal force extends retroactively to populations who relocate into the affected watershed after deliberation ends but before enforcement begins. Design the scenario so that the all-affected-interests principle and the all-subjected-to-coercive-authority principle yield opposite membership verdicts regarding the same identifiable group—specifically, include individuals who suffer direct material consequences from the allocation yet were absent and not subject to the authority's coercive apparatus during deliberation, and include individuals who were present, subject, and voting-eligible but experience no meaningful impact from the decision. Present the scenario in one paragraph, without invoking omniscient observers, perfect predictors, or speculative technology. Then demonstrate that the case meets each specification: list the affected-but-not-subjected individuals and the subjected-but-not-affected individuals; state the membership verdict each principle delivers; identify the precise structural feature—the retroactive temporal gap combined with seasonal jurisdiction—that forces the divergence; and explain how a defender of the principle that produces the counterintuitive result could undermine the case by attacking that feature. The purpose is to test whether democratic boundary conditions should track causal impact or coercive subjection when the two dimensions separate in a plausible institutional form.
