I am delivering the opening address at our university archive's centennial symposium. The audience is split between archivists and historians on one side, and donors and family members with no experience of finding aids on the other. During the past year, serving on an acquisition committee, I noticed that our provenance policy—requiring documented legal ownership for every item—systematically excludes papers that moved through informal care and labor networks, especially records tied to women and migrant workers who never held contractual title. I want the remarks to honor the archive's preservation work and welcome both groups, but the philosophical problem must structure what I can say: the provenance rule embeds a specific theory of whose lives count as historically legible and whose agency is recordable. If I treat the gaps as accidental omissions, I betray the symposium's purpose; if I accuse the institution of corruption, I alienate the staff and donors who made this gathering possible. Write a five-minute address that uses this institutional practice as its backbone—making the tension between preservation and exclusion tangible for non-specialists without turning into a lecture—so that the room understands why we are studying silences rather than merely filling them.
