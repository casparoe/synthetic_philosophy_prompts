I have noticed a recurring pattern in theoretical modeling that sits near, but is not identical to, the standard underdetermination of theory by evidence. Consider climate models that represent ocean heat uptake either through a single-layer diffusion equation or through a multi-layer system tracking internal mixing: calibrated to match observations, both generate identical surface-temperature projections, yet one treats deep-ocean currents as physically real causal drivers while the other treats them as smoothing artifacts with no independent ontology. Researchers often invoke the realist reading when explaining mechanisms to policymakers, then quietly adopt the instrumentalist reading when discussing regions where the variables have no direct measurable counterpart. The same oscillation occurs in macroeconomics, where representative-agent and heterogeneous-agent frameworks share aggregate dynamics but differ radically on whether individual-level states are real or merely bookkeeping constructs. Is there an established term for this specific maneuver—interpretive switching between realist and instrumentalist readings of one and the same formalism, driven not by empirical divergence but by argumentative context? If such a concept exists, how exactly does it differ from ordinary theory underdetermination, from structural realism, and from conventionalism about theoretical entities? If it does not exist, I would like a precise definition together with boundary conditions that distinguish it from mere equivocation and from the addition of ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses.
