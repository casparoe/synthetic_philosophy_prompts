Consider a proposed regulatory framework—call it the Transparency Registry—for conversational AI systems. Under its rules, a system must signal uncertainty when it lacks confidence in a response, but it may do so with soft hedging language rather than with numerical probabilities. The framework penalizes a system that asserts a falsehood with high confidence, treating that as deceptive. However, it does not penalize a system that withholds information it actually possesses if the refusal is framed politely, for example by saying that the question falls outside its scope. I have noticed that we treat human witnesses very differently: if an expert deliberately omits a relevant fact they know to be true, we generally regard that as deceptive by omission, regardless of how politely they decline to speak. This makes me wonder what underlying conception of honesty and deception the Registry is built on. Does it presuppose that deception requires an active, high-confidence false assertion, and that omission is not deception because an AI lacks intentions? Or does it rest on a different view—perhaps that an AI's only duty is to avoid inducing false beliefs in the user, not to correct existing ones? Could you reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory here, say whether its parts are consistent with each other, and tell me what specific rules would have to change if we replaced that theory with the view that an AI should be held to the same standard as a human expert witness, for whom both lying and strategic silence can count as deception?
