I'm drafting a scene in which a philosopher character argues that a fluent large language model should be regarded as a counterfeit person in roughly Dennett's sense — engineered to invite the intentional stance without the internal architecture that makes that stance genuinely appropriate. To sharpen the dialogue, I want to audit an analogy that dominates popular discussion: treating such an AI as a conversational partner is like accepting counterfeit currency. Both circulate through trust, both lack the institutional or metaphysical backing of the genuine article, and both threaten harm precisely because the system cannot tell them apart. Could you map the correspondences explicitly — what serves as the mint, the metal reserve, the circulation network, the counterfeit detector, the economic damage? Then show where the mapping fractures, particularly with respect to Dennett's claim that personhood is not a hidden essence but a stance we adopt. Does the currency analogy secretly import the assumption that there must be some "backing" — biological, phenomenal, or authoritative — that authentic personhood possesses and AI lacks? And once those disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that we ought to reject AI interlocutors as fraudulent survive, or did the analogy do all the normative work?
