A neighborhood association is opposing a proposed four-story apartment building at the corner of a residential block composed primarily of detached homes with direct sidewalk frontage. While most opponents cite increased traffic and diminished property values, one resident offers a structurally different objection. She contends that the block operates as a moral community because each dwelling maintains unmediated visual access to the street and to adjacent porches, a condition she terms accountable proximity: individuals maintain shared spaces not out of contractual obligation but because they feel individually visible and responsible for them. The proposed structure, she argues, would replace this with internal corridors and shared entrances where responsibility is distributed across an anonymous collective, not because future residents are less virtuous, but because the architecture itself severs the link between personal presence and public care. She asks whether defending this specific spatial arrangement can be philosophically separated from mere resistance to change, or whether every appeal to a neighborhood's moral ecology ultimately masks a preference for excluding outsiders. Is there a genuine distinction between protecting the conditions for localized moral responsibility and enforcing an exclusionary right to the city's present form?
