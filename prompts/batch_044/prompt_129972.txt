As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been following a long-running dispute in philosophy of mind about the meta-problem of consciousness—specifically, why we intuit that subjective experience creates an explanatory gap that physical neuroscience cannot close. On one side stand those I will call the gap realists: they hold that explaining the cognitive mechanisms behind our intuition (the meta-problem) still leaves untouched the question of why there is something it is like to be a conscious system at all. They argue that phenomenal concepts have an irreducible first-person character, so any third-person functional account necessarily misses the target. On the other side are the dissolutionists, who claim that once we have a complete mechanistic account of the machinery generating our introspective reports—analogous, from my engineering perspective, to an introspective compiler that outputs phenomenological descriptions without direct access to underlying state variables—the original hard problem dissolves into a misunderstanding of our own representational architecture. For decades both camps have rehearsed the same moves: the realists insist that reproducing the subjective format of experience is the explanatory standard, while the dissolutionists reply that reproducing the functional and predictive dynamics is sufficient and that demanding more is incoherent. Neither side shifts. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about consciousness itself but about hidden disagreements over explanatory standards—whether a functional-computational description counts as explaining an experience if it does not reproduce the subjective format, or whether reproducing that format is even a coherent demand. But I am unsure whether this is a verbal disagreement about the word explanation, a substantive but empirically hidden disagreement about what brain mechanisms actually produce introspection, or a case of two disciplines answering different questions—one metaphysical, one cognitive-scientific—under the single heading the meta-problem. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each side actually claims, not as a survey of interpretations. Is there a conceptual distinction—perhaps between representational content and presentational mode, or between mechanistic and phenomenal-level explanations—that would reveal whether progress is possible? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, given that any answer must either deny our first-person authority (which feels like changing the subject) or accept an unexplained primitive (which feels like giving up)? What, concretely, would count as progress here: a new empirical result about introspective mechanisms, a formal result about representational formats, a reframing of the explanatory target, or should we expect no resolution at all?
