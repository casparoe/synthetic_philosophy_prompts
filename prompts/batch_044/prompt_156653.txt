Construct a decision-tree taxonomy that maps the logical space of positions on moral desert, determinism, and punishment, using the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso as its organizing spine but extending well beyond those two named figures. The instrument should be designed for a reader who can answer each diagnostic question without knowing the philosophical labels in advance, so that the tree sorts by substantive commitments rather than by author loyalty.

Begin with a root case rather than an abstract definition: consider a convicted offender whose violent behavior is traced to a deterministic neural lesion, who expresses sincere remorse, and who voluntarily requests a longer, stricter confinement as a form of self-correction. The first branching question must ask whether granting that request treats him with the kind of respect that requires he genuinely deserve harsher conditions, or whether it merely applies a therapeutically appropriate intervention—this split separates retributive from non-retributive frameworks without invoking the phrase free will.

From that root, build exactly five binary internal nodes. Each node should present a concrete claim or case on which the positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that an informed non-specialist can choose a side. The five splits should be:
(i) whether determinism undermines the control condition for moral responsibility, the sourcehood condition, both, or neither—this captures the Dennett-style compatibilist distinction from Caruso-style hard determinism;
(ii) whether the justification for punishment can be fully captured by forward-looking social-coordination and risk-management goals, or whether it necessarily involves a backward-looking entitlement that the offender has forfeited;
(iii) whether a quarantine model of confinement collapses into disguised retributivism whenever the severity or duration of isolation tracks the gravity of the past act rather than the present risk profile of the agent;
(iv) whether expressive or communicative accounts of punishment can preserve the victim's claim to recognition without smuggling in a metaphysically loaded notion of desert;
(v) whether there is a coherent intermediate position—call it procedural desert—holding that an agent can rationally endorse rules whose sanctions apply deterministically to her, thereby generating a non-retributive but non-instrumental reason for punishment that neither Dennett nor Caruso explicitly occupies.

The tree should terminate in exactly eight leaves. For each leaf, provide:
- a concise label (named position if one exists in the literature, otherwise unnamed);
- one sentence stating the position's core claim about the neuroengineered offender;
- one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment—the consequence or intuition it must sacrifice;
- a brief flag noting whether this combination is actually defended, unexplored but coherent, or incoherent, with a one-sentence diagnosis of any incoherence.

In addition, include a short meta-section—no more than 200 words—identifying which pairs of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate. Specifically, flag any positions that share answers across all five nodes yet diverge in practical recommendation, and explain whether that divergence stems from an unmeasured empirical belief, an unmeasured normative weighting, or a conceptual ambiguity that the taxonomy should have captured but missed.

The prompt should avoid asking for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to obscure texts. It should rely on general philosophical familiarity with compatibilism, hard determinism, retributivism, and public-health models of punishment, combined with the specific conceptual distinctions introduced above.
