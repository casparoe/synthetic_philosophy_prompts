I'm trying to work out what I actually think about how moral consideration scales with both the probability of sentience and the number of entities involved, using my gut reactions to concrete cases rather than a pre-committed theory.

Here are my verdicts, numbered.

1. A single human being with ordinary consciousness has full moral weight; I would prioritize saving one human over saving any number of clearly non-sentient objects, and I do not think the scale argument applies here.

2. A single insect—say, a worker ant—where I assign perhaps a 0.1% chance of minimal sentience, carries almost no direct moral weight on its own. I do not feel I have done something gravely wrong if I accidentally kill one.

3. But a colony of 10^20 ants, or even 10^25, should not collectively outweigh one human, despite the fact that linear expectation might yield hundreds or thousands of expected sentient minds. The numbers do not seem to compound into a human-level claim.

4. If I am genuinely about 60% confident that an octopus is sentient, then one octopus should receive roughly 60% of the moral weight of a human, scaled proportionally rather than multiplied by population size.

5. A digital simulation running 10^18 parallel copies of an ant-level neural process, each with that same 0.1% subjective probability, should not generate a collective claim that rivals humanity. The substrate and the absence of biological integration seem to block aggregation, even if the epistemic probabilities are identical.

6. When allocating limited resources, I think funding a treatment that saves one human life takes clear priority over funding an intervention that prevents suffering in billions of agricultural insects, even though the latter might affect far more expected sentient subjects.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: suppose we discover that a particular beetle species has a 3% chance of possessing a minimal form of phenomenal consciousness, and there are approximately 10^19 living individuals. By linear expectation, that is 300 million expected sentient beings. Does that generate a collective moral claim comparable to humanity's? One part of me says yes, the math is unavoidable; another part says no, because moral weight should not aggregate across epistemic possibilities the way expected utility aggregates.

Is there a single principle, or a small family of candidates, that captures verdicts 1 through 5 without collapsing? For each principle you offer, please state which of my cases motivates each clause, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed—specifically, whether a small group of 500 highly intelligent but non-human animals with 80% sentience confidence should be weighed against one human—and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon. I am especially interested in whether any coherent view can keep both my rejection of the scale argument for insects and my acceptance of proportional scaling for octopuses, or whether those two commitments are genuinely in tension.
