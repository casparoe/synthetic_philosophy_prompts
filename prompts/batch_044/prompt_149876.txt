My judgments about the death penalty across several concrete configurations, and whether they cohere into a single view. Case one: an offender has intentionally killed, guilt is epistemically certain—no procedural gap survives scrutiny, no plausible alternative explanation remains—and execution proceeds. My verdict is that this act is morally required on retributive grounds; deterrence is irrelevant to justifying this particular execution. Case two: the same offender, with the same certainty of guilt, is executed within a legal system that retains a non-zero, irreducible risk of eventually executing an innocent person in some other case. Here my verdict reverses: the particular execution is impermissible, because authorizing an institution that will eventually produce a wrongful killing morally contaminates every individual act that institution performs. Case three: capital punishment is retained solely because it saves net lives—statistical evidence shows it deters enough murders to outweigh its own mortality, including the statistical risk of error—and every person executed is genuinely guilty of a capital crime. I judge the practice permissible, but only on the condition that execution is never driven by evidentiary convenience rather than by moral desert. Case four: within such a deterrence-justified system, selection for execution is driven not by the gravity of the offense but by the strength of the evidence—the state executes those it can convict with highest confidence, even when other offenders are equally or more blameworthy. My verdict is that this is impermissible, because it treats the executed individuals as instrumental to social welfare rather than as recipients of deserved punishment. Case five: a jurisdiction abolishes capital punishment entirely, accepts a modest, measurable increase in homicides, and thereby eliminates both the risk of wrongful execution and the possibility of instrumental selection. I judge this jurisdiction morally superior to the system in case three, despite the lives that abolition costs. These five verdicts feel stable in isolation, but they pull in opposing directions: case one and case two disagree about whether systemic risk can defeat token-level desert; case three and case four disagree about whether deterrence can coexist with a strict desert constraint on selection; and case five seems to reject the life-maximizing logic that made case three permissible in the first place. Is there a unified principle—perhaps one that distinguishes institutional authorization from token execution, or that ranks the avoidance of wrongful killing above the maximization of lives saved—that can preserve all five? If no such principle exists, which verdict should I relinquish, and does abandoning it commit me to pure retributivism, pure consequentialism, or a hybrid position with a precise boundary condition?
