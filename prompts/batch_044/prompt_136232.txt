I'm trying to name a defensive move I keep noticing in arguments where Sartrean vocabulary is available but turned backward. The pattern has three steps. First, someone makes a choice that benefits them at others' expense — for example, a manager restructuring her department to consolidate power, or a resident withdrawing from collective obligations. Second, when criticized, she does not deny freedom or invoke necessity; instead she describes the act as an anguished, groundless exercise of radical freedom made with full awareness of contingency. Third, she inverts the charge: to call the choice wrong is itself an evasion, an imposition of universal essence, a flight into the spirit of seriousness that denies her authenticity. The result is that the language Sartre used to expose self-deception becomes a shield against moral scrutiny. I have seen this in workplace ethics and in debates about civic duty, but I do not know whether philosophy has a precise term for it. Is there an established concept for this inversion — the weaponization of radical-freedom rhetoric to neutralize accountability? If there is not, can you coin a term and specify its boundary conditions, especially how it differs from classic bad faith (the denial of freedom), from ordinary rationalization, and from legitimate appeals to personal authenticity? Please say how confident you are in each part of your answer, and identify the point about which you are least certain.
