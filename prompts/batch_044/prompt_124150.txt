Consider a governance architecture in which citizens are never subjected to explicit statutory prohibitions on association or movement, but in which an opaque, continuously updated predictive algorithm assigns each individual a dynamic “trust score.” This score is not enforced by state agents; instead, it automatically modulates access to housing contracts, employment platforms, and transit networks through private automated agreements. Because citizens understand the scoring logic only imperfectly, they adapt their behavior preemptively—avoiding certain gatherings, altering travel patterns, and self-censoring speech—while those who do attempt unauthorized collective action find that venue-booking services, employer contracts, and mobility passes are downgraded or revoked without human review or legal process. No official ever issues a direct order.

Rank the three conceptions of liberty—negative liberty as non-interference (Berlin), positive liberty as self-mastery or autonomy, and republican liberty as non-domination (Pettit)—in first, second, and third place according to how adequately each captures the specific form of unfreedom present in this architecture. For each rank, provide a concise justification that explicitly references one structural feature of the scenario: the absence of direct command, the role of preemptive behavioral adaptation, or the mediation of constraint through private automated contracts. Then, in a brief concluding evaluation, state whether this case reveals a gap in all three conceptions—specifically regarding what we might call “distributed anticipatory interference”—and identify which of the three is most amenable to revision to accommodate it, with one sentence of reasoning.
