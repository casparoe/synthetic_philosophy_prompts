Suppose you are advising a small philosophy-of-religion research unit—three researchers, two years, no empirical component—on where to direct its efforts. The unit wants to work on conceptual questions that emerge from Wittgenstein's treatment of religious belief, particularly his insistence that religious commitment is not a matter of empirical hypothesis or theoretical explanation but operates according to a different grammar. It explicitly wants to avoid the well-worn taxonomy of whether Wittgenstein is best read as a fideist, a non-cognitivist, or a quietist. Instead, it asks which open lines of inquiry in this vicinity are both tractable for philosophers without theological specialization and consequential for broader debates about religious epistemology, disagreement, and the rationality of commitment.

Please prioritize among the following three lines of inquiry, defending your ordering with explicit criteria—such as upstream dependency on other questions, tractability within a two-year window, potential to change work outside Wittgenstein scholarship, and what argument or counter-example over the next year would cause you to reorder them:

First, whether Wittgenstein's distinction between religious and empirical belief can be made precise enough to support a principled account of how religious beliefs should respond to counter-evidence—without collapsing either into evidentialism or into a form of doxastic voluntarism that severs belief from rational assessment altogether.

Second, whether framing religious commitment as embedded in a form of life implies that inter-religious disagreement is necessarily incommensurable, or whether it can instead be reconstructed as a disagreement about practical orientation that allows for rational arbitration without presupposing a shared evidential framework.

Third, whether Wittgenstein's refusal to treat religious beliefs as hypotheses entails any specific position on the problem of divine hiddenness—specifically, whether hiddenness is even a coherent problem within a framework that denies that the existence of God is the kind of claim for which absence of evidence could be evidence of absence.

In your answer, do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Focus on which line is most worth pursuing first, which depends on resolving another, and what resolving any one of them would actually change for philosophers of religion who do not specialize in Wittgenstein.
