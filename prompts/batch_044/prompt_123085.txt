The philosophical exchange about hypocrisy and moral blame has settled into a repetitive cycle. One position holds that hypocrisy is not merely an ancillary vice but a direct defeater of standing: to blame another for failing X while oneself failing X involves something like a performative contradiction, implicitly exempting oneself from the norm being invoked, and therefore robs the blamer of the moral authority required for legitimate blame. The opposing position maintains that standing depends on the target's actual wrongness and the blamer's epistemic access, not on the blamer's own moral record; hypocrisy may undermine credibility or pragmatic force, but it does not falsify the content of the blame, and treating it as a defeater mistakes a sociological obstacle for a normative failure. For years both sides have appealed to the same cases—the environmental activist who condemns carbon use while flying regularly, the physician who advises against smoking while smoking, the partner who demands fidelity after being unfaithful—and both agree on the descriptive judgments: the blamer is indeed a hypocrite, and the target's conduct is indeed wrong. Yet no position shifts.

My hypothesis is that part of the stall is verbal: one camp uses standing to denote a normative entitlement that requires clean hands or at least non-complicity in the failing blamed, while the other uses it to denote a relational efficacy condition—whether the blame can be received as legitimate rather than dismissed. But there may also be a substantive split running beneath the vocabulary: whether blame is fundamentally an act of holding someone to account, which requires the blamer to stand within the scope of the shared norm, or whether it is fundamentally an expression of a moral judgment, which requires only sincerity and truth. I want a diagnosis that engages these positions concretely, not a survey.

Is the disagreement verbal, with both sides answering different questions—one about the legitimacy of the speech act, one about its pragmatic or social efficacy—under the single heading of standing? Or is it substantive, rooted in a deeper disagreement about moral authority, about whether hypocrisy functions as a defeater at all, or about what kind of consistency blame requires? If substantive, identify the precise point of friction using what each side actually says about the activist, the physician, or the partner. If verbal or bifurcated, specify the distinction—between entitlement and efficacy, between account-holding and judgment-expression, or between first-order and second-order normative conditions—that would dissolve the impasse. Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a sharper conceptual map, a formal analysis of authority conditions, an argument that the framing is ill-posed, or something else?
