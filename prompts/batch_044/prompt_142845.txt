I am grading responses for a graduate seminar on existentialist ontology and need an independent evaluation of one answer before I finalize marks. The exam question asked: Sartre maintains that human reality is radically free and that this freedom is the ground of bad faith (mauvaise foi). First, explain what Sartre means by radical freedom and how it relates to the twin structures of facticity and transcendence. Second, evaluate whether his analysis of bad faith can account for why people systematically adhere to social roles—such as professional or gender identities—without reducing that adherence to either external coercion or mere ignorance of one's options. Be precise about what distinguishes bad faith from simple error or social conditioning.

One student submitted the following response:

Radical freedom, in Sartre's ontology, means that consciousness is never identical with its past actions, its social position, or its bodily situation. Because there is no essential human nature given in advance, each person is continuously responsible for choosing the meaning of their existence. This freedom is not a property we possess but the very structure of being-for-itself: we are always already beyond any particular determination, which Sartre describes as transcendence, even as we remain embedded in concrete circumstances he calls facticity.

Bad faith is the self-deceptive refusal of this structure. It takes two complementary forms: treating oneself as a pure object determined entirely by one's situation, thereby denying transcendence, or treating oneself as a pure, unconditioned subject, thereby denying facticity. The well-known example of the café waiter, who performs his role with exaggerated precision and seems to become nothing but his function, illustrates the first form. He behaves as if his essence were to be a waiter, forgetting that he could resign at any moment. This is not ignorance of his options—he knows he could leave—nor is it external coercion. It is a lie to himself about the nature of his freedom.

When we turn to systematic social roles such as gender or profession, the mechanism is the same but operates collectively. Through what Sartre terms the Look, others objectify me and reduce me to my visible characteristics. Once I internalize that object-status, I live my role as bad faith. Because freedom is absolute, there is no intermediate state: either I fully acknowledge my transcendence or I fall into self-deception. Consequently, Sartre's explanation does not reduce role-adherence to ignorance, but it does treat it as a universal ontological flight from anxiety that functions independently of specific historical or economic conditions. The waiter and the gendered social role are therefore structurally identical instances of denying transcendence.
