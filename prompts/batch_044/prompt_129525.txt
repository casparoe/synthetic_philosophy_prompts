For an advanced undergraduate seminar in decision theory or social philosophy — 16 students, one 75-minute session, with no prior formal game-theory background required — design an in-class simulation that makes participants feel the force of the unilateralist's curse: the dynamic in which rational agents, each capable of acting alone and each preferring that they rather than others bear the cost of restraint, collectively produce a worse outcome by all acting unilaterally rather than coordinating on restraint. The activity should run with only paper, tokens, and a visible payoff board; no real money should change hands. Participants should discover through play — not through any front-loaded lecture — that individual rationality drives them toward over-activation, and they should experience the specific tension that increasing the number of capable agents can worsen the collective result.

Specify the group roles (for example, agents with private, heterogeneous “capability scores” that determine whether they can successfully act alone), the decision rules for each round, the token or point scheme that rewards unilateral success but imposes a collective penalty that scales with the number of actors who choose to act, and the visible mechanism (if any) for attempting coordination. Include a structured debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the formal structure of the curse: the preference ordering over outcomes, the absence of a credible commitment mechanism, and the perverse scaling effect. Predict honestly what will go wrong on the first run — for instance, whether one high-capability player will defect immediately, whether students will miss that restraint is individually costly even when collectively optimal, or whether they will invent an unenforceable agreement that collapses in round three — and say how the facilitator should handle it without lecturing. Finally, be frank about which feature of the theoretical curse — such as an infinite-horizon interaction, common knowledge of perfect rationality, or the absence of communication — this classroom version cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must therefore supply in the closing minutes. The central idea should emerge from the rules, roles, and incentives, not from explanation after the fact.
