Consider a city planner who must decide whether to approve a major redesign of the local bus network. She is relying on a forecasting model that treats every commuter as a perfectly logical calculator: people always choose the fastest route, never miss a stop out of habit, and experience a five-minute delay as exactly twice as bad as a two-and-a-half-minute delay. The model ignores that riders actually share tips about delays through private group chats, feel anxious about unfamiliar transfers, and often stick to old lines simply because they know them. Still, the model correctly predicted how many people would switch lines after two previous redesigns in other cities. One advisor tells her that relying on such a distorted picture of human behavior is dishonest, no matter how well it predicts numbers. Another advisor tells her that if the model gets the results right, the strange assumptions behind it do not matter. The planner is unsure whether to approve the redesign. She asks whether a decision-making tool can be trustworthy when it describes people in ways that are obviously wrong, even if it keeps forecasting outcomes correctly. What should she consider, and is there a meaningful difference between a model being useful and a model being true?
