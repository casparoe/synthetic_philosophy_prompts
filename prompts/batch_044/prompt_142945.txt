I am working through the conceptual architecture of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism—specifically, whether its commitment to a lexical priority of suffering-reduction over pleasure-maximization depends on treating pleasure and pain as commensurable goods. In a seminar discussion, two interlocutors, Alex and Blair, disagreed sharply about this dependency, and I found myself unable to adjudicate their exchange. Alex opened by arguing that the lexical-threshold framework is conceptually incoherent unless suffering and pleasure are measurable on a single cardinal scale. He said that to declare a "threshold" at all—some point below which suffering dominates regardless of compensatory joy—presupposes that we can compare the magnitude of pain against the magnitude of pleasure, which is precisely what value pluralists deny when they treat affective states as incommensurable dimensions, like color against sound. Blair replied that this confuses cardinal commensurability with ordinal lexical ordering. She claimed that lexicographic preferences in decision theory already show how goods can be strictly ranked without any exchange rate: one first checks whether suffering exceeds the threshold, and only if it does not does one turn to pleasure-maximization. No cross-dimensional weighing is required, she insisted, just as one need not compare the "value" of water and diamonds to know that water comes first in a desert.

Alex countered that Blair's move smuggles commensurability back in through the normative back door. To justify placing suffering in the lexically prior position, one must already have determined that avoiding torture is more important than achieving ecstasy—a judgment that implicitly compares the two goods and assigns them relative weights on a shared axis of value. Without such comparison, Alex said, the lexical ordering is arbitrary: it is no more rational than ranking goods alphabetically. Blair responded by distinguishing structural decision rules from comparative valuation. She introduced what she called a "gatekeeping" model: the threshold functions not as a measured point on a common scale but as a qualitative boundary—like the distinction between tolerable discomfort and torture—such that once the boundary is crossed, pleasure is removed from consideration entirely, not weighed and found lighter.

At this point Alex proposed a specific hypothetical to test Blair's gatekeeping model. Imagine, he said, a world in which every conscious being experiences a constant, low-grade "background hum" of mild suffering—say, a perpetual 1/10 physical discomfort—while simultaneously experiencing constant, intense ecstatic joy at 9/10 intensity. Under Blair's lexical rule, we ought to eliminate the hum even if doing so extinguishes all joy, leaving only neutral flatness. But if suffering and pleasure are genuinely incommensurable dimensions, Alex argued, there is no rational basis for preferring the neutral world: we cannot say that the absence of the hum "outweighs" the loss of ecstasy, because "outweighing" is exactly the cross-dimensional comparison that incommensurability forbids. The choice, he concluded, collapses into arbitrariness. Blair answered that Alex's hypothetical actually reinforces the lexical view. The moral asymmetry between suffering and pleasure, she said, does not depend on a shared metric but on independent normative reasons—analogous to the reason one saves a drowning child rather than painting a masterpiece, without ever comparing "life-value" to "art-value" on a single scale. The hum, being genuine suffering, activates the gate; the ecstasy, being pleasure, remains in the secondary tier. Incommensurability, Blair concluded, only prevents precise exchange-rate calculations, not principled lexical ordering.
