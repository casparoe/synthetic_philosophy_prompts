I chair the clinical arbitration board at a large public hospital system. Our allocation protocol assigns priority scores—computed from clinical severity, prognosis, and wait-time weights—to patients competing for scarce specialist slots. The weights are fixed by policy; our mandate is only to resolve ties, not to redesign the algorithm. Ties occur in roughly three percent of cases, always with scores identical to the third decimal place. The board must document every decision, apply it consistently across cases, and avoid introducing demographic or social bias that the score has suppressed. We have about fifteen minutes per tie, no staff philosopher, and the hospital’s legal office must sign off on the rationale.

I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical analysis of Buridan’s ass and rational choice without preference, that the board can apply repeatedly under these constraints. The procedure must distinguish a genuine preference-less tie—where the options are rationally equivalent given the board’s legitimate decision criteria—from a hidden difference that the score has masked but that should still matter. It must then specify a tie-breaking mechanism (for example, a deterministic lexicographic rule, a randomized protocol, or a temporal sequencing principle) that respects rational agency rather than treating the chooser as paralyzed, and it must identify exactly where mechanical application ends and an irreducible judgment call begins.

Deliver a step-by-step checklist, expressed as a flowchart in words, that a non-philosopher can run in under fifteen minutes. Include explicit flags for misfire conditions—such as consistent application of the mechanism producing skewed demographic patterns over time, or the tie-breaking rule conflicting with the board’s documented mandate. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) two patients with identical clinical profiles but different family-care contexts that the score explicitly excludes; (b) a tie between a present patient and a projected future patient whose anticipated score is calculated to be identical; and (c) a tie that survives the primary mechanism, forcing a second-order resolution rule. For each case, state what the procedure outputs, whether the board should accept that output, and what philosophical distinction—between rational paralysis, arbitrary-but-justified selection, and capricious whim—is being invoked or tested. Be explicit about which conceptual distinctions from the literature on choice without preference must survive in the operational version and which can be dropped without distorting the principle. Finally, say clearly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of tie the board should escalate rather than resolve, and where the procedure will reliably produce answers the board will find unacceptable. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or request for further reading; I will supply references separately.
