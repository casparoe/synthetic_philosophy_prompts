I'm putting together a decision-tree handout for an advanced logic seminar that applies non-classical reasoning to formally structured normative systems—specifically, institutional or regulatory codes that generate genuinely conflicting obligations without any meta-rule, ambiguity, or interpretive error that dissolves the conflict. The root case should be concrete: a maximally authoritative framework jointly entails both O(A) and O(¬A) for some specific action A, with both provisions equally binding and no hierarchical resolution available. From there, I want the tree built from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of philosopher-names: first the branch questions, then the combinations they allow, and only then the actual positions mapped onto the leaves.

The branch nodes should ask whether the contradiction is merely an artifact of formalization or genuinely structural; if structural, whether we should preserve classical logic and revise the framework, or adjust the logic; among logical adjustments, whether we merely block explosion while denying that contradictions are true (a paraconsistent but non-dialetheist response) or accept that some contradictions are literally true (dialetheism); then whether that acceptance or tolerance is restricted to normative/institutional domains or extends to empirical science and metaphysics; and finally whether the primary motivation is logical (protecting non-trivial inference), metaphysical (respecting reality), or pragmatic (preserving institutional function despite incoherence). Each leaf should carry a name if one exists—such as global dialetheism, domain-restricted paraconsistent revisionism, institutional deflationism, or classical reconstructionism—or be marked "unnamed" with one sentence diagnosing whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or being unexplored. Please flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, especially where different philosophical motivations converge on the same practical recommendation in this institutional setting, and keep every diagnostic question phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing which label it leads to. Aim for roughly six to eight leaves, include at least one unoccupied combination with a brief diagnosis, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—the deliverable needs to be fully usable without web access.
