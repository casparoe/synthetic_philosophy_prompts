I'm trying to work out whether video-game characters can be proper objects of moral concern, by testing my intuitions across cases. Here are my verdicts.

In The Sims 4, deliberately removing the pool ladder so that a specific named Sim drowns strikes me as genuinely cruel; deleting the save file that contains fifty anonymous Sims strikes me as administratively wasteful but not morally comparable. In XCOM 2, losing a procedurally generated soldier to a tactical error feels like a regrettable strategic failure; losing a narrative-scripted squadmate in a cutscene feels like allowing a harm to befall a character I have reason to care about. In Minecraft, killing a villager for emeralds feels wrong; killing a zombie villager to cure him feels right, even though both actions involve the same entity type. In Super Mario Bros., intentionally jumping Mario into a pit to skip a section feels like exploiting a mechanic, not like harming Mario, whereas in a permadeath RPG such as Dark Souls, deliberately walking a beloved character into an avoidable trap feels like betrayal. In a hypothetical persistent-world simulation where NPCs retain memories across server resets, wiping the entire database feels like something close to genocide; resetting a single instance does not.

The pattern I see involves something like diegetic investment, narrative specificity, and persistence across gameplay loops, but I cannot articulate the principle precisely. Is there a coherent account—perhaps drawing on a distinction between fictional identification, mechanical instrumentality, and simulated continuity—that captures all these verdicts? In particular, what should it say about the case I keep reversing: in a narrative-driven game with a revival mechanic, where in-game currency can resurrect a dead companion, is refusing to pay morally callous, or is it merely an inefficient allocation of resources? And does the principle imply that purely procedural characters can never be wronged, or only that wronging them requires a different kind of moral failure?
