As a retired molecular biologist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I have been struck by how persistently the debate over whether DNA is literally a code has stalled. On one side, researchers treat nucleotide sequences as carrying semantic instructions for building proteins; on the other, critics insist that code is merely a heuristic metaphor, since there is no interpreter with intentions, only chemical causation. Both sides seem to talk past each other, citing the same biochemical mechanisms—transcription, translation, error correction—yet reaching opposite metaphysical conclusions.

I want a diagnosis of this impasse rather than a verdict. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal—centered on whether code requires an intentional encoder—but partly substantive, involving a hidden distinction between three roles that sequence information might play: first, a syntactic correlation structure of the Shannon-type; second, a semantic instruction set whose content specifies an outcome independently of physical law; and third, what I would call an algorithmic scaffold, a spatial and chemical template that constrains polymerization without being reducible to pure correlation or to full semantic content.

Is this three-way framing accurate, or does it misrepresent the positions? More importantly, is the stall primarily a confusion among these three categories, or is there a deeper disagreement about what counts as an explanation in biology—perhaps about whether mechanistic accounts must exclude teleological language? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate: a conceptual clarification of information in biochemical contexts, an empirical result about the fidelity of translation, or a shift in the question itself? Please defend your diagnosis by engaging with what each side actually says about transcription and translation, and be honest about whether any resolution should be expected.
