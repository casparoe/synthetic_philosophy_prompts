I work at a community mental-health intake center, and over the past year I've become convinced—mostly from my own reading and from long conversations with patients—that what we call conscious experience operates more like a user interface than like a direct readout from the brain's underlying machinery. By that I don't mean experience is fake; I mean it's a constructed representation built for interaction, and like any interface it can fail in specific ways: it can omit processes that are running, misattribute processes to the wrong source, or generate phantom renderings of processes that aren't running at all. This isn't an academic theory for me—it's a practical lens I need for intake assessments. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run in about twenty minutes, with staff who have bachelor's-level training and no neuroscience background, to determine whether a patient's report of an anomalous conscious state is best understood as an interface-level distortion (the rendering is off but the cognitive architecture is largely intact), an architecture-level disruption (the underlying processing itself is altered), or an unresolvable mixture. The procedure should specify the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a clear direction—such as counseling-focused follow-up, mandatory psychiatric referral, or supervisor sign-off—and where it must explicitly hand the decision back to human judgment because the distinction is one of values or irreducible uncertainty rather than fact. It should also flag where the procedure is likely to misfire and what it cannot do, especially whether this framework risks dismissing real suffering as mere interface noise.

Please apply the procedure to three intake scenarios. First, a patient under acute stress who reports feeling like an outside observer of their own body and actions, with intact reality testing otherwise. Second, a patient who reports that private thoughts are being broadcast and heard as distinct external voices, with no recognition that these are self-generated. Third, a trauma survivor who describes a complete absence of emotional coloring in conscious experience—everything feels grey and detached—where the procedure produces a recommendation that treats the condition as interface-level and non-urgent, which I suspect a compassionate practitioner will find unacceptable. For each case, show how the procedure classifies the report and where a staff member must invoke judgment rather than the flowchart. Finally, be honest about whether treating consciousness as a user illusion in this operationalized form actually helps intake staff make better decisions, or whether it mainly reorganizes ambiguity into categories that feel more precise than they are.
