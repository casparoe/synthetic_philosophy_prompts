Exam question: Briefly characterize illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, identifying its core claim about introspection. Then present the self-refutation objection and explain how an illusionist might reply.

Student answer: Illusionism, associated with philosophers like Keith Frankish, holds that phenomenal consciousness—the subjective qualitative feel of experience—does not actually exist. Instead, introspection generates representational states that falsely present our mental lives as possessing qualitative properties. The error is therefore not like a perceptual illusion such as a mirage, but rather an introspective misrepresentation concerning the nature of our own minds.

The self-refutation objection runs as follows: if phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, then there must be a subject or system to whom things appear illusory. Yet if things appear illusory to that subject, then there is something it is like to have that appearance, which entails that phenomenal consciousness exists after all.

The illusionist can respond by appealing to Daniel Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater. Just as we mistakenly posit a central observer in the brain where consciousness is displayed, we mistakenly assume there must be an inner audience that receives the illusion. In reality, the misrepresentation is distributed across cognitive processes without being presented to any phenomenal subject. Hence, there is no conscious experiencer being deceived—only false representations occurring within the system.
