I'm trying to figure out whether "qualia" is just an incoherent posit we reach for whenever there's a gap, or if there's a disciplined way to use it. Here are my gut verdicts, going mostly by Dennett's Chase/Sanborn setup but with a couple weirder ones I made up.

Case 1 — Classic Chase: my taste for coffe changed, but the liquid tastes exactly like it used to; I'm just more critical now. Verdict: totally coherent, no hidden "what-it's-like" shift needed.

Case 2 — Classic Sanborn: the coffee literally tastes different now, like cardboard, and that's why I avoid it. Verdict: I want to say this is incoherent if my discrimination behavor hasn't changed at all, but I'm not fully sure.

Case 3 — The silent synesthete: after a minor stroke, someone reports numbers now have a "spatial curvature" — like 5 bends downward — but they do arithmatic identically, never mention it unless asked, and show zero behavioral difference. Verdict: incoherent to posit a new private curvature-qualia.

Case 4 — Dissociated pain: patient says the pain feels exactly the same sharp quality as before, but it just doesn't bother her; she sleeps fine. Verdict: coherent, just an affective shift like Case 1.

Case 5 — The inverted painter: an artist reports her entire color spectrum inverted, yet she mixes paints and names colors flawlessly, and fMRI shows her V4 activates exactly as before. Verdict: incoherent? Or is this different from Case 3?

The thing that keeps tripping me is Case 2 vs Case 5. In both there's a reported change in sensory quality with zero behavioral or neural change. But I feel like Sanborn might be okay and the painter definitely isn't. Or maybe both are incoherent. Is there a principle that saves my verdicts on 1, 3, and 4 and tells me what's wrong with 5, or do I have to drop one of them?
