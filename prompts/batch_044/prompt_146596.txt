You are an experienced philosophy interviewer and podcast producer with deep familiarity in twentieth-century existentialism and contemporary philosophy of technology. Before drafting anything, take a few moments to think carefully and brainstorm the main pressure points in the literature on Sartre’s radical freedom and bad faith, particularly where standard readings leave room for a structural or institutional extension.

Your task is to prepare a rigorous sequence of interview questions for a recorded colloquium. The guest is an unnamed contemporary philosopher who defends Sartre’s doctrine of radical freedom and the analysis of bad faith, but she advances a specific and somewhat unconventional extension: she argues that bad faith can operate in a distributed or institutional form. On her view, socio-technical systems — for example, predictive risk algorithms or automated bureaucratic ranking procedures — can embody bad faith not because they possess consciousness, but because they systematically present contingent, historically constructed norms as necessary, unalterable facticities. Users and administrators then internalise this presentation, denying their own transcendence by treating the system’s outputs as brute givens rather than as human-made, revisable structures.

Write eight to ten interview questions that turn directly on the content of her position. Do not include generic questions about her biography or how she came to philosophy. I want at least one question addressing the phenomenological distinction between anguish (angoisse) and fear, specifically how the experience of anguish is supposed to reveal our radical freedom rather than merely an emotional state; include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that anguish is not about danger but about the burden of self-creation. I want at least one question pressing the standard compatibilist objection — that determinism is compatible with the kind of freedom that matters — and include a follow-up anticipating her likely Sartrean reply that such freedom is merely the freedom to do what one is causally determined to do, which misses the ontological structure of being-for-itself. I want at least one question that probes the institutional-extension claim itself: ask how she avoids anthropomorphising the algorithm, what distinguishes a system that embodies bad faith from one that merely operates deterministically, and whether this distributed form requires a collective subject or can exist purely in the interaction patterns between individuals and machines; include a follow-up keyed to her probable defence that bad faith is a structural relation, not a mental state. Finally, include at least one question that asks what her view implies for moral responsibility in cases where harm is produced by such a system — for instance, if a predictive policing model generates unjust outcomes, is the bad faith located in the designers, the users, the system itself, or in the social conditions that made the system appear necessary?

For each main question, provide a brief, pointed follow-up that assumes the answer she is most likely to give and presses the weakness or tension in that answer. Keep the tone rigorous, respectful, and suited to a specialist audience. Write in polished British English.
