I'm designing a reusable classification protocol for senior transparency officers in a national archive who must review batches of declassified internal government communications and flag those that contain what Hannah Arendt describes as politically destructive lying—specifically, the substitution of a coherent fictional narrative for a shared factual record, rather than ordinary bureaucratic error or strategic ambiguity. The officers have roughly fifteen minutes per batch, must personally sign off on every classification, and work without specialist philosophical training. I need this translated into a practical decision procedure: a sequential checklist with explicit decision nodes, clear markers for the judgment calls that cannot be eliminated, and explicit rules for what to do when documentary intent is ambiguous. The protocol must preserve Arendt's core distinction between attacks on contingent factual truth and legitimate forms of political reinterpretation or negotiation, and it should be honest about where it will misfire or produce results that conflict with conventional ethical intuition.

The deliverable should include: (1) the step-by-step flowchart in prose form, ordered so that cheap, document-based tests come first; (2) precise operational criteria distinguishing destructive narrative substitution from routine misstatement, contradictory eyewitness testimony without demonstrable fabrication, and retrospective moral reframing that leaves the underlying factual record intact; (3) instructions for unavoidable judgment points, including how an officer should document epistemic uncertainty and when to escalate rather than classify; and (4) worked applications to three hypothetical batches—one in which a fabricated statistical claim is inserted into a policy briefing to justify a predetermined decision; one in which two internal accounts of the same meeting contradict each other without either being demonstrably false; and one in which a senior official later describes a past decision in language that reshapes its ethical framing without altering any verifiable fact. For the case where the procedure yields a classification that conflicts with standard moral judgment—likely the third—explain exactly what the protocol outputs and why, within Arendt's framework, that result is consistent rather than erroneous. The protocol should not depend on citations, bibliographies, or external sources to apply, and it should treat the effect on the shared factual world as primary when intent cannot be reliably recovered from the text alone.
