I am preparing the final assessment for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early modern philosophy and its intersections with political economy. I need one essay question—designed for a 45-minute response—that treats George Berkeley’s philosophy of money not as an appendage to his immaterialism but as a standalone conceptual puzzle. Rather than inviting a summary of Berkeley’s well-known claim that coin and paper are merely signs or counters of wealth, the prompt should compel students to resolve a specific internal tension: Berkeley maintains that money is ontologically insubstantial, possessing no intrinsic value and functioning only as a token that represents labor and goods, yet he also contends that a state-managed paper currency—backed not by gold or silver but by a national stock of land and labor—can actively stimulate industry and prosperity in ways that metallic coinage cannot. Ask students to explain precisely how Berkeley separates the ontological status of the monetary sign from its causal efficacy in economic life, and to show why this separation is not equivalent to the modern distinction between fiat and commodity money. To block rote recitation, require them to contrast Berkeley’s account with a commodity-theory position—such as the view that a currency’s value must ultimately be anchored in the labor embodied in its material substrate—and to identify where collective belief and social convention perform the sustaining work that intrinsic material worth performs for the commodity theorist.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks. The scheme must distinguish an A response from a B by specifying that the top answer recognizes three conceptual moves: first, the strict sign/thing-signified distinction applied specifically to currency; second, the claim that credit and collective trust are not psychological embellishments but the sustaining causal conditions of the sign’s efficacy; and third, the argument that labor rather than metal is the true value-substrate, which permits paper to function without intrinsic worth. Penalize answers that collapse Berkeley’s view into contemporary fiat-currency theory or that conflate his social-convention account with a subjective-value theory. Include one identifiable misconception—for instance, the claim that Berkeley’s immaterialism about money entails a denial of real economic exchange—and specify the point deduction for it. The evaluation should depend solely on conceptual precision; do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
