I am preparing a seminar on conceptual fluidity and have come across a colleague’s summary of Douglas Hofstadter’s Copycat framework, specifically regarding slippage within letter-string analogy microdomains. The summary reads as follows: ‘In Copycat, analogies such as abc → abd and mrrjjj → mrrjjk are resolved not by the application of fixed symbolic rules but by populations of “codelets” competing within a workspace according to statistical pressures. Because identical pressure dynamics can yield divergent outcomes—for instance, treating “jjj” as a unified group in one context and as discrete elements in another—Hofstadter demonstrates that concepts like successor or group possess no stable representational content whatsoever. They are entirely fluid, and any appearance of rule-governed behaviour is merely an epiphenomenon of bottom-up dynamics. Hence Copycat proves that human cognition contains no symbolic representations at all; conceptual slippage is not a failure of representation but conclusive evidence that representation was never present.’ I suspect this overstates the anti-representationalist conclusion and perhaps conflates Hofstadter’s critique of rigid, rule-based architectures with a stronger eliminativist claim about cognition generally. Could you provide an accuracy audit of this characterisation? Please distinguish what faithfully captures Hofstadter’s account of pressure-driven analogy and context-sensitive grouping in the Copycat domain, what amounts to a conventional simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation—particularly with respect to whether he denies all representational structures or merely insists that they are fluid, context-dependent, and emergent rather than pre-specified. Does the inference that Copycat demonstrates the complete absence of symbolic representation in human cognition actually follow from the premises about conceptual slippage? I should welcome a careful, nuanced analysis in British English, and I require no bibliographic apparatus or citations.
