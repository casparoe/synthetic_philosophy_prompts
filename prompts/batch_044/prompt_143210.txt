I have been working through the demandingness objection to consequentialism — the charge that requiring agents always to maximize aggregate welfare imposes burdens that exhaust personal projects and well-being beyond what can reasonably be asked — and I find that slogans like “morality should not demand the impossible” are doing more precise work than I can currently unpack. I am fluent in control theory and distributed systems engineering but only informally acquainted with moral philosophy, so I want the debate restated entirely in the vocabulary I think in professionally: set points, error signals, actuator saturation, stability margins, load shedding, feedback loops, bandwidth allocation, graceful degradation, and local versus global optimization. Please translate both the demandingness objection itself and the principal consequentialist replies — appeals to the costs of morality, scalar structures of obligation, and the division of moral labor — using only that engineering vocabulary, keeping the translation honest rather than smuggling philosophical premises in under technical labels or distorting the engineering concepts merely to preserve philosophical comfort.

For each component of the translated debate, provide a residue report: what maps cleanly onto saturation limits or load conditions, what must be distorted — whether by stretching the moral concept or by bending the technical term — to maintain the correspondence, and what, if anything, has no expression at all in this vocabulary, such as a normative distinction between the obligatory and the supererogatory or between failure of capacity and failure of will. Then tell me whether those residues indicate that control-theoretic language genuinely lacks the resources to represent normative constraint, or whether they instead expose ambiguities in the original objection — for instance, whether “too demanding” means “exceeds actuator capacity,” “destabilizes the local system,” or “violates a protected local set point,” and whether these interpretations come apart in ways the slogan obscures. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want to see whether the argument’s structure holds up on conceptual machinery alone.
