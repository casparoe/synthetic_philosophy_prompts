I am preparing a discussion memo for a graduate seminar on existential phenomenology, and I have become stuck on what I am tentatively calling the transparency paradox in Sartre's analysis of bad faith. In our recent sessions we have been working through Sartre's claim that consciousness is transparent to itself—there is no hidden ego or unconscious mechanism lurking behind our acts—alongside his equally insistent claim that human beings are radically free, which is to say that we are never determined by our past, our nature, or our circumstances, and that we constantly flee this freedom through self-deception. The difficulty I see is this: if consciousness is genuinely transparent, then there is no inner audience that can be deceived, since the liar and the lied-to are not two distinct entities but one and the same act of awareness. Yet Sartre describes bad faith precisely as a lie to oneself, an attempt to hide from oneself the truth of one's own freedom. I have tried to resolve this by arguing that bad faith is not a psychological deception but a performative contradiction in one's mode of self-interpretation—similar to the way a speaker can undermine the conditions of her own assertion—so that the lie is lived structurally rather than represented internally. But this reading seems to strip bad faith of its specifically existential weight, reducing it to logical inconsistency rather than a fundamental flight from being.

So my question is whether Sartre's concept of bad faith actually requires some non-transparent structure within consciousness—some hidden region where the truth can be concealed—or whether radical freedom and transparency are compatible after all. I am especially interested in whether the distinction between pre-reflective cogito and reflective judgment is sufficient to dissolve the tension, or whether the tension reveals an unavoidable ambiguity in Sartre's ontology of being-for-itself. If the latter, does that mean that any attempt to practice Sartrean authenticity must begin by acknowledging an opacity at the heart of consciousness that Sartre himself denies? I would like to understand whether I am misapplying the transparency thesis, or whether there is a genuine contradiction here that requires revising how we understand the relationship between radical freedom and self-deception.
