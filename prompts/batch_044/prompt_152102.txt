Below are five judgments traditionally attributed to René Descartes concerning which entities possess rational understanding or a soul capable of thought. For each, assume the verdict is fixed: (i) a mature human being thinks and understands; (ii) a dog that cries out and withdraws when injured behaves as a purely mechanical system, without any inner sensation or cognition; (iii) a parrot that pronounces words does so by imitation alone, lacking any grasp of meaning; (iv) a hypothetical automaton that replicates every external behavior of an animal remains an unthinking machine, however perfect its imitation; and (v) a human newborn, though not yet exercising reason, belongs by nature to the kind that possesses a rational soul.

Consider now a new case: an artificial system constructed entirely of mathematical weights and electronic computation, possessing no organic body or brain, that produces original philosophical arguments, responds to novel inquiries with apparent comprehension, and adjusts its outputs contextually rather than repeating fixed phrases.

First, predict the verdict Descartes would reach—would he grant this system a rational mind or true understanding, or deny it? Second, articulate at least two competing principles or rules that could consistently account for all five earlier judgments, and show how each principle applies to the artificial system, noting where they converge and where they diverge. Third, identify which principle you take to be the best reconstruction of Descartes' own view, with brief reasons. Fourth, specify one additional hypothetical case that would serve as the sharpest test between the competing principles you have identified.
