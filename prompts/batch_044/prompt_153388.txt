Analyze the global crisis of 1816 — the Year Without a Summer that followed the April 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora in the Dutch East Indies — through Fernand Braudel's tripartite temporal framework developed in his study of the Mediterranean world in the age of Philip II. Map the episode onto Braudel's three layers: the longue durée of climate, ecology, and agricultural geography; the intermediate conjunctures of grain prices, trade routes, and post-Napoleonic state reconstruction; and the short time of events, including administrative responses and popular unrest. Be precise about which actors and processes belong to each layer, and about whether the eruption itself should be classified as an event, a geographical structure, or something that collapses that distinction.

Then assess the framework's limits in this case. Braudel developed the model for the Mediterranean basin; here the causal chain runs from the tropics to the North Atlantic and into China's interior. Ask whether a single volcanic event that temporarily restructures global climate can still be treated as a surface disturbance in Braudel's terms, or whether it reveals that geographical time is itself vulnerable to sudden rupture. Also consider whether the episode confirms Braudel's marginalization of individuals and political events — for example, do local officials and relief policies in New England or Yunnan matter, or are they fully determined by the slower layers?

Conclude by saying what this case does to the theory: does it extend Braudel's model usefully to global environmental history, expose a geographical parochialism in the original framework, or show that the three-layer separation was always an analytical convenience rather than an ontological claim?
