hey, i'm putting together a handout for a philosophy grad seminar on moral particularism vs. generalism. i want a taxonomy / decision tree that sorts people by what they actually believe about concrete cases, not by which label they prefer.

start with some brainstorming on the logical space, then build the tree. you're an expert in metaethics and philosophical taxonomy, so think carefully about the diagnostic splits.

the root commitment should be something concrete like "causing unnecessary suffering is wrong" — then each branch should be a question a student can answer without knowing the literature. include splits on:
- whether the same feature can have opposite moral valence in different contexts
- whether principles are defeasible heuristics or just normative summaries
- and definately keep "reason modulation" (context shifts the weight of a reason) seperate from "reason holism" (new reasons only show up in combinations of features). treat these as two different axes, not one collapsed idea.

the tree should have about 6-8 leaves. each leaf gets a position name (strict generalism, dancy-style particularism, moderate principle view, contextualist pluralism, or 'unnamed') and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really seperate, and for any empty spot in the logical space, say briefly if it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

don't ask for citations or a reading list — a model without web access should be able to do this. also please: seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.

make sure it's not just encyclopedia regurgitation; inject the niche idea about modulation vs holism as a real branching factor, and keep the audience in mind (grad students who might not have memorized the standard taxonomy).
