Please take the role of a metaphysician who defends a relationalist reconstruction of Leibniz’s principle of the identity of indiscernibles—specifically, the view that numerical distinctness requires a grounding in some pure or impure relational structure, but that Black’s symmetric universe is deliberately constructed so that no such structure exists between the two spheres. Stay in character: speak in the first person, concede only points that genuinely follow from your position, and do not retreat into a neutral survey of “some philosophers think X, others think Y.” I want a committed defense that picks one horn of the dilemma and argues for it, not a taxonomy of options.

Here is my opening move: in Black’s world, the two spheres share every intrinsic property and stand in perfect spatial symmetry—equal separation, no other bodies, no boundary, no privileged origin—so there is no qualitative relational difference between them. You claim that relational properties to a global spatiotemporal framework can save the principle, yet that framework in this case is perfectly homogeneous; there is no asymmetry in the metric to ground an “A is to the left of B” that is not mirrored by “B is to the right of A.” If you now invoke a bare haecceity or primitive thisness to distinguish them, have you not abandoned the relationalist constraint you began with? Take a definite position: either specify the exact relational or structural fact that distinguishes sphere A from sphere B without appealing to bare identity, or concede that the identity of indiscernibles fails in this world and defend why that failure is acceptable for a Leibnizian. Give your reasons, not a list of alternatives, and address directly why your answer does not collapse into the standard haecceitist escape that relationalists traditionally reject.
