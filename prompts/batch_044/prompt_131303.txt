You advise a deployment-safety reviewer at a mid-sized technology firm who must sign off on “low-impact” or “side-effect-avoiding” AI systems—narrow task models such as warehouse-routing optimizers, inventory-forecasting tools, or document-classification pipelines—without possessing or being permitted to invoke a full specification of human values. The reviewer receives proposals that claim the system avoids side effects, but she knows the conceptual difficulty: defining what counts as a side effect typically seems to presuppose knowing which states of the world are valuable or harmful, and she has no such global utility map. She needs a reusable, written decision procedure—ordered questions, criteria for sufficient answers, explicit stop points (approve, reject, or escalate to a values committee), and clear flags for steps that are irreducibly judgment calls rather than factual checks. The procedure must be runnable in under thirty minutes by someone with engineering literacy but no philosophy background, must say what to do when a quantitative answer is unavailable, and must be honest about where it will misfire or produce answers the reviewer will not like. Ground the procedure in the specific conceptual claim that “avoid side effects” can be partially defined without full value specification by using causal-boundary preservation and option-space metrics—measuring whether the system’s outputs remain within a pre-specified task domain and whether they reduce or preserve the range of future actions available to affected parties—while conceding that any weighting of those metrics still smuggles in normative commitments. Apply the procedure to three concrete proposals: (1) a warehouse-routing algorithm that lowers energy consumption but restructures worker movement patterns and informal social interactions on the floor; (2) a medical-triage scheduling tool that improves average wait times but, through data-feature correlations, subtly shifts the demographic composition of patients seen in the first hour without any explicit harm metric being violated; and (3) a public-discourse content-filter that suppresses borderline political speech to avoid “toxic” outputs, where the procedure’s technical checks suggest approval because no physical harm or legal liability occurs within the causal boundary, yet the reviewer will find that result unacceptable—explain exactly where the procedure hands the decision back to values and why it cannot reject the proposal on its own terms. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do: which categories of impact it systematically misses, which value-laden premises remain hidden inside its apparently neutral metrics, and under what conditions it should be suspended entirely.
