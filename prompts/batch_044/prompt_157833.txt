As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have been trying to understand why scholarly interpretations of Nāgārjuna's tetralemma and Madhyamaka emptiness seem to circle without resolution: some readers treat emptiness as a metaphysical thesis that entities lack intrinsic existence, others as an epistemological boundary on conceptual construction, and still others as a purely soteriological device that makes no ontological claim at all. Despite broad agreement on the fourfold negation—affirmation, negation, both, neither—interpreters rarely converge. Is this stall primarily verbal (a dispute over the denotation of "emptiness"), substantive but hidden (different questions being asked—what exists versus how to practice—under a single heading), or a structural misreading?

To frame it with an idea from my own work: in software verification, if every branch in a four-way decision tree evaluates to false, the failure usually signals a flaw in the evaluation framework rather than an undiscovered fifth branch. Are scholars stalling because they keep forcing emptiness into one of the four rejected positions—treating it as a concealed fifth thesis—rather than accepting it as a rejection of the framing assumptions themselves?

Please diagnose the disagreement using what the interpretive camps actually maintain, indicate what kind of progress—conceptual, methodological, or framing—would be possible, and clearly separate what is settled about the tetralemma's structure from what remains genuinely contested. Do not present any single contested reading as the established consensus.
