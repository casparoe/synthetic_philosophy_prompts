I am a doctoral student in comparative literature studying preservation of form under extreme constraint in poetic translation. I have been working through a passage in Friedrich Schleiermacher's 1813 Berlin lecture on translation methods, where he argues that the translator must choose between bringing the reader toward the author or the author toward the reader. In poetic texts, he suggests, this choice becomes acute because formal structure—meter, rhyme, sound pattern—is not ornamental but part of the poem's cognitive substance; to abandon form is therefore to abandon the text's thinking. Yet he seems to assume that form operates at a level still interpretable as meaningful within the target language's own conventions.

I want to test this framework against a procedure I am calling phonotactic mirroring: rather than preserving rhyme scheme or meter, one reproduces the physical articulatory trajectory of key phonemes from the source line in the target language, accepting substantial semantic drift if necessary. Consider its application to Marot's poem Ma mignonne—the poem examined in Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot. If an English rendering preserves, for instance, the labial-to-nasal progression of the opening syllables even though the literal sense is lost, is this an instance of Schleiermacher's first method (moving the reader toward the author), or does it collapse the distinction altogether because the preserved pattern is sub-lexical and therefore below the threshold of meaning Schleiermacher had in mind?

Third, what this implies for Hofstadter's claim in Le Ton beau de Marot that every translation is ultimately analogy-making between cognitive structures—would phonotactic mirroring be an extreme case of that analogy, or does it violate the conditions that make analogical translation intelligible?
