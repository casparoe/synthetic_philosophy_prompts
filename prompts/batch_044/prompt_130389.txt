I've been prepping my philosophy of law seminar and we got stuck on self-amending rules — specifically this idea that a legal system can contain a rule that authorizes changes to itself. One of my students brought up this hypothetical "Mirror Clause" we invented for teh exercise: Article Ω says the legislature may amend Article Ω by simple majority, but any amendment that removes the amendment power is void unless ratified by the same procedure that created the original charter. The student then asked whether this isn't just Russell's paradox wearing judicial robes — if the clause can license its own destruction, then you can derive both that the clause is valid and that it isn't, depending on which amendment you run first. I tried to say it's not a set-theoretic contradiction but a procedural loop, but I'm second-guesing myself now. Is there actually a formal paradox hiding in self-amending legal rules, or is the confusion just importing logical self-reference where only institutional hierachy applies? And if it's the latter, does the resolution come from Hart's rule of recognition, from Kelsen's basic norm, or from some other mechanism that blocks the loop? I want to be honest about whether every amendable constitution hides a contradiction, but I also don't want to invent a problem that logicians solved decades ago and lawyers never noticed.
