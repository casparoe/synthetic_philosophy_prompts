For a paper I'm writing on logical justification, I want to audit my commitments before I present them. Here is the package I have arrived at: (1) I take Carroll's tortoise to show that modus ponens cannot be justified as an explicit premise without vicious regress, so propositional justification of deduction is impossible. (2) I think the proper response is inferentialist: the warrant for deduction lies in practical mastery and the structural coherence of one's beliefs, not in a theoretical grounding. (3) I am a naturalist about mind: there are no non-physical capacities, and normative practices must ultimately be explicable in physical terms. (4) I hold that small language models, which approximate reasoning without representing explicit logical rules, thereby avoid Carroll's regress entirely, and that in this specific respect they possess a minimal form of rational soundness that human rule-followers lack. (5) At the same time, I maintain that human reasoning is genuinely governed by explicit, normatively assessable rules, and that this capacity for reflective self-criticism — recognizing that one ought not to affirm the consequent — is essential to full rational agency. (6) Consequently, I think human cognition is overall superior to statistical approximation, despite being vulnerable to the tortoise problem. Do these commitments hang together? Specifically, does (2)'s appeal to normative practical mastery conflict with (3)'s demand for physical explanation? Does (4)'s claim that LLMs are structurally sounder because they lack rules undermine (5)'s defense of explicit rule-governance as the mark of rationality? And is there a hidden tension between (1) and (2) — that is, have I simply changed the subject rather than solved Carroll's problem? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate which apparent conflict dissolves once we distinguish propositional from practical warrant, and tell me the least costly revision if I want to keep both (3) and (5) intact.
