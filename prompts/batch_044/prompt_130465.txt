Here is an argument about republican liberty and automated governance that I think proves too much, but I cannot locate the slip. (1) Republican freedom is non-domination: being free from arbitrary power, not merely free from interference. (2) Arbitrary power essentially involves a will that can act on whim or without regard for the subject's interests; a transparent algorithm applying democratically enacted rules has no will and applies uniformly. (3) Therefore, replacing human bureaucrats with algorithmic systems eliminates domination, even when the constraints on citizens are identical or more severe. (4) So a state governed entirely by transparent, democratically authorized algorithms maximizes republican freedom, regardless of how severely it restricts options—assigning jobs, restricting movement, or allocating resources via predictive scores. (5) But this is absurd: a system with no human discretion to appeal to or contest seems like domination in its purest form, not freedom. (6) The error might be in (2): perhaps domination is structural—being subject to a power one cannot control or contest—regardless of whether a personal will is present. (7) But if we reject (2), then every law applied impersonally becomes dominating, which either collapses republican liberty into negative liberty or makes democratic government itself unfree. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost the republican distinction between freedom and slavery? In answering, please separate what is settled about republican liberty's core commitments from what is genuinely contested—especially whether domination requires a personal agent—and do not present any contested view as the consensus among people who work on this.
