I am a high school student taking an advanced philosophy seminar, and I have been discussing with my classmates whether honor cultures, dignity cultures, and victimhood cultures can be ranked as morally superior to one another, or whether comparison itself requires a hidden "fourth culture"—perhaps an audience culture that evaluates status and suffering from outside all three. Most readings present these frameworks as evolutionary stages or lifestyle preferences, but I want our club of about eighteen students, aged seventeen to eighteen, to feel structurally why the superiority question may be unanswerable from within any single framework. Please design a single ninety-minute classroom activity—structured as a role-play with tangible but non-monetary stakes—that makes participants discover the incommensurability themselves rather than hearing it explained.

Divide the group into three factions assigned to honor, dignity, and victimhood, but do not reveal the conceptual labels until after the debrief; instead, give each faction specific internal roles—such as a family elder concerned with reputation, an impartial adjudicator, and a spokesperson for collective harm—so that disagreement can arise within groups as well as between them. Use a concrete scenario: a public accusation of plagiarism during a school debate that damages family standing, triggers institutional fairness rules, and activates claims of systemic bias. Provide a round-by-round timeline, explicit negotiation and veto rules, and a token-based payoff scheme that makes compromise costly and coordination failure visible. The rules should be arranged so that all three factions sincerely attempt to agree on a remedy, fail for reasons embedded in their roles, and only afterwards learn that their failure mapped onto the conceptual architecture—honor treating offense as identity-level injury requiring public restoration, dignity insisting on separating person from act through procedural neutrality, and victimhood demanding that institutional power be named in the remedy.

Then provide a debrief that connects each round's breakdown to these distinctions, and be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—likely the historical embeddedness of shame or the phenomenological weight of indignation. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I facilitate this, with practical guidance on managing it, and state clearly what participants should come away seeing: that moral superiority may depend on which kind of harm one treats as fundamental. Please write the answer in British English, using British spelling and vocabulary, though I have written this prompt in American English.
