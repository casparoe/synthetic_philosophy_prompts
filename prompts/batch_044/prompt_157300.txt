You sit on the Interim Reconciliation Panel of a post-authoritarian state whose transitional justice framework draws loosely on South African truth-commission and Argentine disclosure precedents, but operates under a domestic statute that conditions amnesty on both complete disclosure and demonstrable moral transformation. The Panel must decide the case of an applicant designated R., who from 1978 to 1983 served as the senior systems archivist for the state security directorate. R. never ordered, witnessed, or physically executed a disappearance; his role was to design and maintain the centralized index that linked detainees to clandestine detention facilities. Evidence presented to the Panel indicates that without this index, the directorate's operational coherence would have degraded within months, though individual disappearances might still have occurred in disorganized form. R. has provided exhaustive disclosure—every file, every cross-reference, every backup protocol—and for the past twenty years has worked with victims' families to reconstruct destroyed records and locate remains. A coalition of survivors' groups urges denial of amnesty, arguing that R. was structurally indispensable rather than incidental, that his role was not replaceable in any meaningful counterfactual sense because no other official possessed his technical knowledge at that time, and that granting amnesty to a bureaucrat whose sustained institutional labor made mass harm systematic would collapse the distinction between direct culpability and structural complicity.

Draft the Panel's formal written opinion recommending a specific course of action—grant conditional amnesty, deny it outright, or approve it subject to a defined, non-carceral obligation—with a concrete implementation timeline (for example, verification steps within ninety days, or a permanent institutional duty). The document should be suitable for circulation to the applicant, survivors' representatives, and the oversight ministry. It must include: (1) a clear statement of the recommendation and the conditions or reasons attached; (2) a serious engagement with the survivors' structural-indispensability argument, treating it as the strongest consideration against amnesty rather than dismissing it; (3) direct reasoning about the philosophical problem this case raises—whether moral agency and responsibility can be discontinuous across time when the wrong was not a discrete act but a sustained institutional condition, and whether the applicant now standing before the Panel is, in the morally relevant sense, the same agent who maintained the system of harm; (4) an honest account of the best reason for the opposite recommendation, and an explanation of why the Panel's judgment nevertheless prevails, or, if you recommend denial, why the transformation and disclosure arguments fail to overcome the weight of structural culpability; and (5) a brief note on how the Panel's reasoning applies specifically to bureaucratic roles that are technically indispensable but physically remote from violence, so that the decision does not automatically extend to direct perpetrators or to fully replaceable functionaries.

Write in a formal, institutional register. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not invoke named philosophical theories solely by citation; work out the reasoning in the text of the opinion itself.
