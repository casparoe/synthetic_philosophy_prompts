After a long argument with a friend about whether Hume's reasoning against miracles still holds up once we account for modern technology and formal epistemology, I want to ask a more specific version of the question that we never quite resolved. Hume, in his essay on miracles, weighed testimony against the uniform experience of natural law, treating direct experience as more authoritative than second-hand report and judging the credibility of witnesses by their known fallibility. Now consider two developments he could not have anticipated: first, the formal Bayesian reconstruction of his argument, in which a miracle claim is assessed by comparing the prior improbability of a law being suspended against the posterior likelihood that the reports are erroneous; and second, what I will call synthetic corroboration — a situation in which thousands of ostensibly independent observers document an event through digital sensors and live transmission, yet the recording and distribution infrastructure is vulnerable to coordinated, adversarial manipulation, so that the multiplicity of testimony does not linearly increase reliability because the potential errors are systematically correlated rather than independent. Given Hume's actual commitments to probabilistic balance and to the hierarchy between direct observation and mediated report, how do you think he would have classified such technologically mediated, networked testimony? Would he have regarded it as closer to direct experience, or as a magnified but structurally weakened form of second-hand report? And would the possibility of invisible, large-scale corruption in the observational medium have led him to strengthen his original skepticism about miracle reports, or to reformulate the conditions under which uniform experience can be overturned? I am not looking for a reading list; I want a reconstructed account of his likely reasoning and of any internal tensions that arise when his eighteenth-century epistemic framework is applied to observation that is both massively distributed and potentially adversarially synthesized.
