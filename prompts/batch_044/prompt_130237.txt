I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in applied debates about well-being—particularly where a tranquilist commitment, that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, is pressed against phenomenological counterexamples. The pattern unfolds in two characteristic instances. First, when presented with agents who appear to flourish precisely through sustained, desire-laden engagement—say, the craftsperson whose mastery requires years of restless apprenticeship, or the researcher whose fulfillment is inseparable from ambitious, risk-laden inquiry—the defender does not deny the reported satisfaction but reinterprets it as a form of indirect stillness: the craving is parsed not as genuine aspiration but as managed restlessness, and the satisfaction is treated not as a positive good but as the temporary cessation of an underlying disturbance. Second, in arguments advocating severe reductions in labor and the expansion of leisure, any structured aspiration, from competitive practice to artistic discipline, is treated not as an independent source of value but as a culturally disguised compulsion whose elimination merely reveals the true baseline, an affective null-state. The maneuver is not simple denial of positive welfare, since the phenomenology is accepted; nor is it an empirical adjustment of the theory, since no predictive claim is altered—only the normative interpretation of the experience is rewritten. Is there an established concept that captures this specific hermeneutic reduction—the systematic parsing of every apparent positive flourishing as an indirect preservation of craving-absence? How precisely does it differ from eliminativism about positive goods, and from Stoic distinctions that classify such pursuits as preferred indifferents rather than intrinsic goods? If no precise term exists, could one be defined with clear boundary conditions: for instance, does the pattern include a deliberately cultivated rhythm in which an agent introduces mild, controlled craving precisely in order to enjoy its subsequent dissolution, suggesting that the dynamics of craving and release carry a value that pure absence cannot capture? I am not asking for a bare label, but for a rigorous carving—what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie—particularly regarding whether it ultimately collapses into a variant of negative hedonism or instead exposes a hidden, positive valuation of affective motion itself.
