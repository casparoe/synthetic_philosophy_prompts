I serve on the grant-review committee for a regional agricultural-innovation fund, and we now receive roughly two proposals a week that could affect invertebrate welfare—novel pesticides, automated harvesting architectures, biological-control introductions, and indoor-farming systems. I am trying to apply a framework I have been developing that treats sentience not as a binary threshold but as a continuous credence parameter p(i) assigned to a taxonomic group i, scaled by population exposure N and a rough severity weight s(i) to yield an expected moral load L = p(i)·N·s(i). To keep the scale argument from automatically swamping every other consideration, the framework includes what I call a discontinuity brake: when p(i) falls below an evidential floor—where behavioral analogies to vertebrate nociception are weak—L does not by itself dominate the decision unless an additional flag for direct, intentional, and avoidable harm is present. There is also an explicit phylogenetic boundary condition, acknowledged as value-laden rather than biologically absolute, that stops the scaling argument before it reaches organisms lacking any integrated nervous architecture; and a reversal check that halts the procedure if applying L consistently implies we must prioritize microbial welfare over human welfare.

Please turn this into a reusable decision procedure my committee can run in about twenty minutes per proposal, without anyone needing to read primary neuroscience literature. The deliverable should be a step-by-step flow in words: the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each node, the precise conditions under which the procedure stops with a no, a deferral, or a hand-off to human judgment, and the points where judgment is unavoidable and must be signed off by a designated reviewer. Include a small set of applied test criteria—phrased as questions about the proposal text—that help determine p(i) and whether the harm in question is direct and intentional versus foreseen but structurally unavoidable. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) funding a targeted neuropeptide pesticide that disrupts feeding in aphids but may also affect pollinators with similar receptor pathways; (b) funding a vertical-farm module that eliminates chemical pesticides but uses high-speed mechanical separation that crushes aphids and thrips; and (c) funding the release of a parasitoid wasp for biological control of a crop pest, knowing the wasps cause prolonged internal parasitism in host larvae. Include at least one case—likely (c)—where the procedure yields an answer that supports funding despite substantial invertebrate harm, and specify exactly which node in the flow produces that result. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do, particularly whether it can resolve disputes about where the evidential floor or phylogenetic boundary should sit, or whether it merely relocates those disagreements into explicit parameter choices.
