As a novelist working on a story about a prosecutor who uncovers posthumous proof of innocence, I am struck by an institutional asymmetry: jurisdictions that retain capital punishment often provide formal state exonerations, named apologies, and statutory reparations to the heirs of executed people later proven innocent, while those who survive decades of wrongful imprisonment typically receive financial compensation but rarely the same ceremonial acknowledgment that the punishment itself was a moral error. What philosophical commitments about punishment—retributive, deterrent, or risk-based—does this practice presuppose? Does it implicitly treat execution as uniquely irreversible and therefore uniquely demanding of ritual correction, or does it presuppose that the essential wrong in punishment is the destruction of life rather than the deprivation of liberty? And can the asymmetry be reconciled with the standard risk-based argument that capital punishment is uniquely dangerous because it forecloses any possibility of remedy? In your answer, separate what is philosophically settled in this reconstruction from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of punishment theory, and do not present any single interpretation as the consensus view.
