In my philosophy of mind seminar, two fellow students have been stuck in an argument about Douglas Hofstadter's careenium analogy. Jordan holds that Hofstadter is defending genuine downward causation: the high-level symbolic patterns he calls simms—above all the self-model—actually push around the low-level substrate of simmballs (neurons, molecules, and the like). Jordan insists that if all causal work stayed at the substrate level, mental causation would be epiphenomenal and Hofstadter's claim that strange loops have real causal potency would collapse. Riley replies that the careenium is only a pedagogical image, and that all pushing remains bottom-up: the simms are just useful descriptions, comparable to saying that the shape of a traffic jam pushes individual cars. Jordan then answers that this misses the specific feedback structure of Hofstadter's strange loop—the symbolic self-model does not merely describe substrate states but constrains future ones, which is precisely what downward causation requires. Riley grants that the loop is mechanistically real but argues it is still fully implemented bottom-up: in predictive-processing terms, high-level predictions constrain lower-level dynamics only as statistical summaries of prior bottom-up interactions, not as independent causal forces. Jordan counters that if the self-model is nothing more than a statistical summary, then it cannot genuinely be said to shove anything around, yet Hofstadter explicitly frames the careenium around the substantive causal question of who shoves whom, not merely as a descriptive convenience. They have asked me to referee. Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move—rating each turn as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right with brief justification—and then deliver a committed overall verdict on who is closer to being right about downward causation in the careenium? Please respond in American English, and take definite positions rather than balancing both sides equally.
