I have recently concluded a career of nearly thirty years in civil infrastructure, and the sudden absence of externally mandated goals has left me with a disorientation I did not anticipate—one that has turned my attention to older writings on whether life requires purpose in order to be meaningful. In his 1908 essay Lebensweisheit, Schlick develops a distinction between Zweck and Sinn that I am struggling to apply to my own circumstances. He argues, in effect, that human existence possesses no transcendent purpose—no final end that stands outside the living of it and justifies it from without—and that what we call meaning is not the achievement of such an endpoint but rather the present quality of being fully absorbed in an activity pursued for its own sake. He compares this mode of being to the play of a child, who lives entirely in the present moment without treating the activity as an instrument for some future result, and he contends that adulthood typically destroys this capacity by organizing every action around anticipated rewards, so that the present is continually sacrificed to an imagined future. He further suggests that youth retains an intuitive grasp of this secret precisely because it has not yet been thoroughly trained into the instrumental habits of mature social life.

Given this, I am unsure how to understand Schlick’s framework in relation to my current situation. If I now take up sustained practices—mentoring younger engineers, long-term conservation work, or the slow mastery of a craft—am I genuinely recovering the playful absorption he praises, or am I merely substituting one instrumental structure, oriented toward legacy or productivity, for the career I have left behind? Does Schlick require a complete abandonment of enduring goals and long-range planning in order to live meaningfully, or can there be a form of sustained, multi-year engagement—perhaps analogous to the prolonged attention of an artist or musician—that remains non-instrumental despite extending far into the future? Moreover, since my own sense of coherence over the past decades came largely from deep, long-term professional commitments that were profoundly purpose-driven, must I conclude, on Schlick’s account, that those years were lived largely in self-deception, robbing the present of its meaning for the sake of an illusory endpoint? I would appreciate help in interpreting the passage: clarifying whether Schlick is prescribing the elimination of all lasting purposes, or rather describing a particular attitude of present absorption that might be maintained even within structured, enduring practices; and explaining what, if anything, distinguishes a genuinely meaningful long-term commitment from the kind of purpose-driven existence he rejects.
