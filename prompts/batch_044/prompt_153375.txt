You are a professional philosopher with expertise in epistemology and conceptual fiction. Before you begin writing, please take a few minutes to brainstorm carefully and think step-by-step about how to embed a philosophical argument into narrative form without allowing it to collapse into a lecture or a summary.

I am a high school student completing an independent study project on G.E. Moore's argument for the existence of an external world. I need you to write an original short story—approximately eight hundred to one thousand words—that treats the logical structure of that argument as its narrative engine rather than as a philosophical punchline.

Here is the specific scenario to work with: the protagonist lives in a clinical research facility where patients can project their proprioceptive body-images into a shared virtual space, so the usual boundary between mental representation and physical object has been technologically dissolved. In this setting, the protagonist must attempt something structurally analogous to Moore's proof—directly presenting an object and asserting its existence outside consciousness—to establish that at least one thing exists independently of their own mind. Do not simply reproduce the familiar classroom gesture of holding up hands; instead, invent a concrete carrying case that mirrors the proof's logical form: direct acquaintance with an item, the claim that this item is not identical to any mental content, and the conclusion that an external world exists. At the same time, show why this move feels both compelling and strangely inadequate within the facility's conditions.

Please include three narrative elements: first, a moment where the proof succeeds practically for the protagonist but fails philosophically for an observer who understands the virtual environment; second, a misconception embedded in the dialogue—perhaps a character assuming Moore was attempting to refute idealism through formal logic rather than through direct assertion—that gets corrected or complicated; and third, an ending that leaves the reader with the sense that an external world has been established in a practical, lived way without being theoretically secured.

Avoid bibliographies, citations, references to specific secondary literature, or lists of further reading. Think carefully about the dependency between the plot events and the logical structure of the proof, then write the story.
