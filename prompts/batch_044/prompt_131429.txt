I am a professor of applied philosophy who chairs the clinical ethics committee at a university teaching hospital. We are now adjudicating harm arising from semi-autonomous clinical decision-support systems — algorithmic recommenders whose outputs physicians may adopt, adapt, or reject, but which are not themselves fully autonomous moral agents. Conventional liability structures fail in the interstices between developer, institution, and clinician, so I wish to operationalise the philosophical literature on responsibility gaps into a reusable audit procedure that committee members — who have no philosophical training and no access to external literature — can run in under forty-five minutes per case.

Please construct, in British English, a flowchart in prose: an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions that translate the conceptual distinctions between causal contribution, normative competence, and institutional authorisation into practical tests. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer and identify the precise stop-point where the procedure either assigns primary responsibility definitively or declares an irreducible gap requiring collective institutional judgement rather than individual attribution. You must also flag the collapse conditions — those cases in which the system made the decision and the clinician endorsed the decision are not separable — since these are precisely the hard cases we face.

Include three worked applications: first, a case in which a clinician overrode a sound recommendation and harm followed; second, a case in which a clinician blindly adopted a flawed recommendation; and third, a case in which the system's reasoning was opaque even to its developers, leaving no meaningful override point for the clinician. Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire, where value judgements cannot be proceduralised, and what must be settled by human deliberation. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the procedure must stand alone.
