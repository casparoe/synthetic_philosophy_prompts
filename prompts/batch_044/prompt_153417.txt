I'm writing a piece on the philosophy behind AI governance practices. There's an emerging institutional norm—evident in certain audit protocols and in how regulators interpret transparency mandates—that treats findings from mechanistic interpretability as identifying genuine "representations" within large neural networks: auditors speak of circuits that represent deception, directions in activation space that encode gender, or features that carry semantic content about truth. These representational attributions are then used to justify concrete interventions—retraining targets, disclosure obligations, or risk classifications—yet the same reports typically disclaim that these patterns are mental states, deny that the network has beliefs or intentions, and describe the patterns as purely statistical correlations that happen to be human-interpretable. What philosophical account of representation is this practice implicitly relying on? Reconstruct the view that makes sense of treating a correlation pattern as content-bearing enough to ground regulatory action, while simultaneously stripping it of the intentional or teleological properties usually associated with representation. Is this implicit account coherent, or does the practice quietly oscillate between incompatible theories—say, a causal-informational account and a purely structural one? Further, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well: could a deflationary anti-realist about neural representations adopt the same auditing procedures, or does the normative force of these audits depend on a thicker ontological commitment? Finally, if we replaced the implicit representationalism with a thoroughly deflationary framework—where interpretable patterns are just useful heuristics with no semantic properties—what concrete changes would follow for audit reports, regulatory language, and the justification of interventions? In your answer, please separate what is relatively settled in philosophy of mind and AI ethics from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any one contested interpretation—such as whether neural representations require teleology or whether interpretability findings have normative weight—as if it were the consensus view.
