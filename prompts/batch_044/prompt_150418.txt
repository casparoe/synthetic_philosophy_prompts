I am writing a novel centered on a forger of lost modernist manuscripts, but with a specific conceit: each forged work is engineered so that its concealed true authorship is structurally necessary to its aesthetic logic, in the way that an unreliable narrator depends on the reader's temporary ignorance. This raises a conceptual problem I would like you to explain. Standard accounts treat forgery as purely parasitic: once exposed, the fake either collapses into a failed original or becomes a mere duplicate, its value drained away. Yet these performative forgeries—texts composed specifically to be misattributed, in which the forger's hidden agency is woven into the work's meaning—seem to change status upon exposure without becoming simple copies. Please explain the conceptual tension between causal provenance, the value that derives from the actual mind and hand that produced the artifact, and negative provenance, the value or meaning that depends on the very gap between attributed and actual authorship. Does the exposed performative forgery remain aesthetically and conceptually distinct from both the genuine article and an innocent duplicate, or does revelation of true authorship eliminate all non-derivative worth? Be somewhat in the weeds: address whether Nelson Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic arts applies to literature in this case, and whether hidden intentionality introduces an autographic dimension even in texts that are otherwise allographic. Please also indicate what would change your mind—specifically, which argument or finding would lead you to conclude that hidden intentional structure is always aesthetically irrelevant, or that provenance is merely a market construct rather than a philosophical category—and point to where your reasoning is most vulnerable.
