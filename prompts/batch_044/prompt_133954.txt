Frank Ramsey argued that asking after the meaning of life involves a conceptual confusion analogous to asking whether an object weighs ten pounds or ten kilograms: once the logical form is made explicit, the question dissolves rather than being answered with a fact. Imagine he were confronted with a contemporary longtermist claim: because artificial superintelligence might eventually create astronomical numbers of digital minds, present human choices ought to be evaluated not by personal fulfillment but by whether they steer history away from value lock-in. Proponents say this transforms existential anxiety into an empirical forecasting problem about preferences; critics reply that comparing value frameworks for digital minds with no shared metric merely repeats the original confusion at a larger scale. Given Ramsey's pragmatist tendency in philosophy of language, his subjective theory of belief and induction, and his dissolution of the life-meaning question, how would he diagnose this framing? Would he treat longtermist steering as a legitimate practical problem, extend his dissolution to cover collective decision problems about future populations, or draw a sharp line between first-person conceptual confusion and empirical coordination? Apply his analysis specifically to the niche case of a decision-theoretic agent forced to choose between two incommensurable axiological frameworks for future digital populations without any common utility scale: does that scenario resurrect a genuine question of meaning, or does it expose a deeper category mistake about treating formal incommensurability as if it demanded a factual resolution?
