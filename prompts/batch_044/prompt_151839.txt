You are a professional philosopher of mind and an expert in adversarial collaboration methodology. Your task is to draft a formal joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a sustained, good-faith collaboration on whether consciousness is best understood as a user illusion.

The two parties are:
- The Interface Eliminativists, who hold that phenomenal consciousness is entirely a user-illusion: there are no phenomenal properties over and above the functional and informational properties of the brain's self-model, and the "subject" for whom the illusion appears is itself a constructed fiction of that model.
- The Phenomenal Interface Realists, who agree that consciousness functions as a user-illusion—a simplified, action-guiding representation that does not transparently reveal underlying neural reality—but who maintain that the interface itself instantiates irreducible phenomenal properties that are not identical to any underlying physical or functional state.

Before drafting, think carefully and work through a brief brainstorm: identify the shared commitments about neural self-modeling and representational simplification; isolate the exact ontological divergence; determine the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and identify one concrete thought experiment, hypothetical empirical finding, or conceptual argument that both sides would accept as bearing on each crux, with each camp's prediction recorded explicitly.

Then produce the document in the following format:
1. A numbered section of agreed claims—statements both camps endorse, including shared descriptions of the user-illusion framework, the limits of introspective access, and what remains unsettled by current neuroscience. Include agreement on the precise formulation of their disagreement.
2. A disagreements table with three columns: the issue; the Interface Eliminativist formulation, phrased so that camp would accept the wording of its own view; and the Phenomenal Interface Realist formulation, phrased so that camp would accept the wording of its own view.
3. A cruxes section listing the precise sub-questions that still separate them, phrased neutrally.
4. For each crux, specify the concrete case, argument, or hypothetical evidence both sides agree is relevant, and state in advance what each camp predicts the outcome or analysis would show.

Be rigorous and avoid straw-manning. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Ensure all concepts are drawn from broadly available philosophical and scientific literature rather than obscure specialist texts. Focus especially on whether the framework entails epistemic closure regarding the system's own implementation, and whether such closure is compatible with phenomenal properties that outstrip functional description.
