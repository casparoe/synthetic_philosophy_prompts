I am a high school student taking an introductory philosophy course that recently turned to debates about moral responsibility and determinism. For our next seminar, I have been asked to present a brief analysis of P.F. Strawson's well-known argument concerning reactive attitudes, but I encountered a popular philosophy essay online that summarizes his position in a way that feels conceptually dense yet slightly off. Before I rely on it, I would like an accuracy audit. The essay summarizes Strawson as holding that moral responsibility consists entirely in being an appropriate target of reactive attitudes—specifically resentment, gratitude, and similar emotional responses—which function primarily as social tools that justify punishment or reward. According to this account, Strawson partitions human relationships into two strictly exclusive stances: the participant stance, defined by the presence of reactive attitudes, and the objective stance, in which we treat another person as an object to be managed or repaired, comparable to a malfunctioning mechanism. The essay further claims that Strawson considered determinism to be false, though he maintained that even if determinism were true, reactive attitudes would persist unchanged because they are biologically hardwired evolutionary adaptations for group cooperation. From these premises, the essay draws a sharp conclusion: moral responsibility is purely conventional and binary, such that if a community ceases to feel resentment toward an agent—whether due to mental illness, neurodivergence, or social change—that agent is automatically and fully excused, with no deeper normative facts remaining. The essay also invokes Strawson's distinction between direct participant reactive attitudes and vicarious reactive attitudes—feelings directed at others on behalf of a third party—to argue that even indirect blame is ultimately an instrumental mechanism for maintaining social cohesion.

I am inclined to think that this summary captures the broad outline of Strawson's framework but conflates several distinctions he carefully maintains, particularly regarding the relationship between determinism and reactive attitudes, the exclusivity of the two stances, and the normative status of responsibility. I am especially uncertain whether Strawson treats reactive attitudes as evolutionary instruments, whether he regards the objective attitude as permanently appropriate for neurodivergent individuals, and whether his framework genuinely permits a purely conventional, binary conception of excuse. Could you examine this summary point by point—indicating which claims are essentially accurate, which are common but misleading simplifications, and which are clear misrepresentations? I would also like to know whether the essay's conclusion that responsibility is purely conventional and binary survives once the misrepresentations are corrected, or whether it depends on them. Please address in particular how Strawson's framework handles cases involving mental difference, where the objective attitude might be adopted without reducing the person to a mere object.
