I'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of science and tech, and last week we got into a heated back-and-forth about mechanstic anomaly detection—specifically what makes two detection processes the "same mechanism." My friends Maya and Jonas were arguing about a hypothetical case where you have two sensors monitoring the same industrial pipeline: one is a conventional GPU-based detector running a deep classifier, and the other is a neuromorphic chip using memristive synapses. Both flag cavitation events through what looks like an identical counterfactual dependency graph—intervene on the pressure-node and the flag disappears in both. Maya said that means they share the detection mechanism; the silicon vs. analog substrate is just implementation detail, like the same program running on different hardware. Jonas said that's wrong—mechanism isn't an abstract causal graph, it's a concrete organization of parts and operations. If one fails because of thermal noise in transistors and the other because of ion drift in memristors, and you can't swap parts between them, they're different mechanisms, period. He added that treating them as identical would make you miss substrate-specific failure modes.

Maya pushed back that Jonas is smuggling in a metaphysical preference for material parts. She said in mechanistic explanation, what matters is where you can intervene, not what the parts are made of. If the intervention mapping is isomorphic, then for the purpose of explaining and fixing the anomaly, it's the same mechanism at the relevant level of abstraction. Jonas replied that you can't just pick abstraction levels arbitrarily—the level is fixed by what's actually doing the causal work. He also said that in mechanistic anomaly detection the detector is coupled to the system being monitored, so substrate dynamics enter the detection process itself; you can't cleanly seperate the detection mechanism from the failure mechanism like Maya wants. Maya answered that he's just relabeling functional differences as mechanistic ones, and that if his view were right we'd never be able to compare mechanisms across biological and artificial systems, which is exactly what the field tries to do.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. So please don't just list both positions—take a committed stance on who's right about the core identity conditions, point out exactly where each one is missing something, and say whether there's a middle ground they're ignoring that actually resolves the tension. I want a definite verdict with reasons, not a both-sides shrug.
