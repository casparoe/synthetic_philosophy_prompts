I've come to accept a strong form of tranquilism: not merely that suffering is bad and its absence is good, but that the only state with intrinsic value is the complete cessation of craving and suffering, and that any positive mental condition—pleasure, curiosity, aesthetic absorption, even benevolent concern—introduces a structural dependency or directed tension that makes it incompatible with the highest well-being. On this view, the ideal is not hedonically neutral but structurally empty: a consciousness without lack, without intentional directedness, and without the instability that accompanies positive experience. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want more than the familiar reply that people seem to value positive goods; I am asking for the specific vulnerabilities internal to the view. For instance, does it depend on a particular metaphysics of consciousness—say, that all intentionality necessarily involves suffering? Does it risk collapsing into value nihilism if applied consistently? Which results in philosophy of mind, such as the claim that consciousness requires content or that mental states are essentially relational, would render the ideal psychologically impossible? Which defeaters would a committed strong tranquilist themselves concede as damaging, and which could they absorb by distinguishing craving from other forms of directedness? Please rank these from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the view is genuinely exposed here or has been insulated by distinctions that make it unfalsifiable.
