I am a doctoral student in philosophy of language, currently working with a reading group that focuses on the intersection of philosophy of science and semantic theory. We have been spending considerable time on Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, particularly the familiar gavagai scenario in which a linguist cannot determinately fix whether a native speaker refers to rabbits, rabbit stages, or undetached rabbit parts. I feel I have a reasonable grasp of the standard argument: observational equivalence underdetermines the translation manual, stimulus synonymy fails to secure unique reference, and ontological commitment is relative to the background language in which we regiment the native theory. Nevertheless, I want to push the framework into somewhat less familiar territory, and I am unsure whether Quine's conceptual apparatus accommodates the extension cleanly.

Consider the following hypothetical, which we constructed for discussion. Imagine an isolated scientific community that employs a formal notation system—devoid of any accompanying natural language—to classify crystalline mineral formations. Within this system there is a symbol, call it φ, applied consistently to certain geometric structures observed in the field. One interpreter translates φ as denoting the macroscopic crystal lattice as a unified object; another translates it as denoting the distributed network of atomic bonds that realizes the lattice, with no ontological commitment to a single macroscopic entity. Both translations preserve every observational prediction and every intra-theoretic inference rule encoded in the notation. Crucially, the community has never articulated an explicit ontology, and there is no home language available to anchor reference beyond the formal system itself. My conceptual question is this: does Quine's indeterminacy apply here with precisely the same force, or does the absence of a background natural-language ontology generate a distinct, perhaps more radical, form of reference indeterminacy? More specifically, is there a principled reason within Quine's own philosophy—perhaps tied to his view that ontological commitment is carried by the regimented language of science rather than by pre-theoretic intuition—to privilege one translation over the other? Or does this formal case simply reproduce the rabbit example in different clothing?

I would appreciate a careful explanation that keeps Quine's core conceptual machinery intact—stimulus-meaning, the inscrutability of reference, and the empirical equivalence of translation manuals—without treating the formal notation case as a mere illustration. Please explain whether the scenario introduces a genuinely different kind of indeterminacy, and where exactly the boundaries of Quine's thesis lie in relation to formal, non-natural-language symbol systems. Please provide your answer in British English.
