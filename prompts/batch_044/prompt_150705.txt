I've been thinking about Hofstadter's idea that an 'I' is really just a pattern, a strange loop that can in principle be copied or partially preserved in other minds. But I want to push it further: what if a single self can be genuinely distributed accross two brains not just in the weak sense that I remember your thoughts, but in the strong sense that there's an intercranial feedback loop—like both brains are running coupled predicive models of each other's internal states, so the narrative center of gravity is actually split? Let's call this distributed strange-looping. I think if the synchronization is tight enough and sustained, there's literally one 'I' with two physical loci, not two people who understand each other really well.

What would actually count against this? I don't want the usual hand-wavy 'but consciousness is private' stuff. I want the real defeaters: which empirical findings about brain coupling or split-brain patients would undermine it, which conceptual problems about ownership of action or first-person authority become fatal versus just akward, and whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—like saying 'well if they don't feel like one person then the loop wasn't tight enough.' What would a committed distributed-self theorist have to concede? Rank the defeaters from 'this kills it' to 'this is just embarrassing,' and tell me what the view predicts that a standard psychological-continuity account doesn't. Also be honest: is there any observation that could genuinely change my mind, or have I just built a castle in the air?
