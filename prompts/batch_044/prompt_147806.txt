Examination question: To what extent does an earlier competent self possess normative authority over a later demented self? Discuss the account known as precedent autonomy and one significant objection to it, paying particular attention to whether that authority depends on metaphysical personal identity or can be grounded independently of identity conditions altogether.

Student answer:

Precedent autonomy holds that a competent person can bind their future demented self through an advance directive, in a manner analogous to constitutional law binding future citizens. Ronald Dworkin argues that this authority derives not from property rights over the body, but from the patient's critical interests in living a coherent narrative life. Because the directive expresses those critical interests, to disregard it is to violate the patient's autonomy across time, irrespective of whether psychological continuity is preserved.

A significant objection comes from Rebecca Dresser. She maintains that once psychological continuity is broken, the demented patient is not the same person as the author of the directive; consequently, the earlier self's preferences have no more normative weight than those of a stranger. The present patient's experiential interests—comfort, pleasure, freedom from distress—ought therefore to take precedence.

This objection rests on a false assumption, namely that normative authority requires metaphysical identity. Even if we reject psychological continuity in favour of bodily continuity, or adopt a four-dimensionalist view of persons as temporal stages, the earlier self can still stand in a fiduciary relation to the body. The authority is best understood as that of a trustee appointed by prior consent, rather than as that of a sovereign commanding identical subjects. Hence, Dworkin's position is actually strengthened once we abandon the requirement of psychological continuity.

There is, however, a residual difficulty. If the demented self retains any capacity for present-tense valuing—even minimal appreciation of warmth or familiar voices—then the trustee model must weigh those present values against the earlier critical interests. Yet the model provides no principled method for such weighing, since it treats the earlier values as fixed instructions rather than as defeasible inputs. The correct conclusion, therefore, is that advance directives should carry strong but not absolute weight: they express autonomous choice, but they do not possess binding authority when the present subject has developed genuinely new interests that conflict with them.
