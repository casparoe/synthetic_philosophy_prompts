I have been reading about suffering-focused ethics—the broad idea that preventing intense suffering should take priority over promoting happiness, and that moral reasoning should be asymmetrical in this direction. I am not a philosopher by training, but I have been trying to apply this framework to real-world failures rather than to abstract thought experiments. One case that keeps returning is the 1984 chemical disaster at the Union Carbide plant in Bhopal, India. Most accounts focus on the immediate release of toxic gas and the thousands of immediate deaths and injuries. What troubles me is what happened afterward: the groundwater contamination that persisted for decades, the elevated rates of birth defects and chronic illness in subsequent generations, and the fact that legal liability and cleanup obligations remained contested and incomplete for years. To capture this, I have started using the phrase "residual burden"—the slow, distributed suffering that continues when an institution treats the initial catastrophe as a discrete event rather than as an ongoing structural condition.

I would like you to analyze the Bhopal episode through the lens of negative utilitarianism, but with the distinction between acute catastrophic suffering and this residual ambient suffering built explicitly into the analysis. First, map the moral actors and duties: if the elimination of suffering is the primary aim, what obligations did the corporation, the Indian state, and the broader chemical industry have—not merely to compensate victims of the initial leak, but to eliminate the conditions that produced the lingering harm? Second, tell me where the theory explains the case well and where it breaks down or falls silent. Does negative utilitarianism require total remediation regardless of cost, or does it permit welfare trade-offs once the worst acute phase has passed? How should it treat suffering experienced by people born after the disaster, who never consented to the risk? And finally, does the persistence of the residual burden reveal that suffering-focused ethics lacks an adequate account of structural complicity—of the ways institutions continue to generate harm through inaction, partial settlement, and the normalization of toxic conditions? I am not asking for a bibliography or citations; I want to see whether this framework, stretched to include the idea of a residual burden, can genuinely illuminate an industrial disaster, or whether it needs a conceptual tool that it does not yet possess.
