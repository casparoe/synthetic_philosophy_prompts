As a doctoral student in evolutionary anthropology studying cooperation and cultural transmission, I am facilitating an adversarial collaboration between two camps in my research group that have debated for months whether Zahavi’s handicap principle genuinely explains costly human ritual, or whether ritual waste is better understood as an incidental byproduct of institutional coordination. One side—the Strong Handicap camp—maintains that practices such as prolonged fasting, scarification, or expensive pilgrimage function primarily as hard-to-fake signals of group commitment, with wastefulness being the designed feature that stabilizes cooperation by filtering out free riders. The other side—the Institutional Coordination camp—holds that these same practices generate common knowledge, synchronize collective action, and solve coordination problems, and that treating every expensive element as an honest advertisement commits a category error: the cost is friction, not signal design.

We need a joint document both sides could sign. Please draft it with the following structure and content.

First, a numbered section of claims both camps endorse, phrased in neutral language: for example, that selection can favor traits bearing substantial costs when those costs confer differential benefits to observers or stabilize cooperative equilibria; that many documented rituals involve significant material, temporal, or bodily expenditure; and that purely functionalist accounts that ignore strategic interaction between individuals are incomplete, just as purely signaling accounts that ignore collective-action problems are incomplete.

Second, a disagreements table with exactly three rows, each stating the opposing positions in wording that each camp would accept for its own view. The rows should address: (a) whether the cost of a ritual is primarily a signal-design feature or a coordination byproduct; (b) whether the informational value of ritual participation depends on individual-level reputation tracking among observers or on group-level boundary maintenance and synchronization; and (c) whether the handicap logic applies analogously to modern secular costly practices—such as credential inflation or extreme athletic training—or whether these phenomena are structurally disanalogous because they lack the ancestral social context that made ritual waste informative.

Third, identify the cruxes: the specific sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp. I take the deepest crux to be whether rituals exhibit a measurable credibility surplus—the degree to which observed costs exceed the minimum necessary for pure coordination—and whether that surplus correlates with individual-level benefits (e.g., mating opportunities, alliance formation) versus exclusively group-level benefits (e.g., collective-action success). A second suspected crux concerns signal amortization versus recurrent synchronization: the Handicap camp predicts that ritual costs are front-loaded and depreciate over an individual’s lifecycle as reputation is established, whereas the Coordination camp predicts that costs remain recurrent and synchronized across participants regardless of tenure.

Fourth, for each crux, specify one concrete case, dataset, or thought experiment both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side’s explicit advance prediction. For instance, both might agree that cross-cultural data on the correlation between ritual intensity and individual status mobility would be relevant; here the Handicap camp should predict a positive correlation independent of group performance, while the Coordination camp should predict that correlation disappears or reverses once collective-action outcomes are controlled for. Similarly, both might accept experimental evidence on whether observers infer commitment from ritual participation versus from shared suffering in structurally equivalent non-ritual contexts, with each side stating in advance what pattern would weaken its position.

Finally, include a brief meta-section assessing whether part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—specifically, whether disagreements dissolve once we rigorously distinguish between cost-as-signal-design and cost-as-coordination-friction, and if so, which residual disagreement remains genuinely empirical or normative.

The document should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests, and it should avoid framing the dispute in terms of specific named theorists beyond the broad principle at issue. It should read as a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made as exact as possible.
