I'm trying to work out a puzzle about higher-order evidence and I think I've boxed myself in. (1) If I learn that my reasoning in some domain is unreliable — say, a study shows sleep-deprived doctors misdiagnose 40% more often — then I should lower my confidence in my first-order judgment, like this patient has pneumonia. (2) But the belief that I'm unreliable is itself produced by my reasoning, so it's vunerable to the same worry: maybe I'm misreading my own fatigue, or misinterpreting the study. (3) So I should apply the discount to my second-order belief too, then to the third, ad infinitum. (4) That means any attempt to correct bias via higher-order evidence collapses into an infinite regress where I can never stabalize a corrected confidence level. (5) If I can never complete the correction, then the norm use higher-order evidence to fix yourself is practicly useless — it demands a revision I'll never finish. (6) So either I reject the idea that higher-order evidence always undermines first-order confidence, which seems to license ignoring clear data about my own fatigue; or I accept it and land in a kind of global epistemic paralysis where I can't trust any self-correction. (7) I feel like there's a hidden slide between discounting one specific belief and discounting the whole reasoning chain, but I can't name the equivocation. Which step actually fails, and what's the cost of fixing it?
