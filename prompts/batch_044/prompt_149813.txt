Here is the position. I hold that the Buddhist doctrine of anatta (no-self) is best reconstructed not as a flat denial that persons persist over time, but as a targeted rejection of substrate essentialism: there is no hidden atman that owns the skandhas. On my reading, the five skandhas function not as static parts assembled into a person, but as interdependent processes—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness—that achieve conventional (sammuti) continuity only through causal-narrative integration over time. This allows a qualified psychological-continuity account of personal identity at the conventional level, while the ultimate (paramattha) analysis reveals no binder behind the phases. The view claims to avoid both reductionist elimination of the person and the paradox of a self that must be either identical through change or non-existent. I also inject a further constraint: the skandhas should be understood as intentional modalities—structured relations of citta and mental factors—that generate a retrospective narrative self only through diachronic binding, not through any synchronous aggregation into a whole.
