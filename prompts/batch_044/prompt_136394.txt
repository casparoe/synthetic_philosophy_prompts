I am taking a graduate seminar on early modern women philosophers and have become particularly interested in Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness. I am drafting an essay—still in outline form, not a finished draft—that attempts to read her treatise on well-being as an applied metaphysics of mental dynamics rather than as a conventional Enlightenment manual for living well. The core claim is that du Châtelet treats happiness not as a static emotional possession but as an active equilibrium analogous to the dynamics of force and motion she examines in her scientific work: mental energy must be continuously directed, dissipated, and replenished through study, affection, and strategic illusion. I argue specifically that her defense of necessary illusions, especially those sustaining romantic attachment, should be understood not as a mere concession to human weakness but as a deliberate mechanism for preserving this mental equilibrium against the corrosive effects of excessive rational scrutiny. I also propose that her distinction between the happiness suited to philosophers and that suited to ordinary people is functional rather than hierarchical—describing different regimes for managing mental energy according to different cognitive capacities, both oriented toward the same stable state.

My concern is that this framing may illegitimately import concepts from her physics into her moral psychology, overstate her originality relative to Locke and Fontenelle, and present a synthetic interpretation as uniquely hers when it may be a common Enlightenment trope. I would welcome comments on the essay's structure, its interpretive risks, and its philosophical coherence—not a bibliography or reading list, since I need judgments that can be evaluated without further library access. In particular, please assess whether the analogical move from her physics to her ethics holds up under close textual scrutiny, whether interpreting necessary illusions as active preservation mechanisms conflicts with the standard scholarly account of her ethics, and whether the functional reading of her happiness hierarchy is defensible or anachronistic. Crucially, please separate what is settled in the interpretation of du Châtelet's primary arguments from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who work on her, and do not present my analogical thesis—or any rival interpretation—as if it represented the consensus. Also flag any points where the argument risks depending on contested attributions or on reading metaphysical commitments into passages that do not clearly support them, and indicate where the evaluation must stop being procedural and become a matter of the reader's own judgment about the texts.
