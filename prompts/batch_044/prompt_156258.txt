After a long argument with a friend, I have come to defend the view that genuinely hard choices between incommensurable values do not exist: what appears to be irreducible conflict is actually a failure of triadic preference reconstruction. On this account, if an agent ranks A over B and B over C yet insists A and C are incomparable, this reveals not true incommensurability but unacknowledged hidden dimensions—temporal proximity, identity-salience, or affective framing—that complete the ordering once made explicit. The so-called moral residue or anguish of choice is therefore not an objective feature of value conflict but a predictive-error signal in the agent’s self-model. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Which empirical results in decision science, which formal failures of preference-completeness theorems, and which phenomenological reports of radical value change or deep cross-cultural disagreement would actually undermine it? Please distinguish the defeaters that would be fatal—forcing abandonment of the reconstruction framework—from those that are merely awkward and can be absorbed by refining the hidden-dimension model. I am especially interested in whether there is a live, concrete case where triadic reconstruction demonstrably collapses, and whether conceding such a case would destroy the view or only require local qualification.
