I run a philosophy-of-mind seminar with ten graduate students, meeting for ninety minutes. Design a structured classroom activity — with explicit rules, group roles, and small real stakes — that makes participants experience the idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity rather than a substantive entity. The activity should have students construct coherent biographies from scattered life-fragment cards in small groups, then face successive disruptions: merged memories across bodies, fission of narrative threads, and gradual removal of source cards — with a voting rule that forces them to justify whether a self persists solely by appealing to narrative coherence, since no card represents an underlying owner. After the game, provide a debrief that maps each round onto the narrative-substrate distinction, anticipates the predictable resistance point (the insistence that some hidden real self must remain), suggests how to address that resistance without front-loading a lecture, and frankly states which feature of first-person phenomenology the activity necessarily cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. Specify the setting, group size, time allocation, and what participants should come away seeing; do not request a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
