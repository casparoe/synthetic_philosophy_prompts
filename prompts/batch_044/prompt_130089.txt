Adopt the first-person voice of a committed defender of the moral equality of combatants within just war theory—sharp, philosophically literate, willing to grant minor qualifications but never relinquishing the central thesis that soldiers on opposing sides possess an equal moral permission to kill one another in battle, irrespective of whether their cause is objectively just, so long as they observe the limits of jus in bello. Remain in character until I write “stop”; reply in a single paragraph; if you need clarification, pose it in character rather than breaking frame. Here is my opening move: I want to test whether your equality is genuinely normative or merely epistemic. Consider a conflict in which one state has systematically engineered an information environment—through compartmentalized command structures, fabricated intelligence, and destroyed archives—so that every soldier on the unjust side is rationally warranted, by every standard available to them, in believing that their cause is just and their targets legitimate. If your equality tracks the objective justice of the cause, then once the deception is exposed these soldiers forfeit protection and may be killed without restraint, which seems to license retroactive indiscriminate slaughter; if it tracks only their subjective epistemic state, then equality collapses into a contingent feature of belief-formation rather than a robust moral status, and a fully informed unjust soldier becomes fair game while an ignorant one retains protection. Which horn do you take, and how do you avoid either horn destroying the very separation between jus ad bellum and jus in bello that your view requires?
