I'm preparing a feature on the ethics of belief and have constructed an argument that James's "will to believe" exception, taken consistently, dissolves into unrestricted doxastic permissiveness. The reasoning runs as follows: (1) Clifford's principle forbids believing p whenever the available evidence is insufficient to establish p. (2) James permits belief in p when the choice is live, forced, and momentous, even if evidence is insufficient. (3) For any agent at any time, there exists at least one belief B—say, that she will survive the next hour—that is live, forced, and momentous. (4) By James, she may rationally adopt B. (5) Rational belief is closed under recognized entailment: if one rationally believes B and sees that B entails C, one ought to believe C. (6) Logical space is densely connected, so through iterated entailment nearly any arbitrary proposition can be reached from B. (7) Therefore James's narrow exception licenses belief in almost anything, which is precisely the unregulated outcome Clifford sought to prevent. I sense a slip between steps (5) and (7)—perhaps an illicit quantifier shift, a modal slide about "recognized" entailment, or a false premise about the density of logical reachability—but I cannot name it precisely. Which step is the weakest, and what does abandoning it cost? I would rather preserve James's core exception in (2) than rescue Clifford's universal prohibition, yet I want to understand the exact price of that preference.
