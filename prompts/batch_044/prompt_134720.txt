I'm currently taking a graduate seminar in rational choice theory and philosophy of science, and I've been asked to lead next week's discussion section for a group of second-year philosophy undergraduates who have basic familiarity with probability but no formal decision theory. The assigned topic is coherence arguments for expected utility maximization—specifically, how Dutch-book and money-pump considerations are supposed to constrain rational preference rather than merely describe consistent belief. I want to design the fifty-minute session as a teaching plan rather than a lecture: the precise order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, with explicit attention to what depends on what.

My instinct is to open not with Savage's representation theorem but with a concrete planning failure, then work backward to the axioms that prevent it. For the carrying example, I want to avoid the standard betting-on-horse-races scenario, which my peers tend to treat as an abstract logic puzzle. Instead, I'm considering a case involving a junior researcher deciding whether to preregister an expensive replication study: her funding prospects shift across states (secured grant versus uncertain future support), and her preference ordering over the same monetary gamble reverses depending on whether she anticipates running the study. This seems to make the incoherence feel like a practical vulnerability—she can be pumped through a sequence of commitments—rather than a formal contradiction. But I need to know exactly what properties this example must have and what it must lack: does the state-dependence need to be transparent to her, or hidden? Should the monetary amounts be identical across states, or scaled? And at what point in the sequence should I introduce the idea that coherence here is about preference structure across states, not about risk attitude?

I'm also trying to map the predictable misconceptions: that coherence is just "logical consistency" in one's beliefs about the world; that state-dependent utility is automatically irrational rather than a feature that needs axiomatic containment; and that rejecting the conclusion means embracing arbitrary preference reversal rather than adopting a different normative framework. I need to know where in the dependency order each misconception should be headed off—particularly the belief/consistency confusion, which I think will arise the moment I mention Dutch books. Finally, I need a check for understanding that demonstrates they've grasped the distinction between structural coherence (the axioms) and dynamic coherence (the pump over time), not merely that they can recite the conclusion. I also want to know what I should explicitly leave out and say so: should I avoid representation theorems entirely, defer causal versus evidential decision theory, and perhaps mention but not develop the Allais paradox? And given that our broader course concerns scientific rationality, is there a disciplined way to let the session touch on whether coherence arguments actually constrain methodological planning in science—for instance, in replication decisions—without letting that application swallow the conceptual structure?
