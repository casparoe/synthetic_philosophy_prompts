I am composing a literary novel whose protagonist is a reclusive natural philosopher attempting to organize his entire existence according to the moral architecture of Leibniz's system — one in which goodness is not measured by external action but by the internal harmony of appetitions, and in which justice is understood as charity guided by wisdom and directed toward the greatest perfection of the greatest number of substances. Rather than using this framework as mere historical color, I want the philosophical tensions to be structurally load-bearing for the plot: the character's decisions should turn on which of these tensions he resolves, and how. To that end, I need to know which open questions in Leibniz's moral philosophy are genuinely upstream of the others, and which are either derivative or so intractable that they should not drive the narrative.

Specifically, I am weighing three interrelated problems: first, whether Leibniz's commitment to the principle of sufficient reason and his denial of libertarian freedom leave any coherent conceptual space for moral responsibility that is not merely an anachronistic compatibilist import; second, whether the doctrine that every substance is a self-contained mirror of the universe entails a form of moral solipsism that undermines any genuine inter-subjective obligation; and third, whether the theory of petites perceptions can be extended into the moral domain, so that unconscious micro-appetitions aggregate into judgments in ways that dissolve the transparency traditionally required for valid consent or moral blame. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of specialists; rather, I want a defended agenda that treats my two years of conceptual preparation as a fixed resource. Rank these questions — and any others you think are more fundamental that I have overlooked — according to which must be settled first if the others are to be coherent. For each, explain the criteria you are using (upstream dependency, tractability for a novelist without formal training in seventeenth-century logic, and whether resolving it would actually alter the practical logic of the character's choices), identify what kind of argument or counter-example over the next year would cause you to reorder them, and flag any that seem important in the literature but are overrated for the purpose of constructing a working moral framework.
