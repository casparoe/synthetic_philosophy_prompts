I have been thinking about what holds a self together across radical disruption, because my father was recently diagnosed with frontotemporal dementia and I am now his primary decision-maker, and because I myself left a long clinical career for philosophy and find my own autobiographical thread unexpectedly frayed. I want to know whether there is a principle behind my intuitions about narrative continuity, or whether I must abandon some of them.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case 1. My father retains emotional and behavioral dispositions—he is still gentle with strangers, still irritated by loud noise, still drawn to jazz—but he can no longer order his life into a coherent story, denies that I am his child, and holds contradictory beliefs about his own past. I still think he is the same self; something like a gravitational center persists even where the narrative has collapsed.

Case 2. A hypothetical patient suffers total retrograde amnesia, builds an entirely new life with new relationships, values, and an autobiographical story, and retains no emotional residue from the old life. Here I feel the original self has genuinely ended; a new center of gravity has formed, despite bodily continuity.

Case 3. Imagine a digital archive—trained on every letter, journal entry, and conversation of a deceased writer—that continues to generate new texts in her voice, preserving perfect thematic, stylistic, and biographical coherence. Despite this flawless narrative continuity, I do not think the archive is her; there is distributed echo but no center.

Case 4. A refugee constructs and lives within a false autobiography for thirty years—adopting a new name, inventing a family history, eventually believing parts of it—then abruptly abandons it and resumes her original identity. During those thirty years, I think the false narrative was not a mask but her actual center of gravity; she was genuinely that self.

Case 5. A person’s brain is gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, with synthetic equivalents that preserve every memory, emotional pattern, and narrative connection. I think she survives; the gravitational field is maintained across physical discontinuity.

Case 6. A person suffers a brain injury that leaves every autobiographical memory intact—she can recount her childhood in detail—but completely inverts her emotional and evaluative landscape: she now despises everything she once loved and loves everything she once despised. I think this is not the same self; the narrative is present, but the center of mass has been replaced.

The pattern I keep returning to is unstable. In Case 1 I preserve identity despite narrative fragmentation; in Case 6 I deny it despite narrative preservation; and in Case 5 I preserve it despite physical discontinuity. This suggests the principle might concern the coherence of emotional-evaluative gravity rather than narrative content alone. But Case 4 complicates this, because the false narrative seemed over time to generate its own genuine emotional gravity.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes between a narrative’s explicit content, its emotional center of mass, and its anchoring in conscious experience—that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what is the cost? And what does the best available principle predict for a case I have not listed: a person who, through deliberate practice over ten years, gradually replaces every emotional response and every autobiographical belief with new ones, maintaining perfect logical coherence between old and new at every step, so that there is neither abrupt rupture nor falsehood, only smooth transformation?
