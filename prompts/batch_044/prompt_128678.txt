I've been arguing with my roommate about Kierkegaard's Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical, and I think I've landed on a pretty strange take. Rather than treating Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac as this singular religious exception — where the universal ethical command "don't kill your kid" gets temporarily suspended for a higher divine telos — I want to claim that every genuinely ethical commitment already involves this kind of suspension. The universal is never applied like an algorithm; it's always interrupted by the particularity of the decision, and Abraham is just the case where that hidden structure becomes impossible to ignore because the stakes are literal life and death. So the teleological suspension isn't an exception to ethics, it's the architechture of ethics itself.

But I'm worried I'm just projecting. What would count against this? I don't want people just saying "that's not Kierkegaard's intent" — I want actual defeaters: arguments that ethical reasoning really can be universal without suspension, or cases where acting for the right reason clearly doesn't involve bracketing the universal. Would this view collapse into moral particularism, and is that collapse fatal or just awkward? Which objections would a defender of this structural reading concede, and which could they absorb? Also, if Abraham stops being exceptional, does that ruin the religious point of the story, or can the religious dimension survive even if the suspension is everywhere? I'm taking an existantialism course and my prof keeps hammering "the individual over the universal," so maybe I'm overfitting that idea onto Abraham. Anyway, rank the objections from decisive to merely annoying, and tell me if this position has been qualified into unfalsifiability.
