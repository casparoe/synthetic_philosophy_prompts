I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on philosophy of archaeology and social ontology, and I have become puzzled by a persistent asymmetry in how our discipline institutionally frames rapid societal transformation. In archaeological funding proposals, heritage-site assessments, and peer-reviewed journal guidelines, the sharp decline of palace economies, cuneiform literacy, and long-distance exchange networks in the eastern Mediterranean around the twelfth century BCE, together with the cessation of moai construction and the restructuring of social hierarchy on Rapa Nui, are routinely categorized as collapse events—phenomena treated as failures that require causal explanation. By contrast, the Classic Maya lowland abandonment of monumental architecture, the dissolution of divine kingship, and the fragmentation of exchange networks are typically labeled political reorganization, demographic realignment, or systemic transformation, with explicit editorial norms in several venues discouraging the term collapse for this case, despite the structural parallels in centralization loss, elite literacy disappearance, and network contraction.

What philosophical commitments about societal identity over time, the criteria for institutional failure, and the boundary between descriptive and normative evaluation does this differential labeling practice presuppose? Must it assume an essentialist ontology in which a society can die when its defining institutions vanish, or can a functionalist or relational account explain the asymmetry by treating Bronze Age and Rapa Nui societies as constituted by specific hierarchical functions whose disappearance amounts to dissolution, while treating Maya society as constituted by broader cultural relations that persist through institutional change? Is the practice compatible with the anthropological claim that societies never truly fail but merely change form, or does it implicitly rely on a teleological benchmark of socio-political complexity? Furthermore, if the underlying framework were replaced by one that treated all three cases uniformly as non-normative systemic change—without any presumption of failure—what specific revisions would be required in archaeological reporting norms, grant-evaluation rubrics, and museum narrative design?
