I'm giving the opening remarks tomorrow evening at a small workshop my local legal-aid society is hosting on the legacy of post-war trials. The room will be split between practicing lawyers and law students on one side, and neighbors and family members with no legal background on the other. I'm a retired electrician, not a scholar, but I've been sitting with an uncomfortable idea: at Nuremberg and Tokyo, the Allied powers didn't merely apply law retroactively. They had to perform the trial as if the law—especially "crimes against peace"—had always existed, because the whole moral authority of the verdict depended on hiding the fact that they were inventing it in real time. The judges knew; the audience needed not to know. I want a five-minute address that makes this hidden performance the spine of the speech, not decoration.

Please write the remarks in spoken, respectful language—no academic jargon, no citations, no quotes from specific judgments, and no references to particular books or articles. The lawyers should recognize the philosophical problem; the neighbors should feel it as a human story about the cost of pretending. Structure the response in this order:

- Opening image (about 120 words): a concrete, non-technical scene that introduces the "mask of discovery" idea.
- The core argument (about 250 words): why retroactive justice structurally required this disguise, and what was lost by maintaining it.
- The turn outward (about 150 words): connect the idea to why a local legal-aid clinic still matters—justice that admits its own construction.
- Closing line (about 50 words): a brief, plain send-off that doesn't resolve the tension neatly.
