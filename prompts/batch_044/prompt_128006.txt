Compose the opening remarks for a workshop titled "Repair and Emergence," delivered by a novelist-restorer to a divided audience: master horologists and clockmakers on one side, philosophers of science on the other. The speech must take Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks as its structural spine, using it specifically to separate legitimate bottom-up explanation from greedy reductionism—the error of treating an astronomical clock as nothing but brass and escapement, thereby erasing the emergent temporal purpose that actually guides every physical repair decision. The speaker must reject skyhooks entirely—no vital essence, no phantom designer animating the gears—while insisting that reducing the mechanism to atoms alone destroys the very misalignments that only appear at the level of the whole, such as an escapement calibrated for sidereal rather than solar tracking. Make the argument unavoidable: the speech should be incapable of invoking mystical purpose, yet equally incapable of dismissing purpose as mere illusion, because that dismissal would render the restorer's craft unintelligible. Keep it to roughly four hundred words, formal in tone, and anchored by one precise mechanical image that allows both audiences to feel the distinction rather than merely name it.
