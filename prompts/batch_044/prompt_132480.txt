As a retired physicist who spent decades modeling coupled equilibrium systems, I have in recent years turned to philosophy of economics and institutional design, and I keep noticing a recurring structural pattern that I have not been able to match to any established concept. The pattern concerns proposals—whether in zoning reform, carbon pricing, or research-funding restructuring—that are deliberately engineered to be Pareto improvements over the status quo: every party affected at the time of adoption is offered compensation, a grandfather clause, or an opt-out mechanism that leaves them strictly better off, so that no existing stakeholder can claim to be harmed. Yet the very safeguards required to guarantee this safety do not remain temporary instruments; they crystallize into permanent institutional fixtures—entitlement categories, monitoring bodies, or protected revenue streams—that become the new baseline against which all future change is measured. Once this protected equilibrium is established, further reforms, even ones that would be genuinely welfare-enhancing relative to the original status quo, are blocked because any alteration to the safeguards is treated as a harm to those now entitled to them. The result is a system that is indeed Pareto-superior to its predecessor, but only because it is locked in place by the protective apparatus that made the initial improvement possible.

I have seen concrete instances of this in debates over housing upzoning with permanent developer-exaction requirements, in emissions-trading schemes with irrevocable industry rebates, and in healthcare restructuring with legacy-provider guarantees that never sunset. In each case, the objection is not that anyone is made worse off—everyone is, technically, still ahead of the old baseline—but that the method of achieving the improvement has generated a kind of structural moral hazard: the safeguards create constituencies with a vested interest in maintaining the protected state, and they transform what began as a transitional compensation into an enduring feature of the landscape. This is not status quo bias, because the resistance is directed at changing the new protected state, not at preserving the old one. It is not exactly path dependence, since the dependency is not an accidental historical residue but a deliberately engineered component of the reform. And while it touches on issues near the non-identity problem, it is not that problem, because we are comparing outcomes for the same individuals across time rather than comparing different possible populations. Does this specific failure mode—where the machinery of safety transforms a Pareto improvement into a locked, locally optimal equilibrium—have a recognized name in social-choice theory, institutional economics, or political philosophy? If not, can it be precisely carved and named, with clear boundary conditions that distinguish it from temporary transitional costs, from the second-best problems of welfare economics, and from ordinary institutional inertia? I am less interested in a bare label than in knowing whether the phenomenon is acknowledged in the literature, what its nearest conceptual neighbors are, and where exactly its edges lie.
