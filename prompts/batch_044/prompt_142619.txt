I'm trying to identify whether the following pattern in theory assessment has a recognized label or literature. Consider two competing scientific models that both account for the central phenomena within measurement error, but diverge in how they fail at the margins. Model A achieves a tighter overall fit, yet its residuals are systematically correlated with an auxiliary variable that neither model treats as causally central — the errors have a shape, clustering in a region or tracking a gradient. Model B fits less well globally, but its errors are scattered without structure, looking like random noise. Some practitioners reject Model A not on statistical grounds alone, but on the intuition that a theory closer to the truth should fail like an incomplete instrument — producing noise — whereas a theory with the wrong architecture fails with a signature, leaving structured residues that betray a misaligned framework. The judgment treats the morphology of error as carrying philosophical weight about whether a model is approximately true or merely empirically adequate. Is there an established concept that captures this use of residual structure — directional clustering, correlation with excluded variables, non-random geometry of failure — as an indicator of verisimilitude, distinct from standard predictive accuracy or explicit mechanistic falsification? If not, how should one map this intuition onto existing discussions of truth-likeness, and where exactly does it risk collapsing into an aesthetic preference for "clean" failure?
