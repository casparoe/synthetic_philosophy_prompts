I am drafting a comparative study in communicative ethics, examining Dutch norms of directness against Japanese practices of indirectness and the moral status of so-called white lies. In recent seminars, participants have repeatedly invoked the maxim that directness is respect and indirectness is condescension—typically to dismiss white lies as patronizing falsehoods that presume the listener cannot bear reality. Before I deploy this claim in the paper, I need its internal architecture mapped precisely.

Please disambiguate that maxim into a menu of distinct, precisely formulated theses, ordered from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, specify: (a) whether it is merely definitional or genuinely substantive; (b) whether it depends on a particular cultural framing—such as the Dutch emphasis on open discussability versus Japanese distinctions between honne and tatemae—without collapsing into bare cultural relativism; (c) whether it actually condemns all white lies, only those involving explicit false assertion, or even those that merely withhold truthful information while avoiding deception; and (d) whether it survives the objection that Japanese indirectness often preserves social harmony through strategic ambiguity rather than through belief-manipulating deception.

Finally, indicate which of these readings actually support the dismissal of white lies in cross-cultural argument, and which collapse into platitude, contradiction, or an unargued preference for one communicative style over another. I need this as a structured list I can take directly into writing, not a single defended interpretation.
