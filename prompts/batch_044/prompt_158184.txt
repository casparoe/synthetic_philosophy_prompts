I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of space and time, and we've spent the last few sessions on Newton's bucket and the standard replies to Leibnizian relationism. After class, I tried to extend the substantivalist reasoning into a more general argument, but it now seems to prove far more than I intended. Here is the structure as I have it:

(1) Relationism holds that all motion is relative: there is no fact about whether a body rotates except in relation to other bodies.
(2) In Newton's bucket, the water climbs the sides even if we imagine the bucket isolated from all other matter, which shows there is an absolute fact about rotation.
(3) So relationism cannot account for the physical effect in the bucket.
(4) Any dynamical law involving a second-order time derivative—whether Newton's second law, the geodesic equation, or the Schrödinger equation—presupposes an intrinsic temporal parameter for an isolated system.
(5) Therefore, relationism is incompatible not just with Newtonian mechanics but with any dynamics that requires intrinsic temporal evolution.
(6) If relationism fails for dynamics, then the relationist cannot use dynamical relations (e.g., causal interaction) to explain quantum correlations; instead, entangled particles must share properties indexed to a single absolute spatio-temporal framework.
(7) Consequently, violations of Bell-type inequalities are unremarkable: they are precisely what absolute-space indexing predicts.
(8) But this renders quantum non-locality trivial and makes the relational interpretation of quantum mechanics conceptually impossible.

The conclusion in (8) strikes me as an overreach—I'm in a metaphysics course, not trying to dissolve quantum foundations—yet I cannot locate the weak link. Is (4) illegitimately assuming that dynamics must be intrinsic rather than relational? Does (6) confuse relational dynamics with relational ontology? Or does the error lie back in (2), since the concavity of the water might be explained relationally to the bucket itself? If I must preserve (1) through (3), which step in the quantum extension should I reject, and what does that rejection cost me? I would also like to know whether there is a principled way to stop the argument at (3) without it bleeding into quantum mechanics at all.
