their track record:
1. full formal apology for the 1950s-80s forced assimilation program that sent indigenous children to residential schools. direct state agents ran it, survivors are alive, there was clear bodily and cultural harm, and affected communities asked for it.
2. refusal to apologize for 1890s colonial extraction and land seizure in the northern provinces. state agents were directly involved, but there are no living direct survivors; the harm was mainly economic and territorial; descendants requested an apology but the panel said the wrong was too remote.
3. full apology for 1970s involuntary sterilization of disabled women in state hospitals. direct medical harm, survivors alive, the doctors genuinely believed they were providing therapy; the apology was framed specifically as a breach of bodily autonomy, not as malicious intent.
4. refusal to apologize for 1940s strategic bombing of a civilian factory district during wartime. direct state military decision, survivors and families are alive, casualties were high, but the panel said this was tragic military necessity rather than a moral wrong.
5. 'statement of deep regret' but not a formal apology for the 1960s-70s ban on minority-language use in public schools. direct policy, survivors alive, there's continuing structural harm, but the panel said this was cultural assimilation rather than bodily harm and they didn't want to imply legal liability.

now the new case: there's a proposal for a full apology for the 1980s-90s child-welfare removals. state social workers took children from single-parent households (mostly mothers) into state orphanages based on assessments of 'moral unfitness'. direct state agents did it, survivors are alive and have organized, the harm was family separation and long-term psychological trauma. the policy was framed as child protection, not punishment, and many of the workers genuinely believed they were helping. there's also this intergenerational twist — the children who were removed later had their own children taken by the state at much higher rates, so some advocates argue the harm wasn't just direct but created a kind of self-replicating vulnerability.

what should the panel do if they want to be coherant? is the closest analog case 3 (sterilization — direct harm, well-meaning but autonomy-violating) or case 5 (language ban — direct cultural harm but framed as protection, so only regret)? or maybe case 1?
