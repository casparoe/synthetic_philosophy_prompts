I sit on the Methodology Review Panel of the Meridian Foundation, which funds projects that combine predictive modeling with qualitative institutional analysis. We are deadlocked over a proposal studying cascade failures in urban water infrastructure: the applicants apply epidemiological risk-factor heuristics—treating engineering variables and neighborhood governance variables as independent by default, then adding interaction terms only where data demands—to a dataset that pairs hydraulic failure logs with ethnographic accounts of local trust networks. One faction argues that this commits a category error: in interdisciplinary work, the presumption of independence must be formally earned through explicit structural justification, not adopted as a heuristic scaffold, because treating institutional and physical variables as separable by default systematically obscures the very dependencies the qualitative layer is meant to reveal. The opposing faction insists that heuristic independence is precisely the right epistemic posture for discovery; without formalizing a baseline independence assumption, there is no rigorous way to locate where the model breaks and where qualitative correction should enter, so requiring full ex ante formalization of dependence structures would paralyze innovative cross-disciplinary inference. The Foundation’s charter requires us to issue a binding recommendation—approve with conditions, reject, or approve with a mandatory revision protocol—within ten days, and the memo must be actionable by researchers who could respond to its reasoning rather than merely philosophical. Please draft the panel’s decision memo: state a concrete recommendation with an explicit timeline and conditions if any, provide the reasoning that could survive direct response from the applicants, and give an honest, serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the side that loses. Do not include bibliographies or citation lists; the memo should stand on conceptual argument alone.
