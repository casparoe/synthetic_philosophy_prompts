I am a doctoral student in philosophy of technology preparing an interview for my department’s student-paper colloquium series. Our guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends what she calls the negative-space thesis regarding responsibility gaps for autonomous systems: she holds that high-autonomy architectures do not merely diffuse accountability across the familiar distributed-agency network, but actively generate zones in which retrospective moral blame is structurally suspended—negative responsibility spaces—and that this suspension is normatively productive rather than defective, redirecting ethical labor away from individual attribution and toward iterative institutional design governance.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate the thesis. I want at least one question addressing each of the following, with a calibrated follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

First, the boundary conditions of a negative space: how does she distinguish a genuinely suspended responsibility zone from a merely opaque epistemic situation in which we do not yet know which human actor caused the harm? Follow up by pressing whether her distinction relies on an inaccessible fact about the system’s internal decision topology, and if so, how she avoids turning every unexplained failure into a negative space by default.

Second, the normative status of absence: she argues that treating the gap as productive avoids the mistake of forcing blame onto an inappropriate human node. How does she respond to the objection that this framing risks excusing negligent design—effectively converting a failure of accountability into a design success—without smuggling in a hidden agent who bears the cost? Follow up by asking whether her appeal to prospective structural accountability retains any genuinely moral, rather than purely administrative, force, especially when the audit mechanisms she proposes are staffed by the same institutions that built the system.

Third, the institutional translation: what does her framework require of legal liability regimes that depend on identifying a named defendant? Does she propose replacing tort-based attribution with recursive audit obligations, and if so, how does that mechanism avoid collapsing into standard risk-management bureaucracy? Follow up specifically on cases where an autonomous system’s decision is not merely unassigned but actively contrary to the values embedded in its design protocol—does her framework have resources to treat that as a failure of governance, or does it remain silent because blame has already been suspended?

Keep the tone rigorous and appropriate for a philosophy audience, avoid generic biographical or career questions, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
