I hold that judicial review by unelected courts cannot be justified by the standard democratic-precommitment analogy—citizens as Ulysses binding themselves to the constitutional mast—because democratic polities lack the diachronic personal-identity conditions required for any intrapersonal precommitment to carry normative force. Without a non-arbitrary criterion identifying the people across generations that parallels psychological-continuity conditions for individual agents, the binding party and the bound party are not the same subject, and original constitutional consent dissolves into mere historical causation rather than continuing authority. Call this the temporal-agency-fragmentation objection to judicial legitimacy. What would have to be true for this objection to fail? I want the arguments, institutional analogies, formal results, and conceptual distinctions that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are. Which predictions does this view make that rival defenses of review—pure proceduralist, epistemic-competence, or dialogical accounts—do not? What would a committed defender of temporal-agency fragmentation concede as genuinely damaging rather than merely awkward? Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, for instance by reconstructing the people as an institutional role or by weakening the identity conditions to mere legal continuity, and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a normative argument of this kind? Give an honest assessment of how exposed the objection actually is, distinguishing defeaters that strike at its core from those it can absorb, and say whether you think it risks anything substantive at all.
