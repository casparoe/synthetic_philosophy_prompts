Here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted: The ordinance is clearly an instance of legal moralism of the kind Devlin defended in The Enforcement of Morals. Devlin held that society possesses a collective right to preserve its shared morality, comparing private immorality to treason on the grounds that both erode the bonds necessary for social survival. The normative ecology framing updates this argument in a compelling way: family-related norms are interdependent, and removing the nuclear family as a keystone practice risks cascading failure in child-rearing stability, inheritance customs, and local reciprocity. This structural model answers Hart's empirical objection, because Hart demanded evidence that legal tolerance produces disintegration, and the ecology framework demonstrates precisely the causal mechanism through which such disintegration would occur. Regarding Hart's distinction between positive morality—what people happen to believe—and critical morality—what is rationally justified—the ordinance clearly appeals only to positive morality. The residents feel that non-traditional displays are wrong, but mere popular disapproval does not establish moral correctness. Still, Hart's harm principle is not as narrow as often assumed. He allows coercion not only to prevent tangible injury but also to protect vulnerable parties and to prevent serious offense. If the normative ecology is genuinely interdependent, then widespread public rejection of the nuclear family norm produces diffuse but real harm to children and community stability. Consequently, the ordinance can be justified within a Hartian framework once harm is interpreted ecologically, even though Devlin's direct moralism is not needed. The symbolic role of law in reinforcing shared values, which Hart acknowledges in other contexts, further supports this conclusion. The only unresolved question is proportionality, but philosophically the justification holds.
