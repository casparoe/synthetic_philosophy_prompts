You are a senior examiner in twentieth-century European philosophy and the philosophy of value, and you are designing the final assessment for an MA module that has covered logical positivism’s epistemology but has not yet examined its ethical dimensions in any depth. Before drafting anything, please spend a moment in conceptual brainstorming: identify precisely where Moritz Schlick’s distinction between external purpose (Zweck) and immanent meaning (Sinn) is most conceptually fragile—specifically, whether activities that are instrumentally burdensome, such as sustained moral obligation or long-term care, can genuinely possess Sinn without smuggling back in a hidden teleology, or whether Schlick’s positive claim that genuinely meaningful activity takes the form of play (Spiel) rather than work (Arbeit) collapses once unavoidable suffering is admitted into the picture.

Having done so, write a single 90-minute examination essay question (candidates should answer in roughly 1,500 words) on Schlick’s account of life’s meaning as developed in his ethical and eudaemonistic writings—particularly his argument that life is not an instrument for an external end and that meaningfulness resides in activity pursued for its own sake. The question must be unanswerable by mere summary: it should require candidates to reconstruct Schlick’s rejection of teleological interpretations, to apply the Zweck/Sinn distinction to a concrete case of burdensome moral duty (for example, the prolonged care of a severely disabled relative), and then to evaluate whether Schlick’s anticipated response—distinguishing the internal form or attitude of an activity from its instrumental cost—successfully preserves meaning without implicitly reintroducing purpose. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, page references, or citations; the assessment should be answerable from conceptual knowledge alone, and you should present Schlick’s terminology as paraphrased conceptual content rather than as verbatim quotations.

Provide a model answer of approximately 700 words that demonstrates exactly the five moves a first-class response must contain: (i) a precise reconstruction of Schlick’s argument that asking after life’s meaning as if it were a tool’s purpose is a category error; (ii) an explanation of why play rather than pleasure functions as the bearer of Sinn; (iii) a sustained objection drawn from burdensome obligation that appears to be Arbeit-like in every respect except its emotional cost; (iv) Schlick’s likely reply, separating the mode of engagement from the instrumental structure of the task; and (v) an independent critical assessment of whether that reply succeeds or whether it covertly appeals to the very teleology it rejects. Then supply a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, divided into five criteria worth 5 marks each; for every criterion, specify the precise analytical move required for full marks and the typical failure mode—such as conflating Schlick with hedonism, applying the distinction descriptively rather than evaluatively, or offering a conclusion without first having reconstructed the argument. Write in formal British English, maintain an academic register, and present all instructions as direct tasks rather than as meta-commentary.
