I'm a peer reviewer for an interdisciplinary history and sociology journal, and submissions increasingly invoke cultural values, religious doctrines, or ethical orientations as explanations for macro-level institutional shifts. The authors routinely conflate selecting a phenomenon as historically significant—value-relevance—with demonstrating that it was causally effective, and they rarely distinguish direct motivational causation from Weber's notion of elective affinity. I need a reusable evaluation procedure I can apply to any such claim in about fifteen minutes, without consulting primary texts or constructing a bibliography.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions, with each entry specifying: (a) what kind of evidence counts as a sufficient answer; (b) the exact point where a negative answer stops the evaluation with a specific verdict; and (c) the point where the question is irreducibly a judgment call that I, as reviewer, must explicitly sign off on rather than derive mechanically. The procedure must incorporate Weber's methodological distinctions—between adequacy at the level of meaning and adequacy at the level of causality, the reconstruction of subjective meaning, and the problem of explaining outcomes by reference to values—while remaining usable by someone without specialized Weber scholarship.

Include a decision branch that separates direct causal claims from elective-affinity claims, since the evidentiary burdens differ. Then apply the full procedure to two proposed arguments:

First, the claim that the Protestant work ethic functioned as a necessary causal factor in the emergence of modern rational capitalism in Western Europe, operating through the mediation of subjective economic rationality.

Second, the claim that the widespread adoption of post-materialist values during the 1970s caused the decline of industrial labor unions, operating through a reduced collective willingness to endure economic risk.

For each application, trace the answers the procedure produces, flag the step where judgment replaces procedure, and identify one result that a reviewer is likely to resist—whether because the procedure permits a causal claim that feels intuitively overstated, or rejects one that appears historically plausible.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which classes of cultural-causal arguments lie outside its scope, and which common objections—such as reductionism, functional explanation, or multicausality—it does not by itself resolve.
