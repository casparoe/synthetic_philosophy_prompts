I am developing an argument for an independent study that treats weakness of will—specifically the form of akrasia in which an agent judges that course X is best yet performs Y—as structurally rational rather than as a failure of rationality. On this arbitration view, hyperbolic discounting is not a cognitive bias that distorts intertemporal choice, but instead tracks the diminishing normative authority of temporally distant stages of the self. Because the present self and its future counterpart are not identical in the relevant moral-psychological sense, the steep discount curve measures normative distance rather than calculative error. What philosophers typically call "giving in to temptation" is therefore reconceived as the present self exercising a legitimate veto over commitments made by a past self whose preferences no longer bind it, with the discount rate serving as a weight on that veto. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position. Which empirical results about intertemporal choice, which conceptual distinctions between weakness of will and mere compulsion, and which commitments about diachronic personal identity would have to be false or misapplied for the arbitration model to collapse? I am particularly interested in extreme cases—such as life-threatening health decisions where hyperbolic discounting seems plainly destructive—because I want to know whether they can still be accommodated by the veto-weight thesis, or whether accommodating them shows that the view has insulated itself by redefining weakness out of existence. Please rank the potential defeaters: which would be fatal to the arbitration view, which would be merely awkward, and which ones defenders of this view would themselves concede as genuinely damaging rather than dismissible.
