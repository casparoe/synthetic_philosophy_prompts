I was arguing with my roommate about whether the agricultural revolution was actually a mistake, like some anthropolgists claim. I built this argument for my study group and now it feels suspicious because the conclusion is way too strong — it seems to wipe out all historical evaluation. Here are the steps:

(1) If hunter-gatherer societies had more leisure, better nutrition, and less hierarchy than early agrarian ones, then the switch to farming was a net welfare loss for the people who made it.

(2) If it was a net welfare loss and people weren't forced, then large groups of rational agents can collectively lock themselves into worse conditions for generations — which means collective decision-making (democracy, markets, norms) is systematically unreliable about long-term outcomes.

(3) But we generally think collective processes are somewhat reliable, otherwise democratic goverance wouldn't be defensible.

(4) So to avoid (2) we must say the switch wasn't a loss, which means people valued status or ritual more than leisure — so our "affluence" metric is anachronistic.

(5) But if our metrics are always wrong for past societies, then we can never call any historical transition a mistake or progress. That kills the original question and probably a lot of other history too.

Where does this go wrong? I'd rather keep (1) and (3) than (5). I think the flaw might be in (2) smuggling in an assumption about what counts as a collective mistake, but I'm not sure what's actually broken. What's the weakest link here and what should I defend if they push on it?
