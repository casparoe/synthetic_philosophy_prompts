Exam question: The separateness of persons objection holds that utilitarian aggregation wrongly treats harms and benefits across different people as if they were harms and benefits within a single life, failing to respect distinct centers of experience. Apply this objection to a fission case: one individual divides into two psychologically continuous successors, each suffering a minor harm, but the division prevents a catastrophic harm to the pre-division individual. Would aggregating the two successors’ losses be morally equivalent to an intrapersonal trade-off? In your answer, discuss Rawls’s analogy between justice among persons and rational prudence within one life, and say whether that analogy supports or undermines the anti-utilitarian position here.

Student answer: The separateness objection, which Rawls develops, claims that people are not interchangeable containers for utility, so it is unjust to harm one person in order to produce greater benefits for others. Rawls illustrates this with an analogy: just as a rational agent should not sacrifice one stage of her own life for a larger benefit to a different stage, society should not sacrifice one individual for the good of the group. In the fission scenario, however, the two successors share the original person’s psychological history and memories, so they are not genuinely separate people but rather temporal parts of one distributed biography. Therefore, weighing their combined minor harms against the avoided catastrophe is not an interpersonal aggregation but an intrapersonal one—like distributing costs and benefits across phases of a single continuing life. The utilitarian can thus defend the division: preventing the catastrophic loss to the original justifies the distributed minor losses to the successors. In fact, Rawls’s own analogy supports this conclusion, because if the successors are parts of one narrative identity, then sacrificing some parts for the welfare of the whole is exactly what rational prudence permits. So the separateness objection loses its force in this case.
