I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in cross-cultural ethics and comparative moral psychology, and I am not certain whether philosophy or the social sciences have a specific name for it. A commentator observes aggregate behavioral differences—Dutch directness in refusal versus Japanese indirectness mediated by context—and frames these as features of a "national communicative style." When confronted with internal diversity or individual counterexamples, the commentator immediately retreats to a statistical or historical gloss: the claim applies only to tendencies, not to every member. Yet in the very next step, the commentator relies on that same tendency to license predictions about specific encounters—to advise travelers, to design institutional protocols, or to interpret a particular interaction—without adjusting for base rates or individual evidence. I have seen the same structure applied to "German efficiency" or "Italian emotional expressiveness." The pattern is not quite stereotyping, since the universal individual claim is explicitly disavowed; not quite cultural essentialism, since no immutable nature is asserted; and not ordinary statistical reasoning, since the statistical frame is invoked defensively rather than used consistently for prediction. Does this oscillation between statistical retreat and practical essentialization have an established label? If not, please coin and define a term that distinguishes it precisely from stereotyping, essentialism, and rational base-rate use, with clear boundary conditions. Answer in one paragraph, not exceeding 200 words.
