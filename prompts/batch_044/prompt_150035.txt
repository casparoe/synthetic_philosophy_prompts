I have been trying to work out, on my own, why knowing something is supposed to be more valuable than merely having a true belief about it — the old puzzle about the guide who truly believes the way to Larissa versus the one who actually knows it, though I only came across the idea in a secondhand copy and have never studied philosophy formally. I discussed it with a close friend who also lacks academic training but enjoys reasoning through examples. She has formed clear opinions about whether knowledge is genuinely better than correct belief in various practical situations, and I want to see if her views are consistent.

Here are the judgments she has offered so far:
- If you must find your way back to an unfamiliar hotel through a city where streets may be blocked, she says it is clearly better to know the route than to hold a true belief by accident, because you need to adjust when conditions change.
- If you are deciding whether to carry an umbrella tomorrow based on the weather, she says a correct guess is just as valuable as meteorological knowledge; you either carry it or you do not, and nothing further depends on your understanding.
- If you are teaching a child how to build a safe fire in the woods, she insists that knowledge is superior, both because the child will ask questions and because you must adapt your instructions for wet wood, strong wind, or different terrain.
- If you are choosing a restaurant for a single dinner and your unexamined preference happens to match the best option, she sees no extra value in knowing the reviews; you eat the identical meal either way.

These four seem to fit her view, but I am unsure what principle unites them.

Now consider this new situation, which she has not yet considered. A lone traveler must cross a high mountain pass over several days. The snow conditions, visibility, and safe paths shift constantly, so she must adapt her route repeatedly. She will encounter no other people during the journey, she will never need to explain her choices to anyone, and once she reaches the valley there is no further use for the knowledge. One traveler knows alpine navigation and snow science; the other holds a true belief about the correct path, formed entirely from a misremembered story about a different mountain, yet happens to choose correctly at each stage by luck.

What should my friend say? Is knowledge still more valuable here, or is true belief equally good? More specifically, I am asking you to do the following:

First, identify at least two different rules that could consistently explain all four of her earlier judgments. Second, say what each rule predicts about the lone traveler. Third, give your best guess as to which verdict she would actually reach, with your reasoning. Fourth, tell me what further case — one that changes only whether she must justify her choices to someone else, or only whether the environment remains fixed — would best reveal which rule she is actually running.
