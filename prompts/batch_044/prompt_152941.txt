Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 12 upper-level philosophy students on relational conceptions of personhood, centered specifically on the tension within ubuntu-style frameworks between the ontological claim that personhood is constituted through reciprocal relation and the normative claim that such relations generate obligations of care. The scenario should place students in an asymmetric, institutionally mediated caregiving situation—specifically, a sole long-term caregiver who is the primary relational anchor for an adult with severe cognitive disability, where state compensation and institutional scheduling partially structure but do not replace the personal bond, and where a scarce resource (for example, limited respite hours or a single available assistive-technology allocation) must be distributed among competing claimants. Assign concrete roles—such as the caregiver, an institutional case-manager, a family member arguing for the disabled person's independent entitlement, and an advocate arguing that removing the caregiver dissolves the very personhood being protected—together with deliberation rules that prevent straightforward compromise (e.g., each role must defend a principle, not a preference, and consensus requires unanimous agreement on the grounds for allocation, not merely on the outcome). Structure the session so that participants first map the relationships, then allocate the resource under time pressure, then discover that treating the disabled person as a bounded individual with direct rights to the resource undermines the relational constitution of their identity, while treating them as entirely constituted by the caregiver's presence eliminates their independent claim and risks justifying exclusion if the relationship falters. The debrief should map each deliberation phase onto either the constitutive or the normative reading of relational personhood, identify the exact moment when participants felt the conflict rather than merely understood it, and be explicit about which feature of classic ubuntu—its emphasis on mutual, community-embedded reciprocity—the activity necessarily distorts because the scenario is dyadic, asymmetric, and state-mediated. Include a timeline with checkpoints, a payoff or scoring mechanism that makes institutional efficiency and relational preservation incommensurable rather than tradeable on a single scale, an honest note on what will predictably go wrong in the first run (likely premature resolution through liberal rights-language) and how the facilitator should intervene without lecturing, and a frank admission of which ontological feature of relational personhood—the idea that the self is literally co-constituted rather than merely supported—this staged scenario can make visceral but cannot fully reproduce, leaving the facilitator to supply that conceptual gap verbally. Do not name the view, cite specific philosophers, or provide a bibliography in the prompt itself; the participants should encounter the problem through the rules and roles before any term is introduced.
