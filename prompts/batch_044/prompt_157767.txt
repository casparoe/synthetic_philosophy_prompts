In my philosophy of science seminar we have been examining demarcation problems, and I keep returning to a pattern in institutional practice that strikes me as philosophically loaded. Peer-reviewed history journals and university departments systematically reject chronological revisionism that relies on statistical correlations between dynastic timelines or on retrocalculated astronomical data—exemplified by the New Chronology proposed by the Russian mathematician Anatoly Fomenko, which treats large portions of ancient and medieval history as duplicated or misdated—while they routinely accept chronological adjustments based on ambiguous textual transmission, contested radiometric calibration, or isolated archaeological discoveries. Crucially, the rejection is rarely articulated as a mathematical error; instead, gatekeepers treat such statistical reconstructions as falling outside the disciplinary boundaries of history altogether. What implicit theory of historical knowledge does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the epistemology that licenses privileging hermeneutic and material-source continuity over predictive statistical coherence, and determine whether that theory treats historical facts as socially constructed narratives, as causally embedded events, or as something else. Is the practice internally consistent, given that mainstream historiography itself employs probabilistic weighing of competing textual accounts? Furthermore, if the discipline adopted a straightforwardly falsificationist or probabilistic demarcation criterion—evaluating chronological claims solely by empirical predictive success and statistical robustness—what concrete institutional changes would be required in peer review norms, museum labeling protocols, or textbook authorization procedures?
