I'm trying to identify a specific conceptual move that recurs in discussions linking Gödel's incompleteness results to theories of mind, particularly within the framework popularized in Douglas Hofstadter's writings on self-reference and strange loops. The pattern begins with Gödel's arithmetic of self-reference: the construction of a formal statement, via Gödel numbering, that effectively asserts its own unprovability within the system in which it is expressed. From there, the move extends this syntactic structure into an ontological claim. The reasoning proceeds that if a formal system can encode representations of itself and derive conclusions about its own limits, then any physical system of sufficient complexity—specifically a brain governed by deterministic or algorithmic processes—should analogously generate an internal model of itself. This internal model is then identified not merely with self-knowledge or introspective access, but with the emergence of subjective consciousness, free will, or an irreducible first-person perspective that cannot be captured by the system's underlying rules. I encounter variants of this in Roger Penrose's arguments that human cognition involves non-computable processes lying beyond formal systems, in interpretations of Hofstadter's hierarchical self-modeling where awareness arises from a recursive loop of self-reference, and in popular-science accounts that summarize Gödel's result as demonstrating that no system can fully understand itself and then apply this conclusion directly to human minds. In each case, the argument slides from a precise logical phenomenon—a symbol string referring to its own derivability properties—to a broad phenomenological conclusion about what it is like to be a self-aware subject.

Does this particular analogical extension have an established name within philosophy of mind or logic? More specifically, is there terminology that distinguishes this ontological leap from more restrained applications of the Gödelian analogy, such as those confined to epistemic limitations, cognitive modeling, or the limits of mechanistic explanation? I am less interested in a bare label than in understanding whether philosophers have carved out this exact failure mode or conceptual bridge—how its boundaries are drawn, what distinguishes it from legitimate uses of formal self-reference in explaining cognition, and which nearest related concepts apply if it remains unnamed. A concrete analysis of where the analogy holds and where it breaks, perhaps with reference to specific argumentative steps in the Penrose-style or Hofstadter-style versions, would be especially helpful.
