I sit on the university's neuroethics review panel and we need to circulate a decision memo by thursday about a pilot study that feels philosophically loaded in a way the protocol itself doesn't admit. The researchers want to induce what they're calling a 'null-interface state' — they've built on the view that consciousness is basically a user-illusion cooked up by predictive self-modeling, and they've designed a real-time neurofeedback loop plus a VR suppression task that they claim can temporarily offline that interface without knocking out lower-level cognition. They want healthy volunteers to sit through this state and then respond to moral-dilemma vignettes and self-attribution tasks, testing whether moral judgement and agency-tracking survive when the user's conscious model is suspended. Half our panel treats this as standard cognitive manipulation with proper debriefing; the other half argues that if the protocol genuinely targets the substrate of experienced agency, then informed consent is structurally inadequate — participants can't consent to the temporary absence of the very framework they use to understand harm — and that approving it risks treating consciousness like a switch we can flip for science.

Please draft the panel's formal opinion recommending either approval, rejection, or approval with strict conditions, with clear reasoning that includes an honest treatment of the strongest point from the side that loses. Write it in British English, start with the bottom line in the very first sentence with no preamble and no restating of the question, and keep the tone suitable for an institutional memo rather than a blog post. I don't want a bibliography or reading list — just the argument we can actually vote on. (I was chatting with a friend about Bongard problems over coffee and she reckons induction from sparse examples is overhyped, but that's probably not relevent here — though it made me wonder if we're overinducing risk from this single protocol.)
