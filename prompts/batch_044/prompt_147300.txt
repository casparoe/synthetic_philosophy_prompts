A philosopher of cognitive science applies the taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures to classify systems by their primary adaptive architecture. Her prior verdicts are these: she calls bacteria navigating chemical gradients Darwinian, since their responses are purely hardwired by selection with no learning; she calls rats in operant-conditioning chambers Skinnerian, since behavior is shaped by direct environmental reinforcement without internal modeling; she calls New Caledonian crows that fashion hook-tools through apparent mental planning Popperian, since they test hypotheses in an internal representational space before risking physical failure; she calls human children acquiring language and cultural norms Gregorian, since they download complex symbolic structures through embodied social transmission rather than inventing them individually; and she calls social-insect colonies that construct nests through pheromone feedback Skinnerian, insisting that collective structure is emergent reinforcement without collective internal simulation or symbolic culture.

Consider now a new system: a fleet of autonomous underwater drones that map coral reefs. Each unit is hardwired with basic buoyancy and obstacle sensors. Through direct experience, the drones learn efficient search patterns via energy-collection rewards. Before each dive, every drone runs an internal predictive simulation of currents and reef geometry, selecting a virtual trajectory before committing to physical movement. The drones never interact socially with one another; they only receive the one-way broadcasts.
