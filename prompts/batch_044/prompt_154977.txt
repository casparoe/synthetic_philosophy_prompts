During a philosophy elective discussion on non-Western epistemologies, two of my classmates—Nina and Omar—got into a sustained disagreement over whether the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda (the view that reality is complex and no single standpoint captures it entirely) can be applied to evaluate a specific claim: that within the next fifty years artificial intelligence will become the primary moral arbiter in criminal sentencing.

Nina argued that anekāntavāda requires assessing the claim through the saptabhaṅgī, or seven-fold scheme of conditional predication. From the standpoint of predictive modeling, she said, the claim is true; from the standpoint of moral agency, which requires embodied suffering and compassion, it is false; and from the standpoint of technological trajectory considered apart from ethical constraints, it is both true and false in different respects. She insisted this is not contradiction but syādvāda—the conditional "perhaps"—because Jain metaphysics distinguishes dravya (the enduring substance) from paryāya (the transient modes or attributes). Thus, AI might possess the functional substance of computation without possessing the modal quality of moral authority.

Omar replied that Nina is smuggling relativism into metaphysical language. He said that if every assertion must be qualified by an implicit "from some standpoint," then there is no fact of the matter about whether AI will serve as a moral arbiter, which renders the claim meaningless rather than complex. He argued that the dravya-paryāya distinction operates only within a specifically Jain ontology of jīva (soul) and ajīva (non-soul), and that extending it to a modern technological prediction is an illegitimate analogical leap. Furthermore, he claimed that if syādvāda permits contradictory predicates to coexist as long as they attach to different standpoints, then the principle of non-contradiction collapses, making rational debate—including their own—impossible.

Nina countered that Omar assumes a binary, Aristotelian logic of substance and accident that anekāntavāda explicitly rejects. She said the seven-fold scheme does not deny contradiction within a single standpoint; rather, it denies that any one standpoint is exhaustive. She also noted that Jain ethics demands decisive action guided by ahiṃsā (non-violence), so anekāntavāda does not produce paralysis but epistemic humility: one can act decisively while recognizing the partiality of one's view.

Omar's final move was pragmatic: if both he and Nina can agree that, for now, human judges should retain authority, but they ground that agreement in incompatible metaphysics—his in single-truth realism, hers in a many-sided ontology—then anekāntavāda adds nothing but decorative vocabulary.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: evaluate Nina's initial application of saptabhaṅgī, Omar's charge of relativism, Nina's defense using dravya and paryāya, Omar's claim about illegitimate ontological extension, Nina's counter about binary logic, and Omar's final pragmatic challenge. For each step, indicate whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether there is a hidden assumption about what "moral arbiter" means that neither has questioned.
