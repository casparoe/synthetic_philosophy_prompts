I'm working through David Hume's treatment of liberty and necessity in his 1748 Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, in the section where he distinguishes liberty of spontaneity—acting according to one's own will without external constraint—from liberty of indifference, which he rejects as an incoherent, uncaused power of choice. He appears to argue that moral responsibility attaches only to the first: if an action flows from my own character and is not compelled by outside force, then I am responsible for it, regardless of whether I could metaphysically have acted otherwise.

I'm trying to understand whether this framework survives modern Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. Specifically, I wonder whether Hume's reduction of liberty to spontaneity overlooks a third condition—call it sourcehood liberty—that requires not merely that I act on my own will, but that I am the actual origin or source of that will's determination, rather than being subtly guided by an external controller who leaves my spontaneous choice intact. If Frankfurt is right that an agent can be fully spontaneous and yet not the true source of his action, does Hume's compatibilism collapse, or can he absorb the point by insisting that responsibility tracks only the absence of external constraint at the moment of action? And does his empiricist account of causation even allow him to distinguish being the source of one's will from being one link in a causal chain?

Could you help me understand how Hume's distinction applies here? I am especially interested in whether the modern debate reveals that Hume was answering a different question—about constraint versus causation—while Frankfurt is asking about authorship versus manipulation—and, if so, whether that means part of the disagreement is verbal rather than substantive.
