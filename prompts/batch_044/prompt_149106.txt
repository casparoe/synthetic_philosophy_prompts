I am running an upper-level aesthetics seminar—twelve graduate students, ninety minutes, no assigned reading beforehand— and I want a simulation that makes them feel the instability at the heart of institutional theories of art without hearing the problem named until they have lived it. To that end, I am working with a pedagogical variant I call the “recursive exclusion problem”: the idea that if art-status is conferred by an institutional framework rather than by intrinsic perceptual properties, then the framework could in principle adopt a hidden rule that retroactively revokes status from objects previously endorsed, not because those objects changed, but because the gatekeepers’ criteria of endorsement were silently revised. Design a structured three-round deliberation in which groups of four act as successive “curatorial boards,” classifying the same set of object descriptions (presented on identical index cards that vary only in provenance history) under shifting rules they do not see in full until the debrief.

Specify the physical layout, the rules for each round, and a non-monetary payoff scheme that makes the stakes felt: for example, accumulated “cultural capital” points that determine which board gets to place one real object they have brought into a mock gallery at the end. In round one, boards classify solely by maker intent; in round two, they must reclassify using only institutional endorsement, ignoring intent; in round three, they apply an unrevealed “exclusion clause” that denies art-status to any object endorsed in round two, regardless of properties or history. The descriptions should be perceptually identical across rounds so that participants notice they are reversing judgments on the same material. The sequence should be arranged so that the boards construct the reversal pattern themselves, defend it in brief oral justifications, and only then learn that the hidden clause was embedded in the framework all along.

Then provide the debrief: map round one onto a classic institutional-acceptance view, round two onto an artworld conception that emphasizes historical embedding and gatekeeping, and round three onto my pedagogical “recursive exclusion” variant, showing precisely where in each round participants resisted the shift and what that resistance cost them in points or group credibility. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely that boards will smuggle perceptual or aesthetic criteria back into round two despite instructions, or will treat the hidden clause in round three as unfair trickery rather than as a structural feature of the framework—and how to handle each failure without breaking the revelation. Be explicit about which feature of real institutional theories this simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the slow, historically sedimented, and publicly accountable nature of actual art-world institutions—and instruct me exactly how to supply that missing element in the closing discussion so the activity does not leave students thinking that art institutions change arbitrarily overnight. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the activity must be fully runnable without external sources.
