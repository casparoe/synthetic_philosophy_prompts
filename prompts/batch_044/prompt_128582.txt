I'm taking a seminar on interpretation and formal systems, and we've been working through Hofstadter's discussion in Gödel, Escher, Bach about the location of meaning—specifically his distinction among frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages. As I understand the framework, a symbol sequence can carry one meaning at the level of its literal form (the outer message), another at the level of the interpretive context that makes it readable (the frame message), and yet another at a self-referential level where the message refers back to its own encoding conditions (the inner message). The trouble I'm running into is that the inner message seems to depend on recognizing the frame, but if the frame is itself part of the message being interpreted, then grasping the inner meaning requires already having grasped it. This looks like a vicious circularity rather than a benign loop.

I'm trying to apply this to Gödel's first incompleteness theorem. Gödel constructs a formula in a formal system that effectively says of itself that it is unprovable. On Hofstadter's model, that formula seems to operate simultaneously as a syntactic object—part of the frame of the system—and as a semantic claim about that very frame. If meaning is genuinely distributed across these layers, then the formula shouldn't have a stable location: it should oscillate endlessly between being an inner message (about provability) and a frame message (a symbol in the calculus). Yet Gödel's proof treats the statement as having a single, determinate interpretation—either true or false—within the system. So is there a hidden assumption in Gödel that collapses Hofstadter's layers? Or have I misunderstood the relationship between frame and inner message, perhaps by treating their interdependence as circular in a way that a strange loop avoids?
