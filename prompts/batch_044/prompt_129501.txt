A joint Scandinavian-American workplace-culture board has issued rulings on whether institutions should celebrate or suppress exceptional individual achievement. Its precedents: it approved named cash bonuses for sales staff who exceeded targets by 300 percent; it rejected naming a university library after its largest donor, insisting on collective attribution to staff and students; it approved honoring the originator of a cooperative farming method, but only after her family donated the underlying patent to the commons; it rejected profiling a junior engineer whose innovation reduced energy use by 40 percent, citing disruption to team cohesion; it approved a public monument to an immigrant shipping entrepreneur that explicitly emphasized her personal risk-taking; and it rejected displaying anonymized, percentile-ranked exam scores in a vocational school.

The board now faces a proposal from a publicly funded research institute: publish an annual "breakthrough index" listing the top 5 percent of labs by patent citation impact, but require each listed lab to contribute 10 percent of any associated prize money to a shared equipment fund for lower-performing labs, and prohibit naming any individual researcher in the citation.

Predict the board's ruling if it seeks consistency with its own record. Identify the closest analog among its precedents, then lay out the competing principles—meritocratic celebration, Jantelagen-style leveling, tall-poppy suppression, and conditional or instrumental recognition—that could jointly explain its verdicts. Say which principle best predicts its decision on the index proposal, with reasons, and propose one further case that would best distinguish between the two leading explanations.
