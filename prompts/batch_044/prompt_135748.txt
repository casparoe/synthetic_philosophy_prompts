Please describe the most important implications of Wittgenstein's view that religious belief is embedded in a form of life and functions grammatically differently from empirical belief—so that doctrines such as the efficacy of prayer or the authority of revelation are not falsifiable hypotheses but framework commitments that make certain practices intelligible—for how a secular university chaplaincy should structure interfaith dialogue among students who hold core claims as non-negotiable. The office seeks to avoid treating these commitments as competing scientific theories on one hand, or as merely subjective preferences on the other. What are the most important implications of this framework for (1) the ground rules and facilitation methods appropriate to such dialogue, (2) whether participants can legitimately demand rational justification for one another's beliefs without treating them as empirical claims, and (3) whether institutional neutrality requires the university to abandon truth-evaluative language altogether or instead permits forms of assessment internal to each religious practice? In particular, address whether this Wittgensteinian approach collapses into a strong incommensurability thesis that makes genuine disagreement impossible, and what that would mean for the chaplaincy's mandate to foster mutual understanding rather than mere coexistence.
