A friend who reads philosophy blogs summarized the old Chinese dialogue about the white horse for me as follows. He said that Gongsun Long, associated with the School of Names, argued that a white horse is not a horse because the term "horse" refers only to the animal's shape or form, while "white horse" refers to both that form and the color white. Because these two descriptions cover different sets of qualities, they name different things. He added that the School of Names used such arguments primarily to expose contradictions embedded in ordinary language and to show that rigorous logic should replace everyday common sense. More specifically, he claimed they endorsed a broad principle: attaching any descriptive modifier to a noun breaks the original category membership, which is why "tall man" is not truly a man and "red apple" is not truly an apple. He concluded that this is essentially an ancient Chinese anticipation of the modern linguistic phenomenon in which compound descriptions fail to inherit the properties of their head term—like a toy gun not being a gun.

Something in this account feels partly accurate yet also stretched. I have been trying to think through whether this connects to what I call "sortal leakage"—situations where adding a qualifier seems to cancel membership in the original category, as with wooden tables and wood, or fake diamonds and diamonds. I am unsure whether framing Gongsun Long through this lens illuminates his argument or imports modern ideas that do not belong there.

Is my friend's summary a fair representation of the white horse dialogue and of the School of Names? Please distinguish between what is accurate, what is an overstatement or subtle distortion, and whether comparing the argument to modern cases of sortal leakage is philosophically productive or anachronistic.
