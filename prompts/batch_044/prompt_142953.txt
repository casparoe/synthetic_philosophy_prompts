Write an essay on the nature of emotions, focusing on the dispute between judgment-based, perceptual, and embodied-feeling accounts. Center your analysis on a conceptual test case: imagine a subject who exhibits a stable, object-directed affective orientation—experiencing a particular geometric configuration as profoundly "wrong" or "disordered"—yet who reports no propositional belief about the configuration's value, shows no measurable physiological change, and denies any qualitative bodily sensation beyond the bare sense of orientation. Examine whether this scenario of "silent affective valence" undermines cognitive-judgment theories (which require articulable evaluation) and classical embodied theories (which require interoceptive or physiological markers), or whether a perceptual account can absorb it by treating the orientation as a direct registration of an evaluative property without mediation by belief or bodily feeling. Weigh the main objections to each accommodation, including the worry that silent valence collapses into mood or aesthetic response rather than genuine emotion. Reach and defend a specific conclusion about which framework best survives the pressure of this case, or whether it demonstrates that our tripartite taxonomy needs revision.
