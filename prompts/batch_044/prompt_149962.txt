The special composition question—under what conditions parts compose a further whole?—has divided nihilists, universalists, and restrictivists for decades, yet the literature cycles through the same formal arguments about vagueness, identity conditions, and ontological parsimony without anyone changing position. I suspect the stalemate is not genuinely about existence but about whether compositional boundaries must correspond to causal or explanatory joints: restrictivists seem to treat “object” as marking real interaction thresholds, while universalists treat such boundaries as ideologically dispensable. Is this a substantive ontological disagreement masked as one about explanation, a verbal dispute over what “composition” requires, or two projects—one metaphysical, one methodological—running under a single heading? Diagnose the impasse by engaging what each camp actually says about contact, life, and vagueness, and tell me whether a sharp distinction between ontological commitment and explanatory framing could break it, or whether the disagreement is ultimately anchored in pre-theoretical intuitions about unity that no formal result can overturn.
