I am a retired physicist who, after leaving active laboratory research, have begun attending a philosophy seminar for non-specialists hosted by my university's continuing-education program. We have spent the past several weeks on Enlightenment thinkers who refused to separate natural science from ethics, and I have become particularly absorbed by Émilie du Châtelet's reflections on happiness—not merely as a state of pleasure, but, I think, as a dynamic equilibrium analogous to the mechanical stability of a system held in tension. In her Discourse on Happiness she treats passions such as ambition, the love of study, and sociability not as obstacles to be eliminated but as opposing forces that must be sustained in a precise balance; happiness, on this reading, is not the absence of disturbance but a stable configuration in which no single drive dominates and in which social prejudice functions less like an internal component and more like an external perturbation that destabilizes the equilibrium from without. I want to stage an in-person session—about eight to ten participants, mostly fellow retirees and a few graduate students, meeting for a single ninety-minute workshop—in which they physically or structurally encounter this idea before anyone names it.

Please design a structured classroom game or simulation—using assigned roles, physical props, or constrained negotiation rather than lecture—that makes the group discover for themselves that suppressing one competing drive (for instance, ambition or the need for solitude) causes the collective system to collapse, and that only a specific, maintained tension produces sustained coordination. The rules should be arranged so that participants initially try to resolve all conflict into harmony, fail in a visible way, and then find that sustaining opposition is the only path to stability. Include: the exact timeline and phases within the ninety minutes; the materials or role cards needed; a non-monetary payoff scheme that gives tangible, immediate feedback; the debrief that maps each failure and success onto du Châtelet's conceptual elements (passions as forces, prejudice as external perturbation, equilibrium as happiness); an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how to recover; and a frank admission of which normative feature of her view—perhaps its gendered social context or its specific claim about intellectual freedom—this mechanical analogy cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not ask participants to consult texts or provide citations; the activity should work entirely from embodied reasoning and interaction.
