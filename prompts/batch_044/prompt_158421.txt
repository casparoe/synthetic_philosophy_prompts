I'm presenting a 30-minute paper at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Post-Work Futures." My thesis is that debates about automation and work are stuck between instrumental and dignity-based accounts, both of which miss work's primary social function: it renders individuals institutionally legible as contributors rather than mere claimants. I defend a recognitional-structural account: automation's deepest harm is not task displacement but "occluded contribution," where algorithmic interfaces hide human labor so thoroughly that the recognitional loop—effort, visibility, institutional acknowledgment—breaks even when work technically continues. I apply this to platform-mediated hidden labor but argue the mechanism generalizes beyond platforms.

They will not be hostile, but they will probe for empirical emptiness, conceptual vagueness, and policy uselessness.
