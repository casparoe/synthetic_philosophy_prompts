In debates over the ethical weight of panpsychism, critics and defenders often invoke an analogy to digital image formation: each fundamental physical excitation is treated as a sub-threshold "pixel" of valence—barely positive or barely negative—and macroscopic conscious experience is treated as the high-contrast "image" that results when those pixels are arranged according to some compositional rule. Defenders use the picture to suggest continuity between quantum fields and ethics: harming a human brain is like corrupting a bright, coherent image, while ordinary physical interactions only nudge dark background pixels and carry negligible moral weight. Critics use the same analogy to argue paralysis: if every cubic centimeter contains astronomical numbers of micro-valences, then any action alters an incalculable moral ledger, rendering everyday ethics impossible. Audit this analogy thoroughly. First, lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the pixel, the valence value, the rendering algorithm, the display, and the observer who reads moral significance into the pattern. Second, identify the disanalogies: do quantum fields have discrete, addressable "pixels" with individual valence? Is there any known compositional rule analogous to image-rendering that aggregates micro-experience into macro-experience? Does the analogy smuggle in representational, computational, or intentional properties that physical fields lack? Third, separate the disanalogies that undermine the ethical argument—for instance, assumptions about linear additivity of suffering, or about the moral relevance of intensity versus number—from those that are philosophically inert. Fourth, evaluate whether the conclusion—that panpsychism either integrates smoothly with ordinary ethics or collapses it into background noise—survives once these hidden commitments are exposed. Finally, indicate whether a different analogy, perhaps to neural-network hidden-layer activations or to economic aggregation of preferences, would carry the argument more cleanly without importing emergent computation.
