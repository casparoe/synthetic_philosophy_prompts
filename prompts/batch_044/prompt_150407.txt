Compare the explanatory weight that Yuval Noah Harari assigns to durable shared fictions—myths that outlive individual believers and thereby stabilize large-scale cooperation—with the weight that David Graeber and David Wengrow assign to seasonal, reversible hierarchies that never harden into permanent structures. Focus on whether either framework can account for complex, multi-generational infrastructure projects in a society that lacks both a persistent symbolic substrate and a coercive enforcement apparatus. To test this, construct your analysis around an invented but conceptually rigorous case: a Neolithic river-basin aggregation—call it Kharum—where populations seasonally coalesce into large labor pools to maintain irrigation canals, then disperse completely, leaving behind no permanent settlement, no grave goods indicating rank, and no oral tradition attributing the works to ancestral command or divine mandate. First, does Harari's framework necessarily predict that such sustained coordination requires a durable myth that survives dispersal and continues to "push the substrate around"—that is, to causally organize material labor across temporal and spatial gaps? Second, does Graeber and Wengrow's emphasis on playful, temporary kingship and seasonal assembly adequately explain how technical knowledge is transmitted across dispersals if there is no lasting institution to preserve it, or does their model implicitly depend on a substrate-pushing symbol after all? Third, identify one shared hidden premise in both grand narratives regarding the relationship between symbolic representation and material persistence, and argue whether the Kharum case confirms that premise, undermines it, or reveals that both theories are addressing different phenomena under the same name.
