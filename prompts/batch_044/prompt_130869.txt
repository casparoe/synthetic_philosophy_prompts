I'm preparing remarks for a philosophy-of-history seminar and want a consistency audit before I present. My working view is this: (1) collective memory possesses what I call mnemonic sovereignty over narratives that are identity-constitutive for a community, meaning that in conflicts with academic historiography over such narratives, the community's account has normative priority; (2) for conflicts that are purely chronological or causal and do not bear on present practices of recognition and obligation, the archival record should correct the memory, and a community's refusal counts as an epistemic failure; (3) whether a fact is identity-constitutive is determined functionally—by its role in sustaining mutual recognition—not metaphysically, though (4) material traces still provide objective falsification conditions for functional claims; (5) however, when the archive itself was produced by institutions that systematically harmed the community, deference to the record reproduces epistemic injustice and (2) is suspended. I'm worried that (1) and (5) make (2) unstable, that (3) undermines the sovereignty claimed in (1) by making it contingent on present function, and that (4) conflicts with (5)'s skepticism about archival neutrality. Which pairs are genuinely in tension versus only apparently so? Rank the tensions by severity, indicate the least costly revision for each, and tell me which single change to my package would resolve the most conflicts without abandoning the core motivation—protecting communities from archival domination—behind (1) and (5).
