As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying moral philosophy in earnest, I have been working through Adam Smith’s account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, and I have constructed an argument that seems to show his framework cannot do the work he asks of it. I lay out the steps below.

(1) For Smith, the impartial spectator is not an innate rational faculty but an internalized model built from the actual sympathetic responses of the people who have shaped one’s moral environment.

(2) In this respect, the spectator operates like a measuring instrument calibrated against local conditions: it can register only those sentiments that have actually been expressed, observed, and internalized within its calibration range.

(3) Therefore, if a culture uniformly lacks any sympathetic response to a particular kind of harm—perhaps because that harm is structurally invisible to its members—the calibrated spectator will have no disapproving sentiment to register.

(4) Call such harm “structurally invisible harm”: suffering that is real but distributed so diffusely or distantly that no actual observer in the culture has ever felt a sympathetic reaction to it.

(5) Because the spectator draws only on available calibrated sentiments, he will approve of practices that cause structurally invisible harm, and according to Smith, my own moral judgment ought to align with his approval.

(6) It follows that, within any given culture, Smith’s framework supplies no internal mechanism for recognizing moral errors that the culture itself has not already learned to feel.

(7) Yet Smith clearly holds that mercantilist policies and slavery are wrong, despite being widely accepted in his own society, and he appeals to the impartial spectator precisely as a corrective to prevailing sentiment.

(8) So we face a dilemma: either the spectator can access sentiments outside the local calibration, in which case premise (2) mischaracterizes sympathy, or he cannot, in which case premise (7) is unsupported and Smith’s philosophy collapses into a form of moral sociology incapable of reform.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd—if correct, it would make Smith’s moral philosophy useless for criticizing one’s own culture. But I cannot locate the exact slip. Is the error in treating sympathy as purely environmental calibration in step (2)? Does step (5) illegitimately move from the absence of a felt sentiment to the absence of a judgment? Or is the tension real, and must we surrender either (1) or (7)? I would most prefer to preserve (1) and (7) intact. Which premise should I reject, and what does that rejection cost for the coherence of Smith’s system and for the possibility of moral progress?
