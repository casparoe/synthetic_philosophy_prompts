I serve on my university's Office of Research Integrity disciplinary panel, and we must render a decision by the end of the month in a case that forces us to confront whether there is a morally and institutionally significant difference between lying and misleading. The respondent is a behavioral-science faculty member who designed informed-consent materials for a study on risk-taking under social stress. The materials contain only technically true sentences—e.g., "Participants may experience discomfort and may discontinue at any time"—but employ typographic hierarchy, selective omission, and pragmatic ordering to suppress the fact that the "discomfort" involves an acute simulated-social-exclusion protocol with documented psychological sequelae. The respondent's defense, submitted in writing, distinguishes sharply between propositional falsehood (lying) and strategic framing that generates false belief through Gricean implicature and contextual suppression; she argues that our code prohibits "falsification, fabrication, and plagiarism," not "misleading presentation," and that extending sanctions to framing collapses an important moral distinction and risks chilling legitimate methodological discretion. The complainant contends that in the institutional context of informed consent, deliberately engineered false belief is functionally equivalent to lying and should be sanctioned identically.

Please draft our panel's formal decision memo addressed to the faculty senate. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve a reprimand with an expanded interpretation, clear the respondent with a policy amendment, or adopt an intermediate conditional sanction—together with a specific implementation timeline (e.g., 30, 60, or 90 days). The reasoning must treat the respondent's speech-act distinction seriously, include an honest account of the strongest argument for the position you reject, and avoid collapsing the distinction into a mere semantic quibble.

Please structure the response exactly as follows:

1. Executive Summary (approximately 150 words): the bottom-line recommendation and timeline.
2. The Case and the Philosophical Stakes (approximately 200 words): the concrete facts and how they instantiate the lying-versus-misleading problem.
3. The Case for the Recommended Course (approximately 250 words): the reasoning, including engagement with the respondent's framing defense.
4. The Strongest Consideration for the Losing Side (approximately 200 words): an honest, non-straw-man presentation of why the other position might be right.
5. Implementation and Conditions (approximately 150 words): the concrete steps, deadlines, and any conditions attached to the recommendation.
