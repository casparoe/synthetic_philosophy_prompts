I am delivering the retirement toast for my colleague Dr. Miriam Chen at her university’s faculty dinner this Friday. The room is split: roughly half are philosophers, economists, and decision theorists who will catch every allusion to rational-choice formalism; the other half are family members, former students from other disciplines, and administrators who have never heard of backward induction. I want the toast—approximately five minutes when spoken—to be built structurally around the backward induction paradox and the centipede game, not as decorative metaphor but as the actual frame that determines what I can and cannot say about her forty-year career.

Here is the constraint that should shape the speech. Miriam spent her career constructing interdisciplinary institutions—joint programs, shared governance structures, cross-departmental grants—each one requiring her to cooperate one more step with partners who could, at any node, defect and capture the larger payoff. The paradox is that, if every participant is perfectly rational and this is common knowledge, cooperation should unravel from the endpoint backward, and no institution should survive past the first proposal. Yet she built them, and they persisted. The speech must honor that achievement without pretending she was “irrational” in any naive sense, and without misrepresenting to the experts what backward induction actually proves. It should also avoid the cliché that “trust” or “goodwill” simply dissolves the paradox; the experts will wince, and the family will hear something false.

To make this genuinely difficult rather than a standard game-theory anecdote, inject the following conceptual twist: treat her institutional work not as a repeated centipede game with a fixed, exogenous endpoint, but as a sequence in which the endpoint itself is endogenously determined—each cooperative step she takes actually reshapes the payoff structure for the next round, so that backward induction cannot be applied because the decision tree is being rewritten as it is traversed. This is not one of the standard resolutions of the paradox (it is closer to a structural feature of institutional design), and the speech should use it to explain why her career cannot be narrated as either “rational defection wisely avoided” or “irrational cooperation heroically sustained.”

The philosophical idea must do real structural work: it should determine which compliments I can offer (for instance, not “she ignored rationality”) and which I must withhold, and it should shape the closing image. The toast needs at least one precise, technically accurate reference to the logic of backward induction or to the payoff structure of the centipede game, but it must remain fully intelligible to the non-experts without footnotes or glossaries. Do not include a bibliography, citation, reading list, or any reference to specific papers by author and date. Write the speech in the first person, as if I am speaking it at the dinner.
