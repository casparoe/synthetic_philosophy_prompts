Take the collapse of the Soviet Union between 1989 and 1991 and analyze it through the lens of overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history. Specifically, apply the INUS-condition framework—where a cause is an insufficient but necessary part of a condition that is unnecessary but sufficient—alongside the distinction between causal overdetermination (multiple independently sufficient causes) and causal preemption (one cause blocking another from being sufficient). Map these categories onto the standard explanations: Gorbachev's perestroika and glasnost, long-term economic stagnation, the resurgence of nationalism in the republics, and the failed August 1991 coup. For each factor, indicate whether it operates as an INUS condition, whether it is redundant with others in an overdetermined structure, or whether it preempts an alternative causal path. Then evaluate what the framework illuminates and where it breaks: does it help distinguish structural from contingent causes, does it force a false precision onto narratives that depend on temporal ordering and human agency, and does the episode ultimately show that historical explanation requires selecting among redundant causes in ways that formal causal logic cannot justify?
