For my philosophy of agency seminar, I am struggling to apply our determinism debates to an actual conflict. My roommate was caught fabricating data in a lab report. He admits he planned and executed the deception, yet he insists that "true moral responsibility" is impossible for anyone: he never chose his risk-averse temperament, his anxiety was shaped by upbringing he did not control, and even if he had paused and decided not to cheat, that decision itself would have been caused by prior brain states he did not author. He adds that if he had somehow reformed his character to be honest, that reformation too would have been caused by conditions outside his control, so he can never be the "ultimate origin" of himself—only the latest link in a causal chain. Our university's honor code, however, explicitly draws a line between "excuses" (direct external coercion) and "explanations" (psychological history or upbringing), insisting that only the former cancel blame. My roommate claims this distinction collapses once you accept that all mental states have causes. I feel his reasoning is too quick, but I also see why the honor code's line might be hard to defend if every choice is the product of prior conditions. Does his argument successfully undermine the honor code's distinction, or is there a coherent way to maintain that people can be genuinely responsible even if they are not the first causes of themselves?
