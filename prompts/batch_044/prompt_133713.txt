Exam question: Compare the liberal conception of the self as prior to its social ends—particularly as criticized in Michael Sandel's account of the unencumbered self—with the Confucian understanding of personhood constituted through hierarchical relational roles (ren and li). Specifically, analyze whether community functions as a constraint upon or a condition for moral agency by distinguishing constitutive relations from instrumental ones. Then evaluate which framework better accommodates asymmetric reciprocity, that is, obligations between parties of unequal status such as mentor and student, without collapsing such bonds into pure contract or pure hierarchy.

Student answer:
Liberalism, especially in Rawls, assumes a self that selects its ends like items from a shelf. Sandel, following Charles Taylor's critique of atomism, shows that this view wrongly treats society as a voluntary association. So for liberals, community is mainly instrumental: people cooperate for mutual benefit, and social rules only constrain behavior externally. Confucianism takes the opposite view. The self is entirely formed by its roles—parent, child, ruler, subject—so that without these relationships there is no moral identity at all. Ren means achieving perfect harmony within these roles, while li supplies the ritual rules. This makes community constitutive of the person. Regarding asymmetric reciprocity, Confucianism handles it well because hierarchy is built into the roles themselves; a student owes deference and a mentor owes guidance, with no need for contract. Liberalism, treating all persons as equal contractors, must invent artificial obligations to cover such cases. Yet Confucianism fails because if identity is fully inside social roles, there is no internal standpoint for criticizing them—one cannot be a bad son and still remain oneself. Liberalism avoids this problem since the pre-social self can reject any commitment. Despite this tension, the Western model is ultimately more adaptable.
