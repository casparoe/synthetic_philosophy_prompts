I am a retired physical chemist who has recently turned to philosophy of science. I now serve on a regional advisory board that reviews coupled hydrological-agricultural forecasting models used for watershed-policy decisions. These models are full of unrealistic assumptions—spatially uniform soil permeability in heterogeneous terrain, farmer agents with perfect information and pure profit-maximization, linearized climate-feedback loops at regional scales. The board needs a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophy of unrealistic assumptions including the so-called F-twist and its main objections, to classify each unrealistic element as either an acceptable computational fiction or a problematic representational distortion.

Please write a formal, step-by-step procedure that my board—composed of scientists and local officials without philosophy training—can actually run on a proposed model. The procedure should include:

1. An ordered sequence of tests or questions, with explicit criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage.
2. A precise working distinction, tailored to this applied context, between a "computational fiction" (an unrealistic algorithmic shortcut that does not misrepresent the causal structure bearing on the policy-relevant prediction) and a "representational distortion" (a false ontological or causal claim that undermines the model's reliability for the decision at hand).
3. Clear stop points: where the procedure issues a recommendation to accept, revise, or reject the model; where it flags an assumption for conditional approval with monitoring; and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the conflict is one of values or risk tolerance rather than epistemic assessment.
4. Worked applications to three cases: (a) treating soil permeability as uniform in a flood-runoff model; (b) assuming perfect-information rational-actor behavior in an irrigation-demand submodel; and (c) a hypothetical new proposal in which a model treats regional climate feedback as linear for five-year projections despite well-established nonlinearity at longer scales.
5. An honest accounting of where the procedure will misfire, where judgment calls are disguised as procedural steps, and what kinds of policy stakes should trigger a more conservative default.

Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. You may refer to well-known philosophical positions in your own paraphrase, but do not use verbatim quotation marks around any passage from a real text. Keep the language formal, precise, and suitable for a board document.
