I'm working through John Stuart Mill's essay On Liberty—a nineteenth-century text—for a seminar on liberal toleration, and I'm struggling with the argument he advances in the chapter on the limits of society's authority over the individual. There Mill contends that the sole legitimate ground for interfering with a person's liberty is to prevent harm to others; neither the state nor public opinion may restrict an individual merely for that individual's own good, whether the coercion is legal or moral. He further insists that this principle governs not only physical actions but expressions of opinion, so that even erroneous or dangerous views ought to be tolerated so long as they do not directly cause harm to other people.

What I find genuinely difficult is locating the precise boundary between a protected opinion and an expression that counts as other-regarding harm, especially when the expression is not an argument or belief at all but rather an instrumental set of instructions. Consider the hypothetical case of a widely circulated technical manual that provides exact specifications for assembling an acoustic emitter; when activated, the device produces frequencies that cause severe neurological distress in aquatic invertebrates, and the harm arises not because anyone is persuaded by the manual's content but because the manual enables the physical mechanism to be built and deployed. Under Mill's framework, is the manual protected speech because it remains a form of expression, or does its purely instrumental character place it among the actions that society may restrict to prevent harm? More narrowly, does Mill's classification depend on whether the harm is mediated through the rational agency of those affected, or is causal proximity between the expressive act and the injury sufficient?

I would like help understanding the internal logic of Mill's passage rather than a historical or bibliographic overview. In particular, I want to know whether his tolerance for wrong opinions extends to expressions whose propositional content is minimal or nonexistent, and whether the other-regarding category applies only when the harmed party is a moral agent whose autonomy is overridden, or whether it covers any sentient being that suffers physical effects. I am not asking for citations, a reading list, or an account of secondary scholarship; I simply need to understand how the harm principle operates when the distinction between saying something and doing something via that saying becomes almost indistinguishable.
