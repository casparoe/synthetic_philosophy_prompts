I am drafting an essay for an advanced philosophy-of-moral-psychology seminar that proposes an unconventional structural reading of Benjamin Franklin’s system of moral perfection. Rather than treating his thirteen virtues as a conventional virtue-ethics list or as Christian-Stoic self-discipline, I want to argue that Franklin implicitly constructed a sequential dependency graph of character formation: Temperance stands as the root node not merely for prudential reasons—he says it produces the coolness and clarity of head needed for other improvements—but because it reduces cognitive load, freeing the mental bandwidth required to acquire Silence, Order, and so on in strict succession. I further want to claim that the little book with its weekly grid of violations is not best understood as a confessional or panoptic self-surveillance device, but as an early feedback-control mechanism: a manual loop in which recorded deviation adjusts the weight given to the next cycle’s target virtue, much like modern behavioral “habit stacking” or System-1/System-2 resource-management models. The essay will ask whether this “engineering” framing illuminates Franklin’s actual practice—his admission that he never achieved perfection and that Order was the virtue he found hardest—or whether it imposes an anachronistic architecture onto what was essentially a rhetorical and religious exercise. I am particularly unsure whether Franklin’s ordering is genuinely functional (each virtue enables the next) or largely pedagogical and mnemonic, and whether his tracking grid was meant to generate data for self-correction or primarily to keep the project visible to himself. Please comment on the historical defensibility of reading the virtue sequence as a dependency graph rather than a loose checklist; on whether the feedback-loop interpretation of the grid distorts Franklin’s own descriptions of its purpose; on the coherence of importing modern cognitive-load and dual-process language without attributing those theories to Franklin; and on what feature of his actual moral psychology—perhaps his emphasis on divine assistance or on social reputation—this structural framing necessarily leaves out and that I should address honestly. Also tell me where the argument risks collapsing into a just-so story, and suggest one concrete revision that would make the thesis more precise without requiring external scholarship I could not verify from standard knowledge of Franklin’s Autobiography and his letters on the virtues.
