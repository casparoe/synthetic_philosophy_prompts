Since my sister was recently diagnosed with a disorder of consciousness, I have become personally preoccupied with whether the meta-problem of consciousness is a substantive philosophical challenge or merely an artifact of how we frame subjective reports. Two colleagues—Dr. Maya Chen, a cognitive neuroscientist, and Leo Vasquez, a philosopher of mind—debated this precisely at a recent seminar, and I could not determine where each was going wrong. I am writing to ask you to adjudicate their exchange point by point.

Maya opened by arguing that the meta-problem dissolves once we see phenomenal reports as higher-order representations serving social coordination and self-regulation. She claimed that asking why these reports seem to reveal an explanatory gap is analogous to asking why a thermometer's display feels mysterious: it mistakes an instrument's epistemic limitation for an ontological residue.

Leo responded that even granting that reports are higher-order representations, the meta-problem asks why those representations carry what he termed indexical transparency—the appearance of presenting their object immediately, without representational mediation—rather than presenting themselves as bare computational updates. He insisted that explaining the mechanism of reporting does not explain why the report takes this specific phenomenal form, and accused Maya of conflating explanatory opacity, meaning ignorance of mechanism, with explanatory absence, meaning lack of mechanism altogether.

Maya countered that Leo was reifying a framing effect: indexical transparency is just what occurs when a system lacks introspective access to its own implementation. She noted that the cross-cultural uniformity of phenomenal reports is better explained by convergent evolutionary design of self-monitoring architectures than by tracking some mysterious property.

Leo then introduced what he took to be a decisive distinction between functional opacity and phenomenal opacity. In domains like thermoregulation, he said, we have functional opacity without any intuition of an explanatory gap; consciousness alone generates the gap, which suggests phenomenal opacity tracks something specific rather than generic ignorance. He concluded that Maya's evolutionary account explains why we have reports, but not why those reports feel directed at something irreducibly subjective.

Please go through each move—Maya's opening, Leo's response, Maya's counter, and Leo's final distinction—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Say who is more right overall, identify what both are missing (particularly whether indexical transparency is a coherent explanatory target or a linguistic artifact), and note whether Leo's functional-phenomenal opacity distinction survives scrutiny or collapses into the very framing issue Maya identifies.
