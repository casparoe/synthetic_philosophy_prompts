I’m a retired biologist who got into philosphy after I stopped running the lab — mostly medical ethics and personal identity stuff. Last Tuesday I was at my neigbor’s study group and two people got stuck in a real knot about advance directives. One, Mara, said that if you write a living will refusing a feeding tube, that document should always win, even if years later you’ve got severe dementia and seem to enjoy eating. She compared it to leaving a locked protocol in a lab: the PI’s instructions don’t become invalid just because the equipment is running differently now.

The other, Leo, pushed back hard. He said binding a present, feeling person to a dead author’s preference is basically autonomy grave-robbing — you’re honoring a ghost while harming someone who currently resists the tube. Mara came back that Leo smuggles in a false identity claim: he treats the dementia patient as a totally new person, but there’s still biographical continuity — same body, same legal identity, partial emotional patterns. She proposed a biographical continuity threshold, where only if narrative memory is almost entirely gone should the directive shift from command to bequest. Leo then argued that even if continuity exists, the present subject’s affective sovereignty should override, especially since the directive was made in ignorance about what dementia actually feels like. He mentioned a case he’d read about — a woman with advanced Alzheimer’s who smiled constantly but whose directive demanded aggressive intervention — and said forcing the tube was like writing data into an old experiment log that the current apparatus wants to close. Neither would budge. So who’s right about what here? Is Mara correct that autonomy is diachronic and the earlier self retains authority? Or is Leo right that present experiential sovereignty should trump? And what are they both missing — maybe about whether directives are promises to oneself versus instructions to doctors, or about how much narrative continuity actually survives in late dementia?
