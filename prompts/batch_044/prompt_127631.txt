Read Lord of the Flies through the debate about whether violence is actually declining. Golding's boys go from parliamentary rules to murder in like two weeks—the classic "thin veneer of civilization" story. But I want to take the measurment problem seriously: with maybe 12 boys on the island, killing one is an enormous per-capita rate (around 8%) but tiny in absolute terms. Does the novel's horror depend on treating the island as a statistical population, or does it reject that framing entirely? And what counts—is Piggy being bullied "violence" in the same metric as Simon being killed, or does the book blur those lines in a way that undermines any clean data-set? There's also the weird ending where the naval officer shows up and the violence just stops, like someone flipped an instutional switch. Does that vindicate the Pinker-style view that institutions suppress violence, or does the fact that Jack was still hunting Ralph show the suppression is only skin-deep? I want a reading that takes both Golding and the philosophy of violence seriously—not just "the book shows humans are violent" but whether it actually gives us a reason to think violence is measurable, intrinsic, or context-dependent in the way the big-picture debate assumes. Point to one scene where the measurement framework breaks down.
