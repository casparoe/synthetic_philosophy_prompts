I am redesigning the onboarding for technicians who must diagnose rare, high-stakes equipment failures in real time. One senior engineer argues that we should mainly select for innate perceptual talent through rigorous aptitude tests and avoid overtraining, since the relevant cognitive architecture is largely fixed by evolutionary development and cannot be reshaped much in adulthood. Another wants a purely behavioral regimen: thousands of rapid simulations with immediate reward and punishment for correct or incorrect responses, but zero explanation of underlying mechanics. A third wants every trainee to construct an explicit internal causal model of the machinery so they can mentally simulate failure paths before taking physical action. A fourth wants to abandon individual mental modeling in favor of a shared digital notation, collective checklist, and group vocabulary that lets the team distribute reasoning across people and tools. I sense these four visions rest on fundamentally different pictures of what a mind is and how it improves, but I cannot map the disagreement precisely or see which one fits this task—fast, partially novel, high-cost errors. Which conception should govern the design, what does each one commit me to about cognition, learning, and error correction, and where is the weakest point in the case for the winner?

End with a line beginning 'Verdict:' summarizing which conception should dominate for this scenario, in one sentence.
