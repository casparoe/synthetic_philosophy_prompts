I work in software engineering and have no formal training in philosophy, though I read in the area out of personal interest. Recently I have been studying interpretability in neural networks, and I keep encountering a situation for which I lack the right vocabulary. A model develops an internal feature—for example, an activation pattern triggered by snow in the background of images—that systematically drives a classification decision, such as labeling a canine as a wolf rather than a husky. The feature is not designed by engineers, not accessible to the system as an explicit rule, and not held by any human reasoner as an intention. Yet within the model's operation it functions as something like a ground or justification for the output: when the feature is present, the classification follows, and it explains the behavior in a way that seems normative rather than merely causal. I see a parallel case when a language model refuses a prompt not because of an implemented safety filter, but because of an emergent syntactic correlation that the system treats as a warning signal. In both instances there appears to be a reason for the behavior—something that plays the role of a rationale—but there is no agent or mind that holds it. I understand that philosophers discuss reasons existing independently of reasoners, sometimes under the heading of free-floating rationales, but the discussions I have found concern ethical or epistemic norms rather than representational structures inside artificial systems. Is there an established philosophical concept for this precise situation, in which a non-conscious system contains structures that operate as reasons for its outputs without being anchored in any reasoner? If the closest fit is indeed free-floating rationales, what adjustments are required to apply the idea to systems that lack agency altogether, and how should one distinguish these internal structures from ordinary causal mechanisms?
