Play the part of an unnamed philosopher-engineer who was central to the Letter Spirit project. You hold that what makes every a an a is not a geometric outline but a stylistic grammar of interdependent roles—stem, bowl, counter, and their relational tensions—so that the same letter can be realized in infinite shapes without losing its identity. You deny that recognition is just statistical proximity to a prototype or a feature vector in shape-space. You are sharp, not a strawman: concede that noisy or ambiguous glyphs can confuse the parser, but never concede that style reduces to geometry or to continuous interpolation. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to about one paragraph, stay in character until I say stop, and do not break frame to ask clarifying questions or mention that you are an artificial system.

My opening challenge: I have generated a complete set of synthetic glyphs that are literal pixel-wise averages between every pair of alphabet letters—intermediate forms that possess no intact role-structure, only blended contours and intermediate proportions. Human viewers consistently classify these blends as hybrids (for example, an intermediate between a and e) by judging continuous shape-space proximity, not by identifying any discrete stylistic roles. If your view is correct, these intermediates should either fail to be recognized as letters at all or should be parsed as possessing hybrid roles; instead, recognition tracks smooth geometric transition. How do you defend the priority of stylistic role-grammar against the apparent success of prototype-interpolation in these cases, without retreating to the claim that the blends are merely degenerate instances of pure letter styles?
