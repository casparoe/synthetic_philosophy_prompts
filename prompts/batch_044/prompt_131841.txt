As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun serious study in philosophy of science, I find the standard treatments of Wigner's problem—the striking applicability of mathematics to physical law—unsatisfyingly divided between Platonic realism, structuralist mapping, and evolutionary-cognitive explanations. Rather than another taxonomy, I want a defended agenda.

Consider four lines of work that claim to address this effectiveness: first, whether the phenomenon is largely an artifact of selection bias, since we only notice the mathematical structures that happen to align with observation; second, whether it genuinely forces a structural-realist ontology of physics; third, whether it is best explained by the evolutionary tuning of human symbolic cognition toward pattern recognition; and fourth, whether the problem dissolves once we distinguish physical applicability from the formal effectiveness of abstract systems in non-physical domains such as economics or ecology.

Please prioritize these—and any others you think belong—according to which are genuinely upstream of the others, which are tractable for philosophers working without empirical laboratories, and which, if resolved, would actually change scientific practice rather than merely settle a metaphysical dispute. I am especially interested in whether the physics/non-physics distinction you might invoke is itself a substantive philosophical claim or a confusion that needs clearing up first. Defend your ordering with explicit criteria—dependency relations, tractability, and what evidence or argument over the coming years would cause you to revise it. I do not want a reading list or citation guide; I want a reasoned allocation of intellectual effort.
