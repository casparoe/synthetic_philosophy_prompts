At a recent philosophy of knowledge workshop, two colleagues—Dr. Lena Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen—got into a sustained argument about Plato’s Meno problem: why is knowledge better than true belief? I am a doctoral student working on epistemic value, and I observed their exchange as a neutral bystander with no stake in either side. They anchored the discussion in a hypothetical they invented, the weather-implant case: a neural prosthesis that delivers true beliefs about local weather with near-perfect accuracy, yet gives the user no introspective access to its reasoning, no ability to defend the belief, and no awareness of why it is true.

Move 1. Lena opened by saying that knowledge is more valuable than true belief because knowledge is non-accidentally true. A true belief formed by pure guesswork might happen to be correct, but it could just as easily have been false; knowledge involves a kind of cognitive achievement that makes the truth non-lucky.

Move 2. Marcus replied that non-accidentality can be achieved without knowledge. The implant user acquires true beliefs through a highly reliable process, so the beliefs are not lucky in any relevant sense. Yet the user clearly does not know why the weather will change. Therefore, non-accidentality alone cannot explain why knowledge is better.

Move 3. Lena countered that the implant user’s belief is fragile across counterfactuals. If atmospheric conditions shifted in an unusual way, the implant might fail, and because the user has no grasp of meteorological reasons, she could not adapt her reasoning. Knowledge, by contrast, includes an understanding of supporting reasons that makes belief resilient.

Move 4. Marcus observed that knowledge via expert testimony often lacks that grasp too. A patient can know she has a particular disease because her physician told her, even though she cannot explain the pathology. So Lena’s resilience criterion either excludes testimonial knowledge or applies equally to the implant case—since in both cases the believer relies on an external source.

Move 5. Lena answered that testimonial knowledge still embeds the believer in a social-epistemic network: the justification is accessible in principle through the expert, and the believer can defer to that chain. The implant user, however, is epistemically isolated; there is no justificatory chain she can enter, even in principle. The value of knowledge, she argued, is relational—being tied to reasons—whether or not the individual articulates them.

Move 6. Marcus concluded that this makes the value of knowledge depend on structures external to the individual, which fails to solve Meno’s question about why knowledge is better for the knower herself. If the implant yields correct action just as reliably, then for practical reasoning the true belief is equally good. He added that Lena seems to be smuggling an intellectualist condition—reflective access—into the analysis of knowledge, which is not part of the standard justified-true-belief-plus-anti-luck framework.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point, not merely declaring an overall winner. Structure your response exactly as follows:

1. Reconstruction (approx. 100 words): restate the core disagreement in your own terms, highlighting the role of the weather-implant hypothetical.
2. Move-by-move adjudication (approx. 450 words): evaluate Moves 1 through 6 in order. For each, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify the rating, and note whether either party mischaracterizes the other.
3. The missing piece (approx. 200 words): identify a relevant consideration that neither Lena nor Marcus raised—something that bears directly on whether knowledge is better than true belief in the implant case—without invoking obscure literature or requiring citation.
4. Overall verdict (approx. 150 words): state who is more right overall, specify exactly which points stand and which fall, and say whether the disagreement reveals a deeper ambiguity in the Meno problem itself.
