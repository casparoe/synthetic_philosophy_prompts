You are preparing a speaker for the Q&A after a workshop presentation at an interdisciplinary political-economy seminar. The speaker defends this thesis: in contemporary logistics warehouses governed by algorithmic management, worker alienation is not merely an intensification of the factory conditions Marx analyzed, but involves a structural inversion of the species-being relation—specifically, the algorithm functions as the collective subject into which individual cognitive capacities are subsumed, producing what the speaker calls cognitive extraction, a form of direct appropriation of pattern-recognition labor that cannot be reduced to surplus-value extraction in the traditional sense, and therefore requires an updated metric of exploitation that accounts for this non-wage appropriation without abandoning Marx's labor theory of value.

The audience consists of about twenty scholars: mostly heterodox economists and critical theorists who work within the Marxist tradition but are skeptical of applying alienation to digital or platform capitalism, which they regard as risking conceptual dilution. Several are specialists in Marx's critique of political economy and are familiar with the distinction between formal and real subsumption. A few are labor historians who care about empirical measurability. None are analytic philosophers; they will object in the vocabulary of political economy, not modal logic or ethics. They respect Marx's technical categories and will be hostile to any argument that treats alienation as a loose psychological descriptor rather than a structurally determined relation.

Draft the speaker's prep sheet. Also specify: what should the speaker concede up front to build credibility; which objection is the killer question they are hoping nobody asks; and what is the single reply that must land for the proposal to survive the session.
