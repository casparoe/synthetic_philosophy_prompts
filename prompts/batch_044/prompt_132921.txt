Okay, so in my consciousness seminar we've been stuck on Dennett's heterophenomenology for what feels like months. The basic move is familiar enough: treat first-person reports as texts about beliefs regarding experience, not as direct windows onto some intrinsic phenomenal quality. And the phenomenal-realist pushback is equally standard — if all you're explaining is reportability and cognitive architechture, you've left out the what-it's-likeness entirely, so you haven't explained consciousness, you've just explained talking about it. Both camps cite the same change-blindness and split-brain results, both accept the neural data as given, and yet nobody has budged since roughly the 90s.

What I'm trying to figure out is whether this stall is substantive or just structural. Is heterophenomenology asking one question — how do brains generate stable reports? — while phenomenal realism is asking another — does there exist a non-relational qualitative property? Or is it verbal, turning on whether "explanation" in science requires reproducing the phenomenon or only predicting and controlling it? There's also a hidden empirical bet that might be doing the work: imagine a "zombie reporter" with perfect verbal output but no neural integartion, versus a "mute integrator" with rich global integration but zero reportability. Each side seems to have opposite intuitions about which, if either, is conscious, and maybe that's the real engine behind the debate.

So I want a diagnosis, not a victory lap. What kind of disagreement is this — different questions, bedrock intuitions, a hidden formal disagreement about explanation, or an ill-posed question? And given that diagnosis, what would actually move it: a new neural criterion, a conceptual distinction that splits the questions apart, an argument that one intuition is conceptually incoherent, or should we just accept that progress here means changing the subject?
