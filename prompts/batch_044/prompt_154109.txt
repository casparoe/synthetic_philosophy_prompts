Here is an argument that extends the presumptuous-philosopher worry from cosmology into moral uncertainty, and I think it proves too much. (1) The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) holds that, holding other evidence fixed, you should favor theories predicting more observers like yourself, weighted proportionally to the predicted numbers. (2) Moral theories differ not only in norms but in ontology: a minimalist view counts only present biological humans as moral patients, while expansive views include future artificial minds, all non-human animals across possible worlds, or every sentient process, yielding total populations that differ by dozens of orders of magnitude. (3) So if you treat moral uncertainty like scientific uncertainty, SIA applies: being one moral patient should update you strongly toward whichever theory predicts the largest patient population. (4) A maximalist theory predicting 10^40 patients should therefore dominate a minimalist one predicting 10^10 by a factor of 10^30, swamping any direct normative argument about consciousness, suffering, or agency. (5) Consequently, anyone who applies SIA consistently must converge on the most expansive moral theory almost regardless of other reasoning—making moral disagreement largely a function of demographic arithmetic rather than normative argument. (6) But this is absurd: moral belief should not be settled by population counts alone, and epistemic peers with identical normative evidence should not be forced to radically different obligations just because one theory multiplies entities. (7) Hence either SIA is false, or it is confined to cosmological contexts and does not extend to normative ontology, or moral uncertainty cannot be handled with standard Bayesian updating. Where does the argument fail? If forced to choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (3) than (6), but I want to know precisely what rejecting (4) or (5) costs, and whether there is a principled boundary that blocks the extension without abandoning anthropic reasoning altogether.
