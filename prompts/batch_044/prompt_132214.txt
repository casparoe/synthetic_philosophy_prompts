I've been taking a seminar on practical reason and value pluralism, and after class two fellow students—Marcus and Elena—got into a sustained argument about how to adjudicate hard choices between genuinely incommensurable goods. The case they used was a municipal planning dispute: the city must choose between preserving an informal settlement that sustains irreplaceable communal rituals and oral histories, or replacing it with modern housing that dramatically reduces infant mortality and respiratory illness. Marcus opened by saying that if the values—cultural continuity versus preventable death—share no common metric, then rational deliberation collapses into bare power or arbitrary preference, and any decision is essentially masked coercion. Elena replied that he was confusing incommensurability with strict incomparability: values can be ranked without being reducible to a single currency, just as one might judge one symphony greater than one painting without translating either into units of aesthetic value. Marcus then introduced what he called a residual moral claim: even after the best rational choice is made, the defeated value does not vanish but leaves a standing institutional obligation—specifically, that the city must provide perpetual symbolic recognition, such as renaming districts after displaced traditions and maintaining ritual archives, rather than monetary compensation, since money presupposes the very commensurability he denies. Elena countered that this is incoherent: if the values are truly incommensurable, treating symbolic honor as repayment still assigns the lost tradition a price in prestige, which is just another currency. She argued further that Marcus's talk of a stain on the decision-maker reveals a hidden lexical ordering—if health genuinely outweighs culture, then the regret is merely psychological, not philosophical, and if there is no ordering, then no amount of ceremony can render an arbitrary choice legitimate. Marcus closed by saying Elena reduces rational agency to a calculator that wipes its own slate; tragic choice, he insisted, does not require hidden scales, but rather acknowledges that legitimate agency can still carry irreducible responsibility for what it destroys.

They asked me to referee, and I found both sides persuasive in different registers. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move—rating Marcus's opening claim about the collapse of rationality, Elena's currency analogy, Marcus's proposal of symbolic residue, Elena's incoherence objection together with her hidden-ordering argument, and Marcus's reply about the calculator—indicating for each whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with brief justification? Please also say who is more right overall, identify what both parties are missing about whether incommensurable values can generate rational requirements that survive the choice itself without collapsing into either hidden monism or arbitrary will, and avoid treating either speaker as a strawman.
