I am drafting the final examination for a seminar I have been teaching—an interdisciplinary course that treats evolutionary epistemology as a kind of narrative about minds that survive their own mistakes. Over the term we have worked through Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures, not as a museum display but as a living argument about whether intelligence can evolve without the thinker’s death. I want one essay question that forces students into the conceptual thicket where Popperian and Gregorian cognition overlap and conflict: specifically, whether the capacity to let false hypotheses perish in one’s stead can be fully internalized without the cultural scaffolding—language, tools, shared practices—that defines Gregorian minds, or whether, conversely, Gregorian creatures have not transcended Skinnerian conditioning but merely relocated it into the social environment, outsourcing selection to institutions that still reward and punish. The question must resist cataloguing; it should demand that the student defend a precise dependency thesis, and that they answer the objection that Gregorian tools extend cognition rather than displace its risks. Please write the exam prompt itself, then supply a model answer of approximately 700 words appropriate for a 90-minute response, and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks. The scheme should separate an A answer—one that articulates the dependency relation with conceptual precision and directly addresses the displacement objection—from a B answer that describes the four kinds accurately but fails to argue. For each component, note the characteristic failure mode.
