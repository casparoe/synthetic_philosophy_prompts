I'm trying to work out my actual commitments about who belongs to the demos, and I want to know whether there's a principle that captures them or whether some verdict has to be sacrificed. Here are my judgments, case by case.

A permanent resident who has lived in the territory for two decades, pays taxes, and is fully subject to the law but holds no citizenship: clearly belongs.

A citizen who emigrated permanently thirty years ago, retains no property or family ties, and participates in no domestic institutions: does not belong, despite formal citizenship.

An eight-year-old child who will live with the long-term consequences of current climate policy: does not belong as a voting member, though her interests should be represented; representation, however, does not make her part of the demos itself.

A tourist present for two weeks: clearly excluded.

A sentient artificial system that processes all government data and advises ministers, lacking biological embodiment, social relationships, or territorial embeddedness: does not belong, even if its outputs are indispensable to governance.

A future person who will be born in fifty years and inhabit the territory: belongs in a derivative or proxy sense—her interests constrain present decisions—but I hesitate to say she is literally a current member.

A corporation chartered domestically but wholly foreign-owned: excluded.

Now the tensions.

A "cognitive co-resident": a human being with severe cognitive impairment, living in the territory, affected by laws, but lacking the capacity for autonomous preference-formation. I think she belongs and should have her interests translated into the democratic process through a guardian or advocate, whereas the sentient AI does not belong despite having far greater cognitive capacity. So membership cannot be reduced to being affected, nor to having interests, nor to possessing rational agency.

A "transient stakeholder": a foreign contractor living abroad whose livelihood depends entirely on domestic regulatory decisions for eighteen months. I think he does not belong, even though his welfare is deeply tied to those decisions, whereas the permanent resident belongs despite a similar economic profile. So deep affectedness is insufficient.

A "posthumous beneficiary": a deceased citizen whose estate and legacy projects are shaped by democratic decisions about cultural preservation. The dead are not members.

The case I keep revisiting is the "simulated constituency": high-fidelity digital models of a population used solely for policy forecasting, lacking consciousness, subjective experience, or social claims. I feel strongly they do not belong. Yet I also think that if we ever created conscious digital beings with continuous subjective experience, territorial embeddedness, and reciprocal social accountability, they might belong. The boundary seems to involve something like actual embodied social membership combined with autonomous agency, but I cannot formalize that without either excluding the cognitive co-resident or including the transient stakeholder.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a cluster of conditions involving territorial embeddedness, reciprocal accountability, and a specific form of autonomous agency—that delivers all of these verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn? In particular, what separates the simulated constituency from the hypothetical conscious digital being, and does whatever separates them also distinguish the cognitive co-resident from the sentient AI? And what does the principle predict for a "hybrid case"—a conscious digital being that is territorially embedded and socially integrated but was originally created as a policy-forecasting model?
