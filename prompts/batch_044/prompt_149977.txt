For my advanced philosophy seminar project, I am collecting examples of how formal concepts in decision theory are extended into institutional language. Please write a deadpan parody of a Terms of Service agreement for a fictional life-coaching platform called "Bounded Rationality Solutions," presented as a polished legal document with numbered sections. The platform treats Herbert Simon's framework of satisficing and bounded rationality not as a cognitive limitation to overcome but as an explicit design philosophy governing every user decision, including career selection, romantic commitment, and ethical response. The satire should exaggerate real features of the theory—aspiration-level adjustment, termination of search upon encountering the first adequate option, explicit rejection of global optimization, and cognitive-cost accounting that rationalizes stopping rules—rather than inventing fake ones. Include an invented bureaucratic artifact, the "Adequacy Threshold Disclosure Form," which users must complete before initiating any search, listing minimal acceptable criteria and legally acknowledging that any option surpassing those criteria will be disregarded as cognitively wasteful. Also include a "Counterfactual Optimization Waiver" in which users disclaim any right to regret not having selected the globally best option, on the grounds that such an option is epistemically inaccessible to bounded agents. Write in the voice of an earnest corporate legal department—no winking, no direct admission of absurdity—so that the humor arises solely from rigorously applying Simon's actual logic to contexts where it produces grotesque practical results, such as applying a stopping rule to emergency intervention or resetting aspiration levels for parental obligations. The piece should be approximately 500 to 600 words, readable both as a convincing parody of bureaucratic prose and as an internally consistent extension of bounded-rationality theory.
