I am preparing material for a reading group that treats Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a philosophical investigation into recognition rather than merely a historical novel. I would like you to read the text through the lens of Hegel's master–slave dialectic, but with an unconventional framing: instead of assigning Stevens and Lord Darlington cleanly to the roles of slave and master, I want you to examine whether Stevens simultaneously occupies both positions—serving as Darlington's subordinate while acting as the master of his own affective life, suppressing Miss Kenton's attempts at mutual recognition in order to preserve his professional dignity. Does this doubled structure illuminate Hegel's claim that the slave achieves self-consciousness through labour and the fear of death, or does it show that recognition can be foreclosed by a kind of voluntary self-enslavement?

Please focus particularly on two moments: the scene in which Stevens conceals the romantic novel he is reading from Miss Kenton, and the later revelation, during his road trip, of Lord Darlington's political errors and Stevens's own complicity in them. How do these episodes dramatise the failure of recognition—whether through domination, denial, or shame? Where does the novel resist a straightforward Hegelian reading, and where does it suggest that dignity itself can become an obstacle to the mutual acknowledgement that the dialectic requires? Finally, what does Ishiguro's portrait of service, regret, and emotional restraint contribute to the philosophical debate about recognition that a purely conceptual argument cannot? I am writing an essay on this, so please respond in British English, and do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations.
