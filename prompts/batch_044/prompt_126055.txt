I hold that weakness of will—akrasia—is systematically misdescribed when treated as a unitary rational agent being overridden by an impulsive present self through hyperbolic discounting. Instead, I take hyperbolic curves to expose genuinely distinct temporal agents, each possessing rational authority over its local domain; on this view, what looks like self-defeat is often not a failure of control but the correct democratic outcome of bargaining among plural temporal authorities. The central mistake, I think, lies in assuming there must be one correct intertemporal utility function, rather than accepting a legitimate conflict of rational perspectives distributed across time. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want the decisive defeaters separated from the merely awkward: empirical neuroeconomic findings that reveal unified valuation mechanisms, formal results showing plural-agency models collapse into standard expected-utility representations, and philosophical arguments that distributed agency over time is conceptually incoherent. Which of these would a committed defender of the plural-self view concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be absorbed by reinterpreting the data? Does the view risk being insulated—say, by treating every successful act of self-control as merely another bargaining equilibrium—so that no observation could in principle refute it? If so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a fatal flaw? Please be explicit about what this view predicts that the rival unitary-agent account does not, whether that differential prediction exposes it to empirical risk, and rank the defeaters by severity.
