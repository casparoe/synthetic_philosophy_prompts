For a graduate seminar I am organizing on the intersection of medieval Islamic metaphysics and contemporary philosophy of science, I am asking students to defend positions that are not standard textbook interpretations. I would like you to argue for the following somewhat unconventional thesis: that Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism—reconstructed with philosophical precision rather than treated as a mere theological curiosity—offers a more coherent account of natural regularity and modal properties than modern dispositional essentialism, specifically because it dissolves the problem of finkish dispositions without needing to postulate hidden causal powers intrinsic to material substances.

In developing this argument, please be explicit about what Al-Ghazālī denies, namely that any created being possesses intrinsic causal efficacy or necessary connection to its effects, and what he affirms, namely that the order of nature consists in stable divine habits (sunna) that are rational, predictable, and open to scientific description. Show how this framework treats properties like flammability or solubility not as irreducible modal essences that must be protected against external interference by mysterious finks, but as consistent patterns sustained by direct divine action, and explain why this does not reduce science to skepticism but instead reinterprets scientific law as an accurate mapping of sustained regularities without metaphysical commitment to unobservable causal mechanisms linking distinct substances. You should also address the standard objection that occasionalism renders empirical prediction impossible, since divine volition could in principle disrupt any habit at any moment, and reply to it in a way that preserves both the rationality of expectation and the denial of secondary causation—without smuggling in hidden causal powers through the back door.

Keep the reasoning rigorous and the distinctions sharp: distinguish between denying that fire burns by its own nature and denying that fire burns at all; distinguish between the predictability of a pattern and the metaphysical ground of that pattern; and, if helpful, contrast Al-Ghazālī's position with the Humean regularity theory on one side and with the dispositional essentialism of thinkers like Bird or Ellis on the other, but do not treat those contrasts as substitutes for the positive argument.
