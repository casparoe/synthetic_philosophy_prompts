Last night I had a long argument with a close friend about whether the Enlightenment should be understood primarily as a source of moral and political progress or as an engine of domination, and the discussion stalled in a way I find philosophically puzzling rather than merely frustrating. She defends what she takes to be the normative core: universal human dignity, the dismantling of hereditary privilege, scientific openness, and the Kantian idea that intellectual maturity means reasoning without deferring to authority. I keep returning to the historical and structural record—the co-production of racial taxonomies, the use of rational-bureaucratic methods to organize colonial violence, and the transformation of arbitrary tyranny into what Weber analyzed as instrumental rationality directed at populations. We agree on the basic facts; she treats them as betrayals of Enlightenment ideals, whereas I treat the ideals themselves—universalism as abstraction, autonomy as self-regulation—as conceptually inseparable from the practices of exclusion and administration they enabled.

My working hypothesis is that we are not disputing empirical history but answering different questions under one name: she is asking whether the normative commitments are worth endorsing, and I am asking whether those commitments share a logical form with the domination they accompanied. Yet I am unsure whether this is a substantive philosophical disagreement, a verbal dispute over the extension of "the Enlightenment," or an impasse rooted in bedrock intuitions about whether moral ideals can survive their historical entanglements. I want a diagnosis: do our positions differ about facts, about which cases count as relevant data, about standards of evidence for conceptual claims, or about starting values neither of us can justify? Is there a hidden formal question—for instance, whether universalism necessarily produces an excluded particular, or whether the concept of autonomy structurally mirrors administrative self-management—that is actually driving the stalemate? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the conflict, a historical finding that settles it, or should we conclude that the framing itself is ill-posed?

Please provide your answer in American English. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the analysis to rest on the conceptual structure of our disagreement alone.
