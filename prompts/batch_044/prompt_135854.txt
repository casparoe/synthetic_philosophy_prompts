A philosophy podcast recently offered a summary of Douglas Hofstadter's account of selfhood and its persistence across brains that struck me as both insightful and suspicious. The host characterized Hofstadter's view as holding that the self is not a private strange loop confined to one skull, but a pattern capable of achieving "sympathetic loop closure" across brains that know each other intimately; once established, this creates a single distributed "I" running across both architectures, so that if one brain dies the loop does not break but collapses into the survivor's head and continues with full conscious continuity. From this, the host concluded that Hofstadter denies the finality of individual death—mourning is a category error, because the dead person's consciousness has merely relocated into survivors—and that brain transplantation is conceptually redundant, since one's pattern is already distributed among friends and extracting a single brain preserves nothing not already preserved elsewhere.
