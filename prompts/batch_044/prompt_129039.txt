I am taking an upper-level seminar on conservative political philosophy and institutional epistemology, and I have adopted a qualified Burkean-Chestertonian position: the burden of justification for dismantling any long-standing institutional or conceptual boundary should be not merely that its original purpose is now opaque, but that we have reconstructed its functional role and shown that a replacement outperforms it across conditions we have not yet encountered. I apply this not only to legal and political institutions but also to what I call conceptual fences—inherited distinctions (such as those between deontic and evaluative categories, or between negative liberties and positive capacities) that structure moral reasoning but whose original rationale is no longer transparent to us. What would genuinely count against this view? I am not asking for the familiar objections about reactionary inertia or the genetic fallacy applied to tradition; I want the specific arguments, thought experiments, or formal results—perhaps from institutional design theory, evolutionary game theory, or conceptual engineering—that demonstrate either that the fence heuristic systematically misfires in domains where full reconstruction is impossible, or that it collapses into an unfalsifiable precautionary principle. Which of these failures would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as misapplications? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and offer an honest assessment of whether my qualified version retains real exposure to counterevidence or has instead been insulated against any possible refutation.
