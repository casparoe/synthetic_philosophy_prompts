I have been reading Hannah Arendt’s reflections on deception in public life, and I keep returning to a tension that seems to sit just beneath the surface of her argument. She insists that factual truths are politically fragile precisely because they rest on contingent testimony and plural witness rather than on the self-evident force of rational truths; at the same time, she warns that the deepest damage to reality comes not merely from direct falsehood but from organized efforts to replace the world with a fictional narrative that can no longer be corrected by experience. Between these two poles lies a phenomenon I am struggling to place: the bureaucratic omission, in which a fact is not contradicted but is structurally excluded from the vocabulary and categories through which decisions are made, so that it becomes administratively unthinkable rather than disputed.

I would like an explanation of the conceptual issue at stake here. Specifically, how does Arendt’s claim that political action requires a shared world of appearance relate to her view that factual truth is vulnerable? And does this kind of omission—where nothing false is said, but the conditions for recognizing a fact are dismantled—count as lying in her framework, or does it reveal a more fundamental mode of defactualization that operates prior to falsehood? In working this out, please avoid simply summarizing her well-known discussions of the Pentagon Papers; instead, trace the dependency between testimony, appearance, and institutional framing, and clarify whether an omission that never asserts an untruth can still destroy the common reality that makes politics possible.
