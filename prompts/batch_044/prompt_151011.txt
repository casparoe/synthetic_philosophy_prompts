I've been taking a graduate seminar on historiography and the philosophy of history, and over the past few weeks we've been examining how periodization works not as a discovery of natural joints in time but as a retrospective organizational practice. Our discussions have centered on the claim that "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" are largely constructs developed by nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars—frameworks like Jacob Burckhardt's 1860 characterization of Italian cultural rebirth, or the later historiographical consolidation of the so-called Scientific Revolution—rather than categories that medieval or early modern actors would have recognized in those terms. The instructor kept stressing that recognizing a period as a historiographical invention is not the same as denying that anything interesting happened, but I keep running into what feels like a logical tension that I haven't been able to resolve on my own.

If these three divisions are essentially taxonomic impositions—narrative frames that organize disparate texts, artifacts, and practices into coherent stories long after the fact—then it seems to me that the very phenomena they purport to demarcate must also be products of that framing. That is, if "the Renaissance" is just a label that Burckhardt and subsequent historians attached to a cluster of artistic and humanist developments, then there was never an actual, unified cultural rebirth occurring in fifteenth-century Italy; there were only scattered local changes that we have retrospectively woven into a single epochal transformation. By the same reasoning, if the "Scientific Revolution" is a twentieth-century historiographical artifact, then the shift from Aristotelian natural philosophy to the mathematization of nature must be a fiction imposed on a much more continuous, heterogeneous, and geographically uneven intellectual landscape between roughly 1550 and 1700. So my question is this: once we grant that these period labels are later inventions rather than discovered realities, are we not compelled to treat European history from about 1000 to 1700 as an essentially undifferentiated continuum? And if so, doesn't studying "the Middle Ages" or "the Scientific Revolution" become an exercise in circular reasoning, where we investigate the coherence of categories that we ourselves have manufactured rather than examining genuine historical discontinuities that existed independently of our naming practices?
