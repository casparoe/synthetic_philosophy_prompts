I serve as the procedural officer for the Meridian Research Collective, an interdisciplinary scholarly society governed by a thirty-four-article Charter ratified in 2019 that is explicitly self-amending. Article 28 permits any article—including itself—to be altered by a two-thirds vote of members present at a quarterly assembly, yet it also contains a Reflection Subsection stating that any proposal modifying the amendment mechanism itself must be referred to a special Constitutional Convention. The difficulty is that the rules for convening that Convention are defined within Article 28 itself, so members routinely propose changes that appear procedural on the surface but in fact reconfigure the voting threshold, the eligible electorate, or the entrenchment status of Article 28. I must evaluate each proposal within about fifteen minutes before an assembly, without outside counsel, and document my reasoning for the official minutes, since my sign-off is required before any amendment reaches the floor.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure, expressed as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, that I can apply to any proposed Charter amendment to determine whether it triggers the Reflection Subsection, creates a Nomic-style self-reference loop, or attempts an indirect entrenchment. The procedure should distinguish direct mutations of Article 28 from indirect loops—such as amending Article 5, which defines membership and therefore determines who counts toward the two-thirds vote, or Article 19, which governs quorum—and should include explicit tests for threshold mutations (for example, lowering the two-thirds requirement via a vote that does not itself meet the current threshold) and for cosmetic substitutions that actually relocate decision authority. At each step, indicate what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure must halt with a mandatory referral to the Convention, and where judgment is irreducible, particularly the boundary between a “clarifying” edit and a “substantive” change, or whether a distant indirect loop is close enough to matter. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a popular proposal to reduce the amendment threshold to sixty percent, adopted by a sixty-percent vote at the assembly—a result I will not want to endorse; (2) a proposal to insert a ten-year sunset clause into Article 28 that automatically suspends it unless actively renewed; and (3) a proposal that replaces the phrase “the membership” with “the assembly chair” in the voting clause of Article 28, framed as mere editorial housekeeping. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot settle—such as whether the Charter ought to be easily amendable at all—and flag every point where an answer requires a value judgment rather than a procedural fact.

The collective meets quarterly, the stakes involve shared research funds and authorship protocols, and I have only the Charter text in front of me. Keep the framework grounded in the logic of self-amending legal structures rather than generic parliamentary rules, avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask me to consult external sources; the deliverable should be something I can run repeatedly with no tools beyond the proposal text and the Charter.
