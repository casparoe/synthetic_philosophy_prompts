I'm on our university's Internal Seed-Grants Committee and we're deadlocked over a proposal to kill the double-blind review protocol for faculty seed funding. The rule went in back in 1983, right after a nepotism scandal, but all the files explaining why got destroyed in an archives flood in the nineties. Now the Provost's office wants open peer review to align with modern open-science transperancy norms. Half the committee keeps citing Chesterton's fence — don't tear down a fence if you don't know why it was built — and pairing it with Burke's claim that institutions hold wisdom that outruns any single generation's reasoning. The other half says that's just romanticizing a bureaucratic accident; if we can't state a live justification we should redesign rationaly.

Please draft the memo I'll send around before Thursday's vote. It needs a concrete recommendation — approve the switch, reject it, or approve with conditions — plus a specific timeline for implementation or for a follow-up review. You should map out what exactly plays the role of the fence, the unknown builder, and the person tearing it down in our case, and you need to take the Burkean side seriously rather than dismissing it as anti-progress nostalgia. I also want you to engage with an idea I picked up in a seminar on institutional epistemology: that rules can function as epistemic scaffolding for people who don't have access to the original reasoning, which might mean the fence should stay even if nobody remembers the builder. Either deploy that framing or explain why it collapses here. Give me the strongest argument on the losing side, treated honestly. Write in American English, and don't worry about being overly formal — my notes from our last meeting are kind of a mess and I need something I can actually circulate. End with a clear bottom line.
