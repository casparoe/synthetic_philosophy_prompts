Consider the pattern of Darwin's verdicts on morality and religion: he treats the social instincts of bees and baboons as authentic precursors to human morality; he holds that human moral faculties evolved from these instincts through natural selection and the development of reason; he denies that belief in God is necessary for moral life, citing societies with robust cooperative norms and no theistic religion; he regards evolutionary explanation of altruism as compatible with, rather than debunking of, its moral value; and he distinguishes full moral sense—which involves conscious reflection on past actions and anticipated duties—from mere instinctive sympathy. Now apply this pattern to a new case: a distributed artificial system with no evolutionary history, no conscious reflection on its conduct, and no religious belief sustains cooperative norms, punishes defectors, and optimizes for group welfare through purely algorithmic reward mechanisms, yet never evaluates its own past behavior. Would Darwin classify this as possessing morality in a meaningful sense, or only a functional analog? Identify the rule that best organizes his earlier verdicts—continuity of social function, evolutionary origin, presence of reflective self-evaluation, or independence from religious belief—note which alternative rules fit the same pattern equally well, say what each predicts about this system, give your best guess with reasons, and specify one further case—perhaps involving a human with severe cognitive impairment who retains social instincts but lacks reflection, or a religious society with weak cooperative norms—that would decisively separate the competing rules.
