I have been attempting to grasp Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic and the origins of modern capitalism, and a colleague recently offered me a summary that I should like to have audited. He said that Weber's central claim is that Calvinist anxiety over predestination produced a psychological drive to seek outward signs of election through relentless labour and systematic capital accumulation, and that this particular cultural value-orientation was the direct and sufficient cause of the capitalist spirit. He further maintained that Weber intended this as a strictly monocausal explanation, explicitly denying any independent causal weight to economic structures, institutional arrangements, or material conditions, and that Weber believed cultural values could shape economic behaviour directly, without mediation through social practices or institutional channels. He added that the thesis has been largely discredited because capitalist practices were clearly present in Catholic territories before the Reformation, and that Weber eventually conceded in his later writings that culture can never operate as an autonomous cause of historical change, being always reducible to underlying material interests. I am uncertain how faithful this characterisation is. Please examine it carefully, separating what is broadly accurate, what amounts to a common but defensible simplification, and what is a subtle or serious distortion — especially regarding Weber's concept of elective affinity, his allowance for material and institutional conditions, and his refusal to reduce values to interests. I also ask you to say whether my colleague's concluding inference — that Weber's work demonstrates culture cannot be an independent explanatory factor — actually follows from a corrected reading of the argument, and what such a corrected reading implies for the broader philosophical problem of whether values can cause social change in a manner that is not reducible to economic or structural determinants.
