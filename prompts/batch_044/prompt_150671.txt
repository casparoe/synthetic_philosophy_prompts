Write a poem of roughly 20 lines exploring the philosophical distinction between a finkish disposition and a masked disposition. Do not use the words "fink" or "mask" inside the poem itself; instead, embody the distinction through two recurring lines that function as refrains. The first refrain should represent the fink: a disposition that exists only so long as the conditions for its manifestation are absent, and that disappears precisely when those conditions arise—imagine, for example, a crystal that is brittle only while untouched, but becomes rubber the instant pressure is applied. Let this refrain return each time with a slight alteration, as if the very condition sustaining it is being withdrawn. The second refrain should represent the mask: a disposition that remains fully intact but is blocked from manifesting by an interfering factor—the same crystal wrapped in felt that must be peeled away before it can break. Let this refrain return unchanged, word for word, each time, like an object still present but hidden from view. Between these refrains, weave a brief image of a hand reaching toward such an object, leaving unresolved whether the object transforms upon contact or is merely revealed. The poem's repetition and variation should carry the conceptual weight; avoid any prose explanation within the verse. The distinction should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with the philosophy of dispositions recognizes the classic problem, yet the poem should stand on its own for a reader who has never encountered it. At the very end, provide a one-sentence summary of the tension between the two refrains as the final line, formatted exactly: Summary: [one sentence].
