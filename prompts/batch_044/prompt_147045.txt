In my philosophy of rationality seminar, two classmates have been arguing about Parfit's hitchhiker thought experiment and whether it genuinely demonstrates that rationality can require being disposed to act against one's present interests. The disagreement started after our discussion of commitment problems and direct versus indirect rationality.

Maya presented the standard backward-induction reasoning: once the driver has reached town, the hitchhiker can do nothing to reward or punish him, so paying ten thousand dollars is a pure loss. At that moment, expected utility is maximized by refusing to pay. Therefore, not paying is directly rational.

Rohan agreed that this describes direct rationality but argued that the deeper point is about indirect rationality: the driver should have made himself into the kind of agent who keeps promises, because that disposition is what gets him picked up in the first place. The failure is not in refusing to pay at the end, but in failing to install the commitment mechanism earlier. This is the rational irrationality that Parfit describes.

Maya then pushed back by invoking a reductionist view of personal identity over time. If agency is just a series of time-slices with no metaphysically unified self spanning them, then there is no single agent who can be blamed for failing to prepare. The time-slice in town has no reason to sacrifice ten thousand dollars to benefit some earlier slice that made a promise. Calling the refusal irrational illicitly assumes a unified agent that does not exist.

Rohan responded that even accepting a time-slice view, there is a pragmatic incoherence: the present slice wants to be the kind of agent who gets picked up, which requires being the kind who pays, yet the present slice refuses to bear the cost of being that kind. This is not about punishing a past self; it is about the present self being unable to consistently will the very disposition that serves its interests. So direct rationality undermines itself.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: assess Maya's backward-induction argument, Rohan's appeal to indirect rationality, Maya's time-slice objection, and Rohan's pragmatic-incoherence reply. For each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a way to resolve the tension without taking a stand on personal identity, or whether the argument genuinely requires one.
