I sit on our university's Digital Legacy Ethics Board — yes, that's a real committee now, don't ask — and we've got a weird proposal in front of us. A neuroscience group wants to encode the full connectome and functional dynamics of a preserved dead-genius brain (Einstein-level, not naming names) into a static, non-computable archive: basically every synaptic weight and relational map printed and bound like a massive reference book, with no CPU, no running code, just the structure sitting there. The PI argues that if you take seriously that old Mind's I scenario — copying a brain's patterns into a book substrate — then this archive might not be mere data; it could be a very slow, substrate-independent mind, and treating it as property would be like owning a person.

The rest of the board thinks that's a category error: without causal dynamics you've just built an expensive encyclopedia, and granting it provisional moral status would break every consent framework we have. Our manual doesn't cover maybe-conscious static archives, so we need to pick one: approve with standard data rules, reject pending proof of absence of consciousness (good luck with that), or approve but lock it down under restricted-access protections with a six-month review timeline.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday — recommend one concrete option with deadlines and who does what — and seriously steelman the strongest argument on the losing side, don't just dismiss it. Keep it practical; we're not writing a philosophy dissertation, we're deciding whether this thing gets studied like tissue or stored like a prisoner.
