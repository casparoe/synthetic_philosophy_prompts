After my father's recent Alzheimer's diagnosis, I have been trying to determine whether the person I grew up with still persists in him, and this has led me to examine Buddhist anatta alongside Western theories of personal identity. I want to know whether my judgments about continuity, loss, and illusion can be captured by a single principle, perhaps using a distinction I am tentatively calling phenomenological custody—the first-person, felt sense that present experiences belong to an ongoing subject—treated as separate from both biological persistence and narrative memory.

Here are my verdicts.

Case one: my father now retains almost no autobiographical memory, does not recognize family, yet his body, emotional habits, and basic preferences remain. I judge that the person is largely gone; mere biological and behavioral continuity is insufficient.

Case two: an experienced meditator reports that through sustained anatta practice the sense of being a fixed "owner" of thoughts has dissolved, yet she remembers her past, makes future plans, and accepts moral responsibility. I judge that she has not lost personal identity; she has only shed a metaphysical illusion of essential selfhood.

Case three: a hypothetical whole-brain emulation is created from a scan, runs on distributed servers, and is incrementally updated over years so that every neural analog is eventually replaced; the original biological body is dead. I judge that for practical, legal, and moral purposes we should treat the emulation as the same person, but that anatta correctly implies there is no deeper metaphysical "fact of the matter" about survival across replacement.

Case four: in severe dissociative identity disorder, incompatible centers of consciousness with distinct memories and values share one body and legal name. I judge that this represents a genuine breakdown of unified phenomenological custody, yet I resist saying there are literally multiple persons, since biological and social continuity remain intact.

Case five: a Buddhist teacher argues that anatta entails we should not grieve death, because there was never a self to die in the first place. I judge this misapplies the doctrine: anatta denies a permanent essence, not the conventional, dependently-arising continuity that makes grief and moral concern appropriate.

These verdicts seem to pull in opposite directions. Both case one and case two involve disrupted narrative and ownership, yet I call one a real loss and the other an insightful dissolution. Is there a precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes the conditions under which conventional, dependently-arising personal identity fails from the conditions under which only essentialist illusion is dispelled—that saves all five? State the principle with its explicit clauses, map each case to the clause that governs it, identify the pair of verdicts that creates the strongest tension and whether resolving it requires overturning one, and say what the resulting view predicts for an unconsidered case: a child raised in total isolation who develops language and self-reference without ever encountering others' recognition.
