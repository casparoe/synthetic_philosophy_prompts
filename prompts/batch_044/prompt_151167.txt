Two friends of mine, Maya and Jonas, got into it about whether you can formalize "avoid side effects" for an AI without already knowing exactly what you value. Maya said yes, you can do it structurally: run the AI's plan and compare the world-state to a counterfactual where the task gets done by the smallest possible intervention, then measure divergence in variables that aren't named in the task spec. She called this a "shadow footprint" and said the value stuff only enters in the task description, not in the divergence metric itself.

Jonas said thats just hiding the value judgment in the choice of counterfactual. He asked: if the task is "cure cancer" and the AI restructures the global economy to fund research, is that a huge side effect or part of the plan? You can't answer without knowing whether economic autonomy matters, which is a value call. He also said "smallest possible intervention" is undefined without knowing what's allowed to change.

Maya came back and said Jonas is mixing up two things: the syntactic boundary of the task, and moral evaluation of outcomes. She argued that "side effect" just means "causal change outside the syntactic boundary of the goal," and you can compute that with causal graphs without knowing if the change is good or bad. The metric tells you how much the world shifted; whether you care is a seperate question.

Jonas replied that syntactic boundaries don't track physical reality. He gave the example of heating a room: if the goal is literally "increase temperature" and the AI burns the building, the combustion isn't in the syntactic boundary, but calling it a "side effect" rather than "the mechanism" requires knowing that destroying the building is bad. So the divergence metric either needs a value-laden filter on which variables to watch, or it ends up treating destructive efficiency as low-impact.

Maya said thats unfair, because her method compares against a human-baseline counterfactual, not against zero change. So the building-burning would show massive divergence from "human turned up the thermostat." Jonas said but then you've smuggled in "human-like methods are the right baseline," which is itself a conservative value assumption about preserving existing social and physical structures.
