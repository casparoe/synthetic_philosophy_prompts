I am working through Skolem's remarks on axiomatised set theory from the 1920s, and I need help disentangling two readings of a central argument. Skolem observes that any consistent first-order axiomatisation containing the usual set-theoretic axioms—including infinity and power set—admits a countable model. Within that model, there exist sets that the model's own internal resources classify as uncountable, even though every object in the model is, from the outside, countable. From this he concludes that the notion of an absolute infinite and the corresponding cardinal distinctions are not fixed by the formal system alone, but depend on the interpretation.

What puzzles me is whether Skolem is advancing a purely semantic point about the expressive limits of first-order languages—namely, that uncountability behaves like an indexical whose reference shifts with the model—or whether he is advancing a stronger metaphysical claim that the concept of set itself is indeterminate, so that there is no model-independent fact about whether the power set of the natural numbers is genuinely larger than the naturals. Some interpreters have linked his position to what later philosophers call model-theoretic pluralism, treating cardinality as frame-relative in something like the way geometric properties are frame-relative in certain physical theories. Others read him as an early internalist about mathematical ontology, holding that set-theoretic truth is always truth-in-a-model and never transcends interpretation.

Can you help me understand which of these readings the passage actually supports? Specifically, does Skolem treat the countable model as revealing a deficiency in our formal language, or as revealing that the intuitive domain of sets lacks the determinacy required to ground absolute cardinal judgments? I am also trying to see whether his later emphasis on justifying axioms by their fruitfulness rather than by intuitive correspondence fits better with the semantic or with the metaphysical reading.
