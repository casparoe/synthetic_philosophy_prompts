Write a dialogue between Mara and Leo, two philosophers arguing after a seminar, about whether pure time preference—discounting future welfare just because it's further away in time—can ever be rationally defensible once you rule out risk, uncertainty, and changing preferences. Mara defends a view she calls "chronological positional weighting," where an agent's present temporal index has intrinsic normative weight, kind of like how some ethicists treat spatial proximity, except she applies it across time rather than space. Leo thinks that's just dressed-up present-bias and pushes her on a concrete case: a climate adaptation fund can either disburse one million now to a specific village, or with literally zero risk and guaranteed transmission disburse ten million to that same village's direct descendants in exactly eighty years. He asks whether her view forces her to prefer the smaller immediate payout, and if so, how she distinguishes her position from ordinary impatience without secretly smuggling in uncertainty or identity worries she's already excluded. They should actually respond to each other's arguments—no talking past each other. Include a spot where Mara tries an analogy comparing temporal distance to physical distance in rescue obligations, and Leo corrects her by noting that spatial distance usually only weakens obligation when there's a causal or informational bottleneck, which doesn't apply here since the future transfer mechanism is secured. Keep the tone casual, like they're standing in a hallway with coffee, and feel free to include a typo or two—maybe Leo writes "recieve" or Mara says "definately" at some point. Both should sound equally sharp; neither should be a strawman.
