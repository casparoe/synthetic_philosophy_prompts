I recently concluded a rather prolonged and somewhat heated discussion with a close friend about whether the Buddhist doctrine of anattā can genuinely sustain a coherent notion of moral agency across time. In the wake of that conversation, I drafted the following preliminary notes for an essay, and I would be grateful for conceptual feedback rather than bibliographic guidance. I want to examine whether the Theravada Abhidhammic concept of bhavaṅga—the subconscious life-continuum that bridges discrete moments of consciousness through sleep, death, and rebirth—functions as a concealed diachronic substrate that undermines the radical denial of self. If bhavaṅga retains karmic impressions (vāsanā) and ensures causal continuity, does it not operate analogously to a persisting self, merely reconceptualized under a different designation? I contrast this with the Abhidharmic commitment to momentariness (kṣaṇika), which denies any enduring substance yet permits causal succession through immediate contiguity (samanantara-pratyaya). My tentative claim is that anattā remains defensible only if bhavaṅga is interpreted not as an underlying owner of experience but as an acausal-series-without-synchronic-unity; however, this interpretation appears to strain ordinary attributions of responsibility, since no individual momentary consciousness can be said to bear the moral weight of prior actions. I further consider whether contemporary process-relational metaphysics—specifically the view that entities are societies of actual occasions lacking an enduring core—offers a productive analog: if a person is such a society of mental occasions, moral accountability might be distributed across the series rather than anchored in a singular subject. I am particularly interested in whether this line of reasoning mischaracterizes the Abhidharmic position on causal succession, or whether my comparison with process philosophy obscures more than it clarifies regarding the nature of karmic continuity.
