I am applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship in philosophy of mind. My half-formed project concerns Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self is not tied to a particular brain but is a pattern—what he calls a strange loop—that could in principle exist elsewhere. Most philosophical discussion treats this either as a metaphor for psychological continuity or as an argument for substrate-independent functionalism. I think there is an underexplored middle path: taking the loop idea literally as a structural feature of causally integrated information flow, and asking whether one such loop can be genuinely distributed across physically separated brains that share real-time informational links, rather than merely being copied or simulated in parallel.

Write a formal research proposal of about 1,100 words, structured in the following sections, in this exact order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. Precise research question (approx. 200 words). Formulate the exact problem: under what structural conditions, if any, does a single self-pattern extend across two or more biologically separate brains? Specify what would count as one distributed loop versus two independent loops.

2. Gap in existing work (approx. 250 words). Explain why standard psychological-continuity theories, narrative-identity accounts, and Hofstadter's own informal remarks do not settle whether cross-brain integration can preserve loop-identity. Keep references general—no bibliography required; I will supply citations.

3. Method and timeline (approx. 300 words). Outline the philosophical work: defining structural conditions for loop-identity independent of substrate, constructing thought experiments involving split and merged cognition across linked brains, and comparing the results with existing persistence criteria. Provide a realistic month-by-month plan for six months.

4. Anticipated conclusions and decision criteria (approx. 200 words). Identify the main possible outcomes—e.g., that distributed loops are structurally impossible, that they require a new category of joint persistence, or that the framework collapses into familiar fission problems. State what considerations would decide between them.

5. Failure conditions and scope limits (approx. 150 words). Give an honest assessment of risks: for instance, if formalizing loop-conditions requires empirical neuroscience beyond philosophical analysis, or if the project proves too large for six months without narrowing to one class of integration mechanisms.

6. Deliverable and intended audience (approx. 100 words). Describe the output—likely a 35–40 page conceptual paper with a taxonomy of distributed-persistence conditions—and specify whether it is aimed at philosophers of mind, cognitive scientists, or both.

Keep the proposal conceptual rather than empirical. Do not request a literature review by citation, specific page references, or verification of obscure texts.
