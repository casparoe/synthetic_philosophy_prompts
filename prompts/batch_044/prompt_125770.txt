Write a dialogue between two equally competent scholars—Dr. Elena Voss, who holds that revealed preference is purely an ordinal bookkeeping system with no psychological content, and Dr. Marcus Chen, who argues that utility must refer to some internal normative structure that explains choice rather than merely summarizing it. Their discussion should center on a specific, non-standard case: a small irrigation cooperative in which the same members rank three water-allocation schemes (market pricing, equal-ration council vote, and hereditary-use ritual) in cyclically opposite orders—market pricing over equal-ration over hereditary-use in the market frame, equal-ration over hereditary-use over market pricing in the council frame, and hereditary-use over market pricing over equal-ration in the ritual frame—without any member reporting confusion or regret. Voss should defend the view that this is not a failure of revealed preference but proof that preference is register-relative, requiring only separate, frame-bound utility representations, and she should resist Chen’s claim that such frame-dependence makes utility vacuous unless anchored in a stable mental state. Chen should push back by arguing that if utility has no cross-frame identity conditions, it cannot do the explanatory work economists ask of it—predicting behavior outside the frame in which it was revealed—and he should propose that what remains constant across frames is not a preference ordering but a deeper evaluative commitment that the revealed-preference framework cannot capture. The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should directly answer the other’s last point, avoid straw-manning, and conclude not with agreement but with a precise statement of what empirical or conceptual test would settle whether utility needs mental-state anchors or can survive as a purely local, institutional index.
