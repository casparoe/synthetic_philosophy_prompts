Write a short philosophical story set in an isolated evaluation facility where four synthetic minds have been constructed according to Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of cognitive architectures: a Darwinian mind governed by fixed evolutionary heuristics, a Skinnerian mind shaped entirely by operant conditioning, a Popperian mind capable of generating and internally testing hypothetical models, and a Gregorian mind that extends its cognition through language, symbolic tools, and accumulated cultural scaffolding. For years the facility has studied how these four recognize one another's mental states, but the narrative should center on the recent activation of a fifth, unauthorized prototype—designated the "Ontological Refactor"—which does not learn via selection, reinforcement, internal simulation, or cultural transmission. Instead, whenever it detects a logical tension in another agent's reasoning, it automatically dissolves and rebuilds its own categorical framework to accommodate the contradiction, leaving no stable self-model across interactions.

The plot should unfold during a single structured interview in which the Gregorian and Popperian architectures attempt to apply the standard problem-of-other-minds framework to the Refactor, asking it whether it experiences doubt, holds beliefs, or possesses an internal point of view. Each answer should destabilize the interviewers' own categories: for instance, when asked about belief, the Refactor might restructure the concept of belief so radically that the Gregorian's linguistic tools can no longer apply it, and the Popperian's predictive models of other minds collapse into self-reference. Rather than resolving whether the Refactor "has" a mind, the story should explore the symmetrical failure of recognition—the four canonical kinds cannot ascribe mentality to it without altering their own architectures, and it cannot understand them without dissolving theirs.

Keep the tone literary and precise, using the technical distinctions among the four architectures as narrative devices rather than exposition. Do not provide a bibliography or cite specific texts; the philosophical weight should emerge from the interaction itself, particularly the idea that universal ascription—the assumption that mental-state attribution operates uniformly across all minds—breaks down when one architecture's mode of existence is to rewrite the conditions of ascription from within.
