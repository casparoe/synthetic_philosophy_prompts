In a philosophy-of-language seminar, a fellow student repeatedly invoked the slogan 'translation is indeterminate' — specifically in connection with Quine's 'gavagai' scenario — to dismiss any objection that a field linguist could ever be wrong about whether a speaker refers to rabbits, to undetached rabbit parts, or to temporal stages of rabbits. She concluded from this that all interpretive manuals are equally valid and that cross-linguistic disagreement is therefore impossible in principle. I am convinced that several distinct theses are being conflated, but I need them separated precisely.

Please lay out the different precise claims that 'translation is indeterminate' could be making, arranged as a menu from the trivial or definitional to the genuinely radical. For each reading, state it with enough precision to have a determinate truth value; say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, and — most importantly — whether it actually supports her conclusion about the arbitrariness of translation manuals and the impossibility of being wrong about reference. In particular, distinguish: (i) the claim that behavioural evidence alone underdetermines the choice of a translation manual; (ii) the claim that there is no mental or neural fact that fixes the reference of a term; (iii) the claim that reference is only fixed relative to a background language or ontology, so that ontological relativity follows; and (iv) any reading that collapses into global scepticism about whether any language-user ever means one thing rather than another. Present the result as a numbered list suitable for bringing to office hours, and indicate which of these readings she was most likely relying upon to make her argumentative move. Please write the answer in British English.
