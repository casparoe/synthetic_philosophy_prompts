As a high school student working through the philosophy of economics, I am trying to understand what "utility" actually means in revealed preference theory, and I have reached a point where my intuitive judgments about specific cases do not line up cleanly with the textbook behavioral account. I would like a coherent principle that captures the pattern in my verdicts below, together with an honest statement of which verdicts must be dropped if they cannot all be saved. I also want the principle tested against a further case I have not yet settled.

Here are my judgments.

First, if every week I choose chocolate ice cream over vanilla when both are free, it seems correct to say that I prefer chocolate and that it carries higher utility for me.

Second, if I choose vanilla only when chocolate costs ten cents more, but revert to chocolate when prices are equal, it seems correct to say that my preference for chocolate is weak—overridden by a small monetary cost—even though the direct choice reverses with price. The revealed-preference framework captures this through willingness-to-pay, and that feels right.

Third, consider deliberate ignorance. I am offered either a guaranteed fifty dollars or a sealed envelope that I know contains either nothing or one hundred dollars with equal probability. I take the guaranteed fifty and sincerely say, "I think the envelope has higher expected value, but I prefer avoiding the anxiety." Revealed preference theory seems forced to say that I prefer fifty dollars to the gamble. But my mental state is that the envelope is better by expectation; my choice reflects a preference for certainty, not an all-things-considered ranking that places fifty dollars above the gamble's expected value. So my verdict is that revealed preference here reveals my risk attitude, not my underlying ranking of outcomes.

Fourth, in a restaurant I deliberately avoid reading the description of the chef's special, then order the salmon. When asked whether I prefer salmon to the special, I reply that I do not know, because I chose not to learn. My verdict is that my choice does not reveal a preference for salmon over the special; it reveals a preference for avoiding information combined with a fallback choice.

Fifth, in a secret ballot I vote for policy A, but in a public forum I defend policy B with conviction and at social cost. If revealed preference is defined purely over observable choice, then I prefer A. But if utility is meant to capture what I genuinely value, then my public defense—where the stakes for misrepresentation are higher—might actually reveal my deeper preference for B, while the ballot reveals strategic or private reasoning. My verdict is torn: choice seems authoritative for preference, yet the mental-state account feels more faithful to what "utility" is supposed to measure.

Is there a principle that unifies these five verdicts? Specifically, can revealed preference accommodate cases in which agents deliberately structure their epistemic environment—avoiding information, selecting certainty—without reducing all of that to a simple ranking of final outcomes? Or must I drop either the claim that revealed preference is purely behavioral, or the claim that mental states about value can sometimes correct the choice-based reading?

Please also apply your principle to this further case: a student refuses a free IQ test and then applies to colleges using only her grades. Does her refusal to learn her score, followed by her choice of schools, reveal a preference for ignorance that should be separated from her preference for particular institutions?

Finally, please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to reject the principle you propose, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail? I am especially concerned that I may be conflating preference for an outcome with preference for the epistemic conditions under which I face it, and I want to know whether that distinction can be maintained within a unified theory of utility.
