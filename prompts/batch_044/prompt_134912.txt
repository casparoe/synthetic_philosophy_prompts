I have been discussing the philosophy of historical explanation with a senior colleague who specializes in path dependence across technological, biological, and institutional domains. She has offered consistent verdicts on several cases: she holds that the dominance of the QWERTY keyboard layout was genuinely path-dependent, since an early network of typist-training and equipment manufacturing locked in a suboptimal design despite the availability of more efficient alternatives; that the global adoption of the metric system outside the United States was not path-dependent, because the functional demands of scientific coordination and industrial scaling would have produced a decimal standard regardless of its specific revolutionary origins; that the extinction of Neanderthals was path-dependent, because slight variations in climate fluctuations or interaction dynamics with Homo sapiens could plausibly have produced long-term coexistence rather than replacement; that the emergence of double-entry bookkeeping in Renaissance Italy was not path-dependent, because the informational requirements of long-distance trade created functional pressures that would have generated an equivalent accounting innovation in any comparable commercial center; and that the persistence of left-hand traffic in Japan and the United Kingdom is path-dependent, since initial feudal and colonial contingencies generated self-reinforcing infrastructure investments that now resist conversion despite clear efficiency costs.

The case I now wish to test concerns the dominance of decoder-only, causally masked transformer architectures—rather than encoder-decoder or bidirectional recurrent architectures—as the standard paradigm for large-scale generative language modeling after 2017. My colleague is aware that parallelizable attention mechanisms were technically available, that GPU cluster availability was expanding rapidly, and that the natural language processing community was already oriented toward sequence-to-sequence tasks, yet she also recognizes that the specific framing of unsupervised pretraining on autoregressive next-token prediction was only one of several viable technical routes, and that its entrenchment was accelerated by particular open-source release dynamics and subsequent investment patterns rather than by an unambiguous performance gap at inception. Would she judge this architectural dominance to be path-dependent or functionally inevitable? I am less interested in the binary classification than in the reasoning structure behind it: please identify the two or three distinct general rules that could consistently account for all five of her earlier verdicts; specify which of those rules predict path-dependence for the transformer case and which predict inevitability; offer your best assessment of which rule she actually applies, with the considerations that distinguish it from the alternatives; and propose one hypothetical further case—drawn preferably from institutional design or scientific practice rather than computing—that would serve as a decisive test between the leading candidates.
