I am preparing a long-form feature for a publication that tracks philosophy and aesthetics, and after a roundtable with curators and critics about whether an artist’s moral failures should diminish our judgment of their paintings, I realized that the familiar debate—separating the work from the artist—quietly collapses three very different moral failures into a single question. I want to map the conceptual architecture first, before I interview anyone.

Please write the desiderata that an adequate theory of artistic value must meet if it is genuinely to keep these failures distinct and still yield a coherent evaluation. Specifically, I need the full specification: the cases it must handle (a technically masterful portrait by a perpetrator of violence; a collaborative installation in which only one contributor committed fraud; a film whose production relied on exploitative labor practices yet whose narrative condemns exploitation; and a digitally generated image trained on stolen visual archives where no single human author can be identified); the distinctions it must preserve—between a moral flaw in the creator’s biography, a moral flaw embedded in the work’s representational content, and a moral flaw in the conditions of the work’s material production or economic circulation—without allowing any one of these to determine the others automatically; and the findings from empirical aesthetics or moral psychology it cannot contradict (for example, whether knowledge of an author’s crimes reliably alters viewers’ formal appreciation, or whether moral disgust operates independently of aesthetic judgment). For each requirement, provide the motivating case or consideration behind it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (such as those who insist that aesthetic value is purely formal versus those who allow contextual knowledge to inform judgment).

Then tell me which single desideratum does the most damage to the three common positions—pure formalism, pure moralism, and the moderate separatist view that looks only to the work’s internal properties. Finally, flag any pair of requirements you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable given the current state of the debate, and briefly indicate how those three positions fare against the full list. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need the structure of the problem laid out so my readers can see exactly where the fault lines are before they encounter any particular philosopher’s solution.
