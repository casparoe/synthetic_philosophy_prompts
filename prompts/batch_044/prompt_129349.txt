As a retired condensed-matter physicist who has begun reading philosophy of science and metaphysics in earnest, I have found myself returning repeatedly to the problem of material constitution—specifically the relation between a statue and the lump of clay that composes it. In my former discipline we routinely distinguish microscopic constituents from macroscopic phases that exhibit emergent properties through symmetry breaking and collective behavior, yet I am unsure whether that same apparatus can illuminate, or only obscures, the ontological relation between statue and clay. I want you to treat me as fluent in the vocabulary of statistical mechanics, renormalization-group thinking, emergent phases, and collective excitations such as phonons, but as a novice in formal metaphysics.

Please restate the principal analytic positions on material constitution—the view that the statue is identical to the clay, the view that the statue is constituted by but not identical to the clay, and the view that there is strictly only one object present—using exclusively that physical vocabulary. For each position, say precisely which physical concepts it maps onto, where the mapping requires distortion or stretching, and whether any metaphysical claim lacks any counterpart in condensed-matter theory. Be explicit about how notions like persistence conditions, modal properties, or artistic form might be translated into talk about phase stability, boundary conditions, or symmetry-breaking patterns.

Then provide a residue report: identify the claims that survive translation intact, those that can be preserved only by fudging the physics, and those that vanish entirely. Conclude by defending a specific judgment—do not remain non-committal—about whether the residue points to a genuine limitation in using physical vocabulary to capture ontological relations, or whether it suggests that the original metaphysical disagreement was less substantive than it appears, analogous perhaps to disputing the "true" identity of a phonon rather than recognizing it as an emergent excitation with no independent microscopic counterpart.
