I have been trying to formalize an argument about persistence that seems to collapse endurantism into either self-contradiction or verbal redundancy, but the conclusion feels too destructive to be right. Here is the chain as I currently have it.

(1) Endurantism holds that a persisting object is strictly numerically identical at every time it exists; there are no temporal parts, only the complete object present at each moment.

(2) If strict numerical identity holds across distinct times, then there must be some explanatory ground—call it a diachronic binding relation—that makes the entity at t₂ literally the same one that existed at t₁, rather than a perfect qualitative duplicate.

(3) Endurantists reject the idea that this binding is constituted by relations among distinct temporal parts; therefore the ground must either be primitive or reduce to the preservation of the object’s essential properties.

(4) Now consider total gradual replacement: every part of O is replaced over an interval, yet functional and causal continuity are maintained throughout (the ship pushed to its limit). At the endpoint, O*, no part is shared with the original O.

(5) By (2) and (3), if O and O* are strictly identical, that identity must be grounded either in shared parts (there are none) or in preserved intrinsic essential properties (all have changed).

(6) So the endurantist must either deny that O survives—despite intact continuity—or admit that strict identity does not require shared parts or invariant intrinsic properties, but only the appropriate continuity of form and function.

(7) Once continuity of form and function is admitted as sufficient for diachronic identity, the endurantist’s “whole object present at each time” is just the temporal stage that stands in those continuity relations to other stages; reconstructed across time, these stages form a four-dimensional worm. That is precisely the perdurantist ontology.

(8) Therefore endurantism is either false (it denies ordinary survival) or indistinguishable from perdurantism.

(9) But if the two views are indistinguishable, then the endurantist’s insistence that objects genuinely lack temporal parts is merely verbal, with no ontological weight—yet endurantists present it as a substantive metaphysical disagreement.

(10) So the argument proves too much: it either eliminates persistence through change or dissolves the dispute into semantics.

If I must choose, I want to keep (1), (6), and (9)—I think objects do survive total replacement, I think continuity rather than shared parts is doing the work, and I think the disagreement with perdurantism is genuinely substantive rather than verbal. Where does the slip occur? Is there a hidden equivocation between “binding relation” as an explanatory primitive and “continuity conditions” as constitutive criteria, or does saving (9) force me to reject (7) and pay the price of allowing unexplained primitive identity?
