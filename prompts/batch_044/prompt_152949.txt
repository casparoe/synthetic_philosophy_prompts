I'm preparing a ninety-minute interdisciplinary workshop for graduate students in design and engineering who have no formal background in philosophy or law. The session asks whether ideas can be owned, but I want to avoid the usual copyright-versus-patent framing and instead build everything around one conceptual distinction I'm introducing: the gap between an idea's provenance—the mental and social pathway by which it reached its present form—and its stewardship—the labor, risk, and refinement required to make it usable by others. Plan the session's explanatory arc: what prior idea must be secured before this distinction can even be introduced; the one concrete scenario that should carry the whole discussion, specifying what it must include and what it must deliberately omit; the misconceptions the students are likely to bring about mental ownership, collective invention, and economic reward, listed in the order they will probably surface, with the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a final check that would reveal whether they have understood the provenance-stewardship gap rather than merely taken a pro- or anti-intellectual-property position; and which standard legal doctrines or economic theories should be named and explicitly set aside at this level.
