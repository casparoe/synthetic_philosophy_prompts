I am writing a term paper for my upper-level philosophy of action seminar on moral responsibility, and I have been working closely with Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. I understand the regress in its standard form: to be truly responsible for an action, one must be responsible for the mental states that produced it, and responsible for the prior states that shaped those, with no legitimate stopping point short of being causa sui. But I keep encountering presentations that frame this as an essentially diachronic problem—one that requires a literal temporal origin point at which an agent first authored her own nature. It strikes me that if we reject that temporal framing and substitute an atemporal, modal-structural conception of self-authorship—where one's character is not causally produced over time but is instead a structural feature that holds across one's modal profile, analogous to the way an axiom system is architecturally fixed by its axioms regardless of temporal order—then the regress never gets started, since there is no prior temporal state that stands in need of authorization. Given that this modal-structural view appears to dissolve the temporal regress driving Strawson's argument, why is it not treated in the literature as a straightforward reply? Specifically, does the Basic Argument depend on a hidden premise about the temporal continuity of agency rather than on the bare impossibility of being causa sui? And if that is the case, does the argument only target libertarian conceptions that require a temporal origin point, leaving compatibilist accounts of attributability and answerability untouched?
