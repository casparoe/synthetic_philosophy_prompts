You sit on the Metropolitan Infrastructure Resilience Board, the body that approves or rejects major adaptive-engineering contracts for the region's aging water-management system. Before you is a proposal to replace a 1950s storm-surge barrier with either Design A or Design B, together with a procedural motion that has split the board.

Design A is endorsed by the in-house hydrology team. It relies on an inside-view framework: high-resolution modeling of local bathymetry, sediment dynamics, tidal resonance anomalies specific to this estuary, and projected sea-level curves calibrated to the region's subsidence patterns. Its advocates argue that generic base rates drawn from other ports mislead here, because this estuary's geomorphology is genuinely atypical, and that abandoning bespoke physical modeling for aggregate statistics would discard the very causal detail that determines failure modes.

Design B is endorsed by an external forecasting consortium. It relies on an outside-view framework: a reference-class analysis of comparable barrier-replacement failures in North Atlantic ports over the past sixty years, adjusted for climate stress and maintenance regimes. Its advocates argue that inside-view engineering models suffer from systematic optimism bias—local teams consistently overestimate structural longevity because they overweight favorable local parameters and underweight institutional decay, budget compression, and unforeseen interaction effects that only appear across large populations of projects.

The board must choose one design, approve neither, or approve one with conditions. Separately, half the members have proposed a mandatory "epistemic modesty clause" for any approved contract: before final sign-off, both the inside-view and outside-view assessments must be published in full, unmerged, with explicit confidence intervals, and no single synthesized recommendation document may be issued. Proponents say this prevents false precision and protects future boards from hidden model risk. Opponents say it institutionalizes paralysis, allowing contractors to exploit contradictory expert judgments and delaying construction past the next storm season.

Draft the board's formal decision memo, addressed to the regional authority and due before the fiscal deadline. Recommend a concrete course of action—approve Design A, approve Design B, approve one with the modesty clause attached, reject both pending further review, or another clearly defined option—and provide reasoning the authority and the researchers could actually respond to. Your memo should apply the distinction between inside-view and outside-view reasoning directly to this comparison, not merely summarize the concepts in the abstract. It should also exhibit genuine epistemic modesty: rather than declaring one methodological family globally superior, it should argue for how this particular board ought to weight them, given that the board itself lacks direct technical expertise and must rely on contested expert judgment. Finally, it should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly—whether that is the risk of false synthesis, the danger of decision paralysis, or the possibility that both models share a hidden structural assumption.

Write the memo in polished American English, in a formal institutional voice. Do not include footnotes, bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholars, texts, or studies. The document should be self-contained and actionable.
