I have been trying to understand Black's two-sphere universe in light of Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. The spheres share every intrinsic property and differ only in the relational feature of being two miles apart; if such spatial relations are not genuinely intrinsic to either sphere but belong to the system they form, then they seem to share all properties and Black's scenario actually confirms rather than refutes the principle. So why do philosophers treat it as a decisive counterexample? And if we push this relationalist reading further—removing any external coordinate frame so that the separation is defined only relative to the spheres themselves—doesn't the universe collapse into a description of a single sphere whose state is duplicated within the same region, which would imply that saving the identity of indiscernibles requires smuggling in an observer-dependent frame?
