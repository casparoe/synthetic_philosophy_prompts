Most scholars treat Kierkegaard's critique in his 1846 literary review of modernity as a broad sociological complaint about mass media producing apathy through "levelling," but I suspect the real philosophical target is more specific. I propose to examine whether his critique depends on a particular temporal mechanism—what I shall call "delayed simultaneity," in which print culture allows individuals to hold opinions about events without being physically or ethically present to them, thereby severing commitment from exposure. The further, more niche claim is that this mechanism is not unique to the press. If I can demonstrate that an analogous structure of mediated absence operates in Kierkegaard's pseudonymous treatments of the theatre crowd and the church congregation, then "the public" is not merely a media-specific phenomenon but an institutional form of anonymous presence that can migrate across different social contexts. This would imply that existing readings, which treat the press critique as either purely theological or purely sociological, leave underspecified the very mechanism that makes "the public" philosophically dangerous.
