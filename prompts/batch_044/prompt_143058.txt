Since retiring from a career in condensed-matter physics, I have been reading Schrödinger's later philosophical essays—particularly What Is Life? and My View of the World—alongside introductory work in philosophy of mind, and I am attempting to formulate a coherent stance on consciousness, evolutionary explanation, and clinical ethics. Before presenting these ideas to a discussion group, I want a consistency audit of the following commitments, which I take to be largely defensible but whose interrelations I do not fully trust.

First, I endorse Schrödinger's Vedantic interpretation of consciousness: the doctrine of advaita is metaphysically correct, in that there exists exactly one universal subject of experience, and the multiplicity of individual minds is analogous to the multiplicity of waves on a single ocean—an appearance without ultimate separateness. Second, I do not think this conflicts with natural science if we adopt a neutral-monist framing: physical structures, including neural dynamics, can be understood as the structural manifestations of this single consciousness, comparable in some respects to how a crystal lattice manifests underlying quantum symmetries without being reducible to them. Third, evolutionary biology offers a complete causal explanation of why organisms develop robust self-boundaries and self-models: the belief in being a distinct, persistent self is an adaptive fiction selected for survival, which implies that our intuitive moral judgments—especially those involving interpersonal harm—are systematically shaped by a biologically useful deception. Fourth, from the unity thesis it follows that the distinction between killing and letting die is metaphysically empty, since there is only one consciousness to be affected and active versus passive causation therefore lacks ethical weight. Fifth, despite this collapse, I want to defend a robust principle of patient autonomy in clinical settings, including the right to refuse life-sustaining treatment, on the grounds that honoring the patient's experienced separateness is not a mere concession to illusion but a direct expression of the universal consciousness respecting its own particular modes of appearance. Sixth, I accept reflective equilibrium as the proper method in ethics, but I propose that we should begin not from raw intuitions about harm—which are corrupted by the evolutionary fiction—but from the metaphysical fact of unity and derive practical norms downward.

I am particularly uncertain about three pairs. Between the second and third commitments, there may be only an apparent tension: physical manifestation and evolutionary deception might be compatible descriptions at different levels, or they might genuinely pull apart if neutral monism cannot accommodate an evolutionary account of illusion. Between the third and sixth, I worry that reflective equilibrium cannot get off the ground if all our starting intuitions are unreliable, yet I also think the downward derivation avoids this problem—though I am not sure whether that revision is consistent with calling the method reflective equilibrium at all. Between the fourth and fifth, I sense the deepest conflict: if killing and letting die are identical, it is unclear why individual refusal should have special moral force, yet I want to preserve autonomy without abandoning the unity thesis. Please rank these tensions by severity, and for each indicate precisely what must be surrendered—whether a metaphysical claim, a methodological commitment, or a practical norm—rather than listing them as equally damaging. I do not need references or a reading list; I need an audit of whether this particular constellation of views can be held together, and if not, what the least costly revision looks like.
