Consider the divergence between Germany’s completed nuclear exit in 2023 and France’s decision to maintain, extend, and in some proposals expand its nuclear fleet as a pillar of decarbonization. Both are wealthy constitutional democracies confronting climate urgency and supply insecurity, yet one treats commercial nuclear power as a moral hazard that democratic societies should not inherit, while the other treats it as indispensable public infrastructure. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments that make each posture coherent: what view of catastrophic risk and intergenerational obligation does Germany’s exit presuppose—does it treat the initiation of a technology with millennial-scale waste and low-probability, high-consequence failure modes as generating an indefinite, non-delegable duty of care that future citizens should not be forced to manage? Conversely, what does France’s retention presuppose about the institutional containment of risk, about the moral priority of near-term climate mitigation over tail-risk catastrophe, and about the state’s authority to bind future generations to complex technological systems?

Then probe a deeper tension. Does Germany’s policy implicitly commit to a principle of technological reversibility—the idea that democratic legitimacy requires preserving future generations’ capacity to abandon or radically restructure inherited infrastructures—while treating nuclear power as uniquely irreversible? Or is the divergence better explained by different weightings of shared values (precaution, climate urgency, energy autonomy) rather than by genuinely incompatible philosophies?

Also examine the idea of energy sovereignty. In this debate it is usually framed as geopolitical independence, but it can also be read as a philosophical claim about whether a polity’s energy metabolism is merely instrumental to politics or partly constitutive of its form of self-determination. Do the two national practices presuppose different answers to whether large-scale energy infrastructure shapes the kind of democracy that can be exercised?

Distinguish, in your answer, what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates or could be reconciled with. Say whether the divergence is philosophically underdetermined—whether, for instance, a consistent precautionary framework could license retention under robust expert governance, or whether a climate-first framework could justify exit if renewable scaling were faster. Note where philosophers and risk theorists genuinely disagree, and do not present either national model as the obvious rational default.
