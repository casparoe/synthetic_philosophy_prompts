You run a 90-minute upper-level seminar on value theory with 12 students. Design a structured deliberation that makes them feel incommensurability—not just a hard choice, but the failure of rational comparison across value domains—before the concept is named. Divide them into three councils of four, each assigned a distinct framework: stewardship of irreplaceable place, distributive fairness in basic capabilities, and generational duty to specific lineages. Present a concrete collective decision: choosing one of three development plans for a resettled community, where each plan fully honors one framework and severely violates the other two. Give each council a non-convertible token system representing its internal goods, with an explicit rule that any attempt to translate tokens into a common scale collapses the system. Introduce a “Mirror Rule” at the 40-minute mark: each council must defend its plan using only the vocabulary and criteria of a different council; if they cannot translate their core reason without betrayal, they must record it as an “unrepresentable loss” rather than a weighted cost. Specify the timeline (opening framing, deliberation, mirror round, final vote by secret ballot with symbolic stakes—e.g., each student stakes one “intellectual reputation point” they can lose), the physical materials (framework cards, plan briefs, token trays, loss-record sheets), and the debrief. The debrief should map moments in the activity onto the philosophical claim that incommensurable values resist rational arbitration; predict what will go wrong the first time (students will try to invent a meta-language or hidden exchange rate and must be blocked by the facilitator); be frank about which feature the activity cannot reproduce (the lived, retrospective regret of having lived through an unchosen life) and must be supplied by framing; and say what participants should come away seeing—that compromise can be a form of corruption rather than rational settlement, and that the absence of a common metric is not merely an epistemic gap but a structural feature of the conflict.
