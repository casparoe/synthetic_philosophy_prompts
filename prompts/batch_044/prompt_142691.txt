I'm taking a course on philosophy of history and we had a seminar on whether history moves in circles or in a straight line. My tutor summarised the cyclical camp like this:

"Polybius thought constitutions go round in a fixed loop — monarchy to tyranny to aristocracy to democracy to mob rule and back — mainly because human nature never changes, but he also said geography fixes the speed of the rotation. Ibn Khaldun in the Muqaddimah said groups carry this asabiyyah energy that burns bright for about three generations then dies, so every empire collapses into tribalism like clockwork. Spengler in The Decline of the West treated cultures as plants that all sprout, flower, wither and die on the same timetable, and he literally mapped Rome’s fall onto ours point-for-point. Toynbee in A Study of History just copied the same loop but called it challenge-and-response, except he insisted every civilisation eventually rots from inside because the creative minority becomes a dominant minority and stops answering."

Then the tutor said linear thinkers like Marx or Hegel reject all this because they see history as one-directional progress, but actually Toynbee was linear too because he thought religions might carry civilisation forward forever if they adapt right. So the whole cyclical vs linear split is a bit of a false dichotomy, really just a disagreement about whether the loop can be broken.

Is that a fair summary? I feel like something’s off with the Polybius-geography bit and maybe Toynbee is being sqeezed into the loop unfairly. Can you go through it — not necessarily every sentence, but flag which thinkers are being misrepresented, which parts are roughly right, and whether the tutor’s conclusion that the split is false actually follows from the accurate bits?
