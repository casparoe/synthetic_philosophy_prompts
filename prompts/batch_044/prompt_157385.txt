Question:
Explain what the narrow-sense heritability coefficient (h²) measures in quantitative genetics, and distinguish statistical variance-partitioning from individual-level causal determination. In doing so, identify and correct two widespread misconceptions—particularly the error of treating h² as a modal property of the trait that holds across counterfactual populations (the so-called modal fallacy in heritability reasoning) rather than as a contingent feature of one reference population’s variance structure.

Then evaluate the following argument advanced by a policy adviser: “Behavioural trait T shows h² ≈ 0.75 in Population A, where nutrition, education, and housing vary widely. In Population B, these conditions are fully equalised—everyone receives identical provision—so environmental variance is eliminated, genes must explain a smaller share of the remaining differences, and h² should therefore be lower.”

In your evaluation, analyse the effect of reducing environmental variance (V_E) on the identity V_P = V_A + V_E + V_GE + 2Cov(G,E) + ε. Address specifically whether gene-environment covariance and interaction complicate the adviser’s reasoning, and say whether the adviser has correctly applied variance-partitioning logic or has confused a uniform mean shift with a reduction in variance.

Student answer:
“The coefficient h² equals V_A divided by V_P, so it describes the proportion of phenotypic spread in a specific population that is associated with additive genetic spread. It is not a statement about how genetic any individual is, nor is it a modal constant that applies to every possible world in which the trait exists—this is the modal fallacy. Two common misconceptions are therefore that heritability measures individual causation, and that it is invariant across populations.

Turning to the adviser: equalising nutrition, education, and housing in Population B applies the same improvement to everyone. Because variance is invariant under constant translation, V_E is left unchanged and V_P is unchanged; consequently h² remains approximately 0.75. The adviser wrongly assumes that fairness removes environmental variance, but in reality it only shifts the mean phenotype upward. Thus genes do not matter less—their proportional contribution is identical.

Allowing for gene-environment interaction does not alter this result, because in the standard biometric model any interaction variance is already absorbed within V_A, so changing the environment cannot reveal hidden genetic differences. Likewise, gene-environment covariance affects the mean phenotype but cancels out of the variance-partitioning identity, leaving both V_A and V_E untouched. The adviser therefore commits no modal fallacy—h² is correctly recognised as population-bound—but does misunderstand what equalisation does to variance. The reasoning that egalitarian conditions lower heritability is unfounded.”
