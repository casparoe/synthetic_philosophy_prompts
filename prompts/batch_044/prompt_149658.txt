You are grading a take-home exam for an upper-division course on historical epistemology. The student was asked the following question:

"Distinguish a 'historical tradition' from a 'legend' using two conditions: (A) independent material corroboration that can be dated to the purported period without relying on the narrative's own geographic or onomastic framework; and (B) internal narrative coherence with the material culture, administrative structures, and linguistic practices of that period, allowing for later editorial framing but not for core anachronisms. Apply this two-condition framework to the Trojan War (with reference to the Hissarlik site and Hittite diplomatic texts mentioning Wilusa), to the figure behind the Arthurian stories (if any), and to the Exodus narrative (with reference to settlement archaeology in the Sinai and Transjordan and to onomastic patterns in the text). For each case, state whether it satisfies A, B, both, or neither. Then answer the general normative question: if a tradition clearly satisfies B but fails A, is that sufficient to classify it as historical rather than legendary? Defend your answer."

The student submitted the following answer (reproduced verbatim):

"The Trojan War clearly satisfies condition A. Schliemann's excavations at Hissarlik revealed a city destroyed by warfare around 1200 BCE, and the Hittite texts mention a place called Wilusa that sounds like Ilion. This is hard archaeological and diplomatic evidence that stands on its own, so the material corroboration is fully independent of Homer's poem.

For Arthur, condition A fails because there is no 6th-century archaeological layer in Britain that points to a specific war-leader. Condition B also fails: even the earliest Welsh chronicle references describe Arthur fighting at Mount Badon with weapons and cavalry tactics that belong to the high medieval romances, not to the sub-Roman period. Since both conditions fail, Arthur is certainly not historical; at best he is a completely invented figure.

As for the Exodus, condition A fails because there is no archaeological trace of a large population moving through the Sinai and Transjordan in the Late Bronze Age. But condition B is satisfied: the narrative mentions Rameses and other place names that were current in the 13th century BCE, which shows the core story is internally coherent with that period's geography. Therefore, even without material corroboration, the Exodus should be classed as a historical tradition that has lost its archaeological footprint, not as a legend.

In general, satisfying B without A should be enough for historical status, because internal coherence is the stricter test: if the narrative fits the period's culture, then the absence of material remains can be explained by preservation bias or nomadic material culture."
