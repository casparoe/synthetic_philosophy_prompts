In my graduate seminar on the ethics of armed conflict, the discussion of the moral equality of combatants has reached what feels like a structural impasse rather than a simple failure of imagination. On one side, defenders of the traditional view—roughly in the Walzerian spirit—maintain that just and unjust combatants share an equal moral permission to kill one another in war. They argue through three familiar moves: the separation of jus ad bellum from jus in bello; the claim that individual soldiers typically lack the epistemic access needed to judge the justice of their state's cause; and the idea that combatants act within an institutional role that distributes moral responsibility upward rather than downward onto each fighter. On the other side, revisionists—following lines associated with philosophers such as Jeff McMahan—deny that any of this generates symmetry. They hold that liability to defensive killing tracks individual contribution to an unjust threat; therefore unjust combatants are not permitted to kill just ones, just combatants are not required to spare unjust ones, and talk of equal rights obscures real moral differences that should govern conduct on the ground.

What makes the stall peculiar is that neither camp seems to misunderstand the other. The Walzerians answer the revisionist charge that epistemic humility cannot license killing by saying it does not license killing per se, but rather undermines the kind of individualized moral judgment that revisionism requires, leaving institutional rules as the only workable framework for organizing lethal force. The revisionists, in turn, answer the institutional-role objection by conceding that states may organize armies, but deny that organizational structure can launder individual culpability into collective innocence. For years our seminar has rotated through these same exchanges—epistemic limits versus liability tracking, collective authorization versus personal moral agency—without anyone conceding even a minor point. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about the empirical facts of war or about the concept of liability itself, but about whether the question "May this soldier kill that soldier?" should be answered at the level of first-order interpersonal morality or at the level of second-order institutional design. The Walzerian may be defending a regulatory framework that makes war governable, while the revisionist insists on the underlying moral reality that any framework must ultimately answer to.

But I want a sharper diagnosis than this framing suggests. Is this genuinely two different questions being asked under the single heading moral equality of combatants? Is there a hidden verbal dispute about whether permission means all-things-considered justified versus not liable to punishment or sanction? Or is there a substantive disagreement masked by shared vocabulary—perhaps about whether moral reasons can be structurally distributed across institutions in a way that genuinely changes the moral status of individuals within them, a possibility that neither side has fully articulated? Given your diagnosis, what would actually move the needle: a formal model of distributed responsibility, a conceptual distinction between institutional authorization and interpersonal justification, an empirical study of how combatants actually reason about orders, or should we conclude that the framing is ill-posed and abandon it altogether? Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each side actually says rather than surveying the literature, and tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress.
