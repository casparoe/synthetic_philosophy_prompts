I am currently taking a final-year module on moral philosophy and historical change, and in preparing an essay I have become stuck on the precise relationship between Bernard Williams’s scepticism about rational moral progress and Kwame Anthony Appiah’s account of honour-driven transformation in The Honor World. Appiah argues that duelling disappeared not because participants were suddenly persuaded that killing was immoral, but because honour itself was reconstructed—partly through the state’s monopoly on legitimate violence and partly through egalitarian norms that made aristocratic honour codes appear ridiculous. Williams, by contrast, insists that such shifts are usually better explained by changes in power and material interest, with moral arguments serving largely as post-hoc rationalisations. Here is my confusion: if honour is a social institution sustained by specific power relations and economic conditions, then when those conditions shift, honour must shift with them. Does this not imply that Appiah’s “honour mechanism” is really just Williams’s “interest and power” mechanism operating at a finer grain of social description? In other words, is there any historical case in which honour genuinely functions as an independent causal force—perhaps through what we might call an internal logic of recognition—rather than as the ideological clothing of deeper structural changes? If not, should we conclude that every moral revolution, including the abolition of slavery, is ultimately explicable without any genuine reference to normative insight whatsoever?

I should be very grateful for a response in British English that addresses whether Appiah offers a genuinely distinct explanation from Williams, or whether his honour thesis collapses into the very materialism he seems to resist, and that also clarifies whether there is a principled way to distinguish honour operating autonomously from honour serving merely as epiphenomenal dressing for shifts in interest.
