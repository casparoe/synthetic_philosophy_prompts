As a novelist tracing the moment when a people’s inner convictions become the hidden scaffold of their factories and banks, I find myself returning to Weber’s old puzzle: can culture genuinely cause economic change, or does it only provide the moral costume for forces that are really structural? I wish to examine this through the South Korean industrial transformation from Park Chung-hee’s 1961 coup to the democratic transition of 1987—the period commonly called the Miracle on the Han River—not through Weber’s Protestant Europe, but as a parallel and perhaps corrective case.

The familiar narrative invokes Confucian values—hierarchy, educational zeal, collective duty—to explain rapid growth. The rival narrative invokes authoritarian state planning, American strategic investment, export discipline enforced by coercion, and the suppression of labour dissent. I ask you to analyse this episode through Weber’s thesis, particularly through the concept of elective affinity between an ethical system and economic conduct, and through the philosophical problem of explaining with values: whether values can be causal agents rather than post-hoc rationalisations of structural force.

To sharpen the analysis, please distinguish two roles that Confucian culture might have played. The first is a propulsive mechanism, actively generating the behaviours—frugality, deference to industrial authority, willingness to defer consumption—that produced growth. The second is a permissive frame, removing moral objections to growth driven by state violence and external capital without itself supplying the motive force. Map which features of the Korean episode—Park’s Saemaul Movement, the export-led model, the American security umbrella, the violent suppression of union organisation—fit each role, and say where Weber’s framework illuminates them and where it falls silent or misdescribes them, especially regarding coercion and geopolitical luck.

Then turn the case back upon the theory: does the Korean trajectory confirm that culture can be a cause, undermine it by showing that structural and coercive factors were sufficient, or reveal that the distinction between culture and structure collapses in practice? I want a disciplined mapping—who acts, what values are at stake, what counts as the causal pathway—not an undisciplined assertion that Confucianism explains everything. Please offer no bibliographies, no reading lists, and no citations; I am after the conceptual architecture, not the scholarly apparatus.
