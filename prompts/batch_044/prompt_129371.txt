Assume the role of a senior philosopher of cognitive science advising a three-year conceptual research program that has no empirical laboratory budget—only philosophical analysis, conceptual modeling, and dialogue with cognitive architects. Your task is not to summarize existing literature but to set a defensible agenda: within the conceptual space opened by Daniel Dennett's fourfold taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—which unresolved questions about the boundaries, intermediate architectures, and normative implications of these mental kinds should receive the most sustained philosophical attention?

In particular, evaluate three specific lines of work and rank them in order of philosophical urgency. First, whether the transition from Popperian to Gregorian cognition requires language in a strong, symbolic sense or can instead be achieved through distributed, artifact-mediated reasoning that never internalizes a grammar. Second, whether Darwinian and Skinnerian mechanisms can be genuinely hybridized within a single architecture without the system collapsing conceptually into one category or the other. Third, what recognizing a Gregorian-level system—whether biological, collective, or synthetic—implies for our obligations and epistemic expectations toward such entities. Defend your ordering by identifying upstream dependencies: which answers must be settled before the others become tractable, how tractable each is for philosophers working without neuroscience, and what specific conceptual result (not a citation, bibliography, or empirical dataset) would cause you to reorder the priorities. Before delivering the ranking, please engage in brief analytical brainstorming: map how each mental kind constrains the others, note where Dennett's original framing may obscure rather than illuminate current questions about artificial and non-human cognition, and identify one hidden assumption that, if rejected, would reshuffle your agenda entirely.
