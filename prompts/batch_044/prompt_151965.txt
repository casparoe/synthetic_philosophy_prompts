I am preparing a seminar on democratic legitimacy, and my students and I have been working through the boundary problem—specifically, the question of who belongs to the demos and by what principle we draw that line. Rather than stay at the level of abstraction, I want to press the issue through a concrete institutional episode: the 2016 United Kingdom referendum on membership in the European Union. The franchise rules are well known but worth stating precisely: British citizens and qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK were eligible; EU nationals resident in the UK were generally excluded, with the exception of citizens of Ireland, Malta, and Cyprus; and British citizens who had resided outside the UK for more than fifteen years were also disenfranchised. The outcome, of course, reshaped conditions not only for those who voted but for long-term residents and future cohorts who had no formal voice.

My request is that you analyze this referendum as a case study for the boundary problem, but with a conceptual framing I want you to treat with care: distinguish between synchronic membership—those presently subject to the state's coercive authority—and diachronic membership—those whose fundamental life prospects will be substantially shaped by the decision across its relevant temporal horizon. Map this distinction onto the actual franchise exclusions and inclusions. Does the synchronic principle justify excluding long-settled EU residents who were fully subject to UK law? Does the diachronic principle require including future generations or expatriates whose interests were profoundly affected? Where does the theory illuminate the institutional design, and where does it fall silent or produce results that seem implausible? And what does the case, in turn, reveal about the boundary problem—does it suggest that the core difficulty is not merely locating a boundary, but justifying the binding authority of a synchronic demos over a diachronic population?

Please be disciplined in the mapping: specify who counts as the affected or subjected party, what relation grounds membership, and where you are forcing the theory to speak beyond its standard resources. I am not asking for a generic commentary on Brexit politics, but for a rigorous two-directional analysis in which the theory illuminates the episode and the episode tests the theory's limits.
