Consider a rehabilitation-resource allocation protocol used in some public-health triage systems. Patients are scored on a cardinal quality-of-life scale running from 0 to 1. The protocol applies a convex weighting function: an improvement of 0.1 for a patient currently at 0.2 counts twice as heavily toward the allocation score as the identical 0.1 improvement for a patient at 0.6. Administrators defend the rule as giving more weight to the worst off, not as egalitarian, since it assigns no special value to raising everyone equally when all remain below a threshold. Critics reply that this exposes a leveling-down vulnerability: if resources could either lift one patient from 0.2 to 0.4 or lift two patients from 0.5 to 0.55, the protocol strongly favors the first option; yet if the choice were between leaving both patients at 0.5 or lowering both to 0.4 so they become equally badly off, the protocol supplies no reason to resist the downward equalization—suggesting that equality plays no independent normative role.

What theory of distributive value does this scoring practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit normative framework: does the convex transformation strictly commit to prioritarianism, or is the same weighting formula compatible with sufficientarian or relational-egalitarian justifications? Does the reliance on cardinal, interpersonally comparable quality-of-life scores presuppose a specific meta-ethical view about the measurability of well-being, and is that view consistent with the protocol's silence on equality-based reasons? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, and say what would have to change—both in the weighting formula and in the eligibility rules—if the underlying view were replaced by an explicit prioritarian rule that rejects leveling-down altogether, or by an egalitarian rule that treats equality itself as intrinsically valuable regardless of absolute levels.
