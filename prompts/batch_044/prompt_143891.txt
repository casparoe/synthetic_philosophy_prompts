I'm drafting a long-form piece on the politics of memory and I want to check if my own commitments actually hang together before I file. Here's my working profile: I think Margalit is right that societies carry a shared duty to remember atrocity—it's a political obligation, not just private grief. At the same time I feel Rieff's point that compulsive remembrance can become a kind of cultural neurosis, and that forgetting is sometimes the healthier social choice, especially for survivors who don't want to be turned into permanent monuments. I also think hte real problem now isn't human forgetting but the fact that digital archives never decay, so the ethical battle has shifted to who controls the archival layer and who can demand erasure from it. I want to treat forgetting as an active practice—curated absence, not just data loss—because communities need that space for cohesion. To bridge the gap between collective duty and individual refusal I came up with this idea I call asymmetric consent: society must keep the record alive, but the individual can opt out of being its medium—refusing interviews, blocking archival ingestion, etc.

Can you audit this? Which pairs are in real tension, which only look tense, and rank them by severity. For each tension, what's the cheapest fix—do I weaken Rieff, drop the archival-layer framing, or kill asymmetric consent? And if I had to make just one revision to clean up the worst conflicts, what should it be? I don't want a lecture on memory ethics; I want an audit of my own set.
