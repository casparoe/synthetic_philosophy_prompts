Germany’s nuclear exit framework fixes reactor shutdown dates by statute and prohibits license renewal regardless of updated safety assessments, whereas France’s regulator extends operating licenses in decadal increments contingent on revised probabilistic risk analyses—both under comparable climate targets. What philosophical commitments about the nature of technological risk, intergenerational obligation, and epistemic authority does this institutional divergence presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory embedded in the calendar-certain exit: does it treat catastrophic risk as accumulating irreversibly over chronological time in a way that probabilistic updates cannot redeem, or does it encode a deontological constraint against imposing long-lived waste burdens on future generations regardless of emission benefits? Say whether this practice strictly requires that view or is compatible with rival consequentialist or precautionary frameworks, distinguish what the legal design presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and specify what would have to change in institutional decision rights—between legislatures and technical regulators—if a polity adopted the French-style probabilistic model of risk governance without altering its energy mix.
