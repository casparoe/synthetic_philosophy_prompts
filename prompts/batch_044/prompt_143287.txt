For Rahman, asking "who started it" is structurally analogous to asking which party first violated a contract: it isolates the agent whose choices transformed possibility into actuality, and to abandon the question is to allow structural explanations to function as excuses for deliberate aggression. She insists that moral responsibility does not dissolve merely because an environment is complex, and she demands that Whitfield specify what he would say to a Polish or Hungarian citizen who asks why their democratic institutions were crushed if not by a specific agent's decision.

He accepts that Stalin acted aggressively and that Truman pursued containment, but he argues that the Cold War was overdetermined by the collapse of multipolarity and the security dilemma. For Whitfield, the relevant distinction is not between innocent and guilty parties but between "trigger causation" (the specific decision that crossed a threshold) and "systemic causation" (the conditions that made that decision rational and inevitable). He challenges Rahman to specify a counterfactual world in which Stalin behaved differently—say, by permitting free elections in Poland—yet the structural conflict still failed to materialise; if she cannot, he claims, her attribution depends on an impossible standard of "counterfactual sovereignty" that no international actor ever possessed.
