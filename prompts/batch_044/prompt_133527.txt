I am examining an emerging certification norm in AI safety governance: a protocol requires a system to generate its own proof of harmlessness through an internal reasoning trace, which is then evaluated by the same underlying architecture with only a prompt-level role change—generator versus evaluator—before any external auditor may examine the output. The framework treats this self-evaluative loop as a necessary filter, yet it never demands independent, non-circular justification for the loop’s reliability. Given the Löbian obstacle—that an agent capable of formalizing its own soundness can derive arbitrary conclusions—what theory of self-trust does this practice presuppose? Does it implicitly assume that architectural role-partitioning circumvents the obstacle, or that self-trust is merely a pragmatic heuristic rather than a rational entitlement? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: distinguish whether the protocol strictly requires a solution to Löb’s problem, whether it remains compatible with the view that no agent can rationally endorse its own reasoning without outside corroboration, and specify what would have to change in the certification design if one adopted that stricter, externally-dependent view of rational self-trust.
