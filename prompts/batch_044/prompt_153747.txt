As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been thinking about what makes an action morally worthy rather than merely correct, especially when reasoning is delegated to habits, tools, or prior design choices. Here are my verdicts on five concrete situations, and I am struggling to see the unifying principle.

1. A developer implements end-to-end encryption in a messaging app only because a security audit requires it; she never considers user privacy. The act is right, but I judge her to have little moral worth.

2. A developer refuses to add a deceptive dark-pattern to a checkout flow because he vividly imagines his own parents being misled. He acts from that empathic identification, not from a code of conduct. I judge this highly worthy.

3. A developer writes an open-source data-sanitization tool for fun; years later it is adopted to protect refugee identities. At the time of writing she had no ethical aim. I think the later good outcome does not confer moral worth on her original act.

4. A developer once carefully reasoned through the ethics of algorithmic transparency, then built an automated CI gate that blocks opaque models. For five years the gate runs without her revisiting the reasoning; she maintains it mechanically. The blocks are correct, and I feel she retains significant moral worth—her present action is still guided by her earlier, genuine reasoning, now cached like a memoized function.

5. Another developer copies the exact same CI gate from a repository she does not understand. It blocks the same opaque models, but she has never engaged with the underlying ethical argument. I judge her to have almost no moral worth, despite identical outcomes and behavior.

Cases 4 and 5 feel very different to me, yet both involve correct action executed through an automated system with no present moral reasoning. I cannot reconcile why caching my own prior reasoning should preserve worth if borrowing an unexamined tool should not—unless the principle is about historical ownership, but that seems arbitrary.

Could you state a precise principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? For each principle, say which clause is motivated by which case, what it predicts for a case I have not listed (for instance: a developer who disables the gate after forgetting why it was installed, but leaves it running out of laziness), and be explicit about which of my five verdicts—especially the tension between case 4 and case 5—must be overturned to preserve simplicity. I am not looking for citations or a reading list, just the conceptual reconstruction.
