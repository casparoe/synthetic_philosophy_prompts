I was talking recently with two friends—Marcus and Lena—about how much ordinary people should hide from their government, and they ended up in a sharp disagreement that I couldn't settle. I'd like an outside adjudication of the actual moves they made.

Marcus started by saying citizens should aim for maximum opacity. He'd read about the German principle of data minimization—Datensparsamkeit—and thinks it should be the universal standard. To him, American culture treats people as "default-public": your location, purchases, and social ties are just out there for corporations and agencies to harvest. He sees Chinese social credit not as some alien system but as the logical endpoint of transparency culture—once you accept that the state should see you clearly, there's no principled line that stops it from scoring you.

Lena pushed back hard. She said total opacity is a luxury good. Wealthy people hire lawyers and build shell structures to hide; regular people can't. So "maximum opacity" actually produces what she called "asymmetric opacity"—the powerful disappear, while the poor stay exposed. She argued that Chinese social credit is horrifying not because visibility itself is evil, but because it's one-directional: the state sees citizens, yet citizens can't see or correct the state's algorithms. She then introduced "civic legibility"—the idea that democracy needs a shared baseline of citizen information so collective decisions about health, infrastructure, and taxes can be made fairly, not arbitrarily.

In the second round, Marcus introduced what he called "informational self-ownership." He said your behavioral data—movement patterns, friendships, consumption—isn't just information about you; it's part of your narrative identity. If the state collects it, it isn't observing you, it's co-authoring your biography. He insisted on a bright line between "functional visibility" (legal identity, taxes, voting) and "behavioral visibility" (everything else). Only the first is legitimate for a state to demand.

Lena agreed the concept sounded right but asked where the line actually sits. She pointed out that modern governance can't cleanly separate the two: your movement patterns determine tax zones and public-health jurisdictions; your social networks affect epidemiological tracking; your consumption drives environmental regulation. She also noted that German protection works partly because Germany pairs citizen privacy with strong state-transparency laws—citizens can inspect and correct government files. In the U.S., she said, corporations often see more than the state, and citizens lack tools to correct either. So comparing countries isn't about opacity alone; it's about "directionality"—who controls the flow, and whether it's reciprocal.

In the third round, Marcus said Lena's "civic legibility" was a slippery slope. Once you grant that the state needs behavioral visibility for collective goods, you've opened the door to social credit. He admitted the line is hard to draw in practice, but insisted it's essential as a norm: functional data serves legitimate administration; behavioral data serves control.

Lena replied that Marcus was drawing a line technology had already erased. She didn't defend Chinese social credit, but said pretending there's a clean boundary lets Americans ignore how corporate and state surveillance already merge. The real question, she argued, isn't "how much should we hide?" but "under what conditions is visibility legitimate?"—conditions that include reciprocity, correctability, and democratic authorization. Without those, both American openness and Chinese social credit fail, just in different directions.

Evaluate the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

What's missing from both sides (around 200 words): Identify at least one important consideration neither Marcus nor Lena raised—perhaps whether the scale of government changes the calculus, whether non-state actors alter the moral of hiding, or whether there is a meaningful difference between being seen and being scored.

4. Overall verdict (around 150 words): Say who wins on balance, but specify exactly which of their individual claims survive and which don't. Then sketch what the correct position looks like once the best points from both sides are combined.
