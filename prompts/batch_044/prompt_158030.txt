I have recently turned to philosophy after a career in the natural sciences, and I am trying to organize my views on social choice and individual liberty. I hold the following commitments regarding Sen's liberal paradox and its broader implications, but I am uncertain whether they form a jointly tenable package. I would appreciate an audit: which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, and what the least costly revision would be in each case. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging.

(1) I accept Sen's impossibility result as rigorously established: with unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism—each individual having decisive authority over at least one pair of social alternatives—no social decision rule can satisfy all three. I do not regard this as an artifact of the formalism, whether of assuming complete and transitive preferences or of restricting the analysis to two agents and three outcomes.

(2) I treat the weak Pareto principle as a minimal rationality constraint that is not negotiable. If every member of a society strictly prefers outcome x to outcome y, and no one is made worse off by selecting x, then a collective mechanism that selects y is defective in a manner analogous to a physical theory that violates a conservation law.

(3) At the same time, I take minimal liberal rights to possess intrinsic, non-instrumental value. There are pairs of states over which the affected individual alone should have decisive authority; for a collective rule to override that authority is a direct wrong to the agent, not merely an instrumental welfare loss.

(4) I believe the paradox is best resolved by restricting the domain of admissible preference profiles—specifically by excluding preferences that are "meddlesome," i.e., those that concern how other individuals exercise choice within their protected spheres—rather than by weakening either liberalism or Pareto.

(5) Drawing on my scientific background, I think the distinction between meddlesome and self-regarding preferences is objective rather than culturally relative or subjectively assigned. I propose that it can be formalized through a causal-intervention criterion of the kind used in structural causal modeling: a preference is self-regarding with respect to an agent's protected sphere if, holding fixed the exogenous causal parents of that agent's welfare, the agent's ranking depends only on states within that sphere.

(6) I want a single, unified framework for collective decision-making—applicable to economic allocation, political choice, and personal domains—without domain-specific exemptions, special pleading, or adjustments appended after the fact. The domain restriction should emerge as a structural feature of the preference space itself, not as an external patch.

(7) I also suspect that the conflict between liberalism and Pareto is in some sense emergent: in societies below a certain scale (few agents, few alternatives) the paradoxical preference profiles do not arise naturally, and the two principles coexist without friction; only beyond a critical threshold does the contradiction become robust. This suggests the tension is a collective phenomenon rather than a deep normative contradiction at the individual level.

My particular concerns are these: Can (5) and (6) be sustained together without circularity, given that applying the causal-intervention criterion seems to require a prior assignment of protected spheres—the very thing that minimal liberalism is supposed to establish? And does (7) undermine the absoluteness I assign to Pareto in (2), or is there a way to treat the conflict as scale-dependent without relaxing the rationality constraint? Please address these directly, but also trace any other hidden tensions in the set.
