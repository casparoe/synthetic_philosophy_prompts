Here is my situation. I am a doctoral student in sociology, serving on the organizing committee for a small, invitation-only interdisciplinary workshop on qualitative research methods. Two months ago we invited a senior scholar to deliver the keynote, selecting her solely on the strength of her recent empirical work, which is directly relevant and methodologically rigorous. Last week, a former student released excerpts from lecture recordings made eight years prior. In them, the scholar ridicules interpretive and ethnographic methods as “intellectual theater” and implies that scholars who practice them are either dishonest or intellectually incompetent. Several committee members—especially those whose own research falls within the traditions she mocked—are now insisting that we rescind her invitation. They argue that allowing her to speak would make our workshop an accomplice to a culture of dismissal, and that withdrawing the invitation is not an act of censorship but a legitimate social sanction: a community defining its boundaries by refusing to extend its platform to someone who has demonstrated open contempt for its members. Other committee members respond that this reasoning treats an intellectual invitation as a reward for moral character rather than a judgment of present contribution; they warn that using our gatekeeping power to settle a moral score over old, unrelated speech collapses scholarly exchange into a system of status punishment, and that the proposed exclusion is disproportionate because the remarks, however cruel, have no connection to the work she was asked to present.

I am genuinely divided. The old remarks strike me as wrong and harmful. Yet I also feel uneasy about deploying the workshop’s institutional authority as an instrument of moral punishment, particularly because the proposed sanction seems designed less to protect anyone present than to perform our collective disapproval. At the same time, I worry that my discomfort may reflect an unjustified neutrality: perhaps social sanctions are legitimate precisely because they are not state penalties, and communities have every right to deny cooperation to those who have shown bad faith toward them.
