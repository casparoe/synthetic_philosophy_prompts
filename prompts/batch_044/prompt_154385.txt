For my high school philosophy of science seminar, I am writing about the disagreement between etiological and causal-role theories of biological function, and I want to understand the conceptual tension rather than just memorize textbook examples like hearts and kidneys. To push past the standard illustrations, consider this hybrid case: a bacterium is engineered in a lab so that one specific metabolic pathway produces an enzyme essential for survival in the lab environment; after accidental release into the wild, natural selection favors an entirely different mutation that creates a second pathway, which eventually becomes the primary source of energy, while the original engineered pathway remains active and still makes a measurable causal contribution to metabolism, even though it was never naturally selected for in evolutionary history and is now redundant for survival. Would an etiological theorist say the original pathway has lost its biological function entirely, or would they distinguish between its historical "design function" and its current biological status? Conversely, would a causal-role theorist assign it a genuine present function solely on the basis of its ongoing causal contribution, regardless of its evolutionary origin and despite its redundancy? More fundamentally, does this overlap of artificial origin, evolutionary drift, and present causal contribution show that the two accounts are simply answering different questions—one about historical explanation and one about system analysis—or does it reveal a deeper normative disagreement about whether a function must be beneficial to the organism's survival? Please explain the conceptual issue at stake, particularly whether allowing engineered selection and natural selection to coincide in one organism causes the distinction to collapse or instead exposes a hidden ambiguity in what we mean by "function" in the first place.
