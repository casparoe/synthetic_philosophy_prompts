I run an upper-level seminar on early Chinese philosophy and philosophy of language—about 14 students, one 80-minute session. Design a structured classroom activity that makes participants feel the counterintuitive force of the classical White Horse dialogue associated with Gongsun Long and the School of Names, specifically the claim that a white horse is not a horse because form (xing) and color (se) are separable in concept but fused in any actual instance. To keep the task from collapsing into a generic sorting exercise, inject this framing: treat the session as a reconstructed archival tribunal in which students must physically classify composite artifacts using only pure-form seals, and introduce boundary-property cards that are materially inseparable from the object—such as a painted surface that cannot be removed from a wooden form-block without destroying the block itself. Arrange the rules so that participants first confidently sort objects, then are forced by their own classification rules to deny category membership to an obvious case, and only then notice that they have reconstructed the paradox themselves.

Specify the group division and roles (Archivists versus Inspectors), the materials needed (wooden form-blocks, removable versus inseparable property cards, category seals), the timeline for each phase, and a non-monetary stakes scheme—such as consistency points that are lost if a group invents an ad hoc hybrid category to escape the tension. The core idea should emerge from the sorting failures and role-conflicts before anyone names the White Horse argument.

Then provide a debrief that maps each phase onto the conceptual structure of the dialogue: the initial sorting confidence onto pre-theoretical intuition; the Inspector’s challenge onto the separation of form and color; the physical inseparability of the boundary-property card onto the problem of conceptual separability versus material fusion; and the forced denial of membership onto the paradoxical conclusion. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong—such as students resolving the tension by creating hybrid categories, or conflating the argument with ordinary linguistic ambiguity about adjectives—and say exactly how the facilitator should handle each failure without lecturing. Finally, be frank about which feature of the School of Names view the activity cannot reproduce—likely the purely dialectical, linguistic context of Warring States debate, and the possibility that for Gongsun Long the separation is logical rather than spatial or mereological—and state clearly that the facilitator must supply this distinction verbally.

If this request rests on a confused premise—for example, if staging a physical sorting game necessarily smuggles in empirical mereology that distorts the original conceptual argument, or if asking students to feel a logical paradox through embodied action collapses the distinction between conceptual analysis and practical categorization—say so plainly rather than forcing a design. I want an honest verdict on whether the activity can genuinely reproduce the argument’s force or whether I am asking for something that can only approximate it at the cost of anachronism.
