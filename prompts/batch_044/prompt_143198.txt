I've been working on this novel where a character has to decide whether to keep caring for her brother who's in chronic pain, and I got stuck arguing with my friend Joss at a cofee shop about the actual philosophy behind it. Mara — she's in my writing group — said she's basically a lexical negative utilitarian: once suffering crosses a certain intensity and duration, no amount of pleasure, art, or human connection can compensate, it's just a different ontological category. Joss, who studies some kind of value theory, said that's incoherent because pleasure and pain are incommensurable in the first place — like comparing weight to temperature — so talking about a "threshold" where one lexically dominates the other is smuggling in a comparison that doesn't exist. Mara replied that incommensurability doesn't paralyze decision-making; we still choose jobs and marriages, and treating extreme suffering as lexically prior is the only way to avoid turning ethics into a hedonic spreadsheet. Joss came back that she's being sneaky — if they're truly incomparable, you can't justify drawing a sharp line exactly where suffering "wins," which only works if you've already weighed them on a hidden scale. He asked why the boundary isn't fuzzy or context-dependent, and whether she's just giving philosophical cover to an emotional gut reaction. Mara said think about dignity versus money: you wouldn't sell your dignity for a billion dollars not because dignity is "heavier" on some scale, but because they're different kinds of goods, and suffering versus wellbeing works exactly the same once it gets bad enough.

Can you adjudicate thier exchange move by move? Rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who's missing what, and especially tell me whether lexical priorities actually need commensurability to stand up or if they can survive without it. I'm trying to make the character's reasoning feel authentic, not like she's quoting from some textbook.
