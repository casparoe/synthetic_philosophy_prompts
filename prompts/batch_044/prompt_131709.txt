Construct a thought experiment, presented in exactly one paragraph and set in a realistic organizational context (for example, a municipal water-safety review board or an aviation maintenance oversight committee — not a trolley problem), that isolates an institutional feature I call moral buffering: an optimally designed, non-defective protocol that decouples an individual agent's decision from the causal pathway to the outcome, without altering the agent's information, competence, or intentions. Design the case so that agent A operates within the buffered system and agent B operates outside it; both make the same approval decision with the same reasoning and the same hidden risk present. In B's case the risk materializes and causes serious harm; in A's case the buffer intercepts the risk and produces a benign outcome. Crucially, peer and institutional blame diverge sharply — B is severely sanctioned, A is praised or exonerated — despite the identity of the individual choice. After the scenario, explicitly identify: (a) the belief or judgment about moral assessment that is contested; (b) the precise structural feature of the buffer that creates the divergence; and then (c) the verdict delivered by each of three positions — the control principle (assessment tracks only features within individual control), a standard individualist outcome-sensitive view (assessment tracks actual outcomes but only through the agent's own causal contribution), and a structural moral-luck view (assessment is legitimately redistributed by optimal institutional architecture regardless of identical individual contribution). Then explain which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work, what happens to the divergence if the buffer is removed entirely, and how a defender of the control principle could attack the scenario without conceding that the buffer is poorly designed.
