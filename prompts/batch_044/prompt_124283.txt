I've been working through the socialist calculation debate on my own, largely through the exchange in which Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek argued that rational economic calculation requires genuine market prices formed under private property, and in which Oskar Lange responded with competitive socialism: a central board adjusts prices by trial and error, directing managers who respond to shadow prices much as firms respond to market prices. Despite this exchange being nearly a century old, contemporary discussions seem to replay the same structure. One side points out that modern computing could handle the data volume Mises thought intractable; the other side replies that local, tacit knowledge can never be fully centralized, regardless of processing power. The arguments feel recycled rather than resolved.

My own hypothesis is that the stalemate persists because at least two distinct disagreements are running under the single heading of planning versus markets. One layer concerns whether prices can serve as information-compression mechanisms without the underlying institution of private capital ownership. A second, possibly deeper layer concerns whether economic coordination is primarily a problem of solving for equilibrium given known preferences and technologies, or whether it is essentially an open-ended discovery procedure in which entrepreneurs identify previously unknown opportunities. If that is correct, then when Hayek emphasizes dispersed knowledge and Mises emphasizes rational action requiring price signals, they may be describing a dynamic discovery process, while Lange's appeal to Walrasian simultaneous equations and iterative price adjustment describes a static allocation process. The two sides may therefore be answering different questions while using the same vocabulary of calculation and planning.

Can you diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each position actually commits to? Is it a substantive empirical dispute that a particular institutional design could settle? Is part of it verbal, with terms like calculation, market, and planning functioning differently across the two camps? Are they addressing the same economic phenomenon, or are they describing different tasks — allocation versus discovery — and treating them as competing answers to one question? Might there be a hidden normative disagreement about whether entrepreneurial initiative ought to be distributed or centralized, masked as a technical argument about feasibility? I am not asking for a survey of every later commentator, nor for a declaration of which side is correct. Instead, I want an analysis of where the arguments genuinely engage and where they talk past one another, followed by an honest assessment of what would count as progress given your diagnosis. Would it take a conceptual distinction that separates allocation mechanics from discovery procedures? A specific institutional experiment that holds one variable constant? Or is the disagreement rooted in bedrock intuitions about the nature of economic knowledge that no experiment or distinction could dissolve?
