I have been trying to stabilize my views on falsification and long-run prediction, taking the Malthusian case as my central test, and I want an audit of whether my commitments actually cohere. Here is where I stand: (1) I endorse a broadly Popperian demarcation criterion—if a theory can absorb any observed outcome by adjusting auxiliary hypotheses, it forfeits scientific status. (2) I take the sustained historical record of fertility decline, agricultural productivity growth, and the absence of global resource catastrophe since Malthus wrote to amount to a decisive falsification of his specific predictive mechanism as applied to human societies. (3) Nevertheless, I want to maintain that Malthus was correct in principle but premature in timeline: the structural pressure of exponential growth against finite resources remains intact and will eventually dominate, even if technological and demographic transitions have postponed the binding constraint for centuries. (4) I hold that rescuing a falsified theory by projecting its conditions onto a distant future is methodologically legitimate only when the rescue generates near-term, independently testable implications; otherwise it is ad hoc. (5) I further hold that normative ecological economics can invoke Malthusian dynamics without committing to the descriptive accuracy of the original population prediction. Do these hang together? In particular, does (3) survive (2) without violating (1) and (4)? Is (5) genuinely in tension with (3), or does the distinction between descriptive and normative registers dissolve the conflict? And does (1) contain a hidden commitment that rules out the very notion of being correct in principle but wrong in the short run? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify what must be abandoned or qualified in each case, and separate what is settled in the philosophy of science from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding the logic of long-run rescue, the interpretation of Malthus's intended scope, and whether technological change can be treated as an ad hoc auxiliary or as an integral part of the theory's domain. Do not present any disputed interpretive claim about Malthus's actual intentions as if it were the consensus view.
