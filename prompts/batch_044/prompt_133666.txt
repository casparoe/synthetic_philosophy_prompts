I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I have been trying to understand the metaphysical debate between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the transworld-identity view associated with Saul Kripke and Alvin Plantinga. As I understand it, counterpart theory holds that I exist in only one possible world, and that in other worlds there are merely similar 'counterparts' of me, related by resemblance rather than by strict identity; transworld identity, by contrast, allows the very same individual to inhabit multiple worlds, as if each world were a branch and the individual a persistent object with a global identifier across branches.

This framing makes counterpart theory look like a database schema in which every table (world) has its own primary keys and no foreign-key relations to other tables, so that asking whether 'I could have been a musician' is asking whether some row in another table resembles me closely enough to inherit my name. But that seems to make the modal claim either false—since I am not in that table—or at best a metaphor about similarity, not a literal claim about my own potentialities. Transworld identity, on the other hand, looks like a distributed system with globally unique identifiers, which seems to preserve the literal truth of such claims.

My confusion is this: if counterpart theory denies that I am literally present in other worlds, how can it genuinely accommodate modal statements about me without reducing them to statements about strangers who happen to look like me? And does the debate ultimately turn on whether one accepts 'haecceitism'—the view that worlds can differ solely in which particular individuals they contain—or is there a deeper issue about whether modal realism requires concrete worlds? More specifically, I have seen it suggested that anti-haecceitism (the denial that there are non-qualitative differences between worlds) is naturally paired with counterpart theory, yet I struggle to see how anti-haecceitism can be consistent with a form of modal structural realism in which only relational structures, not individual nodes, are preserved across worlds. Could you clarify whether my database analogy misrepresents the logical structure of counterpart theory, correct the false presupposition in my reasoning, and explain what the real disagreement is about? I would also like to know whether a small-scale model of modal reasoning—say, one that treats worlds as state spaces and individuals as state-bound entities—would be forced into one camp or the other, or whether the choice is largely independent of such formal modelling. Please write your answer in British English, using formal but accessible language.
