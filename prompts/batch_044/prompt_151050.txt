Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the lens of the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism—specifically the Rawlsian charge that aggregation treats burdens and benefits distributed across distinct lives as if they occurred within one life. The story depicts a utopia whose collective happiness depends on the perpetual suffering of a single child, and it makes the citizens' choice—remain in comfort, or walk away into darkness—the narrative center of gravity. I want a reading that asks whether the text actually vindicates the separateness objection or whether its narrative structure inadvertently undermines it. Note that the child is never named, never speaks, and is never granted an interior perspective; the suffering is visible only through the citizens' awareness and their rituals of explanation. Does this suppression of the child's separateness reproduce the very aggregation the story seems to condemn? Then examine the walkers: if individual withdrawal is the only permitted moral response, does it restore the child's moral separateness, or does it merely redistribute the burden of conscience so that the remaining citizens can continue the aggregation with cleaner hands? If the story adds something to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot—perhaps about the impotence of conscientious exit when structural harm remains intact—identify what it is and point to specific scenes or narrative omissions that justify the claim. Be equally honest about where the philosophical lens distorts the story's literary aims, and about where the story resists reduction to an ethical parable.
