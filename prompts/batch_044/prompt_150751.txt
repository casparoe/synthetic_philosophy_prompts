I am a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I am trying to map the logic behind a set of judgments I found in lecture notes on Bayesian confirmation and old evidence. The instructor's rulings on whether previously known data can confirm a newly adopted theory are as follows.

First, she allows that a cosmologist may use Mercury's long-observed orbital precession to confirm general relativity, even though the precession was documented long before the theory existed. Second, she denies that a regression model gains confirmation by retrodicting the very dataset that was used to fit its parameters. Third, she permits a mathematician's confidence in a new theorem to rise when she derives a lemma she already believed, but only if the derivation reveals a structural dependency she had not previously seen. Fourth, she accepts confirmation when a climate model reproduces a public temperature record from the 1980s, provided that record was excluded from the calibration pipeline. Fifth, she rejects confirmation when a debugging heuristic developed for backend errors happens to match a frontend bug pattern that the engineer had catalogued years earlier but never analyzed.

The unsettled case is this: a machine-learning researcher trains a vision transformer on a novel medical-imaging dataset. A decade earlier, she published a study showing that a specific frequency-filter improves diagnosis on that exact dataset, but she had forgotten the study and never incorporated it into her architecture design. After training, she rediscovers her old paper and sees that the transformer's learned attention maps reproduce the filter's behavior precisely. She asks whether this old, previously forgotten empirical result should now increase her confidence in the architecture.

Predict the instructor's verdict. Then identify the implicit rule she seems to be running—whether it centers on counterfactual prior conditioning, calibrational independence, logical or structural learning, or something else—along with at least two other rules that fit the five earlier judgments equally well. Finally, specify one further case that would cleanly separate the leading rule from its nearest competitor. I care more about seeing the reasoning structure laid out clearly than about the final yes-or-no answer.
