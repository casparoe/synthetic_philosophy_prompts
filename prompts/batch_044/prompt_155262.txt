I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on consciousness and causation, and after our recent session on epiphenomenalism I found myself unable to explain to a fellow student why the paradox of phenomenal judgment does not immediately collapse the view. Rather than asking for a standard expository answer, I want to test whether the position can be rendered in a form that exposes its own structural tensions. Please write a sustained satirical piece—approximately 450 to 500 words, in the form of a deadpan bureaucratic Frequently Asked Questions document issued by a fictional entity called the Institute for Non-Causal Phenomenology—addressing subscribers who have raised concerns about the causal isolation of their phenomenal experiences. The document should parody the familiar epiphenomenalist strategy of distinguishing access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness and treating all phenomenal judgments as purely physical-causal processes that merely correlate with, but are never caused by, the qualia they purport to describe. To keep the exercise conceptually demanding rather than merely repetitive, please inject an invented but philosophically coherent extension: introduce what the Institute terms a third-order exclusion clause, according to which not only are first-order judgments about qualia physically determined without phenomenal input, but second-order reflections on those judgments, and even third-order recognitions of the entire pattern, are equally isolated in a nested hierarchy of causal exclusion. The Institute should also reference a correlation warranty—an invented bureaucratic guarantee that statistical alignment between brain states and phenomenal properties will be maintained across all orders without any interactionist mechanism. The satire must remain entirely deadpan and internally consistent: the Institute should calmly remind subscribers that their objections to the warranty are themselves acoustic outputs of deterministic neural processes and therefore fall outside the scope of valid feedback. A specialist should recognize that the exaggeration is fair rather than straw-manning. Present the entire response as continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, exactly as one might write in a formal letter or email. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not quote any real philosophical texts verbatim.
