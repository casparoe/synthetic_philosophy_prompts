I have been working through the classic Damascus flight scenario for a seminar on modal reasoning, and I find that most treatments stop at the surface-level observation that attempting to evade a prophecy can be the very mechanism that fulfills it. The story involves an agent who receives a credible warning that he will encounter Death in Damascus on the following day; he immediately departs for Samarra, only to discover Death waiting there with the explanation that the appointment was always for Samarra, and that the flight itself was the causal path encoded in the prophecy rather than an escape from it. What interests me is not the folklore about fate, but a precise conceptual difficulty that arises when we try to model this situation within a branching-time framework rather than treating it as simple linear determinism.

Specifically, I am trying to understand whether the agent's belief update upon hearing the prophecy should be treated as part of the mechanism that collapses the tree of possibilities, or as a rational response within a tree that was never genuinely open in the relevant modal sense. If we evaluate the counterfactual "had he stayed in Samarra, he would not have met Death" by holding fixed his psychological disposition to avoid danger, we seem to generate a world in which he ignores the prophecy—yet that world may be modally distant in a way that renders the counterfactual false or irrelevant. Conversely, if we allow belief-revision in the evaluation of the counterfactual, we risk making the prophecy false by construction. Could you explain, without invoking any specific named formal system or author, how this tension between epistemic rationality—fleeing because one believes the prophecy—and modal fixity—the prophecy apparently selecting one branch—should be resolved? In particular, I want to understand whether there remains any coherent sense in which the agent "could have done otherwise" that does not already presuppose the falsity of the prophecy, and whether distinguishing between thin counterfactuals that preserve the agent's psychology and thick ones that permit belief-revision dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it to a different point in the reasoning.
