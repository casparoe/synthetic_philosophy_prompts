A friend who has been reading recent work in environmental history summarized the "non-human agency" position like this: "The argument is that germs, climate anomalies, and volcanic eruptions should be treated as genuine historical actors, not merely background conditions. Agency here is defined strictly by counterfactual efficacy—if the Tambora eruption of 1815 had not occurred, European social and political developments in 1816 would have unfolded radically differently—so intentionality is simply irrelevant to the analysis. This vindicates environmental determinism: human societies are primarily driven by these forces, and the only error of older determinism was assuming they act in isolation rather than in complex networks with human responses. Consequently, the real peril is that historians will treat human choice as a mere afterthought to geological and biological necessity."

This sounds coherent, but I suspect it collapses distinctions that the actual view tries to preserve. Is this summary fair? Please separate what accurately captures the methodological claim, what is a common simplification or overstatement—particularly regarding whether determinism is vindicated, whether intentionality is dismissed rather than bracketed, and whether human response is integrated or relegated—and indicate what a defender of the view would object to. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want to understand the conceptual structure well enough to see exactly where the summary misleads.
