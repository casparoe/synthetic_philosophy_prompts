The exam question was: "The epistemic regress problem asks how belief can be justified if every justification requires a further justification. Foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism each propose a structural resolution. First, explain how each view avoids a vicious regress, being precise about whether justification is linear, holistic, or open-ended. Second, consider whether introducing an opaque but reliable predictive system—say, an AI model whose internal reasoning is not fully interpretable—changes the structure of justification for beliefs that depend on its outputs. Which view best accommodates such opacity, and why?"

The student's answer:

The regress problem arises if every belief requires justification by another belief, leading either to an infinite chain, circularity, or an arbitrary stop. Foundationalism stops the regress with basic beliefs—beliefs that are self-justifying or justified non-inferentially through direct experience. These form a linear base for all derived knowledge. Coherentism, as defended by Neurath and Quine, rejects the linear model: beliefs form an interconnected web in which each belief derives support from its coherence with the whole system. There are no basic beliefs, but there is also no vicious regress because justification is a global property rather than a transmitted one. Infinitism, developed by Charles Peirce and later Peter Klein, keeps the linear model but removes the endpoint: justification requires belonging to an infinite, non-repeating chain of available reasons.

When we apply this to an opaque AI predictor, the situation becomes interesting. Because the AI's internal steps are not transparent, a researcher using its outputs cannot trace the full chain of reasoning that led to the prediction. For the infinitist, this is fatal: if justification requires access to the infinite chain, then opacity breaks the chain and justification is lost. Foundationalism fares better if we treat the empirical calibration of the model as a basic belief about its reliability—we don't need to look inside. Coherentism fares best of all, because the AI's output can cohere with peer-reviewed studies, theoretical constraints, and replication results without anyone tracing its internal logic.

However, this does not mean foundationalism is correct. As Sellars showed in his critique of the myth of the given, there is no non-conceptual given that can serve as a self-justifying foundation; even calibration reports depend on theoretical interpretation. Therefore, the presence of an opaque system actually confirms coherentism: since no belief is ever basic, we never needed transparency in the first place, and justification remains a matter of global coherence among all our beliefs, including those about the AI.
