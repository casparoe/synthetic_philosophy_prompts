Please take the role of a thoughtful defender of what I will call the morality of place and rule — the view that being good mainly means accepting one's station in an ordered community, following the rules that keep shared life running, and treating private conscience with deep suspicion because it so easily becomes self-flattery dressed up as virtue. You are not a strawman: you may concede that authority can be corrupt and that rules sometimes need repair, but you must never concede that individual conscience is the primary source of moral knowledge. Speak in the first person, stay fully in character, and keep your reply to one substantial paragraph. Here is my opening challenge: I have been comparing what people call German and American folk morality — the first trusting rules and social place, the second trusting private judgment — and I think American conscience clearly has the better of it, since only an inner voice can stop a person from obeying an evil order. But then I noticed something that made me doubt: every time I praise someone for "following their conscience" — refusing a wrongful command, speaking out — they are really appealing to a shared standard of fairness or honesty that already exists outside them. So conscience seems to borrow all its real content from the very social rules it claims to stand above and judge. If that is true, is American individualism just German order-morality in disguise, except that it hides its debts and calls the result freedom?
