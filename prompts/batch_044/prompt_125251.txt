You are a philosopher of history with expertise in causal explanation, metaphysics of agency, and the historiography of environmental change. Before responding, please take a few moments to think carefully about the different senses in which non-human entities—such as pathogens, climate systems, or volcanic aerosols—can be integrated into causal narratives without being treated as intentional agents, and about whether causal overdetermination necessarily eliminates human efficacy.

I have been studying recent environmental history that treats entities like the bacterium Yersinia pestis, the oomycete Phytophthora infestans, and the 1815 Tambora eruption not as passive background conditions but as active forces that reshaped labor regimes, state policies, and demographic patterns—sometimes described as historical actors. At the same time, I am troubled by what seems like an unavoidable dilemma. If the sulfur-dioxide veil from Tambora genuinely caused the 1816 harvest failures and thereby altered the diplomatic calculus in post-Napoleonic Europe, then the causal explanation of those political shifts points to stratospheric chemistry rather than to the intentions of statesmen. More generally, if non-human forces are always present in the causal background of every human decision, and if allowing them explanatory weight means granting them priority over human choices, then human agency appears causally redundant in every case where environment matters—which is to say, in every historical case. So it seems we face a forced choice: either we speak of germs and volcanoes as actors only metaphorically, stripping them of real explanatory weight, or we accept that human historical agency is an illusion whenever ecological factors are involved. Given that neither horn is acceptable—historians want real causal weight for both climate and culture, not metaphor or determinism—where exactly does this reasoning break down? Is the error in treating causal contribution as a zero-sum competition, in conflating acting with intentional agency, or in assuming that background conditions cannot share explanatory space with deliberate human action? Please identify the precise confusion and show how one can consistently maintain that Phytophthora infestans contributed causally to the dynamics of the Great Famine without thereby rendering Irish land-tenure politics causally inert.
