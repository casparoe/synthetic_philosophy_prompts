I retired five years ago from a long career in condensed-matter physics, and since then I have been working through philosophy and political theory in a somewhat unsystematic but earnest fashion, mostly with a small reading group of former colleagues. In our discussions of American political rhetoric, I have repeatedly noticed a particular argumentative architecture that I cannot map cleanly onto the categories I have read—exceptionalism, nationalism, civil religion, and so on—and I would like help in carving it precisely, whether or not it already carries an established name.

The architecture appears in debates about the United States in three successive moves. First, a speaker advances a historical thesis of radical uniqueness: the nation was constituted by an explicit act of philosophical founding, lacks a feudal or aristocratic past, and expanded its territory without ever establishing a formal colonial empire in the European mode. Second, this descriptive uniqueness is transformed, usually tacitly, into a normative exemption: because the nation’s history is structurally unlike that of other states, it ought not be judged by the same international legal standards, treaty obligations, or moral expectations that apply to ordinary powers. Third, when interlocutors point out that other nations have advanced structurally identical claims—uniqueness of origin followed by exemption from universal norms—the defender does not retract the descriptive claim but shifts to a meta-level argument: the difference lies not in the logic of the claim but in its sincerity and institutional embodiment, so that the belief in uniqueness is itself part of what makes the exception real rather than merely rhetorical. I have seen this sequence in arguments about refusal to submit to international criminal jurisdiction, about the exceptional status of constitutional originalism, and about the framing of military intervention as liberation rather than conquest.

What I want to know is whether philosophers, historians, or political theorists have given this specific three-layer configuration—descriptive anomaly, normative license, and sincerity-based meta-defense—an established name, and how it relates to the standard tripartite analysis of American exceptionalism as historical thesis, normative claim, and ideology. If no single concept captures the slippage between these layers and the particular rhetorical defense that seals them together, then I would welcome a precise coinage, with clear boundary conditions: for example, does the pattern require that the historical claim be factually contested, or only that it be presented as non-comparable? Does it collapse if the normative exemption is made explicit rather than tacit? I am satisfied neither with a bare label nor with a general account of exceptionalism; I want the conceptual edges of this specific move, including what it is not—whether it is distinguishable from ordinary special pleading, from civil-religious self-conception, and from the strategic use of founding mythology in other republican traditions.
