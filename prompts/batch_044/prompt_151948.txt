I am in an upper-division seminar on rational choice and decision theory (about 16 students, 90 minutes). We have already encountered Newcomb's problem in the standard one-shot, omniscient-predictor form, and the discussion has become predictable: students declare themselves one-boxers or two-boxers by reflex and then retreat to “the boxes are already filled” without actually feeling the evidential pull. I want a classroom game that forces them to experience the tension between evidential and causal reasoning in real time, with simultaneous choices and small stakes, before anyone names Newcomb’s problem, mentions causal decision theory, or refers to “the predictor.”

To keep the framing from feeling like a tired science-fiction oracle, inject the following niche twist: the predictor is not an all-knowing being but an adaptive statistical model that updates its guess about each student round by round, using only that student’s own prior choices in the game. Because the model learns, a student’s choice in round 3 is both causally too late to change the envelope for round 3 and simultaneously fresh evidence about her “type” that will shape the model’s forecast for round 4. This should make the evidential-causal loop concrete and block the easy dismissal that “nothing I do now can affect a fixed past.”
