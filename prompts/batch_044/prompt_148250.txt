Two friends of mine, neither with formal training in philosophy, have been arguing about a news story describing a lost nineteenth-century ship's log that records a precise Atlantic temperature pattern. A climate model developed in 2010—without any access to this log—had predicted exactly that pattern. The log was recovered only last year.

Alex said this should not strongly confirm the model, because in Bayesian reasoning this is a case of "old evidence." The log existed before the model was built, and Bayesians generally maintain that evidence already present in the background when a theory is formulated cannot genuinely support it. He argued that the model's creators ought to have incorporated all available historical records, so discovering one more old document merely fills a gap in what they should have known, rather than testing a risky prediction.

Jordan replied that this confuses the age of the data with its epistemic status. The model's creators never knew the log existed; for them, it was genuinely unknown. Therefore it functions exactly like a new experimental result. What matters is not the calendar date of the record, but whether the specific datum was part of the evidential background that actually constrained the theory's construction.

Alex countered that Jordan's view makes the total pool of past facts an open treasury of potential confirmations. If you evaluate confirmation only against what scientists personally happened to read, then every time an archivist uncovers a forgotten notebook a theory gets fresh support. He insisted that Bayesian evaluation should be measured against all evidence that existed at the time of formulation, not merely against the subset the researchers accessed. Otherwise the distinction between accommodating known facts and predicting unknown ones collapses.

Jordan answered that this makes confirmation depend on historians' archival luck rather than on how the world actually is. If a precise pattern was genuinely inaccessible to the theorists, and the model still reproduces it, that is exactly the kind of risky success that ought to raise confidence—regardless of when the paper was originally written.

Alex then objected that Jordan removes the old-evidence problem altogether: whenever an old document surfaces, one can always claim the theory did not know about it, turning every forgotten archive into fresh evidence. Jordan said this overstates his claim—only when the match is specific enough that post-hoc fitting is implausible, and the inaccessibility is real rather than conveniently asserted, does the point hold.
