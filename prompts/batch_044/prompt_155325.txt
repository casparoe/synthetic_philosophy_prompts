I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I am trying to stabilize my position on phenomenal consciousness before writing a dissertation chapter that uses Dennett's Quining Qualia as a critical foil. Rather than asking for a literature review, I want a consistency audit of the specific commitment-set I have assembled, because I suspect my attempt to split the difference between eliminativism and a reconstructed realism may be more evasive than coherent.

My commitments are as follows. (a) Intrinsic qualia—understood as non-relational, immediately given, epistemically private properties of experience—are indeed incoherent in roughly the way Dennett's Chase-Sanborn intuition pump suggests: if two subjects can diverge in every behaviorally and functionally detectable respect yet make opposite first-person reports about a change in taste, there is no stable fact of the matter about which hidden intrinsic quality was altered, and the posit fails basic criteria of empirical determinacy. (b) Despite (a), phenomenal experience possesses a real, objectively describable relational structure—similarity spaces among perceptual states, attentional modulation patterns, temporal integration dynamics—and this structure is what explains why both Chase and Sanborn genuinely experience something different, without requiring any private "what-it's-likeness" to be modified. (c) Quine's principle of ontological parsimony should be applied rigorously to mental ontology: if a posit plays no indispensable explanatory role in our best psychological or neuroscientific theories, it should be eliminated outright rather than retained as a mysterious given; so intrinsic qualia should be quined, not reduced to physical correlates. (d) First-person phenomenological reports—e.g., "this tastes flat" or "the pain is dull"—are typically literally true, but their truth conditions are satisfied not by correspondence to private intrinsic qualities but by the subject's relational phenomenal structure together with contextual calibration norms, analogously to how an uncalibrated thermometer can still genuinely measure temperature once we understand its structural dynamics. (e) The method of reflective equilibrium applies here: we should preserve as many pre-theoretic phenomenal judgments as possible while achieving theoretical coherence, which means we should reinterpret rather than wholesale eliminate the surface vocabulary of experience. (f) I think Dennett overreaches when he moves from the incoherence of intrinsic qualia to the claim that all phenomenal concepts must be eliminated or treated as mere heterophenomenological fictions; they can instead be reconstructed as theoretical terms referring to the relational structures in (b). At the same time, I worry that this reconstruction smuggles back in exactly the kind of heuristic fiction that (c) should forbid, especially since these reconstructed terms may do no indispensable explanatory work beyond what neuroscience already provides.

Please trace which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only appear to be in tension and dissolve on inspection, and what the least costly revision would be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging. I am especially unsure whether (c) and (e) are compatible, given that Quinean parsimony seems to demand more radical elimination than reflective equilibrium permits, and whether (f) ultimately undermines (b) by conceding that phenomenal vocabulary is dispensable after all. If my framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if Dennett's Chase-Sanborn case is intended to target relational structures as well as intrinsic ones, or if I am misapplying Quine's criterion of ontological commitment—please say so plainly rather than complying politely. Likewise, if the task as posed cannot be done well because there is no stable middle ground between quining intrinsic qualia and retaining phenomenal structure, I want to know that directly.
