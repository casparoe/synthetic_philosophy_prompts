At a recent symposium on democratic theory, I observed a sharp exchange between a political philosopher and a historian of pragmatism over what Dewey's experimentalism actually demands of democratic institutions. The philosopher argued that Dewey's conception of democracy as cooperative social inquiry requires that citizens themselves continuously frame and reframe the problems being solved; without that participatory restructuring, she said, representative government collapses into technocratic administration that merely uses democratic language as cover. The historian replied that this overstates Dewey's participatory commitments: for Dewey, she noted, democratic legitimacy comes primarily from treating policies as fallible hypotheses tested by their consequences, which modern pilot programs, regulatory sandboxes, and iterative feedback loops already approximate well enough. The philosopher countered that those loops are now fragmented by algorithmic sorting and platform architecture, so the "community" that Dewey required for shared observation of consequences no longer exists in any meaningful form; the experiment has been privatized. The historian responded that Dewey was an enthusiastic modernist who welcomed scientific management and distributed expertise, so demanding face-to-face communal inquiry romanticizes his view—technological mediation can serve democratic cognition if the institutional design permits continuous revision. The philosopher then pressed further: if democratic participation is reduced to reacting to outcomes designed elsewhere, then democracy becomes purely instrumental to efficiency, and Dewey's claim that democratic life is itself a mode of inquiry is emptied of content. The historian rejected the dichotomy, insisting that for Dewey participatory intelligence and scientific efficiency are co-constitutive within a naturalistic framework, and that separating them imposes a distinction foreign to his thought.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. First, is the philosopher correct that Dewey's framework requires citizens to actively frame problems, or does the historian rightly limit experimentalism to policy-level testing? Second, does the philosopher's concern about algorithmic fragmentation genuinely undermine modern approximations of Deweyan feedback, or is the historian correct that Dewey's modernism comfortably accommodates technological mediation? Third, is the philosopher's charge—that accepting technocratic management of inquiry collapses democracy into pure instrumentality—fair, or does the historian's co-constitutive reply successfully block it? Fourth, and more generally, what are both parties missing—perhaps a distinction between inquiry at the level of problem-definition versus problem-solution, or an ambiguity in Dewey's own writings about whether "the public" is a pre-political given or an achievement that inquiry itself must construct?

In your response, please separate what is settled among scholars of Dewey from what remains genuinely contested—particularly regarding whether he should be read primarily as a participatory democrat or as a scientific-managerial reformer—and do not present a contested interpretive claim as if it were the consensus view.
