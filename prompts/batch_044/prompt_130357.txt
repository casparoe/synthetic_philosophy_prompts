I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level philosophy seminar on collective responsibility and the legacy of political violence. I need a single essay question that turns on Karl Jaspers's fourfold typology of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—as developed in Die Schuldfrage (1946), but it must not be answerable by reciting the definitions. To force genuine conceptual work, the prompt should embed a contrived but precisely specified scenario: the democratic republic of Novara was founded sixty years ago by refugees who had fled the genocide perpetrated by the neighboring state of Aldoria. Every living Novaran citizen was born after the genocide; the founding generation is deceased; and Novara's constitution explicitly repudiates Aldoria's crimes and severs all institutional ties, except for one fiscal continuity—the state treasury still collects revenue from agricultural lands that were expropriated during the genocide, never restituted, and now form part of the ordinary tax base.

The exam question should ask students to write an integrated essay of approximately 900–1,100 words within a 90-minute period, addressing three parts:

(a) Apply Jaspers's four categories precisely to Novara and to individual citizens of Novara, explaining which forms of guilt—if any—can survive complete demographic turnover and explicit constitutional repudiation.

(b) Analyze whether the remaining fiscal link (tax revenue derived from expropriated property) constitutes a form of inherited political guilt, or whether it is more properly understood as a problem of unjust enrichment that does not implicate guilt at all. In answering, students should clarify what conditions Jaspers requires for political guilt to attach—for example, whether it depends on institutional membership, collective agency, or causal contribution—and whether revenue collection satisfies those conditions.

(c) Respond to the following universalist objection to metaphysical guilt: if this form of guilt rests on a shared human solidarity that makes every person co-responsible for the existence of evil regardless of personal causation, then it applies equally to all human beings. Consequently, it cannot generate any special obligations specifically for Novarans. Students must either defend the differentiated practical significance of metaphysical guilt for Novara despite its universal scope, or concede that it provides no distinctive reason for Novaran action and explain the normative consequences.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, formatted clearly with parts a, b, and c and with explicit instructions about length and time; (2) a model answer of about 850 words that demonstrates the specific conceptual moves required for a top-grade response; and (3) a marking scheme built for 30 total points that separates an A answer from a B answer. The marking scheme should identify five specific conceptual moves (for example: correctly distinguishing criminal guilt as strictly individual and non-transferable; correctly identifying that political guilt requires institutional membership rather than mere causal benefit; correctly analyzing moral guilt as personal and non-inheritable; correctly addressing the universalist challenge to metaphysical guilt without collapsing it into political guilt; and correctly assessing whether the fiscal continuity amounts to unjust enrichment rather than guilt). Each move should be assigned point values that sum to 30, and each should include a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode (e.g., conflating political guilt with collective moral blame, or treating metaphysical guilt as if it were a legal liability).

The difficulty should come from the need to navigate the tension between institutional discontinuity and fiscal continuity, and from the demand that students keep Jaspers's categories analytically separate rather than blending them into a generic notion of collective blame.
