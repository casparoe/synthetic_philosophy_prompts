I am designing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course in rationality and practical reasoning. I want one essay question that tests whether students can do more than recite the standard definition of the sunk cost fallacy. The question should force them to engage with a somewhat niche framing: the idea that certain sunk costs function not merely as unrecoverable expenditures but as constitutive investments that partially form an agent’s identity or the value of an ongoing practice.

Specifically, present students with the case of a long-running collaborative scientific project—say, a fifteen-year multi-laboratory study of an anomalous physical effect—that has produced consistently null results. The researchers continue not because they anticipate a breakthrough (they do not), but because abandoning the project would dissolve the specialized epistemic community, shared methodological norms, and inter-laboratory trust that the investment itself created. Ask whether honoring the sunk cost here is rational, and whether this case reveals a structural flaw in the standard forward-looking model of rational agency, which treats the agent as a time-slice maximizer of expected utility.

The essay question should require students to:
(1) Articulate the standard economic argument that rational choice is forward-looking and explain why unrecoverable past costs must be ignored.
(2) Analyze the scientific-collaboration case through the lens of constitutive investment, explaining how past expenditure might generate present reasons that are not reducible to anticipated future benefits.
(3) Address the objection that allowing such reasons collapses into mere psychological attachment or commitment bias, and propose a principled criterion for distinguishing legitimate path-dependent rationality from fallacious reasoning.
(4) Evaluate whether there is a coherent concept of diachronic rationality—where an agent’s history constrains present choice without being instrumentalized—that can accommodate the case without undermining the core insight of the sunk-cost critique.
(5) Take and defend a clear position on whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational, with explicit reference to the collaboration case.

Please provide:
- The exam question itself, suitable for a 90-minute essay (approximately 800–1000 words expected).
- A model answer of about 600 words.
- A marking scheme totaling 20 marks, with explicit point allocations. The scheme should separate an A answer (16–20) from a B answer (13–15) by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain: for example, identifying the atomistic agency assumption embedded in the forward-looking argument in part 1; using the collaboration case to analyze the dissolution of epistemic community rather than treating it as mere emotional attachment in part 2; offering a structural, revisable criterion—not just an intuitive feeling—to distinguish path-dependence from bias in part 3. For each point, include a brief one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the answer must be evaluable without web access.
