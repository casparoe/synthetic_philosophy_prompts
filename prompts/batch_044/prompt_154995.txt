I was arguing with a colleague in the philosophy department who insists that the question of why some nations are rich remains empirically open—merely a matter of better datasets and sharper instruments—whereas I think the difficulty is conceptual: institutions, geography, and culture are structurally entangled in every historical case, so isolating one as the true cause requires counterfactual dependencies that the historical record does not license. I want to teach this idea to a reading group of philosophy graduate students who have worked through logic and Hume on causation but have no background in econometrics or development economics. I need a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself.

Specify the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must arrive: which distinction—between correlation, intervention, and historical counterfactual—has to be secured first, and which can only be introduced once the prior one is in place, with a brief justification of why reversing the order collapses the argument. Then identify the single carrying example: a concrete comparative case (not a generic pairing) that must illustrate the three-way entanglement, describing exactly what properties it needs to possess—similar geography, divergent institutional paths, yet shared cultural inheritance—and what it must lack, namely any clean exogenous shock that separates the variables. Map the misconceptions the students will predictably form: for instance, treating institutions as freely chosen rules rather than path-dependent equilibria, interpreting geography as deterministic climate rather than as an interactive constraint, or believing that a natural experiment like colonial borders could disentangle culture from institutions—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted before it hardens. Propose one check-for-understanding question that distinguishes students who grasp the structural impossibility of isolation from those who merely conclude that economists disagree. Finally, state explicitly what technical apparatus—potential-outcomes notation, instrumental-variables logic, or regression discontinuity—should be named, briefly characterized, and then deliberately set aside, with a justification for leaving it out at this philosophical level. Keep the plan self-contained and do not request readings, citations, or a bibliography, since the session must work entirely from what is developed within it.
