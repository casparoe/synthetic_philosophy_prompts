Analyze the Theranos episode (2003–2018) as a test case for whether self-deception is conceptually possible. Elizabeth Holmes publicly and internally insisted that the company’s Edison devices performed accurate blood diagnostics, even as internal engineering reports documented persistent failures and regulatory correspondence directly contradicted those claims. Frame the episode as one in which a central agent appears to hold, or at least to act upon, beliefs that are incompatible with readily available evidence she herself helped generate.

First, apply the partitionist account—according to which the mind can maintain compartmentalized doxastic states—to Holmes’s position: under what conditions could she genuinely believe the technology worked in one mental compartment while registering its failures in another, and what institutional structures at Theranos (board composition, information filtering, investor pressure) would be required to sustain such a division? Second, apply the unified doxastic account—according to which one cannot simultaneously believe p and not-p—to the same facts: if the account is correct, does the episode reduce to straightforward fraud, or is there a coherent intermediate state (e.g., a highly weighted but still false belief sustained by motivated reasoning)? Third, introduce an affective or motivational variant in which the agent need not hold a false belief at all, but rather possesses a misaligned emotional investment in a desired outcome; use this to analyze the dynamics between Holmes, the engineering staff who raised objections, the board that approved her narrative, and the regulators who eventually intervened.

Then turn the analysis around: does the Theranos case show that self-deception requires robust institutional scaffolding to survive, that it collapses into ordinary deception once external verification becomes unavoidable, or that the boundary between self-deception and other-deception is inherently unstable in high-stakes entrepreneurial contexts? Be precise in mapping each actor onto the theoretical roles—who occupies the position of the deceiver, the deceived, the external check, and the institutional enabler—and explicitly identify where the theory offers no clear verdict rather than stretching to cover every detail.
