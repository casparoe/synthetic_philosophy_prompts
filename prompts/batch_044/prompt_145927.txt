Here's an argument about Hobbes's geometric method in civil philosophy that I keep returning to and cannot dissolve, because each step looks locally sound but the conclusion seems to undermine the very project it serves. (1) Hobbes treats geometry as the only genuinely demonstrative science, since its objects are constructed by precise definitions and its theorems are derived synthetically without empirical observation. (2) He proposes to model civil philosophy on this method, arguing that the commonwealth is an artificial construct—an artificial man—whose parts can be defined and synthesized just like geometric figures. (3) From this synthetic procedure he derives that sovereignty must be absolute and indivisible; to divide sovereignty is to commit a conceptual error analogous to locating a point between two non-adjacent lines. (4) At the same time, Hobbes maintains that legitimate authority requires authorization: subjects must have genuinely consented to and recognized the sovereign, and without such recognition the sovereign is merely a de facto tyrant. (5) But authorization and recognition are empirical, historical conditions; they depend on what actually happens in the world and cannot be derived synthetically from the concept of covenant alone. (6) Therefore, either sovereignty is absolute by definition regardless of actual recognition—in which case Hobbes's normative distinction between legitimate sovereign and mere usurper collapses—or the derivation of sovereignty requires empirical premises, in which case civil philosophy fails to be geometrically demonstrative. (7) Hobbes clearly wants to preserve both the demonstrative status of his science and the normative force of authorization. (8) So it seems his geometric method proves too much: it either renders his central political distinction vacuous or abandons the synthetic ideal that was supposed to secure certainty. Where does the argument fail? I am inclined to defend (4) and reject (5), but I worry that treating authorization as synthetically derivable makes consent into a definitional fiction. If I instead reject (3), what do I lose about the coherence of Hobbes's concept of sovereignty, and does that loss force me back into (5)?
