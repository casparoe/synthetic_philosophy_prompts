I am a retired biologist who has recently begun attending a philosophy seminar on metaphysics and modal logic. At our last meeting, two members—both with formal training in logic but with very different metaphysical instincts—became stuck in an exchange about modal persistence, and I am writing to ask whether you can adjudicate their disagreement step by step. My scientific background gives me some intuition about identity conditions in organisms and in counterfactual modeling, but I lack the philosophical apparatus to settle where their reasoning actually breaks down.

The first member, Elena, was defending a version of transworld identity. She argued that when we ask whether a particular oak tree in my garden could have grown taller had it received more sunlight, we are asking about that very tree—numerically identical across worlds—not about some substitute. She said that counterpart theory, which treats the tree in another world as merely a similar object existing elsewhere, strips modal claims of their genuine subject: we want to know what could have happened to this specific thing, not to a doppelgänger.

The second member, Marcus, defended Lewisian counterpart theory. He replied that if the tree literally exists in worlds where it was never planted—say, worlds with different initial conditions—then we have abandoned any principled account of origin and composition. The tree's parts are arranged tree-wise here; in a world with no acorn from this parent, there is no basis for calling the resulting organism identical rather than merely similar. He added that transworld identity forces us to accept that an object can exist without any of its actual causal history, which he takes to be metaphysically extravagant.

Elena answered that origin essentialism need not be abandoned: she can hold that the tree has its actual origin essentially, and still appear in worlds where that origin is satisfied but downstream conditions differ. The tree in the counterfactual world is not uncaused; it is caused in the same essential way, then diverges in height.

Marcus countered that this reply covertly relies on modal notions—namely, that the same essential origin can be satisfied differently across worlds—that presuppose the very cross-world identification Elena is trying to defend. He claimed this makes her view circular: she uses modal identity to ground the conditions under which modal identity holds.

Elena then turned the circularity charge back on Marcus. She noted that counterpart theory requires a similarity relation that must be evaluated across worlds, but to evaluate similarity we must already be able to identify which properties belong to the original object in other worlds—precisely the identification counterpart theory denies. She said Marcus hides behind intrinsic similarity, but deciding which intrinsic properties are relevant to similarity seems to require comparing the object to itself across worlds.

Marcus replied that similarity is grounded in shared qualitative properties and relations that need no prior identification of individuals across worlds; we match worlds by overall resemblance in kind, not by tracking a specific individual. He then introduced a point drawn from evolutionary biology—relevant, he said, to my background—suggesting that if we think about speciation events across evolutionary histories, we naturally treat the resulting organisms as counterparts rather than as literally the same individuals persisting through divergent lineages. He asked whether scientific practice would not support treating modal variants of an organism as distinct entities rather than one entity with many lives.

Elena conceded the biological analogy but argued that evolutionary divergence involves actual causal separation over time, whereas modal divergence involves no causal interaction at all; the comparison is therefore misleading. She insisted that in science we often run counterfactual simulations—what if this mutation had not occurred?—and in doing so we treat the organism as the same system under altered parameters, not as a replaced counterpart.

Neither would concede. I found both moves genuinely forceful, but I am unsure where the reasoning actually breaks down on either side, and whether there is a missing distinction—perhaps about the difference between causal continuity and qualitative resemblance, or about how scientific modeling relates to metaphysical identity—that both are neglecting.

Could you please adjudicate this exchange point by point? For each of the main moves—Elena's initial defense of transworld identity, Marcus's origin-based objection, Elena's essentialist reply, Marcus's circularity charge, Elena's reverse circularity charge, Marcus's similarity reply together with the evolutionary analogy, and Elena's counterfactual-science reply—please rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify each rating. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing, particularly with respect to whether the debate conflates metaphysical identity with the practical identity conditions used in scientific modeling.
