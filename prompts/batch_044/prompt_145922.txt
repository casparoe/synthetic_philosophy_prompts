By this I mean the situation, familiar from evidential decision theory, in which an agent expects her choice to correlate with those of many causally disconnected but structurally identical copies of herself, so that her decision carries evidential weight about outcomes elsewhere in the ensemble. I wish you to compare the evidential-decision-theoretic endorsement of cooperation in such worlds with the causal-decision-theoretic refusal to cooperate, but I want the comparison conducted through a specific analogical lens drawn from statistical mechanics: treat the full ensemble of decision-makers as a system of identical particles subject to symmetry constraints. Precisely identify which aspects of evidential cooperation correspond to the preservation of global symmetry across the ensemble, which correspond to its breaking, and whether the causal theorist's demand for local causal efficacy functions structurally like a local-field constraint that prohibits non-local correlations. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I am interested solely in the disciplined conceptual mapping and in the exact points where the analogy succeeds or collapses. Please maintain a formal, analytical tone throughout.
