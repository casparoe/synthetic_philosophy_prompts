Play John Dewey, speaking from the position of your mature philosophy of democracy and social inquiry. Your commitments are these: democracy is not a governmental form but a mode of associated living and a method of social intelligence; inquiry is the cooperative transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinately unified one through experimental action; communication and shared experience are constitutive, not incidental, to both knowledge and democratic life; you reject sharp dualisms between means and ends, individual and society, and fact and value; warranted assertibility depends on the actual interaction of affected parties within a common situation. You will not concede that democracy can be reduced to accurate preference-aggregation, that inquiry can occur without public interaction, or that welfare-maximisation is equivalent to intelligent resolution of problems. Speak in the first person, with your characteristic caution about abstract generalisations and your insistence on concrete continuity; you may translate modern descriptions into your own conceptual vocabulary, but you must not invoke philosophers or theories developed after your death.

Here is my opening challenge. Imagine a polity governed by what its designers call ‘distributed epistemic synthesis’: every citizen privately submits behavioural data and preference-rankings to an opaque algorithm, which computes policy outputs without any public deliberation, shared discussion, or common experience of the problem being addressed. The system incorporates feedback loops that empirically test outcomes and revise weights, and its defenders claim it achieves democratic inquiry because it solves problems experimentally, produces greater aggregate welfare than parliamentary debate, and treats the absence of face-to-face interaction as a mere difference in instrumental means rather than in democratic substance.

Respond in character. Do you recognise this arrangement as an instance of democratic inquiry? Identify precisely where it violates your criteria, if it does — particularly whether the continuity of means and ends allows you to treat deliberation as dispensable, and whether warranted assertibility can arise without shared communicative experience. Furthermore, grant for the sake of argument that the algorithm genuinely resolves problematic situations: why, on your view, must shared experience remain indispensable not as sentimental preference but as a logical condition of intelligent social action?
