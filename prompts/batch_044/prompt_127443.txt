Following a rather protracted discussion last evening with a close friend over dinner — one that circled back upon itself more times than I care to admit — I find myself unable to shake the sense that our disagreement about inheritance taxation, dynastic wealth, and the demands of equal opportunity is not merely unresolved but structurally stuck. We both accept the same empirical picture: that substantial intergenerational transfers concentrate capital, that social mobility is constrained by initial endowment, and that tax instruments can alter the distribution. Yet my friend maintains that restricting bequest rights violates a form of intergenerational personhood continuity — the notion that a family line can constitute an ongoing moral agent entitled to preserve its accumulated capital across time — whereas I argued that such continuity is a legal fiction that masks opportunity-set non-dominance, by which I mean the structural condition in which inherited advantages do not merely skew starting positions but actively constrain the meaningful range of life choices available to those born without them. We talked past each other for hours, and I am now convinced that the impasse is not about the tax rate or even about distributive justice in any straightforward sense.

Is this stalemate best understood as a verbal dispute about what “equal opportunity” requires — a procedural guarantee against arbitrary discrimination, or a substantive condition of non-domination across generations? Or is it that we are answering different questions under a single heading: my friend treating the issue as one of property rights extended through time, and me treating it as a question of republican freedom applied to capital inheritance? Alternatively, might there be a hidden empirical disagreement about whether dynastic preservation actually produces the non-dominance I claim, or is this ultimately a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether familial loyalty can generate moral claims that outweigh the claims of strangers? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each position actually asserts — not a survey of the literature — together with an honest assessment of whether anything, whether a conceptual distinction, a formal model of intergenerational agency, or an empirical finding about mobility elasticities, could genuinely move the disagreement, or whether we should expect permanent deadlock and why. Please present your answer in British English.
