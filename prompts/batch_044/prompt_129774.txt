So I'm a retired biologist — spent thirty years on signal-transduction stuff — and lately I've gotten into philosophy of science and decision theory mostly on my own. I now sit on the advisory board for the Meridian Foundation, which funds weird interdisciplinary grants. Next Tuesday we have to decide whether to pilot a new selection protocol: instead of our usual panel deliberation, reviewers would score proposals anonymously and we'd aggregate with a Condorcet-style pairwise majority vote. The claim is that this leverages the jury theorem — if reviewers are each a bit better than random and independant, the majority converges on the best projects.

I'm skeptical because the conditions feel wrong for our context. Reviewers aren't uniformly competent; a physicist judging a bioethics proposal might be worse than coin-flip, and we're definately not independant since we all read the same preliminary reports and share training biases. There's also a philosophical issue I keep circling back to: grant quality might not be a binary true/false thing like the theorem assumes, but more like a multi-dimensional tradeoff. Some people on the board are calling this the heterogeneous-competence problem — treating every vote equally drowns niche expertise rather than amplifying it.

We need a memo from me recommending approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, with a concrete timeline. It should take the jury-theorem case seriously, not dismiss it, but also honestly engage the strongest counter-argument — probably that applying a binary-aggregation model to a non-binary evaluation task is a category mistake that could systematically mis-rank innovative but polarizing proposals. No bibliographies please; I just need clear reasoning I can circulate. Could you draft it?
