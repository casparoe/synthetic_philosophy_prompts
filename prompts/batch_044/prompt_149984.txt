I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level seminar that straddles normative economics and philosophy of decision under radical uncertainty. I want a single essay prompt — answerable in roughly 90 minutes — on safe Pareto improvements, understood here not in the textbook sense but in a more demanding, model-robust form: a change is safe only if it is Pareto-dominant with respect to every world-state within each affected agent's credible set, not merely with respect to the actual world. The prompt must be designed so that reciting the standard Arrow-Sen framework or defining Pareto efficiency earns at most passing marks. Instead, it should compel students to wrestle with what I will call the epistemic-parallax problem: two agents with identical utility functions but structurally different economic models — say, one treating climate damage as linear and one as threshold-governed — can disagree on whether a carbon-tax-and-dividend scheme is safe, because the scheme harms high-emission industries in the linear model but not in the threshold model. The exam question should ask students to determine whether safe Pareto improvements can exist at all when credible sets diverge in this way, whether the safety condition covertly privileges a particular risk posture or intersubjective robustness criterion, and whether a rationalist framework that takes both model uncertainty and value pluralism seriously should abandon the concept, refine it, or accept that it is necessarily empty in domains of deep disagreement. Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, formulated precisely; (2) a model answer of approximately 700 words that does not merely resolve the tension but maps exactly where it becomes unavoidable and which horn is more defensible; and (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit analytical moves, each worth 4 marks (total 20), together with a one-line description of the characteristic failure mode for each move — for instance, defining safe Pareto improvement correctly but treating model divergence as merely an empirical disagreement rather than a normative one.
