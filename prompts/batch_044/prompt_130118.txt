I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm preparing an in-depth interview with a philosopher who defends what she calls practice-based communitarianism. Her core thesis is that liberal theories of justice—particularly those that give priority to the right over the good—systematically mischaracterize the conditions of autonomous agency. Rather than claiming that individuals are embedded in thick traditions of shared value in the familiar MacIntyre-style manner, she argues that the very capacity for choice is constituted by pre-political practices of mutual recognition and role-based obligation. On her view, these practices are not merely instrumental to autonomy but form its ontological substrate; therefore, liberal attempts to bracket communal goods behind neutral procedures do not protect autonomy but erode the social conditions that make it intelligible. She also maintains that this theoretical error carries practical institutional consequences: policies that treat citizens as abstract rights-bearers—such as purely market-based allocations of care work—tend to dissolve the interdependent practices that sustain both care and agency.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for a serious, intellectually engaged audience. The questions should go well beyond having her restate the position. I want at least one question that presses the liberal charge that practice-based obligations collapse into coercive conformity, paired with a follow-up keyed to her most likely reply—that these practices are dynamic and revisable from within. I want at least one question that asks how her account distinguishes legitimate mutual recognition from oppressive social roles, with a follow-up anticipating her answer that role obligations are subject to internal contestation rather than external veto. I also want at least one question probing whether her framework can accommodate deep pluralism without smuggling in a particular conception of the good, followed up by asking what happens when the practices of recognition themselves come into conflict across communities. Additionally, include a question that addresses a specific internal tension: if autonomy is constituted by these practices, how can individuals mount a genuinely internal critique of the practices themselves without that critique being interpreted as mere dysfunction or false consciousness? Conclude with a question about institutional translation—what would a policy framework look like that genuinely preserves these constitutive practices rather than merely subsidizing them?
