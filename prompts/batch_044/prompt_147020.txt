I retired from evolutionary biology about five years ago and have been slowly working through philosophy texts in my spare time, mostly on ethics and metaphyics. Lately I've been hung up on the moral realism debate, specifically the old charge that moral properties are causally inert and therefore can't be part of the real furniture of the world. In biology we're constantly dealing with entities that don't have direct causal efficacy—think of adaptive landscapes or selection gradients—which are real explanitory tools but don't literally push genes around. So I keep wondering: is the anti-realist's causal-inertness argument just missing how science already tolerates non-causal explanatory constructs, or is there a conceptual mistake in comparing moral "oughts" to biological fitness functions?

What I'd like is a clear explanation of where exactly the analogy collapses, if it does. Is the problem that moral explanations lack the quantitative, predictive structure that makes adaptive landscapes scientifically respectable? Or is there a catagory error in treating normative commitments as ontological posits at all? I'm not asking for a survey of every view out there—just take a firm position, tell me whether my biologist's intuition is salvageable or total nonsense, and lay out the specific conceptual distinction (maybe something about levels of explanation, or about normativity versus description) that actually does the work. Be direct about it; I'd rather have one sharp, committed answer than a balanced menu of options.
