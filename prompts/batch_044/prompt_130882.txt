I'm drafting my philosophical profile on epistemic sources for a comparative epistemology seminar and want a consistency audit before I present. Here is where I've landed, working within a broadly Nyāya framework but with some modifications. (1) I accept the classical quartet—perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), testimony (śabda), and comparison (upamāna)—as exhaustive of basic epistemic sources, and I treat absence (anupalabdhi) as reducible to inference with a negative predicate rather than as an independent pramāṇa. (2) I hold that each pramāṇa is intrinsically valid (svataḥprāmāṇya): a cognition arising through a properly functioning source carries a defeasible default entitlement that does not depend on corroboration by other sources. (3) When deliverances conflict—say, perceptual appearance versus authoritative testimony—I think the rational resolution is reflective equilibrium: we weigh coherence with background beliefs, explanatory power, and the specific conditions of the source's operation, rather than applying a rigid lexical hierarchy. (4) I believe evolutionary debunking arguments apply selectively: they undermine inference and testimony, since both depend on mechanisms shaped by selection for survival rather than truth-tracking, but they leave direct perception untouched because perceptual mechanisms are tightly coupled to present environmental stimuli. (5) I maintain that phenomenal self-knowledge is delivered through direct acquaintance functioning as mental perception (mānasapratyakṣa) within the Nyāya taxonomy, yet I deny this requires a fifth "inner sense" pramāṇa; phenomenal concepts are special only in their mode of presentation, not in their epistemic source. (6) I take comparison (upamāna) to be genuinely irreducible: it establishes a relation of similarity (sādṛśya) that is not decomposable into perceptual or inferential elements, and it plays an indispensable role in linguistic learning. (7) Finally, I hold that tarka—counterfactual and consistency-checking reasoning—does not itself yield knowledge (it is not a pramāṇa), yet it functions as a structural filter on inference; without it, inferential evaluation collapses, so in practice it operates as a shadow source of justification.

Does this package hang together? I sense friction between my intrinsic-validity claim in (2) and my reflective-equilibrium method in (3), and I worry that my selective debunking in (4) sits awkwardly with treating all four sources as uniformly self-certifying in (2). I also wonder whether treating mental perception as a species of pratyakṣa in (5) conflicts with my rejection of a fifth pramāṇa in (1), and whether my "shadow pramāṇa" view of tarka in (7) undermines the exhaustiveness claim in (1). But I am not sure which of these are real tensions, which dissolve on closer inspection, and which are the most severe.
