As a physicist recently turned to philosophy of mind, I have been reading the thesis—associated with Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander—that analogy-making is not a peripheral cognitive skill but the very core of cognition, underlying everything from basic perception to mathematical abstraction. Before I evaluate any particular theory that takes this claim seriously, I want the full specification of what such a theory must be able to deliver. Write the desiderata: the phenomena it must cover, the distinctions it must preserve, the empirical and formal constraints it must respect, the boundary conditions it must specify, and which of these requirements are genuinely non-negotiable versus camp-specific preferences.

Specifically, include the following, with the motivating case or constraint behind each item:

The range of phenomena: linguistic metaphor and perceptual categorization, of course, but also scientific model-building—such as the nineteenth-century hydraulic and electrical analogies used to understand fluid dynamics before the Navier-Stokes formalism was available, analogies that were later discarded once the mathematics was fixed rather than retained as literal descriptions—and cross-domain mathematical reasoning where source and target share no surface features.

The conceptual distinctions that must not collapse: between productive analogies that generate genuinely new inferences and dead metaphors that are merely lexicalized; between structural alignment and superficial feature matching; between analogies that guide scientific discovery and those that serve only pedagogical illustration; and between analogical mapping that requires explicit representation of relational structures and mapping that can emerge from distributed pattern completion.

The empirical and computational constraints: consistency with evidence that analogical reasoning is effortful and context-sensitive rather than automatic; compatibility with limited but real analogical capacities in non-human animals and young children; and consistency with the observation that distributed computational systems can approximate analogical transfer without explicit symbolic rules, even though they also exhibit characteristic failure modes unlike those in human cognition.

The boundary conditions: the theory should specify precisely when analogy fails or is inappropriate, and whether it predicts the specific patterns of human failure—such as over-extension of surface similarities or resistance to distant structural mappings—rather than treating all errors as noise.

Then identify whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, particularly any tension between requiring analogy to be truly fundamental (present in basic perception) and requiring it to be representationally explicit enough to explain disciplined scientific discovery. Indicate which single requirement does the most work in excluding rival theories, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied.

Finally, briefly score how three broad approaches fare against your list: a structural-alignment account in the tradition of Gentner, Hofstadter's own high-level perception view, and a modern distributed-computation account that treats analogy as emergent statistical pattern completion. Keep the weight on the criteria and their justification rather than on the scoring, since the criteria themselves are the deliverable.
