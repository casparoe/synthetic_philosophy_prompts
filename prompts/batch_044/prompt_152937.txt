I have been working through indirect normativity—the view that an act is right just in case it is what an agent possessing full information, perfect reasoning, and no affective bias would choose after fully idealized deliberation. A colleague constructed the following argument, which seems to show that the view collapses into practical self-defeat for every actual agent.

(1) Indirect normativity defines rightness solely by reference to the output of an idealized deliberative procedure: an act is right if and only if it matches what the agent’s fully rational, fully informed counterpart would choose in the same situation.

(2) The economic theory of the second best establishes that when one condition necessary for an optimal outcome is violated, satisfying all of the remaining conditions is typically not second-best; rather, the best achievable result generally requires violating some of the other conditions as well.

(3) Every actual agent necessarily violates at least one condition of the ideal—whether through incomplete information, cognitive limitation, emotional interference, or temporal constraint.

(4) Therefore, by (2) and (3), for any actual agent the best achievable action is generally not the one that satisfies all remaining ideal conditions, i.e., not the one the ideal counterpart would select.

(5) But indirect normativity, by (1), still identifies the right action with precisely that ideal output, regardless of the agent’s actual constraints.

(6) So the theory systematically directs actual agents toward actions that are, by (4), not the best they can achieve, and often actively worse given their real limitations.

(7) A normative theory that systematically directs agents away from the best available outcomes is not merely impractical but normatively defective.

(8) Hence indirect normativity is false, or at least unusable as a guide for actual deliberation.

The argument strikes me as suspicious, yet I cannot locate the precise escape route. If forced to choose, I would most want to preserve (1) and (7)—that is, to keep the core definitional claim about idealized deliberation and the judgment that a normative theory should not systematically misdirect. Which premise or inferential step should I reject? Please explain why that step looked plausible, what rejecting it costs—particularly whether it forces me to abandon the idea that rightness is fixed by the ideal, or whether it only requires separating a criterion of rightness from a decision procedure for actual agents—and whether the second-best reasoning in (2) can be blocked without denying its validity in economic contexts.
