In a philosophy-of-mind seminar about artificial self-modeling, a colleague invoked Douglas Hofstadter’s notion of “jumping out of the system” (JOOTSing) from the MU-puzzle discussion in Gödel, Escher, Bach. She compressed her point into the line: “If you’re trapped inside the rules, you can’t solve the puzzle until you jump outside,” and used it to argue that an AI can never fully understand its own architecture, since any model it builds of itself is just another component running inside the same formal framework. Before I respond, I want to understand exactly what work that slogan is doing. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that “you have to jump outside the system to solve it” might express? I am especially interested in separating: (1) the formal/logical reading about the MU-system, where derivability of MU is undecidable within the fixed rule-set but decidable in an arithmetical meta-system; (2) the cognitive/psychological reading about human “strange loops” and self-reference that allegedly let minds transcend any fixed representational frame; (3) a normative or methodological reading about hard problem-solving requiring an external perspective, interdisciplinary reframing, or literal change of formalism; and (4) the specific AI claim that complete self-modeling is impossible without an external vantage point. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and—crucially—whether it actually supports her conclusion about AI. In doing so, be precise about what “the system” denotes in each case—the MU-rule system, a particular computational architecture, a conceptual framework, or a Tarskian language hierarchy—since I suspect much of the argument’s force comes from sliding between these senses. I also want you to inject one further distinction: in the MU-puzzle, “jumping out” is not leaving mathematics altogether but entering ordinary arithmetic, which is still fully formal, so please say whether that undermines the mystical or transcendental aura the slogan often acquires. Finally, please be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail: what specific result about reflective self-modeling in computation, or about the later development of Hofstadter’s strange-loop idea, would cause you to change your verdict on whether the slogan legitimately supports her anti-AI conclusion?
