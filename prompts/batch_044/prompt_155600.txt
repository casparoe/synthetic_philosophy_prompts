I am a high school junior enrolled in an elective philosophy course, and for the past several weeks our seminar has been working through Plato's Republic, particularly the passage in which Glaucon challenges Socrates with the story of the ring of Gyges—an artifact that renders its wearer invisible—to ask whether anyone would remain just if they could escape all social consequences. Despite extensive discussion, our class has reached a frustrating impasse: one group maintains that the challenge collapses once justice is recognized as a socially constructed convention rather than a natural virtue; another group, drawing on virtue ethics, responds that the invisible agent still corrupts their own soul regardless of external recognition; and a smaller contingent insists that these responses are not actually in conflict but are answering different questions—one about normative justification, the other about psychological flourishing and character formation. I am writing a long-form essay for this course, and I need a careful diagnosis of whether this deadlock is a substantive disagreement about the nature of moral reasons, a verbal confusion turning on ambiguous uses of terms like justice and the good, or a case of hidden divergence in which some participants are asking why a rational agent should obey moral constraints while others are asking whether morality requires visibility or social acknowledgment to have value at all. Please analyze the structure of the disagreement by engaging with the actual commitments each side holds rather than the shorthand labels we have assigned them in class, and tell me whether there is an unstated empirical premise—perhaps about how anonymity genuinely affects human motivation—that both sides implicitly share and that might be doing hidden work in the argument.

I also want to explore whether introducing a contemporary analog—specifically, modern digital environments in which individuals can act without reputational accountability—helps illuminate the logical structure of Glaucon's challenge or instead imports misleading psychological assumptions that Plato's original scenario deliberately leaves open. Given your diagnosis, I want to know what kind of move would actually count as progress here: a conceptual distinction that separates the questions being asked, a reframing that shows the challenge rests on a false dichotomy, or an acknowledgment that the disagreement stems from bedrock intuitions about recognition and self-interest that no further argument is likely to resolve. My aim is not to survey every response in the literature but to understand which dimensions of this debate are upstream of the others and where a focused philosophical effort should be directed if one hopes to move the discussion forward rather than restate familiar positions.
