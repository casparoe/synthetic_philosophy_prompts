In my school's philosophy circle, a fellow student recently invoked the slogan "if you genuinely know something, you necessarily know that you know it"—which he called the luminosity thesis or the KK principle—to argue that large language models cannot possess real knowledge. His reasoning was that because these systems lack introspective access to their own correct outputs, they fail to satisfy the second-order condition built into the slogan, and therefore fail the first-order condition as well. I suspect that this one-liner is concealing several very different claims, and that the argument against artificial knowledge may depend on smuggling in a particular, contentious reading. Could you lay out the precise principles this slogan might be expressing? I am especially interested in distinguishing: (1) a purely logical or formal claim about knowledge operators; (2) a psychological claim about conscious accessibility or introspective reporting; (3) an epistemic claim about the transparency of justification to the subject; and (4) a normative claim about intellectual responsibility or accountability. For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivial, substantively defensible, false, or unsupported by standard epistemology, and—crucially—whether it actually supports the conclusion that lacking second-order certainty disqualifies a system from knowing. I am also curious about a "marginal-case" interpretation, associated with arguments about gradual changes in conditions (for instance, shifts in illumination or temperature near a boundary), where one might know that p without knowing that one knows it. Does this kind of case undermine every version of the slogan, or only specific ones? And if it undermines the psychological or transparency readings, can the logical or normative readings survive intact? I do not need bibliographies, citations, or lists of further reading; I want a menu of interpretations that I can bring back to the circle to see which version my interlocutor actually needs for his argument to work.
