I am taking part in a graduate philosophy of language reading group that has just finished a unit on deflationary theories of truth, and I keep running into the same rhetorical move: whenever I press the question of whether a minimal account of truth can really do without a substantive notion of correspondence or satisfaction, a fellow student interjects that "truth is not a property" and treats the debate as settled. The most recent occasion was during a discussion of Tarski's material adequacy condition, when I suggested that requiring every instance of the disquotational schema to be derivable might still presuppose a genuine, non-deflationary relation of satisfaction linking sentences to states of affairs; she responded that if truth lacks any metaphysical nature, then insisting on a "real" satisfaction relation is simply a category mistake. I want to return to the group with a precise taxonomy rather than another circular exchange.

Could you disambiguate the slogan "truth is not a property" into its distinct, precisely formulated readings? For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, whether it advances a substantive claim in semantics or metaphysics, whether it is defensible from within a deflationary framework, and—crucially—whether that particular reading actually undermines my worry about material adequacy and satisfaction or merely changes the subject. I am especially interested in separating: (i) the claim that the predicate "is true" does not denote a natural kind; (ii) the claim that the concept of truth plays no explanatory role in a theory of meaning; (iii) the claim that there is literally no property corresponding to the concept of truth at all; and (iv) the claim that the disquotational schema is analytically true and therefore needs no grounding in a correspondence relation. Present these as a menu with verdicts, indicating which readings remain compatible with retaining a robust, non-deflationary account of satisfaction, and which, if genuinely adopted, would render my objection moot rather than unanswered.
