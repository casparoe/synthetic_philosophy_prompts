ok so i'm drafting my position for a hegel working group and want to check if i'm being consistent or just smuggling in whatever sounds nice. here's roughly where i stand:

1. i think hegel's basically right in the philosophy of right that war has an ethical moment—not the "bloodletting" thing where death purifies the state, but the structural point that it suspends private right and forces citizens to see the universal as something they actually belong to.

2. i also think just-war constraints (discrimation, proportionality, right authority) are fully compatible with that. a state can fight justly and still get the ethical awakening.

3. but i also think modern drone warfare can't deliver that moment, not just because risk is outsourced to operators, but because it turns conflict into bureaucratic administration—like hegel's warning about the police-state extending its management logic into everything, except here it's applied to killing at distance.

4. yet i hold that hegel's logic requires actual negation, not symbolic substitutes. so if a state tried to replicate the awakening through, say, mandatory crisis-service with real mortality risk, that wouldn't recieve the same ethical weight—only actual interstate conflict works.

5. and i think all this applies strictly to sovereign states in international relations, not to civil wars or insurgencies, even though those also suspend private rights in pretty dramatic ways.

6. one more: i think if drone tech makes just wars impossible (because discrimation is still imperfect and proportionality is hard to calculate at distance), then states should avoid war altogether rather than fight unjustly—so the ethical moment can't be pursued at the cost of violating just-war norms.

do these hang together? specifically, i'm worried about the tension between (2) and (3)/(6): if just war demands conditions drone war can't meet, and drone war is all we've got, then either we abandon modern warfare or we abandon the idea that just-war constraints apply to hegel's ethical moment. but there's also (4) vs (5): if actual negation must be interstate, why can't a civil war provide it? and (1) vs (4) might look tense but i think they dissolve—am i right?

please rank the real tensions by severity, say which commitment has to go if i want to keep the rest, and tell me what the cheapest fix is. also flag any pair that looks like a problem but actually isn't, with why. don't just list them all as equally bad—i want to know which ones are fatal and which are cosmetic.
