I'm trying to identify a recurring argumentative pattern I encounter in discussions of revealed preference and the ontology of utility. The pattern involves two stances that share a common vocabulary but pull in opposite directions. Stance one is deflationary: utility is nothing but a formal representation of observed choice, so to speak of "real welfare" beyond choice is to commit a category error. Stance two is normative and substantive: because agents reveal preferences through choice, policy can legitimately use choice data to promote welfare, correct for biases, and compare outcomes across individuals.

What I notice is that proponents of revealed-preference frameworks move between these stances strategically, but not in the straightforward motte-and-bailey fashion. When critics object that people choose against their own interests—through addiction, framing effects, or incomplete information—the response often invokes stance one: revealed preference only records what was chosen, not what should have been chosen, so there is no failure to track welfare. Yet when the same framework is used to justify welfare comparisons, cost-benefit analysis, or the design of choice architectures, the justification suddenly relies on stance two: that revealed preferences carry normative weight precisely because they reflect genuine well-being.

The two stances are not a defensible core and an indefensible extension; they are equally contentious, and neither is clearly subordinate. They share slogans—"choice reveals value," "preferences are authoritative"—but the slogans do different work in each stance. The shift is not mere equivocation, because the speaker is not sliding between two senses of a single term; it is more like using the ambiguity of a theoretical framework to deflect objections in one direction while deriving conclusions in the other.

Is there an established term for this specific dialectical configuration? If not, I would like a precise definition that distinguishes it from motte-and-bailey, from conceptual stretching, and from ordinary theoretical pluralism. What are its boundary conditions—when does a framework legitimately combine deflationary and normative uses of choice data, and when does it cross into the pattern I have described?
