In popular explanations of Russell's paradox, the set of all sets that do not contain themselves is compared to a library's master catalog that lists every catalog that does not list itself. The analogy is used to argue that naive set formation is like unregulated cataloging: allowing any description to generate an entry eventually produces an entry that undermines the system's consistency, so the remedy must be a hierarchical restriction on admissible descriptions. Audit this analogy precisely: map sets onto catalogs, membership onto listing, and the contradiction onto the catalog's self-reference; identify where the mapping breaks, especially regarding whether the paradox stems from grammatical structure or from an ontological commitment about sets; and determine whether the analogy's conclusion—that hierarchical restriction is the inevitable remedy—survives once we recognize that some resolutions reject the idea of a universal catalog altogether, treating self-applicative descriptions as grammatically malformed rather than as failed ontological admissions. Does a better analogy exist, and if so, what is it?
