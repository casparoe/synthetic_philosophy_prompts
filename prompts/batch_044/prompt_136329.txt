I am observing a recurring disagreement in my philosophy of science seminar between two colleagues—let me call them Mara and Jonas—about a variant of the old-evidence problem in Bayesian epistemology, and I would like an outside adjudication. They both accept the standard framing: if a scientist already knows fact E and then formulates a theory T that entails E, straightforward conditionalization seems to prevent any increase in credence for T, since P(T|E) equals the prior P(T) once E is in the background. Where they diverge is on a hypothetical case they invented to test whether this problem can be circumvented without abandoning standard Bayesianism.

Mara proposed the following scenario. In 2024, an astronomer advances a modified gravitational theory that retrodicts Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession—long documented since the nineteenth century—but also derives a precise prediction about the statistical noise profile in the brass micrometers used by historical observatories. Specifically, her model predicts that thermal expansion in those instruments should have left a characteristic periodic distortion in the raw measurement logs. She examines the archived notebooks, finds exactly that distortion, and notes that it had previously been dismissed as random instrument error. Mara argued that this solves the old-evidence problem: the theory is being tested on the archival noise pattern, not on Mercury's orbit, and since no one had ever treated that noise as confirmatory data, the observation genuinely raises T's probability. Jonas replied that this is sleight of hand. The only reason anyone examines those particular brass-micrometer logs is because they already know about Mercury's orbit and need to save the theory; selecting which forgotten archival feature to check is guided entirely by the old evidence, so the apparent new test is constructed post hoc. Mara countered that Bayesian confirmation depends on the prior probability of the conjunction of predictions, not on the psychology of discovery, so if the noise pattern was genuinely unlikely under rival theories, the boost is legitimate. Jonas then raised a regress worry: if Mara's reasoning holds, then for any old dataset a clever theorist can always dig up some previously ignored feature, claim it was predicted, and declare confirmation—making old evidence arbitrarily powerful and collapsing the distinction between genuine prediction and retrospective accommodation.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate Mara's initial framing of the archival-noise test, Jonas's selection-bias objection, Mara's reply about prior probabilities and discovery psychology, and Jonas's data-mining regress concern. For each, indicate whether it is correct, partly correct, or mistaken, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and point out anything important that both seem to overlook—perhaps regarding how hierarchical Bayesian structures or the distinction between learning the value of a parameter and learning the structure of the model might change the analysis.
