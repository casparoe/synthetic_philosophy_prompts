I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of international law and the ethics of historical memory, and I serve on our university museum's Exhibition Review Board. We are finalizing a permanent gallery on state violence in the twentieth century and must vote by Thursday on whether to approve the curators' proposed signage, catalog text, and educational framing for one specific display.

The display documents a well-archived campaign in which a state systematically dissolved a group's autonomous political institutions, banned its language in education and public administration, forcibly relocated large portions of its population, and carried out targeted killings of leaders and intellectuals over roughly a decade. The board's legal advisers note that under the 1948 UN Convention the targeted group does not clearly fall within the four protected categories in a manner that establishes specific intent to destroy it physically, whether entirely or partially, since the documented primary intent was political assimilation and cultural erasure, though lethal violence was used instrumentally. The curators, invoking Raphael Lemkin's original 1944 formulation—including the cultural and political dimensions he treated as central—argue that genocide applies descriptively and morally regardless of whether a strict legal prosecution would succeed. Descendant-community representatives have urged adoption of the term precisely because legal narrowness, they argue, reproduces the historical erasure the gallery seeks to document. Some board members have proposed a dual-label framework: using genocide in the display but pairing it with an explicit definitional note that distinguishes the broader historical-moral conception from the narrower Convention category. Others worry that such a framework collapses an important distinction and misleads visitors into treating a moral judgment as an established legal finding.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should lead with a concrete recommendation—approve the curators' label outright, reject it for a specified alternative, or approve it only with the dual-label conditions and a defined review timeline—and then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of this request. The reasoning should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine seriousness: either the risk that museums conflate moral categories with legal ones and thereby weaken institutional credibility, or the risk that strict legalism commits an epistemic injustice by rendering invisible forms of group destruction that fall outside the Convention's scope. Please also specify whether the museum should include an explicit definitional note in the display itself, and address what should happen if future scholarship revises the archival interpretation of intent. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument should stand entirely on its conceptual structure.
