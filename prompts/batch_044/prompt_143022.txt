Assume John Stuart Mill is confronted with the institutional architecture of modern algorithmic content curation: not the explicit suppression of particular viewpoints, but the structural amplification of affectively charged, micro-targeted messages that remain technically public yet are practically invisible outside narrowly defined psychological profiles. Drawing specifically on his distinction between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct, and on his account of when opinion crosses the threshold into actionable harm, construct a reasoned prediction of how Mill would assess state-mandated algorithmic transparency or auditing requirements. Would he classify the resulting distortion of public discourse as a harm analogous to direct incitement—therefore falling within the legitimate scope of the harm principle—or as a diffuse social condition that lies beyond the reach of coercive remedy? Defend your interpretation by appealing to the internal logic of his arguments, avoiding anachronistic appeals to rights or frameworks he did not employ.
