My colleague applies Scanlon’s contractualist framework—wrongness as the reasonable rejectability of principles—to distributive rescue and risk-allocation cases. Her track record: she rejects any principle that permits killing one innocent person to save five, no matter how selection is made; she accepts a principle allowing a trivial tax on millions to fund medicine saving thousands; she rejects a principle that forces one person to endure a year of severe but non-fatal suffering in order to save ten others from identical suffering; she accepts a principle imposing a small risk of temporary discomfort on a large population to avert near-certain catastrophic harm to a few; and she rejects a principle that distributes a fatal risk equally across a group (each facing a 10% chance of death) rather than concentrating it on a single consenting volunteer.

Now consider a proposed protocol that can save twenty people from certain death, but only by permanently and irreversibly depriving exactly three randomly selected individuals—drawn from a pool that includes the twenty—of autobiographical memory. The harm is severe, non-fatal, and leaves reasoning, affect, and social functioning intact; the three are not volunteers, and no alternative protocol exists.

Predict her verdict on whether the principle permitting this protocol is acceptable. Identify at least two plausible contractualist rules that fit her earlier rulings equally well but diverge on this case, explaining what each predicts here. Specify the further hypothetical case that would best distinguish those rules. For each possible verdict, state what underlying principle about the aggregation of severe harm or the separateness of persons she would be adopting. Conclude with a one-line summary beginning “Verdict:”.
