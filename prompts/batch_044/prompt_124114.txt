Write a poem of exactly eighteen lines, divided into three stanzas of six lines each, with no rhyme scheme and no title. The speaker should be a single act of justification that is successively interrogated about its own grounds, moving through three epistemic postures corresponding to foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism without ever naming them. In the first stanza, speak as a belief that claims to rest on bedrock: use imagery of stone, root, or unmoving ground, and keep the grammar declarative and self-sufficient. In the second stanza, shift to a voice that can only point to neighboring beliefs: use imagery of web, mirror, or circular light, and make every line syntactically depend on the one before it through repetition or echo. In the third stanza, speak as a belief that accepts an unending chain: use imagery of stairs, receding horizon, or doors that open onto more doors, and let the syntax become permanently open, with subordinate clauses that never resolve into a main verb. The poem must enact the regress structurally: the final line should carry no terminal punctuation, and the line immediately before it should be grammatically incomplete, so the text literally cannot close. At the same time, present the three voices not as rival doctrines but as three accurate descriptions of the same epistemic situation, so that a reader familiar with the regress problem can identify each posture and see why the form refuses resolution. Do not cite philosophers, include a bibliography, or end with a moral that resolves the tension.
