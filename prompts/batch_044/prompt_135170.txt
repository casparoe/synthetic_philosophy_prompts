In a philosophy of mind seminar, a classmate keeps invoking the phrase "you can't be conscious without being conscious that you're conscious" to dismiss first-order representational theories, treating reflexivity as self-evident. I suspect the slogan is smuggling in several different claims. Can you lay out the precise theses it could be expressing—such as a phenomenological claim about what experience feels like, a metaphysical claim about the constitution of conscious mental states, an epistemic claim about self-knowledge, or a conceptual claim about how "consciousness" must be analyzed? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, whether it genuinely favors a higher-order theory over a first-order one, and whether it is false. In particular, distinguish an "actualist" reading, which requires a currently occurring higher-order representation, from a "dispositionalist" reading, which requires only that such a representation be available, and indicate which version the slogan naturally supports. Finally, specify which of these readings could actually justify ruling out first-order theories, and which merely rename them.
