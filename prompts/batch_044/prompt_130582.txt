Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the philosophical problem of distinguishing moral preferences from moral biases. The narrative describes a utopian city whose happiness depends on the perpetual suffering of a single child imprisoned in a squalid room; citizens are shown this fact in youth, and most accept the bargain, while a minority silently depart. Your task is to determine whether the citizens who remain are expressing a coherent moral preference for aggregate well-being over isolated suffering, or whether their acceptance reveals a structural bias—specifically, a form of scope-insensitive in-group preference that systematically devalues suffering that is hidden, non-reciprocal, and numerically singular. To guide the analysis, apply this criterion: a genuine preference should survive both reflective equilibrium and role-reversal, whereas a bias typically collapses when the agent imagines herself or those she loves occupying the victim's position. Examine the scene in which young citizens are brought to see the child, noting the range of emotional responses—some weep, others rationalize, others accept with relief. Do these responses map onto the preference-bias boundary, or does the story suggest that collective identity and social ritual can stabilize what is in fact a bias into the appearance of preference? Pay equal attention to the ones who leave: is their departure the exercise of a purified moral preference, or an escape from a cognitive dissonance they cannot resolve? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce Le Guin's narrative to an illustration of a conceptual distinction, and do not distort the philosophical problem to fit the plot. Conclude by identifying what the story contributes to the debate that conceptual analysis alone tends to overlook—particularly regarding how moral emotions are socially calibrated and how the visibility of suffering shapes what people are willing to call a preference rather than a prejudice.
