Read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through the joint lens of Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence. Treat the conch, the signal fire, and Piggy's glasses not merely as props of democracy but as inherited institutional "fences" whose utility the boys understand only after they have destroyed them. Does Golding dramatize Chesterton's warning that one should not remove a fence without knowing why it was erected—here, the conch's authority collapses not because it is unjust but because the boys lack the historical memory that made it work? At the same time, read Ralph's attempt to govern by rational contract and explicit rules as a Burkean failure: authority that is designed from first principles rather than received from tradition proves brittle, and Jack's counter-order succeeds precisely because it supplies the ritual and symbolic inheritance that Ralph's rationalism lacks. Focus on three scenes: Simon's encounter with the beast (is the beast a fence against savagery or a projection of the boys' own unacknowledged tradition?), the shattering of the conch (does this moment vindicate Chesterton, or does it show that the fence was always an illusion?), and the naval officer's arrival (does the restoration of adult order validate Burkean prescription, or does it expose that the boys were only replicating the violent institutional inheritance of the war-torn world?). Where the novel resists the conservative reading—particularly in its suggestion that the adult world's "fences" (uniforms, hierarchy, war) are themselves the source of the violence the boys imitate—say so honestly. I want a reading that takes both the narrative logic of the novel and the conservative philosophy seriously, and that concludes with what Golding's dramatization adds to the philosophical debate about tradition and rationalism that abstract argument alone cannot provide.
