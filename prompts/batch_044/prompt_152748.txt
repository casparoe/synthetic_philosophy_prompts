exam question: Some philosophers argue that consciousness isn't binary but comes in degrees, yet others say without a unified subject there's nothing it's like to be that animal. Evaluate whether the 'distributed phenomenal field' view — the idea that in highly decentralized nervous systems like octopus arms or colonial cnidarians, phenomenal properties might be spread across subsystems with no single integrating owner — can be coherent without collapsing into panpsychism or eliminativism. Address the objection that lacking a single 'owner' makes attributing consciousness to the organism a category mistake.

student answer:

The distributed phenomenal field view is a middle path. In organisms with decentralized neural architecture — octopuses, where two-thirds of neurons are in the arms — phenomenal properties aren't anchored to one central subject but spread across functionally coupled subsystems. This avoids panpsychism because it restricts phenomenal properties to biological systems with sustained information integration, not every physical system. It avoids eliminativism because there's still something-it-is-like for the organism, just not for one unified self.

The category-mistake objection assumes a Cartesian theater model where experience must be presented to an inner observer. Reject that model, and there's no need for a single owner. The view predicts that damaging one arm might diminish phenomenal quality locally without destroying consciousness globally, which seems empiricly plausible.

But there's a moral-status problem. If phenomenal properties are genuinely distributed without a single integrating subject, then no one entity suffers. So the organism lacks moral standing, and only subsystems could be patients — but they aren't agents. That means obligations run to parts of animals, not the animals themselves. That's a reductio, so we should abandon the distributed view for a unified-subject model.
