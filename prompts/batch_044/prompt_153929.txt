I'm writing a scene in my novel where Dr. Elena Vasquez, an evolutionary biologist who's just turned seventy, gives the opening speach at a small conference called 'Darwin's Ghost: Morality Without Design.' The room is half her old colleagues—folks who've actually read Darwin's private notebooks from the late 1830s—and half her family, including her granddaughter who thinks biology is boring and her brother who still goes to mass every Sunday. I need the speach she delivers, about five minutes, and it has to do real structural work: she wants to use Darwin's actual view that moral feelings come from social instincts, but that knowing their origin doesn't license any moral conclusion—so she can't let the scientists in the room hear 'morality is just evolved biology' and she can't let her brother hear 'therefore religion is useless.' The thread holding it together should be Darwin's strange, almost embarrassed worry that our sympathy for strangers is really an overextension of parental care that natural selection never meant to travel that far. That idea needs to shape what she can and can't say about obligation to people we'll never meet. Make it warm, not a lecture, with a mixed audience in mind. Don't include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists—Elena wouldn't do that live, and I dont want a model guessing at page numbers. And please, include one small typo in her notes, like 'recieve' instead of receive or 'occured' instead of occurred, because she's speaking from slightly messy cards. Write it in her voice.
