I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind seminar, and we have been working through the absent-minded driver problem: the familiar case in which a person drives a well-known route home, responds correctly to traffic signals, adjusts for other vehicles, and arrives safely, yet reports having had no conscious awareness of navigating and only "wakes up" upon reaching the driveway. For my presentation, I need to audit an analogy that keeps coming up in discussion sections. Several classmates have compared the absent-minded driver to an aircraft autopilot system engaged in an instrument landing: both take in environmental data, execute a complex, sequential task with high reliability, and do so without any subjective experience or an internal "pilot" monitoring the process moment to moment. From this, they draw a normative conclusion: just as we do not hold the autopilot morally responsible for a safe landing or blame it for a near-miss (we assign responsibility to the human pilot who engaged the system, or to the maintenance crew), we should not attribute moral agency to the absent-minded driver, and by extension we should treat consciousness as largely irrelevant to the attribution of responsibility in routine human action.

Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in action theory, and think carefully before answering. Begin with a brief brainstorming session that lays out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the autopilot computer, the pre-programmed flight plan, the sensor array, the human pilot who initially set the mode, the event of retaking manual control, and the missing conscious state? Then provide the full mapping and identify precisely where it fractures—particularly regarding embodied intentionality, real-time error correction and plasticity, biological homeostasis and affective regulation, and the normative structure of responsibility. Note any features of aircraft automation that the analogy quietly imports—such as design intentionality, external programming by an engineer, and the absence of learning—that the human driver either lacks or possesses in a different form. Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that consciousness is irrelevant to moral agency in automatic human behavior—actually survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the autopilot comparison was carrying the argument all along. If you think a different analogy (for instance, to procedural memory in a skilled musician, to sleepwalking, or to another form of automatic human behavior) would be more illuminating for thinking about the driver’s mental state, propose it and explain why it avoids the hidden commitments of the autopilot comparison. Be precise but accessible to an advanced undergraduate audience, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
