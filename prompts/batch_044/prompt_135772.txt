The debate over David Lewis's Principal Principle—roughly, that rational credence in an outcome should match one's knowledge of its objective chance—has been running since the 1980s without clear resolution. The familiar choreography is well rehearsed: Lewis's undermining argument against Humean supervenience about laws; the replies that distinguish admissible from inadmissible evidence; the New Principal Principle and its variants; and the counter-replies that these revisions either save the letter but lose the spirit of deference or smuggle in non-Humean modal commitments. Yet despite decades of refinement, neither side has persuaded the other, and the literature now reads less like a progressive research program and more like a stable equilibrium of objections and amendments.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about the metaphysics of chance or the logic of belief revision, but about a hidden ambiguity in what deference requires. One camp treats the Principal Principle as a synchronic coherence constraint: at any given time, your credences should align with the chances you take the world to have. The other camp treats it as a diachronic updating rule: when you learn that the chance of E is x, you should shift your credence in E to x, but only if that learning is not itself undermined by further information about the actual outcome. On the surface both say defer to objective chance, but the first group is defending a structural feature of rational belief states, while the second is defending a norm of epistemic humility toward physical theory. If this is right, the disagreement is partly verbal—both sides use the same label for different norms—but partly substantive, because the synchronic reading makes the principle vulnerable to undermining in ways the diachronic reading avoids, while the diachronic reading makes the principle trivial or redundant in ways the synchronic reading does not.

Diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal about the word deference, substantive about whether Humeanism can accommodate objective chance, or are the two sides answering different questions—one about the semantics of probabilistic laws, one about rational belief dynamics—under the single heading of the Principal Principle? Use what each side actually says: for instance, how proponents of revised bridge principles handle admissible evidence, versus how critics treat the relationship between laws and credences. Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work—perhaps about whether there are genuinely chancy processes that outrun any frequency data—or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Would it take a formal result showing that no bridge principle can satisfy both the synchronic and diachronic readings simultaneously? A conceptual distinction that cleanly separates calibration from updating? Or should we expect no resolution, because the two norms are genuinely incompatible but each serves a different explanatory purpose?
