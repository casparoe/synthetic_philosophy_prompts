Design a taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with exactly six leaves—classifying positions on anthropic reasoning with respect to the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, and the presumptuous-philosopher objection. The root node should pose a concrete scenario: you discover that your cognitive architecture belongs to one of several physically possible observer-types (for instance, biological human cognition versus large-scale artificial neural-network cognition), and you have no prior reason to favor one architecture's prevalence. The first branch asks whether this discovery should update your beliefs about the total number of observers in your reference class at all. The second branch asks whether any such update should depend on your indexical position within that class or only on the non-indexical total population. The third branch asks whether a system capable of modeling its own reasoning—whether biological or artificial—should treat its own existence as stronger evidence for universes containing more observers with that specific reasoning capacity, and whether that move avoids or merely repeats the presumptuous-philosopher error. From these branches derive leaves that include: standard self-sampling, unrestricted self-indication, a restricted self-indication that weights only observers with this specific reasoning capacity, a cosmology-only rejection of both anthropic updates, and at least two further combinations either carrying a standard name or marked unnamed. For every leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward commitment—for example, being forced to treat one's own reasoning as evidentially inert, or being licensed to infer a near-infinite observer population without further empirical check. Identify any logically available combinations of answers that yield no occupied leaf, diagnosing briefly whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Every diagnostic node must be phrased as a question answerable by someone who grasps the basic problem but does not already know the scholarly taxonomy; no node should ask a respondent to identify themselves by label. The instrument should sort people by what they say about concrete cases—such as whether an AI system should reason differently about population sizes than a human would—not by which position name they recognize. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or dependencies on specific publication dates or page numbers.
