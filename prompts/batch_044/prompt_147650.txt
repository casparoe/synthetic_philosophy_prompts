I am drafting a literary novel centered on a philosopher-scientist who has embraced the view that conscious awareness is best understood as a user illusion—an interface model produced by predictive processing rather than a direct window onto reality. In the narrative, she struggles with the implications of this view when she considers whether the illusion is singular or stratified, and I want her internal monologue to carry real philosophical weight rather than recycled popular-science summaries. To that end, I need an essay that engages with a specific conceptual tension I have been developing for her character.

The controversy is this: if consciousness is indeed a user illusion, and if that illusion operates not as a single misrepresentation but as a layered, self-correcting structure—what I have been calling a "palimpsest of absence," in which each representational layer revises its predecessor without ever accessing a non-illusory substrate—does this layered architecture collapse into an infinite regress that only evolutionary teleology can halt? Or, conversely, does the very absence of any ground-truth substrate actually reinforce the illusionist position by demonstrating that no "raw" phenomenology was ever necessary for the system to function? Weigh the principal arguments on both sides, paying close attention to how predictive-processing accounts of self-modeling and error correction might accommodate or resist such stratification. Do not end on an ambivalent or purely descriptive note: defend a clear verdict about whether the palimpsest structure undermines or strengthens the user-illusion theory of consciousness, and justify that conclusion through conceptual analysis of the architecture itself rather than through appeals to empirical neuroscience, named experiments, or citation of particular authors.
