Analyze the 1991 restoration of legal state continuity in Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania through the joint lens of Hobsbawm’s invented tradition and Anderson’s imagined communities, with special attention to the constitutional work these narratives performed rather than treating them as mere pageantry. The salient facts are these: upon declaring independence in 1991, all three states rejected succession from the USSR and instead claimed uninterrupted legal continuity with their 1918–1940 republics, reinstating interwar constitutions, flags, and anthems and framing 1940–1991 as an illegal occupation. This doctrine was not decorative; it directly shaped citizenship law, excluding many post-1940 Soviet-era settlers from automatic citizenship in Estonia and Latvia, and it structured international recognition.

I want a disciplined two-directional mapping. First, apply the theories precisely: identify the actors constructing the community (the titular ethnic majorities, restored interwar political elites, international recognition bodies), the options they actually faced (continuity versus succession, ethnically bounded versus inclusive citizenship, modular adoption of Westphalian institutions versus locally reconstructed governance), and the evidence they treated as authoritative (the brief interwar statehood, the Molotov-Ribbentrop pact narrative, pre-1940 legal archives). Use Hobsbawm’s distinction between practices that invoke an older continuity and those that are genuinely new rituals dressed in old clothing, and ask whether the Baltic continuity claim is misclassified by a framework that expects invented traditions to serve symbolic social cohesion rather than to perform hard constitutional and demographic work. Use Anderson’s idea of modular, exportable nationalism and the “imagined” community sustained by print-capitalism and bureaucratic categorization: was the Baltic nation-state being re-imagined from scratch using an imported Westphalian template, or was a suppressed imagined community being reactivated? Does the thinness of the 1918–1940 window—only about two decades—strengthen the constructivist reading, or does it show that even a brief historical interval can serve as a functional anchor for legal continuity?

Then reverse the direction and ask what the episode does to the theories. Where does the invented-tradition framework illuminate the rapid, nearly identical adoption of flags and anthems across three states, and where does it fall silent about the legal fiction of uninterrupted sovereignty? Does Anderson’s modular-nationalism model explain why these small states adopted such similar institutional packages within months, or does it miss the specific local mechanism of legal restoration and the ethnic boundary-drawing that accompanied it? Be explicit about where the theories have nothing to say—do not force them to adjudicate citizenship ethics or international-law doctrine if they do not bear on them.

Conclude by judging whether this case is best read as confirmation that nations are recent constructions maintained by selectively revived narratives, or whether the functional, constitutive role of the continuity doctrine reveals a limit: that some “invented” traditions are not ornamental additions to a pre-existing nation but legal fictions without which the state itself cannot be constituted. In your response, please state how confident you are in each major claim and single out the point about which you are least sure—whether that concerns the applicability of Hobsbawm’s symbolic-versus-functional distinction, the historical accuracy of framing 1940–1991 solely as occupation, or the strength of the modular-nationalism reading in explaining the near-simultaneous institutional choices.
