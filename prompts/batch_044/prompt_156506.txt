I'm on the ethics review board for our county correctional services and we just adopted this strange dual-track review policy that I now have to run on every sanction proposal that hits my desk. The idea is that each proposed punishment or mandatory intervention has to survive two seperate screens: first a Dennett-style compatibilist desert check—did the offender actually exercise the kind of rational self-control and reflective agency that makes them genuinely answerable for this act, or was it closer to a compulsive breakdown they couldn't steer?—and then a Caruso-style skeptical audit, which holds that nobody ultimately deserves suffering in a deep metaphysical sense, so any punitive or burdensome element in the proposal has to be strictly necessary for public safety, rehabilitation, or victim restoration, and can never be justified just as retributive payback. We recieve about ten to fifteen files every Tuesday and Thursday, we've got maybe twenty minutes per case, and the board is mostly lawyers and community advocates with zero philosophy training, so I need this turned into an actual proceedure: a step-by-step checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient yes/no/unclear answer, where the flow stops with approval, where it stops with rejection, and exactly which nodes are values judgments that have to be kicked to a human rather than decided by the rules.

Be honest about where the two tracks conflict—like if someone clearly had full rational control but the only available sanction is mostly symbolic punishment—and say what the proceedure simply can't resolve. Then run it on three real proposals: (1) six months of geofenced electronic monitoring plus mandatory cognitive-behavioral therapy modules for a first-time fraud offender who was in a severe gambling-addiction spiral; (2) a five-year prison term extension for a violent offender who refuses all rehabilitation programs and seems to actively enjoy provoking staff; and (3) fifty hours of restorative labor assigned to a vandal who caused serious property damage but turned themselves in within hours and already paid full restitution. I want at least one of these to come out in a way I'll personally dislike—probably the prison extension case—so I can see where the proceedure forces an ugly answer. Also spell out the constraints: we can't search external literature, we have no clinical psychologist on staff, and we have to sign off in writing. Don't cite anyone, don't ask me to read Dennett or Caruso, just build the operational tool.
