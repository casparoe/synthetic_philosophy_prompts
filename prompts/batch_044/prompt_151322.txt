As the philosophy representative on our university medical center's Therapeutic Goals Review Board, I must circulate a formal recommendation memo before our June meeting regarding a proposed clinical framework—tentatively designated "negative-state optimization"—developed by an interdisciplinary consortium of clinical psychiatrists and affective researchers. The framework proposes that the sole legitimate endpoint of mental-health treatment is the elimination or substantial reduction of craving states and aversive experience; it explicitly withholds evaluative weight from the presence of pleasure, desire satisfaction, improved social functioning, or subjective reports of life satisfaction, defending this restriction on explicitly tranquilist premises: only absences—of suffering and of craving—carry intrinsic therapeutic value, so to measure success by any positive presence is to commit a category error.

Please draft the Board's decision memo—addressed to the center's director and suitable for circulation to clinical staff, institutional reviewers, and patient advocates—recommending approval, conditional approval with pilot constraints, or rejection, with a clearly stated bottom line in both the opening and closing sections. The memo should be written in formal institutional prose and should include the following.

First, articulate the strongest philosophical version of the tranquilist position as applied to clinical practice, including the argument that introducing positive-state metrics does not merely distract from therapeutic goals but conceptually corrupts the evaluation of whether treatment has succeeded.

Second, identify and take seriously an internal tension within tranquilism itself when operationalized: specifically, address whether a patient who achieves complete absence of craving and suffering but simultaneously reports no remaining motivation for continued existence, social engagement, or self-preservation should be counted as an unqualified therapeutic success, and whether this reveals a structural limitation in using absence alone as the success criterion.

Third, present the strongest opposing consideration—that a therapeutic framework which systematically disregards positive affect, agency, and flourishing-oriented interests risks reducing persons to mere absence-states rather than treating them as beings with interests in living well, not merely in not suffering—and explain why this consideration does not automatically defeat the proposal but does impose a substantial burden of proof on its defenders.

Fourth, propose a concrete recommendation with a defined evaluation timeline—such as a six-month pilot restricted to a specific patient population, with predetermined measurement protocols that track both absence-indicators and at least one carefully specified agency-related safeguard—rather than offering an open-ended philosophical endorsement or rejection.

Fifth, avoid appeals to authority, bibliographic citations, named philosophers, and references to literature that would be inaccessible without external research; the reasoning should stand on conceptual and clinical grounds alone.

The memo should run approximately 900 to 1,100 words, maintain a formal, deliberative tone appropriate to an institutional ethics body, and ensure that the reasoning for the recommendation is transparent enough that both supporters and critics of the framework could understand precisely where the Board stands and why.
