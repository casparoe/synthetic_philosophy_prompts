I'm writing this after a session in my seminar on intergenerational justice and climate ethics, where we spent considerable time on Parfit's non-identity problem and its implications for long-term carbon policy. The instructor kept emphasizing that if our emissions help determine who will exist in the distant future, then we cannot wrong specific individuals by choosing a high-emission path, because those very individuals owe their existence to that choice. I understand that point, but I think I've noticed a further wrinkle that wasn't fully addressed in our discussion, and I'm unsure whether it carries an established label in the literature.

Consider two climate stabilization pathways—say, a rapid decarbonization trajectory and a delayed mitigation trajectory—that produce radically different global conditions by 2300. Under each pathway, a different set of people comes to exist, differing not only in identity but in demographic scale and in the spatial distribution of welfare. Now suppose we apply a person-affecting criterion of justice: every future person must have access to some minimal ecological baseline, such as stable freshwater availability or a habitable temperature range. Under both pathways, every actual person meets that baseline—there is no one who falls below it and who could have been above it in the other world. Nevertheless, the delayed pathway might generate a vastly larger population, with a much greater proportion living close to the baseline threshold, whereas the rapid pathway produces a smaller population with more secure surplus. The standard non-identity framing tells us we haven't harmed anyone in particular, but it doesn't seem to capture why we might still regard the delayed pathway as distributively inferior, or why indexing the baseline itself to world-dependent conditions feels conceptually slippery. Is there an established concept—perhaps distinct from the non-identity problem, from the repugnant conclusion, or from simple aggregation—that describes precisely this configuration: a person-affecting threshold applied across non-identical populations, where no individual is made worse off relative to counterfactual non-existence, yet the structural relation between population size and baseline proximity generates a normative asymmetry? If so, how is it formally distinguished from those nearby ideas; and if not, what would be the clearest way to define its boundaries?
