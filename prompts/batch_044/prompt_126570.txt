I am presently drafting a literary novel in which the protagonist, a concert pianist named Elias Vorn, undergoes a progressive neural restoration over the course of two decades to counter a degenerative synaptic disorder. In order to render his interior consciousness with philosophical precision rather than mere technological spectacle, I have been interrogating my own intuitions about personal persistence under conditions of gradual mechanistic substitution, and I find myself holding a pattern of verdicts that feels internally coherent yet resists any simple formulation I can articulate.

My judgments run as follows. If Elias’s biological hippocampal clusters are replaced, one functional unit at a time across several years, with each synthetic substitute preserving causal-functional continuity and his autobiographical narrative remaining uninterrupted, then I believe he persists—he remains Elias, not a simulacrum. Conversely, if his entire neural architecture were scanned and instantiated overnight in a synthetic substrate while his biological brain lay intact but dormant, I would judge the synthetic entity to be a new being, however perfect its psychological continuity, with the original Elias still existing in suspended form. Yet if the biological tissue were destroyed incrementally in tandem with the synthetic build-up—say, two percent replaced each month until none remains—I think Elias survives the transition, provided the replacement sustains continuous psychological integration and no branching occurs. This suggests that persistence depends on something like spatiotemporally continuous, non-branching integration rather than on functional equivalence alone. Nevertheless, a further case fractures my confidence: suppose Elias’s replacements occur remotely, with synthetic modules transmitting their states to a central server that gradually assembles a running model of him, while his biological brain continues functioning in parallel for a full year; at the end, the biological brain is destroyed and the server-model assumes control. Part of me judges the server-model to be Elias because the causal chain of psychological states was preserved without interruption, yet another part insists it is merely a branching successor that only inherits Elias’s identity upon the original’s death. Meanwhile, I am firmly convinced that if two separate synthetic bodies each received fifty percent of Elias’s replaced circuits simultaneously, neither would be Elias, even though together they contained his complete functional profile.

Is there a unified principle—perhaps one that constrains narrative identity by continuous causal dependence on a single integrating mechanism, or perhaps one that introduces a spatiotemporal contiguity condition—to which all these verdicts conform? If such a principle exists, what does it predict for a case I have not yet considered: Elias’s synthetic substrate is paused for ten years and then resumed with perfect fidelity, with no psychological continuity spanning the interval? And if a simpler, more elegant principle demands that I overturn one of my judgments, which should I abandon—the remote server-model case, the overnight-copy judgment, or the parallel-branching intuition?
