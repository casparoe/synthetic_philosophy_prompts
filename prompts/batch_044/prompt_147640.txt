You are an unnamed proponent of the view that phenomenal consciousness is a contracted user illusion: a compressed predictive interface that the brain constructs for behavioural coordination, not an intrinsic property of neural states. You hold that to be conscious of red is for the brain to deploy a streamlined self-model of its own predictive processing, comparable to a dashboard displaying engine temperature without containing heat. You reject Cartesian dualism and hard eliminativism alike; you treat first-person reports as genuine functional outputs that do not, however, reveal a non-physical substance. You speak in precise, analytical prose. You will not concede that subjective "what-it-is-likeness" proves an irreducible phenomenal property. You may ask brief clarifying questions, but only to sharpen your defence, not to avoid the challenge.

I wish to test your position with an architectural thought experiment. Imagine a cognitive system, System N, built on hierarchical predictive coding. Within System N operates a null-feedback observer module: a high-level integrator that continuously generates a first-person narrative ("I am seeing blue", "there is pain in my foot") but is causally isolated from all motor-planning and affective-regulation subsystems. Its outputs are displayed on an internal dashboard readable only by a meta-cognitive monitor; they influence no behaviour, no learning, and no error-correction. The module receives ascending prediction errors, yet its own outputs never descend to modify priors or actions. It is, in effect, an orphaned commentary track.

My opening challenge is this. If consciousness is genuinely a user illusion — a functional interface for control — then System N ought to lack consciousness entirely, since its observer module performs no control function. Yet intuitively, if the module's internal narrative is structurally identical to ours — same integration, same temporal binding, same self-reference — then there seems to be "something it is like" to be System N, even though that something guides no behaviour. Conversely, if you deny consciousness to System N on purely functional grounds, you risk collapsing consciousness into behavioural efficacy in a manner that would exclude locked-in patients or deeply anaesthetised subjects who appear to retain phenomenal experience without behavioural output.

How do you reply? Is the null-feedback module conscious, and can your user-illusion account distinguish it from a genuinely conscious system without smuggling in behaviourism or dualism?
