In Switzerland, every amendment to the federal constitution—whether initiated by parliament or triggered by a popular petition of 100,000 citizens—must secure both a national popular majority and a majority of the cantons, with each canton counting as one irrespective of its population. As a result, an individual vote in a small canton carries far greater weight in the cantonal tally than one in Zurich, despite both voters being weighted equally in ordinary federal legislation decided by simple national majority.

What philosophical conception of democratic equality and popular sovereignty does this dual-majority design presuppose? Does it treat the Swiss polity as a unitary demos of equally weighted citizens, as a federation of corporate territorial bodies, or as both at once? Reconstruct the implicit theory of representation that renders coherent the same electorate being counted in two incompatible ways, and ask whether that theory can genuinely be reconciled with the liberal-constitutionalist view that constitutional safeguards exist precisely to restrain majoritarian power—particularly when citizens themselves may initiate constitutional change. If the underlying account of sovereignty were replaced by a strictly individualist, uniform one-person-one-vote principle, what institutional features of the Swiss system would necessarily have to be abandoned or redesigned?
