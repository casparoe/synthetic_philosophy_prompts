Consider the consistent judgments of a moral philosopher, Dr. V., on the following six cases involving the distinction between actively causing harm and allowing harm to occur:

(1) The switch version of the trolley problem: diverting a runaway trolley onto a side track where it kills one person, thereby saving five. Verdict: morally permissible, indeed required.

(2) The footbridge version: pushing one large person off a bridge onto the tracks to stop the trolley and save five. Verdict: morally forbidden.

(3) A hospital has one dialysis machine. Patient A is currently using it and will live with continued use but die without it. Patients B through F each need it for thirty minutes in sequence and will live if treated sequentially, but will die otherwise. The physician decides not to interrupt A’s treatment and instead treats B–F in turn; A dies, B–F live. Verdict: permissible.

(4) The same hospital, but the physician actively disconnects A from the machine in order to begin treating B–F. Verdict: impermissible.

(5) A physician administers a lethal injection to a competent, terminally ill patient who has explicitly requested death. Verdict: impermissible.

(6) The same patient requests withdrawal of life-sustaining ventilation; the physician complies and the patient dies within minutes. Verdict: permissible.

Now consider a new case. An autonomous clinical triage system—operating without real-time human oversight—is programmed with the rule that, if a single patient occupies a scarce life-support resource that could save N other patients (N > 1), and if that patient’s probability of survival with continued use of the resource falls below a predetermined threshold (say, 5%), the system automatically withdraws the resource and redistributes it. In one actual instance, the system withdraws oxygen from Patient G (projected survival with continued oxygen: 3%; he dies approximately eight minutes earlier than he otherwise would have) and saves five others. The withdrawal is executed by mechanical valves; no human bodily movement performs the disconnection, and no individual makes the decision at the time.

If Dr. V. wishes to remain consistent with her own precedents, what should she judge about the system’s action? Identify the closest analog among cases (1)–(6). Then articulate at least two distinct principles that could jointly explain all six of her earlier verdicts but diverge on this new case; for each principle, state whether it renders the system’s action permissible or forbidden. Offer your best guess at which principle she actually holds, with reasoning. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—one not yet mentioned—that would most sharply distinguish between these two principles and reveal which one she is running.
