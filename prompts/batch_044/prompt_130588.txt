My first interlocutor takes a broadly Margalit-inspired position. She argues that in societies recovering from mass atrocity, maintaining what she calls structural remembrance—deliberate institutional practices that keep the wrong visibly present, rather than merely depositing documents in an archive—constitutes a direct moral obligation owed to victims and to future generations. On her view, shared memory of injustice binds a community together in a specifically moral way, and institutional forgetting amounts to a second injury because it allows descendants of perpetrators to inherit practical denial. When pressed on whether this demands perpetual resentment or freezes grief in public display, she proposed a hypothetical mechanism she terms a mnemonic contract: survivors and the state agree to preserve only a minimal, curated record—sufficient to prevent denial but deliberately allowing other details to fade into illegibility—thereby honoring the duty to remember without requiring that suffering remain permanently on exhibit.

My second interlocutor, drawing on Rieff, responded that even this refined mnemonic contract treats historical suffering as a form of moral capital that must yield continuous communal interest, which is precisely the therapeutic distortion Rieff identified in modern culture. He contended that transforming grief into an institutional resource—whether through comprehensive archives or through calibrated minimal contracts—replaces genuine moral culture with grievance management. The therapeutic impulse, he insisted, is not absent from the contract but only refined: remembrance becomes an administrable emotional technology that serves the collective need for moral self-image rather than the private reality of loss. Against this, he proposed what he calls ethical forgetting, which is not amnesia but a principled refusal to bureaucratize grief, allowing suffering to remain genuinely private and therefore genuinely moral rather than publicly managed.

My first interlocutor then replied that this distinction between forgiveness and forgetting is exactly what Margalit's framework is designed to preserve: one can forgive without erasing the record, and institutional visibility need not entail perpetual resentment. She accused my second interlocutor of collapsing remembrance into its bureaucratic caricature, and she asked whether his ethical forgetting does not ultimately benefit perpetrators by removing the very social conditions that make denial costly. At this point she also introduced the concept of mnemonic fatigue, suggesting that communities often suffer not from remembering too little but from being compelled to perform remembrance through standardized rituals—mandatory curricula, commemorative calendars, institutionalized testimony—that strip memory of its particular moral weight and turn it into administrative routine.

My second interlocutor agreed that bureaucratized remembrance is deeply problematic, but insisted that this proves Rieff's point rather than undermining it. Once moral obligations are translated into institutional procedures—whether archives, contracts, or calibrated opacity—they lose their moral authority and become therapeutic technologies designed to manage collective anxiety rather than to honor the dead. He concluded that any defensible position must allow remembrance to wither naturally where it has done its moral work, and that the refusal to institutionalize grief is the only reliable way to prevent the ethics of memory from becoming exactly the kind of therapeutic culture it claims to resist. He then pressed my first interlocutor to specify any point at which remembrance has fulfilled its obligation and can be permitted to fade without that fading itself constituting an injustice.
