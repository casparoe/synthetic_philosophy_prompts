Two colleagues of mine—both trained in philosophy of mind and now advising on clinical machine-learning systems—recently argued about whether it makes sense to say that a diagnostic model “believes” a particular patient is unlikely to respond to a standard therapy. The model in question had learned, across its training distribution, that patients carrying a rare genetic marker M showed poor outcomes; in reality, M was largely confounded with advanced age, which was the actual driver of non-response, but the model had no explicit representation of that pathway. The first colleague said we should treat the system as holding the belief that “M implies non-response,” because doing so lets us predict its outputs and design targeted corrections—if we know what it believes, we can update its weights or constrain its recommendations—just as Dennett’s intentional stance allows us to predict human behavior without reducing it to neurobiology. The second colleague replied that this is precisely the wrong diagnosis: the failure is not a false belief inside a rational agent but a spurious statistical correlation, and attributing a belief directs engineers to try “reasoning with” the system or supplying counterexamples, when what is actually needed is data rebalancing or feature removal; since the model lacks any mechanism for reason-responsive belief revision, the intentional stance here is not merely unnecessary but actively misleading about the kind of error we face.

The first colleague then countered that his partner was smuggling in an internal-representational criterion that Dennett explicitly rejects. We attribute beliefs to humans based on sustained behavioral patterns and predictive success, not on access to neural implementation; the model consistently assigns high probability to outputs linking M to poor prognosis, and that behavioral coherence is structurally analogous to human belief. The fact that belief-revision in this substrate requires dataset intervention rather than argumentation shows only that revision mechanisms are substrate-dependent, not that no belief is present. To deny belief-attribution to the model while accepting it for humans, he said, requires either an unjustified biological chauvinism or an admission that all belief-talk is merely predictive heuristics—either of which undermines the partner’s objection.

The second colleague answered that this misses a normative asymmetry built into human belief-attribution. When we say a person believes p, we presuppose a rational agent who is accountable to evidence and can be criticized for failing to revise p when shown good counter-reasons. Applying the same framework to the model generates false expectations: it suggests we can correct the system by presenting it with reasons, when in fact it has no normative structure that makes reason-giving appropriate. The intentional stance is defensible only where rational agency can genuinely be presupposed; here it licenses a category mistake that treats a distributional artifact as a rational mistake, obscuring both the nature of the failure and the proper remedy.

Say who is right about which specific point, whether one side wins overall or the disagreement is partly a verbal dispute, and identify anything important that both parties overlook—particularly regarding whether the intentional stance requires predictive success alone, or also some form of rational coherence, and how that distinction applies to systems that behave consistently but lack any architecture for reason-responsive revision. Do not take sides as if you were a participant; report and evaluate the arguments as a bystander.
