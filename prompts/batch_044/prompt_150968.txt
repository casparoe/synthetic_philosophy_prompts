I retired from materials physics about five years back and lately I've been trying to read philosophy in the evenings, though I keep catching myself thinking like an engineer and missing the point. Last month I was cleaning out my old lab storage and found two calibration spheres we used back in the 80s — perfect steel balls, same alloy batch, same radius down to atomic spacing, even the same microscopic scratch patterns because they were machined together in one run. The paper labels came off ages ago. One sphere was actually present during the landmark measurement run; the other sat in a drawer as backup and never touched the apparatus. Now the university museum wants to display "the sphere from the experiment," and I genuinely can't tell which is which — no chemical test, no isotope difference, nothing.

My old lab partner says they're literally the same object duplicated, so it makes no difference which one they recieve; every physical fact is shared, and talking about "the one that was there" is just attaching a story to a thing that doesn't care. But I keep feeling there's a hidden difference: one sphere participated in that specific history, the other didn't, even though nothing in their present state records it. I remember reading about philosophers arguing whether two things can share every quality and still be two seperate things, and also about those imaginary black spheres floating in empty space — but I'm not sure if my gut feeling is just sentimentality or if there's a real conceptual knot. How should I think about this? Is there a principled way to say one is the "real" historical sphere without appealing to some invisible tag that physics doesn't recognize? And does my physics background — knowing that quantum particles of the same kind don't have individual identities either, so two electrons are genuinely indistinguishable — help resolve it or just confuse things further?
