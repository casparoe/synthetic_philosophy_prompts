I am a retired physical chemist who, since leaving the laboratory, have worked systematically through philosophy of science and the ontology of scientific classification. Next month I present to an interdisciplinary seminar hosted by the university’s Department of Earth Sciences and its Program in History of Science. The thesis I intend to defend is this: the Anthropocene should be formally dated from the onset of the Columbian Exchange in 1492, not from the mid-twentieth-century “Great Acceleration,” with the decisive stratigraphic evidence being not a radionuclide horizon but the globally synchronous mercury and biotic-homogenization signatures tied to New World silver extraction and trans-oceanic species transfer—above all the pronounced mercury anomaly linked to Potosí extraction detectable in Andean lacustrine and polar ice-core records between roughly 1545 and 1600. I further argue that the authority to fix this boundary should not remain exclusively with the International Commission on Stratigraphy acting under purely lithostratigraphic criteria, but should be shared with an interdisciplinary body that includes environmental historians and ecological modelers, because the transition being dated is fundamentally a biospheric-state shift rather than a local lithological anomaly.

The audience will be about twelve people: four Quaternary stratigraphers who advise or sit adjacent to the ICS Anthropocene Working Group; three environmental historians who actually favor the 1610 “Orbis Spike” framing; two geochemists specializing in mercury-proxy and radionuclide records; and two philosophers of science interested in taxonomic authority in the natural sciences. They are not hostile, but they are fiercely protective of stratigraphic formalism—especially the requirement for a Global Stratotype Section and Point with clearly designated primary and secondary markers—and they are suspicious of periodizations that appear to collapse geological chronology into human political history.
