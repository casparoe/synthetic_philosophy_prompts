For my philosophy coursework, I am investigating whether biography can serve as a genuine vehicle for moral learning, or whether it necessarily devolves into hagiography that distorts the past for present instruction. Give an example of a conceptual issue arising specifically within Plutarch's method of pairing Greek and Roman lives to illuminate virtue, when that comparative framework is applied to figures whose social structures are not merely different but conceptually incommensurable. I should like the example to turn on a concrete, non-standard pairing—say, a contemporary democratic whistle-blower and an ancient Roman patron—where the trait celebrated as exemplary virtue in one life functions, by the internal logic of the other, as a moral deficiency rather than merely an anachronism. Identify exactly where the contradiction lies in the comparative judgement itself, and explain why excising one figure to preserve consistency defeats the pedagogical purpose of using biography for moral formation. I do not require a bibliography or list of further sources; my aim is to work through the conceptual tension directly.
