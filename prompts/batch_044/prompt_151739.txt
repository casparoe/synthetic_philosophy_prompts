I've been trying to formalize the reasoning in the White Horse Dialogue—specifically Gongsun Long's claim that "white horse" is not "horse" because the satisfaction-conditions for shape and color differ, so that seeking one does not guarantee finding the other—and apply it to contemporary problems of cross-register classification. The dialogue's core move is that compound terms restrict the class picked out by the bare term: if you seek a horse, yellow or black ones satisfy the search, but if you seek a white horse, they do not. This suggests a broader principle about predicates drawn from different "registers," where a register is defined by the kind of satisfaction-condition involved.

Here is the argument I have built, and it seems to prove far too much. (1) "Machine" belongs to the functional-artifactual register: its satisfaction-condition is being an artifact arranged to perform operations. (2) "Conscious" belongs to the phenomenal register: its satisfaction-condition is the presence of subjective experience. (3) If two predicates belong to different registers, then seeking an object that satisfies both is not identical to seeking an object that satisfies either alone, because the satisfaction-conditions are not reducible to one another. (4) Therefore, "conscious machine" is not substitutable for "machine" in any context where only the functional-artifactual register is relevant—such as arguments about moral status that depend solely on artifact-hood, or impossibility results in algorithmic fairness that treat "fair classifier" and "classifier" as competing specifications of a single kind. (5) By parallel reasoning, "fair classifier" (normative register) is not substitutable for "classifier" (functional register), which means alleged conflicts between fairness and accuracy are not internal trade-offs but category mistakes: they treat two non-substitutable terms as if they named the same class of thing. (6) But if this holds for all cross-register compounds, then "red apple" (color versus botanical), "moral agent" (normative versus descriptive), and even "tall building" (spatial versus architectural) involve terms that are not substitutable for their bare components. (7) That eliminates compound predication entirely, which is clearly absurd.

The chain must break somewhere between (1) and (6), but I cannot pinpoint the damage. I suspect the error lies either in (3), where I slide from "different satisfaction-conditions" to "non-identical seeking-contexts," or in (4), where I move from a semantic distinction about substitutability to a normative claim about what arguments are permitted. If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (2)—that registers are genuinely distinct—than accept (7) and abandon all compound description, but I want to know exactly what rejecting the weak step costs me. Does giving up (3) collapse the distinction between physical and phenomenal properties, or does rejecting (4) merely limit the scope of substitutability failures without undermining the register distinction itself? Which step should I reject, and why did it look so plausible?
