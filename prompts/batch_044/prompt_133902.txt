I'm preparing a ninety-minute seminar session for an interdisciplinary reading group—mostly philosophy graduate students with a couple of computer-science students who work on autonomous systems—on Elizabeth Anscombe's critique of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and her argument that killing non-combatants was not a merely foreseen side effect but was directed by the very logic of the act itself. I do not want the session to become a historical recitation of 1945; I want the conceptual architecture to be what remains. Could you plan the explanatory sequence rather than deliver the explanation?

Specifically, I need the dependency order: which distinction must be in place before any other—likely the logical difference between directing an action at a particular outcome and accepting that outcome as a collateral occurrence—so that the audience sees why Anscombe treats psychological innocence (not desiring civilian death) as irrelevant to the moral assessment. I also need the single carrying example that should run through the entire session. I am considering a hypothetical modern analog: an autonomous targeting system that computes civilian proximity but treats that data as a predictive constraint rather than as part of its aim, allowing the strike to proceed precisely when civilian presence is anticipated but not targeted. I want your judgment on whether this example can carry the idea without collapsing into a utilitarian calculation, what features it must include and exclude, and at exactly which point in the sequence it should be introduced so that it illuminates rather than distracts.

Then the misconception map: the audience will almost certainly arrive thinking Anscombe is advancing a simple pacifism, that she is applying the principle of double effect in an unmodified Thomistic form, or that she is making an empirical claim about Truman's private mental states. I need to know in which order these misunderstandings will surface and precisely where in the explanatory arc to head off each—especially the computer-science students' tendency to translate 'intention' into system objective function or optimization target. Finally, what check at the end would reveal whether they have grasped the logical structure of directed action rather than merely adopted a moral posture? And what should I explicitly leave out at this level—perhaps the full metaphysics of human action, the biographical context, or the subsequent literature on just war theory—while saying so openly? The plan should account for the fact that half the room reasons in terms of design specifications and predictive models rather than in terms of moral psychology.
