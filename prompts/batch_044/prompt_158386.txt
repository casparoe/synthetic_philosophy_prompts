Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on epistemic disagreement, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to force a dilemma: either rational peer disagreement is impossible in principle, or evidentialism about belief collapses. I want to know exactly where the slip occurs, and what blocking that step costs me.

(1) Call two agents epistemic peers with respect to a proposition P just in case they share the same total evidence E and possess identical reasoning competence.
(2) The dogmatism paradox shows that if peer A initially assigns rational credence c to P on E, and then learns that peer B assigns c' ≠ c on the same E, then consistency requires A to revise her credence away from c and toward c', on pain of arbitrarily privileging her own reasoning.
(3) Now introduce what I call the symmetry-of-deference principle: if A defers to B by adjusting her credence by some amount d, then B, upon learning of A's adjustment, must defer back by the same amount d; otherwise one peer arbitrarily treats her own updated state as more authoritative than the other peer's symmetric state.
(4) Model the mutual update as an iterative process in which each peer's credence at stage n+1 is the arithmetic mean of the two credences at stage n.
(5) Because this is a linear contraction mapping on the space of credence pairs, the sequence converges to a unique fixed point—specifically, the arithmetic mean (c + c')/2—independent of the order in which the revisions are revealed.
(6) Therefore, whenever rational peers initially disagree, rationality forces convergence to a midpoint that depends on the contingent initial values c and c', not on E alone.
(7) But evidentialism holds that rational credence is fully determined by one's total evidence; if the rational value for P on E is not unique, evidentialism is false.
(8) Conversely, if evidentialism is true, then c must equal c' for all true peers, which means peer disagreement is impossible from the outset.
(9) Hence we face an exclusive disjunction: either evidentialism fails, or the concept of rational peer disagreement is incoherent.

I am inclined to preserve (1) and (2), and I would strongly prefer not to abandon evidentialism if I can avoid it. Should I reject the symmetry-of-deference principle in (3), or is there a hidden scope shift between the one-time revision in (2) and the continuous iteration in (4)? Does the convergence argument in (5) illegitimately assume that rational updating must be symmetric and continuous, or is there a more subtle modal slide between "must revise" and "must converge"? Tell me which step to reject, why it looked plausible, and precisely what I pay for giving it up.
