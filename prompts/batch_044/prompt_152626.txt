Within the framework associated with Émilie du Châtelet's reflections on happiness—where happiness is not mere pleasure but a state appropriate to beings with rational and reflective capacities, requiring both the exercise of one's highest faculties and an absence of self-deception about them—I have arrived at the following verdicts about concrete cases, and I want to know whether a single coherent principle can capture them all.

Case 1. A philosopher lives in material poverty, suffers persistent physical discomfort, spends nearly every waking hour in metaphysical study, desires no social pleasures, and reports that her life is genuinely flourishing. Verdict: she is genuinely happy.

Case 2. A wealthy merchant enjoys excellent food, widespread social admiration, and constant leisure travel; he has no interest in study, reflection, or the development of his rational capacities; he reports feeling completely fulfilled. Verdict: he is not genuinely happy—he lacks an essential component appropriate to his nature.

Case 3. A mathematician achieves significant breakthroughs but is driven entirely by terror of obscurity and competitive anxiety; she feels no joy in reasoning itself, and her internal state is one of dread. Verdict: she is not genuinely happy—the orientation of her activity is wrong, regardless of external success.

Case 4. A woman of exceptional intellectual gifts abandons scholarship permanently to care for a dying parent; she finds deep peace and meaning in the care, never returns to study, and reports she has never been happier. Verdict: she is genuinely happy—but this sits uneasily with Case 1 if study is strictly essential.

Case 5. A person of moderate intellectual capacity studies diligently in a field well-suited to her level, takes clear pleasure in the work, maintains close friendships, and enjoys moderate sensory goods without excess; she does not aspire beyond her reach. Verdict: she is genuinely happy, and perhaps in a more stable form than in Case 1.

Case 6. A patron of science lives comfortably, reads widely for display, and secretly believes she has wasted her highest intellectual potential; she suppresses this belief and insists to herself and others that she is content. Verdict: she is not genuinely happy—self-deception about one's capacities undermines the state.

Is there a coherent principle that accommodates all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If so, state it precisely, identifying which clause is motivated by each case, and apply it to two further cases I have not considered: (a) a grieving scholar who works obsessively not for love of truth but to avoid confronting loss, yet reports that the work saves her; and (b) a being with no rational or reflective capacities whatsoever who lives in constant mild pleasure with no suffering. If the principle cannot save all six verdicts, say explicitly which one must be dropped, whether dropping it favors Case 1 or Case 4, and whether the resulting principle is simpler or merely more accommodating. Finally, flag any point where the principle relies on a judgment call—such as how to identify one's "highest capacities" or whether suppression of belief always counts as self-deception—rather than on a determinate test.
