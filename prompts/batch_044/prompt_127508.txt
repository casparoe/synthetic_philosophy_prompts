After a long argument with a friend about democratic backsliding, I want to check whether his summary holds up before I respond. He writes: “Democratic backsliding should be defined as the incremental erosion of horizontal accountability—courts, legislatures, and independent media losing autonomy—combined with what he calls discursive closure, in which the ruling party systematically frames opposition parties and civil society as illegitimate enemies of the nation. By this combined standard, Hungary under Orbán, Poland under PiS, Turkey under Erdoğan, and the United States since 2025 all exhibit the same core pattern: court packing, media capture, and the delegitimization of political opponents. The concept is therefore not a partisan label, because indices like V-Dem and Freedom House apply uniform metrics globally; once executive constraints and civil liberties fall below a defined threshold, a country is classified as an electoral autocracy rather than a liberal democracy, with no remaining ambiguity. Consequently, he argues, the debate over whether the US belongs in this group is purely empirical—either institutional capture has occurred or it has not—rather than a matter of interpretation or normative disagreement.” Which parts of this account would a comparative-politics scholar accept? Where does it conflate the process of backsliding with the endpoint of autocratization, overstate consensus on the American case, or present contested classification thresholds as purely objective measurements? And does his claim that the partisan-label objection collapses once we invoke uniform metrics actually survive scrutiny, or does it overlook the normative judgments embedded in selecting, weighting, and interpreting those metrics?
