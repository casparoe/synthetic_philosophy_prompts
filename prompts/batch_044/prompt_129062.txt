I am a novelist who has been invited to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop next month titled "The Dead on Screen," convening academic historians and film producers who will spend three days debating the ethics of biographical drama. The audience is evenly divided: half are scholars who regard any invented dialogue as an archival betrayal, and half are screenwriters who treat historical records as raw material for emotional narrative. I do not want a ceremonial welcome. I want the remarks to propose, defend, and structurally apply a specific ethical constraint governing our dramatization of real people who are dead and cannot consent. My position is that what we owe the dead is not biographical accuracy—since selection and framing are already interpretation—but rather the preservation of moral distance: we must not collapse their alien reasoning into contemporary psychological categories. I will call this obligation respect for the architecture of their commitments, by which I mean the internal logic that made their choices coherent within a worldview we no longer share. The speech should open by stating this principle, then demonstrate its practical force through a single concrete hypothetical: imagine dramatizing a nineteenth-century civil engineer who destroyed his own patent applications because he believed that proprietary knowledge violated a theological doctrine of common stewardship now largely forgotten. The example should show that inventing a private scene is permissible—perhaps even illuminating—when it renders that alien logic visible, but exploitative when it reduces his refusal to a modern narrative of anxiety or ambition. The remarks must navigate both halves of the room: rigorous enough that the historians recognize the argument as substantive ethics, yet concrete enough that the filmmakers understand it as a creative boundary rather than a prohibition. The tone should be formal and polished, approximately eight minutes when spoken, and written in American English. Please write this speech.
