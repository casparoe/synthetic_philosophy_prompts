I have been trying to extend what I understand as Sen’s liberal paradox beyond its usual form. The basic idea I grasp is that if each of two people has a minimal right over some personal choice, collective decision rules can be forced to violate at least one of those rights even when everyone agrees on efficiency. I think I have built a chain that pushes this further and shows that liberalism cannot coexist with any collective governance at all, but the conclusion feels too strong. Here is my reasoning:

(1) Minimal liberalism assigns each person at least one protected personal domain: there exists some pair of alternatives over which that person’s preference alone should determine the outcome.

(2) If conditions in domain C causally shape what I can do or prefer in my protected domain D, and C is governed collectively, then my liberal right in D is held hostage to collective choices in C.

(3) For every realistic domain D—what I read, whom I marry, how I work—the relevant conditions in C (education norms, infrastructure, information environments, legal defaults) are indeed collectively determined.

(4) Therefore every first-order liberal right is held hostage to collective choice.

(5) To prevent collective choice in C from eroding my right in D, liberalism must extend protection to the conditions in C, giving me a second-order liberal right over those collective inputs.

(6) But the conditions in C affect everyone; granting me a decisive right over them violates the liberal rights of others in their own domains.

(7) So liberalism faces a forced choice: either deny second-order rights and let collective governance dissolve all first-order rights in practice, or grant them and make collective governance impossible because every input is privatized.

(8) Since collective governance is unavoidable, minimal liberalism promises protected private spheres and then necessarily dissolves them; it is therefore incoherent.

I want to keep (1)—that people should have some genuinely protected domains—and (7)—that there is a deep tension between those domains and collective life. Which step should I reject? Is the error in treating causal influence as normative interference, or in sliding from “influenced” to “must be controlled”? Or is the argument actually valid, and the cost is accepting that liberalism must abandon either minimal rights or collective decision-making altogether?
