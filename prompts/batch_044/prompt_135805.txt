As I work through the architecture of my next novel, I find myself returning repeatedly to Borges’s conception of time—not the popular caricature of branching universes, but the more austere denial of successive duration that animates essays such as A New Refutation of Time and narratives like The Circular Ruins. I have been testing various structural devices against my intuition for whether they genuinely instantiate that denial, or merely decorate linear chronology with paradox, and I would like to know whether my judgments trace a coherent principle. A narrative in which each chapter recounts the same fatal encounter with progressive amnesia—so that the protagonist never retains the prior iteration, yet the reader perceives the loop—feels authentic: the repetition is real for the form, not for the consciousness within it, and the present moment is never privileged. By contrast, a plot that allows a protagonist to move laterally between two fully realized timelines, treating both as co-present and equally traversable, strikes me as a science-fictional misreading; it preserves the very reality of temporal branches that Borges collapses into a single, timeless configuration. A causal loop in which the final page reveals that the entire narrative was authored by a character introduced in the opening scene—leaving no temporal origin—seems to me an equally pure structural analog, since linear priority is dissolved by authorship itself. Meanwhile, a deterministic narrative in which every event is symbolically prefigured by an earlier image feels closer to Schopenhauer’s eternal repetition or Nietzsche’s recurrence than to Borges’s specific negation of succession; I reject it. A memoir composed entirely in the future perfect tense, describing a life that will have been lived without any present-tense anchoring, also strikes me as authentic, though here the denial operates linguistically rather than structurally, and I am unsure whether that distinction matters. The case that continues to unsettle me is a text composed solely of footnotes to a missing main body, where the footnotes cite one another in an order that contradicts their numbering, so that temporal priority becomes purely relational within the apparatus: I cannot decide whether this is a profound formalization of Borges’s view or merely an arch exercise in self-reference.

Is there a single principle that unifies the verdicts I have affirmed—the amnesiac loop, the originless authorship loop, and the future-perfect memoir—while explaining precisely why the lateral-branch narrative and the deterministic prefiguration fail? I want the principle stated with enough precision that each clause is tied to the feature of its motivating case, and I want an honest statement of which of my judgments it must sacrifice if simplicity is preferred over saving every verdict. Please also apply the principle to the footnote case I keep revising, and tell me what it predicts for an unlisted scenario: a narrative in which the identical sentence appears on every page, each time rendered in a different language, with no indication of which translation is authoritative and no progression of meaning across the sequence.
