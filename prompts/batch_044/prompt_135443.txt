The author summarised Gödel's position in this way: Gödel constructed an exact rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations in which closed timelike curves exist—paths that loop back into the observer's own past. Rather than treating these curves as a physical prediction, Gödel interpreted them philosophically: he claimed that if general relativity, a well-confirmed theory, permits journeys into one's past, then linear objective time cannot be a fundamental feature of reality, and the experience of temporal passage must be a subjective phenomenon, closer to Kant's view of time as a form of intuition than to any physical parameter. The summary added that Gödel observed the absence of astronomical evidence for universal rotation, and that he used this absence not only to conclude that our own cosmos avoids these loops, but also to argue that the very possibility of such loops within the theory demonstrates that Einstein's framework describes a world without an intrinsic temporal direction. Finally, the author suggested that Gödel's reasoning anticipates modern eternalism—the view that all temporal moments exist equally—because if every moment lies on a closed loop, there is no privileged present that distinguishes past from future. I am inclined to accept this last point, yet I suspect the summary may conflate Gödel's cosmological model with his philosophical conclusions, and perhaps misrepresent his attitude toward Einstein's theory. Could you audit this account? Please go through it sentence by sentence, indicating what a scholar of Gödel's work would accept, what they would correct or qualify, and whether the eternalist reading is a fair extension of his view or an anachronistic imposition. In particular, does the author's inference—that Gödel ultimately sought to defend the objectivity of physical time—survive once these distinctions are drawn? Please reply in British English.
