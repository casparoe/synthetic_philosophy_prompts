(1) In his Autobiography Franklin describes moral perfection as living without committing any fault at any time, and he pursues it through a weekly ledger tracking 13 virtues.
(2) If you've genuinely achieved a perfect state in some virtue — say, perfect temperance — then you have no faliures to record in that row.
(3) But Franklin's method requires you to keep tracking every virtue every week, regardless of past performance; the ritual itself is part of the habit-formation.
(4) So once you're perfect at temperance, you're still performing the tracking ritual for it, which takes mental effort that could be spent elsewhere.
(5) Now Franklin also lists Industry and Order among the virtues, and wasting mental resources on solved problems violates both.
(6) Therefore achieving perfect temperance forces you into violating Industry and Order through redundant tracking.
(7) By the same reasoning, reaching perfection in all 13 virtues forces you to violate Industry and Order across the board, since you'd be maintaining a full ledger with nothing in it.
(8) But Franklin defines perfection as having zero faults at any time, so this is a straight contradiction.
(9) So Franklin's conception is incoherent: you can't reach it without breaking it.

He wants me to reject the asymptotic reading and say Franklin should have dropped the ledger once he improved. I'd rather keep the idea that perfection is an asymptotic target, not a binary state you ever fully occupy. But if I keep that, am I just redefining the problem away?
