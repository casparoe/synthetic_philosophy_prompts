I have been thinking about Wittgenstein's ideas about language games and family resemblance outside of any formal coursework, and I want to test whether I actually understand the concept or am just repeating phrases like no common essence. To check myself, I am designing an exam for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar—students who have encountered Wittgenstein's later writings but are not specialists. I need one essay question that can be completed in about fifty minutes in roughly seven hundred to nine hundred words, together with a model answer of about six hundred words and a marking scheme.

The question should turn on a specific, somewhat unusual scenario so that a student cannot answer it by reciting standard examples. Here is the scenario: imagine a small, isolated community that gathers once a week for a ritual. During the ritual, members point at various objects—stones, trees, water—and chant single syllables in unison. They never correct one another, never use these syllables outside the ritual, show no reaction if a syllable is chanted at the wrong object, and have no practice of disagreement or justification about the pairings. The essay prompt should ask whether this ritual counts as a Wittgensteinian language game, and whether applying the idea of family resemblance to this case saves Wittgenstein from essentialism or instead makes the concept of language game so vague that it loses explanatory power.

The model answer must contain five specific moves: first, explain why the ritual superficially resembles a language game; second, identify which normative or practical features of ordinary language games—such as correction, rule-following, or embeddedness in a form of life—are absent here; third, argue either that these missing features are unnecessary because family resemblance denies any essential core, or that some minimal normative structure is still required for the concept to do real work; fourth, respond directly to the worry that if this ritual counts then language game applies to almost anything; fifth, reach a clear, defended conclusion about whether Wittgenstein's framework helps us understand or mischaracterizes the ritual.

The marking scheme should distribute twenty points total: four points for each of the five moves above, plus four points for clarity, direct engagement with the scenario, and coherent prose. For each point value, please note the specific mistake that should cost marks—especially the mistake of merely defining family resemblance without applying it to the ritual, or listing features of language games without arguing about the boundary case. Please write the entire response in American English, and do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation requests, since this is meant to be answerable without external sources.
