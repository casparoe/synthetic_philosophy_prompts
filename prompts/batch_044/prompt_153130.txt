Having just spent three months helping a relative navigate benefit sanctions triggered by a missed appointment she never received notice for, I have come to see that philosophical defenses of workfare rarely examine how bureaucratic verification itself manufactures the category of the "undeserving poor." I am setting the final exam for my upper-level undergraduate political philosophy seminar: 90 minutes, approximately 1,500 words, 25 marks total. I need one essay question that cannot be answered by summarizing familiar contractarian justifications of conditionality or by rehearsing the standard moral distinction between deserving and undeserving recipients. Instead, the question should require students to argue whether the "undeserving poor" is a pre-political moral kind that welfare administration merely discovers, or whether it is a bureaucratic artifact produced by procedural opacity, asymmetric information, and the institutional demand that claimants perform docility and gratitude in order to prove moral worth. Please provide the exam question, a model answer that makes four key argumentative moves, and a marking scheme that assigns specific marks to each move (total 25), together with a one-line note on the characteristic error students make on each point; the standard should penalize answers that treat fraud detection as a neutral epistemic exercise rather than as a normative practice that constructs the very failure it claims to find.
