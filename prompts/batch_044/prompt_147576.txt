I'm working up my position for a meta-ethics seminar on Confucian role ethics and want a consistency audit before I present. Here is my package:

(1) The moral self is exhaustively constituted by relational roles—parent, child, ruler, subject, friend, and so on—with no pre-social authentic core or Cartesian residue standing beneath them.

(2) These roles generate thick, particularistic obligations: what I owe this specific parent or this specific ruler is not fully reducible to universal moral principles.

(3) Roles are nevertheless open to immanent critique; a father or ruler can violate the normative structure of fatherhood or rulership so radically that resistance is required, not merely permitted.

(4) The ground for such critique lies in the innate four sprouts—compassion, shame, deference, and discernment of right and wrong—capacities present in human nature prior to full ritual cultivation.

(5) Ritual propriety (li) is the exclusive mechanism of moral development: the sprouts become virtues only through disciplined participation in li, and there is no moral growth outside this framework.

(6) Therefore, all legitimate resistance to corrupt roles must be articulated entirely within the vocabulary of the roles and rituals being criticized; invoking a role-independent standpoint—whether natural rights, Kantian autonomy, or an unmediated true self—is conceptually illegitimate.

Please audit this: which commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision for each genuine tension? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each say exactly which commitment bears the cost and what that revision does to the rest of the package. I am not signaling which view I would drop; treat the set as a live position that needs internal repair.
