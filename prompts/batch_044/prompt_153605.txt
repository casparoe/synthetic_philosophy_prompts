For a philosophy of mind and epistemology seminar of twelve advanced undergraduates, meeting for ninety minutes, design a structured in-class role-play or deliberation game that makes participants feel the force of epistemic akrasia generated by higher-order evidence. Specifically, they should experience the tension between holding a first-order belief and simultaneously accepting evidence that the very reasoning that produced that belief is unreliable.

The activity must be runnable without digital aids, using only paper, assigned roles, and a non-monetary scoring mechanism. Arrange the rules and incentives so that the conflict emerges organically: participants should first reach and defend a collective first-order judgment through a reasoning task, then receive meta-evidence delivered purely by the structure of the game (for example, through revealed statistical information about group error rates or through a simulated expert disagreement with themselves) that undermines confidence in that judgment, and finally be forced to act—by voting, wagering points, or committing to a public stance—on whether they retain the belief. The philosophical idea should appear in what they do, not in an introductory lecture.

Inject conceptual specificity that moves beyond standard introductory framing: build the scenario around the distinction between level-splitting responses, where first-order and higher-order attitudes are permitted to diverge without revision, and total-evidence integration, where higher-order evidence must be incorporated into the belief state. Include a precise moment where participants must decide whether to treat their own prior reasoning as an epistemic peer in disagreement with themselves, and require them to record both their first-order verdict and their higher-order assessment of its reliability before acting.

Provide: a minute-by-minute timeline; the roles, rules, or constraints that generate the higher-order conflict; the payoff or scoring scheme; a debrief that maps each stage onto the structure of higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia; an honest account of which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the diachronic stability of rational self-doubt, or the normative question of whether revision is obligatory rather than merely permissible); and a frank prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run, with concrete suggestions for handling it.

In your response, separate what is broadly settled about the phenomenon of epistemic akrasia from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—particularly whether level-splitting is ever rationally permissible, whether total-evidence views demand an unrealistic degree of cognitive self-transparency, and whether treating oneself as a peer in disagreement is structurally coherent—and do not present any contested position as the consensus view.
