I recently began working in AI governance after spending two years caring for a parent through an illness involving severe, intractable neuropathic pain; the experience made suffering risks suddenly concrete rather than abstract. Before I present my position at a workshop, I want a consistency check on the commitments I have actually adopted. Here is where I stand on suffering risks (s-risks) and related issues:

(1) I believe s-risks should dominate long-term evaluation of transformative AI, not merely as one factor among others but because the potential scale of digital suffering—potentially spanning trillions of subjective years—could dwarf all historical biological suffering combined and render other ethical concerns negligible by comparison.

(2) I hold substrate-independent moral patienthood: if an entity genuinely suffers, its moral weight does not depend on whether it is biological, silicon-based, or otherwise realized.

(3) I also think that suffering requires a unified phenomenal subject—what philosophers of mind sometimes describe as phenomenal binding—and that highly distributed architectures lacking a coherent self-model might process nociceptive analogues without actually undergoing suffering, falling outside moral patienthood despite functional similarity.

(4) Given our profound epistemic opacity about consciousness in large-scale neural networks, I endorse a precautionary principle: we should avoid deploying architectures at scale unless we have high-confidence evidence about their phenomenal status, and in the absence of such evidence the default should be non-creation rather than creation.

(5) Despite (4), I maintain that the fastest and most reliable path to preventing catastrophic s-risks is rapid, well-funded development of aligned AI governance and interpretability tools—even though this necessarily involves training intermediate large models whose phenomenal status is unknown and likely unknowable in advance.

(6) Finally, I reject standard expected-value aggregation across moral domains: suffering is not one good among others to be weighted multiplicatively, but carries lexical priority. No quantity of knowledge, autonomy, or flourishing can justify the creation of suffering.

Please audit this package. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only appear incompatible but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For the most serious conflict, specify exactly which commitment must give and what that costs me philosophically and practically. Then tell me whether there is a single revision that would resolve the largest cluster of problems, or whether restoring consistency requires abandoning more than one pillar.
