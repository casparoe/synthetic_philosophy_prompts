I've been thinking about what separates doing the right thing from doing it for the right reason, and I've started framing it with two distinctions I keep returning to: what I call "motivational ownership"—the reason actually causing me to act—and "normative alignment"—whether that reason matches the justification for the act. They usually overlap, but I've noticed they can split: I might help a stranger out of genuine compassion when I have no actual duty to do so (ownership without alignment), or I might file my taxes correctly out of pure habit, where the correct reason—civic obligation—is present in my beliefs but plays no causal role in getting me to the desk (alignment without ownership).

My question concerns the second split. If an act is normatively aligned—I do what morality requires, and I could sincerely cite the right justification—but motivational ownership is absent because the real engine was routine, social expectation, or simple inertia, does the act possess any moral worth? Specifically, I've wondered whether philosophers allow for what I call "reflective ratification": the idea that I can look back at my habitual action, genuinely endorse the reason that should have moved me, and thereby confer moral worth retroactively, almost like applying a signature after the fact. If ratification works, then ordinary moral life—keeping promises out of custom, returning lost items because that's what one does—might retain its value. But if prior motivational ownership is strictly necessary and cannot be substituted by later endorsement, then it seems a vast portion of everyday decent behavior would lack moral worth entirely, which feels implausible but also hard to reject.

So I want to ask: is there a coherent philosophical position that treats reflective ratification as sufficient for moral worth, or does the absence of prior ownership always defeat it? And if the latter, how do philosophers avoid the conclusion that only deliberately reasoned, moment-by-moment moral choices count?
