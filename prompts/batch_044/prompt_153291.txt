In discussions of Kelly betting and geometric rationality, a recurring analogy likens maximising expected logarithmic wealth to tuning an engine for fuel efficiency over an indefinite voyage, whilst treating arithmetic expected-value maximisation as tuning the same engine for peak speed on a single, terminal lap. Advocates deploy this to argue that geometric rationality is the only framework fit for an agent with a genuinely long-run perspective, and that arithmetic rationality is implicitly myopic—treating every decision as if there were no tomorrow. Analyse the analogy precisely: set out the correspondence table (engine, voyage, fuel, speed, driver, mechanic, destination, and so on, mapped onto wealth, utility, growth rate, bankruptcy risk, and the decision-maker's temporal horizon); then locate the points of collapse—especially the fact that real agents are finite and mortal, that bankruptcy is often irreversible rather than merely a low-fuel warning, that utility may saturate or shift with scale, and that the voyage has no predetermined endpoint—separating those disanalogies that undermine the conclusion from those that do not. Does the claim that arithmetic rationality is necessarily shortsighted survive, or was that conclusion carried entirely by the analogy's hidden premises about survival and continuity? If a different analogy—perhaps drawn from evolutionary dynamics or information-theoretic compression—would represent the distinction more honestly, indicate what it would be and why.
