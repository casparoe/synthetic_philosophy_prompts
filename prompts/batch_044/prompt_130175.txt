After an exhausting argument last night with a close friend about whether vague predicates like "tall" or "bald" hide sharp boundaries or are genuinely open, I want to see whether two committed opponents could actually write a joint document rather than talk past each other. Please draft the adversarial-collaboration statement that a committed epistemicist and a committed supervaluationist should have produced together.

Name the two parties explicitly: the Epistemicist Camp and the Supervaluationist Camp.

The document should have three parts, written in a formal, polished style suitable for publication in a philosophy venue, not as a transcript.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. Include agreement that the sorites paradox reveals a genuine tension in ordinary reasoning; that tolerance principles are initially plausible yet generate contradiction when iterated; that classical bivalence holds for fully precise languages; that vagueness is not reducible to ordinary speaker ignorance about an already-determined fact; and that purely degree-theoretic or contextualist resolutions that deny bivalence altogether are inadequate. Both sides should also agree on the structure of a synthetic case we will call the Gradient Artifact: an engineered entity whose measurable properties vary continuously across every proposed sharp boundary for "organism" versus "artifact," where different expert panels draw different regulatory lines, no natural discontinuity is detectable, and iterative re-annotation does not converge—both camps accept that this is structurally analogous to standard sorites cases and that any adequate theory of vagueness must account for it.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one precise divergence in wording that the named camp itself would accept. For the Epistemicist Camp: that every vague predicate has a unique precise extension, that borderline cases involve pure epistemic ignorance of a hidden sharp boundary, and that calibrated uncertainty in any classifier reflects our cognitive limitations rather than semantic openness. For the Supervaluationist Camp: that there is no unique precise extension for a vague predicate, that truth for vague sentences is "supertruth" (truth on all admissible precisifications), that in borderline cases there is genuinely no fact of the matter, and that the set of admissible precisifications need not itself have sharp boundaries. Include also their disagreement about second-order vagueness—whether the meta-level distinction between admissible and inadmissible precisifications is itself sharp—and about whether a machine-learning system's calibrated confidence near a boundary tracks hidden fact or reflects genuine indeterminacy in the predicate.

Third, the cruxes. Identify at least three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. (1) Whether second-order vagueness undermines the epistemicist's claim that there is always a hidden sharp boundary, even at the meta-level. (2) Whether the Gradient Artifact's failure to converge under expert re-annotation is better explained by our ignorance of a single correct cutoff or by the absence of any cutoff to be known. (3) Whether the supervaluationist's appeal to "admissible" precisifications covertly relies on a sharp boundary that replicates the very problem vagueness was meant to solve.

For each crux, specify the concrete case, argument, or observation both sides agree is relevant—such as the behavior of the Gradient Artifact under iterative expert labeling, or the logical properties of a formal system that either selects one unknown extension or permits all admissible ones. Then record each camp's prediction in advance: state exactly what empirical or logical result each would take as evidence favoring its view over the other, and what result would genuinely weaken its own position.

Throughout, separate what is settled in the philosophy of vagueness from what remains genuinely contested among specialists. Do not present either the epistemicist or the supervaluationist position as the consensus view. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or specific page references. Maintain the voice of someone who has just finished arguing with a friend and now seeks intellectual clarity and precise disagreement rather than victory.
