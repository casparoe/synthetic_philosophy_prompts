I'm working on a piece about Hume's essays on money and trade, and I keep running into this anology everyone uses: money is like the oil in a machine — not one of the wheels, just the lubricent that lets commerce turn smoothly. Hume basically said this, and now people use it to argue that monetary policy is just maintenence: add more oil when things grind, but never confuse the lubricant for the engine. I want to audit how far that mapping actually holds. Can you lay out the correspondences explicitely — what's the wheel, what's the oil, what's the machine? Then tell me where it breaks: does money actually get consumed like oil, is it uniform, can it be created endogenously by banks rather than poured in from outside? What does the image quietly import about value or neutrality that Hume's actual theory doesn't commit to? And given those breaks — especially once you factor in modern endogenous money creation or digital currencies that don't flow like a physical fluid — does the conclusion that money is always neutral still survive? Or should we swap in a different analogy — electricity, language, scorekeeping — and if so, what would it change about Hume's point?
