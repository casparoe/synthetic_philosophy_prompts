I am currently taking a philosophy of science seminar focused on the epistemology of simultaneous discovery, and I have come to defend a position I will call the convergence thesis. The thesis holds that cases such as Newton and Leibniz's independent formulation of calculus, and Darwin and Wallace's concurrent presentation of natural selection, demonstrate that scientific discoveries are largely inevitable once a discipline reaches the appropriate stage of conceptual maturity and technical capacity. Specifically, I maintain three claims: first, that the convergence of independent researchers onto functionally equivalent formal structures indicates the discovery was dictated by the objective problem-space rather than by individual genius; second, that divergences in notation, emphasis, and auxiliary commitments—Leibniz's differential notation versus Newton's fluxions, or Darwin's extensive treatment of sexual selection versus Wallace's stricter focus on adaptation—constitute merely superficial packaging that does not alter the core epistemic content; and third, that priority disputes are therefore sociologically interesting but philosophically hollow, since the knowledge would have entered the scientific community regardless of which name was attached. I want an honest assessment of what would genuinely count against this view. Not merely the familiar point that personal temperament shapes research trajectories, but which specific historical patterns, conceptual analyses, or sociological findings would have to obtain for the inevitability claim to fail? For example, would it be decisive if we could demonstrate that a discovery decomposes into modular conceptual components, only some of which were actually reached by both parties, with the remaining modules turning out to be essential for the theory's later development? Or that one formulation contained a latent conceptual resource—say, a particular interpretation of continuity or variation—that the other formulation structurally excluded, and that this exclusion later constrained the research program? Please distinguish defeaters that would be fatal from those that would be merely awkward, indicate which qualifications defenders of the convergence thesis could reasonably absorb without abandoning the position, and assess whether the thesis risks unfalsifiability—particularly through the strategic move of reclassifying any divergence as mere packaging. If it has been insulated in this way, is that insulation a genuine problem for a thesis of this kind, or is it an appropriate feature of a structural claim about scientific development?
