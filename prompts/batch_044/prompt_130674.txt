I have recently had to judge whether my late grandmother's ceramic figure, shattered and rebuilt by a conservator who injected synthetic resin that chemically bonded with the remaining clay into a continuous gradient substrate with no discrete boundary between original and foreign matter, remains the same artwork she made. I now hold that an artifact survives material replacement only when its original matter remains numerically dominant in the structurally essential core; when the substrate becomes an inseparable hybrid in which original mass is no longer dominant, what persists is not the original statue but a new object that merely coincides with it in form and location. What would count against this view? I want more than the familiar Ship of Theseus intuitions: tell me which formal accounts of material constitution, which restoration cases in which synthetic material becomes structurally load-bearing, and which parallels with biological prosthesis or organ replacement it stands or falls with. Which defeaters would a defender of substrate dominance concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be dismissed by refining the dominance condition? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and assess honestly whether the view can be qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that insulation is a vice for a claim about concrete objects.
