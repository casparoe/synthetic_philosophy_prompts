In a seminar on the postcolonial critique of modernity, a colleague repeatedly invoked the one-liner “Enlightenment universalism was never universal; it was particularism in disguise” to reject any appeal to human rights, scientific objectivity, or autonomous reason as mere ideological cover for European domination. Before I respond, I want to disentangle exactly what work that sentence could be doing. Please lay out the distinct precise claims it might express, arranged as a conceptual menu rather than a single interpretation. For each reading, specify whether it is primarily a historical claim about the actual intentions or beliefs of Enlightenment figures; a conceptual claim about whether universalist reasoning contains an internal logic of exclusion; an institutional claim about how Enlightenment categories—especially taxonomic and classificatory schemes ordering nature and human populations—were operationalized in colonial administration; or a normative claim about whether Enlightenment values can be separated from the contexts of their emergence. Distinguish the weak, almost tautological version—acknowledging that every thinker is culturally situated—from stronger versions that posit a necessary link between reason and domination. In particular, address the thesis that hierarchical classification was not an accidental misuse but a structural feature of the Enlightenment project itself, and clarify whether that thesis targets Enlightenment reason as such or only its colonial applications. Please separate what is broadly settled among historians and philosophers of science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists in this debate, and do not present any single contested interpretation as the consensus view. I am not looking for a bibliography or list of sources; I need a precise disambiguation I can bring back to the discussion.
