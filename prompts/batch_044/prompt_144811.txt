I am trying to work out what I actually think about who belongs to the demos, going by my intuitions on particular cases. Here are my verdicts.

A permanent resident who has lived here for thirty years, pays taxes, and raises a family, but holds no citizenship and never applies for it, ought to have full voting rights in both local and national elections; legal citizenship should not be a barrier.

A citizen who emigrated permanently four decades ago, retains no property or family ties here, and participates only by casting an absentee ballot in national elections, ought not to be part of the demos for domestic policy; her formal status should not override her complete absence.

A worker who lives just across the border, holds no citizenship, but whose livelihood depends almost entirely on trade with our economy and who will be severely harmed by a proposed tariff, ought to have a formal consultative voice in that specific decision, though not full membership of the demos.

A tourist present for only a weekend, who will be inconvenienced by a sudden policy change that affects her travel plans, ought to have no say whatsoever; being temporarily affected is not enough.

People who will live two centuries hence and suffer the climate consequences of our current carbon policy do not yet exist, and different policies will create different people, yet I still think they ought to be represented, perhaps through trustees, in the relevant decisions; this feels like membership of a derivative kind.

The case I keep reversing on involves two people. One was born here to citizen parents but was adopted abroad as an infant, raised with no knowledge of her origin, and now holds citizenship by birthright alone; she has never lived here and feels no connection. The other is a stateless refugee who has lived for a decade in a camp that lies physically inside our borders but is administered by an international body, contributing to the local informal economy, with no path to citizenship. I cannot settle whether the birthright citizen belongs more fully than the present-but-unrecognised resident, or whether neither should be in the demos, or whether both should be, but for opposite reasons.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? What does it say about the two people in my uncertain case? And which of my other verdicts must I abandon if I want to keep the principle simple? Please also tell me what the principle predicts for a situation I have not mentioned: a person who spends exactly half of each year here, working remotely for foreign employers, using local services, but maintaining no social ties and holding citizenship elsewhere.

Please reply in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write in a letter or an email.
