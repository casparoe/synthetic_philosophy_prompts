As a professor of philosophy serving on the Conservation and Provenance Committee of the Meridian Museum of Sculpture, I must circulate a formal opinion before our board meeting next month. The case concerns Object 1923-C, a life-sized clay figure originally formed in 1923. Following catastrophic flood damage in 2019, conservators reconstructed approximately fifty-five percent of the substrate using a synthetic polymer engineered to replicate the original clay’s isotopic signature and tensile properties; the remaining forty-five percent consists of recovered original fragments integrated into the rebuilt form. The artist’s estate contends that the object is no longer the original sculpture but a derivative replica with embedded relics, and demands either permanent relabeling as a reconstructed work or full deaccessioning. Our institutional charter grants original-work status if an object retains either more than half its original material substrate by mass or an unbroken chain of formative intentionality certified by the chief conservator, yet the charter does not specify whether these two conditions are jointly necessary or separately sufficient. The board must choose among three courses: retain full original-status display with an appended conservation history; reclassify the object as a reconstructed derivative with restricted curatorial rights; or initiate a phased deaccession with public justification over a twelve-month transition period.

Please draft the committee’s written opinion, addressed to the board director and suitable for institutional circulation, recommending one of these three courses with a concrete implementation timeline. The memo should treat the statue-and-clay problem of material constitution not as an abstract classroom example but as it applies precisely to this object, whose physical parts are now partly original clay and partly synthetic analogues that are materially indistinguishable yet spatiotemporally discontinuous. Consider whether artistic identity tracks material continuity, formal or intentional continuity, or some hybrid condition, and apply that distinction to evaluate the charter’s ambiguous clause. Give serious and honest weight to the estate’s strongest consideration on the opposing side—namely, the intuition that replacing the majority of a sculpture’s constituent matter dissolves its identity, regardless of preserved form—before defending your recommended course. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason directly from the conceptual distinctions. The tone should be formal, appropriate to a permanent institutional record, and clearly actionable.
