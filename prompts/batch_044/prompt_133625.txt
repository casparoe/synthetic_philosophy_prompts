I'm preparing materials for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of international law, and I'm trying to work through a tension that doesn't seem to get its own name in the standard literature on Lemkin and the 1948 Convention. Most discussions I encounter focus either on the exclusion of cultural genocide from the Convention's text or on the strategic politics of labeling contemporary atrocities. But I'm interested in a different, somewhat narrower problem—call it the nominative shadow effect. By this I mean the specific epistemic and distributive harm that occurs when an atrocity is systematically excluded from the genocide category not merely because it lacks prosecutable intent, but because its mechanisms—bureaucratic dissolution, forced assimilation, child removal, economic dispossession—do not match the Convention's physical or biological template, even when the intent to destroy the group as such is well documented. The result is not just non-prosecution; it is a downward reclassification, to crimes against humanity or domestic rights violations, that reshapes archival priority, reparations eligibility, and collective memory in ways that seem to reproduce the very group-based erasure the category was designed to prevent. I want to explore whether this shadow is an acceptable price for maintaining the legal specificity of genocide, or whether it reveals a structural failure that requires either expanding the legal core or creating a formally recognized parallel status with equivalent commemorative weight.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—who disagree precisely on this point, without either being a strawman. Vasquez should defend a strict-constructionist position: she should argue that the shadow effect is regrettable but unavoidable, that the Convention's narrowness is what gives genocide its distinctive moral gravity and prosecutorial feasibility, and that parallel moral categories, rather than legal expansions, should carry the burden of recognition for borderline cases. Chen should defend a nominative-justice position: he should argue that the shadow is itself a form of structural harm, that distributed bureaucratic intent and non-lethal mechanisms should not automatically place an atrocity outside the category's protective scope, and that without either legal expansion or a recognized genocide-adjacent status with equivalent commemorative weight, the politics of naming will continue to undermine the framework's purpose. They should work through a concrete hypothetical scenario together: a documented, multi-decade campaign of forced assimilation, child removal, and resource deprivation targeting a distinct ethnolinguistic minority, executed primarily through administrative and economic mechanisms with state intent dispersed across agencies rather than centralized in a single order, and involving minimal direct killing. Each should respond to the other's actual objections—about evidentiary burden, jurisdictional fragmentation, prosecutorial dilution, the risk of semantic inflation, and whether commemorative equivalence can be institutionally secured without legal equivalence. The exchange should feel like a seminar discussion or an extended coffee conversation, not a courtroom argument. Please do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific texts or real-world cases that would require external verification; ground everything in conceptual reasoning and the hypothetical case. If you reference the Convention's provisions, paraphrase them without quotation marks. I am especially interested in seeing whether the dialogue can identify a precise point of disagreement—perhaps over whether the harm of misnaming is primarily epistemic, distributive, or moral—that explains why they cannot agree even when they share the same factual premises about the hypothetical campaign.
