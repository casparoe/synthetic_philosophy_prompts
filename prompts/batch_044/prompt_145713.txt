I have recently been asked to convene a small graduate seminar on rational decision theory, and I find myself unable to map precisely where my own intuitions about Hofstadter’s Platonia dilemma and the Scientific American Luring Lottery sit relative to the standard taxonomy. To prepare the first session, I would like you to design a sorting instrument—preferably a decision tree or flowchart, though a labelled taxonomy will do—that takes a participant from an initial concrete commitment about symmetric, non-communicating agents to a named philosophical position on superrationality and self-referential coordination.

The instrument should begin with a single answerable claim (for example: “In the Platonia scenario, I hold that a rational agent ought to send the telegram with some positive probability”). From there, each branch node must be one substantive diagnostic question on which the positions genuinely diverge—phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing which interpretation it leads to. The leaves should include at least:

1. A standard non-cooperative, Nash-style position that treats each agent’s choice as causally independent of the others;
2. Hofstadter’s explicit-randomisation superrationalist strategy (rolling against the expected number of participants);
3. A view that replaces external randomisation with logical or algorithmic correlation—treating structurally identical reasoning processes as selecting a single joint output rather than as separate causal interventions in one world;
4. An evidential-reasoning approach that treats one’s own entry decision in the Luring Lottery as statistical evidence about the total number of entries submitted;
5. A sceptical stance that denies the applicability of rational-choice framing altogether when agents are structurally identical and possess no private distinguishing information.

Please locate these within the logical space of answer-combinations rather than listing them by name first, and then assign the actual positions to the occupied nodes. Note any unoccupied combinations—whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored—and briefly diagnose why they are empty. Also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, particularly where behavioural predictions coincide across both the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery.

Every question must be concrete: present a specific case or claim (e.g., “If you expect exactly 5,000 other entries in the Luring Lottery, how many postcards should you submit?”) rather than an abstract label. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the instrument should be fully answerable from the concepts alone. Avoid framing that depends on obscure secondary literature. Write in British English, and maintain a formal, precise tone throughout.
