Consider the following argument, which applies a Gricean analysis of speaker meaning to institutional discourse—specifically to contract clauses containing ambiguous terms. (1) Grice distinguishes what is said, carrying truth-conditional content, from what is implicated, which depends on the speaker's intentions and the audience's rational recovery of them via the Cooperative Principle. (2) For an implicature to be calculable in Grice's sense, the audience must be able to derive the speaker's intended meaning by recognizing not only the primary intention behind the utterance but also the speaker's second-order intention that the audience recover it through precisely that reasoning. (3) In contractual interpretation, if party A uses an ambiguous term T in a clause, and intends interpretation I rather than J, then for this to constitute speaker meaning rather than mere subjective preference, party B must actually perform the meta-cognitive recognition described in (2)—namely, that A relies on B's grasp of the Cooperative Principle to derive I. (4) Legal interpretation, however, operates through objective, public standards: a clause means what a reasonable counterparty in B's position would understand, irrespective of whether B actually tracks A's private second-order intentions. (5) Consequently, applying the Gricean speaker-meaning framework to contracts entails that the objective, enforceable content of a clause depends on B's actual recovery of A's meta-intentions about B's reasoning. (6) If B fails to perform that specific recognition—perhaps because B interprets T through industry convention rather than through reasoning about A's intentions—then on the Gricean account A has failed to communicate any determinate speaker meaning, and the clause lacks binding content. (7) Because this would render virtually all contracts with ambiguous language void for indeterminacy, and because such contracts are routinely enforced, extending the saying/implicating distinction to institutional language produces an absurd result. (8) Therefore, the Gricean account of speaker meaning must be restricted to casual cooperative conversation, and in institutional domains speaker meaning collapses into whatever a reasonable interpreter assigns, with calculability playing no role.

The author concludes that this demonstrates the inapplicability of Gricean pragmatics beyond everyday talk. Diagnose the failure: identify the specific step where the reasoning breaks down—whether through an equivocation between conversational and institutional norms, an unwarranted scope extension of the calculability condition, a modal confusion between possibility and necessity, or another error—and explain what rejecting that step costs. If forced to preserve one claim, I would keep (1) intact, since the distinction between saying and implicating seems essential to explaining how we routinely communicate more than we literally state; but I am prepared to abandon even that if the diagnosis requires.
