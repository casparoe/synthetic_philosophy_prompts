Here is an argument that was put to me about logical omniscience, and I find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to destroy the very idea of specialised knowledge.

(1) In standard epistemic logic, knowledge is closed under logical consequence: if S knows φ and φ entails ψ, then S knows ψ.
(2) Consider a physicist who genuinely knows the axioms of quantum field theory and who also knows the rules of first-order deduction.
(3) By (1), she therefore knows every logical consequence of those axioms, including number-theoretic statements that happen to be technically derivable but carry no physical interpretation whatsoever.
(4) Now adopt the knowledge-first view: knowing φ is fundamentally being rationally entitled to treat φ as a premise in one's reasoning.
(5) Rational entitlement, on this view, is transparent across valid inference: if one is entitled to φ and φ validly yields ψ, then one is entitled to ψ.
(6) Hence the physicist is rationally entitled to treat those remote number-theoretic statements as premises, in exactly the same normative standing as her entitlement to treat the Schrödinger equation as a premise.
(7) But this erases any principled distinction between her physical expertise and logical omniscience; her knowledge is uniformly distributed over an infinite, conceptually heterogeneous domain.
(8) That result is absurd: we ordinarily want to say her knowledge is genuinely confined to physics.
(9) So at least one of (1), (4), or (8) must be rejected.
(10) Rejecting (8) and embracing omniscience removes all disciplinary boundaries.
(11) Rejecting (1) requires a principled restriction on closure, yet any restriction that appeals to conceptual relevance or cognitive accessibility contradicts the transparency claimed in (5).
(12) Thus we seem forced to reject (4), the knowledge-first view — but that means admitting that knowledge involves substantive cognitive or architectural constraints, which undermines the claim that knowledge is purely normative and irreducible.

I should like to preserve both (4) and (8), yet I cannot see how to do so without either denying closure altogether or allowing an arbitrary line between "near" and "far" logical consequences. Which step is the actual weak point, and what does abandoning it cost? If one must pay in either direction, which payment is least damaging?
