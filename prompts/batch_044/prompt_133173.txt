Read Franz Kafka’s The Trial through the lens of comparative folk morality, specifically the contrast between a German ethos of institutional rule-following, procedural order, and role-bound duty—what moral psychologists sometimes characterize as an “Ordnung” orientation—and an American ethos of individual conscience, personal authenticity, and autonomous moral judgment. Joseph K. is arrested by an opaque bureaucracy and spends the novel attempting to understand, navigate, and ultimately assert himself against its rules. Consider the lawyer Huld, who treats the law as a professional game of influence and delay; the painter Titorelli, who grasps the system’s arbitrariness yet offers only aesthetic accommodation; and the prison chaplain, whose parable of the law suggests that access to justice is structurally withheld. Does Kafka present Joseph K.’s insistence on his own innocence and his refusal to perform the unwritten rituals of deference as a heroic assertion of individual conscience, or as a category error—an attempt to apply a morality of personal authenticity to a system that recognizes only procedural existence? Where does the novel illuminate the strengths and human costs of an order-based morality, and where does it expose such morality as morally bankrupt when rules become opaque and outcomes arbitrary? Conversely, does Joseph K.’s individualism fail precisely because it lacks the communal or institutional grounding that either folk morality requires? I want a reading that takes both the novel and the comparative framework seriously: identify specific scenes or character choices where this tension is dramatized, say where the work resists being reduced to an illustration of either side, and conclude with what Kafka’s vision adds to the philosophical debate about whether rule-following or conscience has the stronger claim on moral life—particularly in institutional contexts where the rules are unknowable and no appeal to inner conviction changes one’s fate.
