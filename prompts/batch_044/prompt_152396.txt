I've been thinking about Raphael Lemkin's original conception of genocide, which I understand covered not only mass killing but also the deliberate destruction of a group's culture, language, and social institutions, and about how the international legal framework that followed adopted a much narrower definition focused mainly on physical destruction carried out with specific intent. I've also noticed that in contemporary conflicts, affected communities often insist on the label "genocide" even when the primary harm was not large-scale killing but rather systematic cultural erasure, forced assimilation, or conditions designed to make group life impossible, while perpetrators sometimes structure policies precisely to achieve group destruction through indirect means—resource deprivation, displacement, or coercive assimilation—while maintaining that they never intended physical extermination. Given this gap between the broader moral idea and the stricter legal category, what are the most important implications for the politics of naming atrocities? Specifically, how does this tension affect which groups can successfully claim international moral recognition and legal protection, and does it create strategic incentives for perpetrators to design destructive policies that fall just outside the legal definition? I'm particularly interested in the deeper consequences for justice and acknowledgment, not in a detailed legal history.
