I am running an upper-division seminar on social ontology—sixteen students, one 90-minute session, in a flat classroom with movable desks and a shared whiteboard. Most have read introductory work on race and gender as socially constructed kinds, and a few have encountered basic accounts of institutional facts, but none have studied the metaphysics of money in any depth. Last week, over coffee, a friend in economic sociology described something she called a reverse-constitutive loop: in certain credit-scoring regimes, a predictive category does not merely cause people to behave differently; the institutional rules are arranged so that the category retroactively redefines the very properties that justify applying it in the first place. I want my students to feel the specific force of this inversion—particularly as it applies to quasi-monetary kinds—without being told about it in advance.

Please design a runnable in-class simulation or structured role-play for this exact group and room, using no real money and requiring no lecture up front. The phrase reverse-constitutive loop should not be spoken until the debrief. The activity should begin with a purely descriptive classification—perhaps derived from a trivial physical rhythm, the ordering of colored cards, or the pattern of a hand-clap—that initially seems meaningless. Through coordinated rules introduced in round two, this classification should determine access to physical resources in round three (seating position, speaking turns, whiteboard space, distributed via tokens or cards). By round four, participants should be asked to explain their own choices by reference to the category, even though they initially treated it as arbitrary. Include a precise timeline with roles or rules for each round; the materials needed and room layout; a payoff or incentive structure that uses only these access tokens; instructions for launching the simulation without philosophical framing; and a debrief that maps each round onto a stage of kind-formation (descriptive tagging, institutional reinforcement, explanatory inversion, and normative entrenchment). Tell me what will predictably fail the first time—likely resistance to arbitrary assignment, or students over-identifying with high-status categories—and how to manage that without breaking the simulation. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the loop the classroom setup cannot reproduce, almost certainly the long historical sedimentation that makes monetary kinds feel natural rather than invented, and state exactly what I must supply verbally at the end to close that gap.
