My advisor applies a moral framework shaped by G.E.M. Anscombe's objection to the 1945 atomic bombings and her distinction between direct and indirect killing. Her verdicts so far: she judges Truman's decision murder rather than merely wrong; she approves RAF bombing of German ball-bearing plants that foreseeably kills civilian workers, provided the plant is the target; she condemns the Dresden firebombing as the deliberate destruction of non-combatants; she permits a naval blockade that stops enemy munitions despite incidental food shortages, but forbids a siege designed specifically to starve residents; she rejects terror bombing meant to break morale, yet accepts a targeted strike on an enemy commander that foreseeably kills two nearby bystanders, treating those deaths as tragic but not murderous.

Consider a state-sponsored cyber-operation that corrupts an enemy's electrical grid to disrupt military command, knowing with near-certainty that hospital generators will fail and kill a predictable number of patients. The corruption targets only infrastructure nodes, no person is targeted, the civilian deaths are neither intended nor the means of victory, and the military gain is substantial.

Predict her verdict on this case, identify whether she is implicitly running a strict direct-intention prohibition, a double-effect criterion coupled with proportionality, or a discrimination rule focused solely on explicit targeting, and specify which further case—say, a mandatory vaccination that kills a few to save many, or an assassination using a precisely sized bomb placed where bystanders are unavoidable—would best distinguish these competing interpretations.
