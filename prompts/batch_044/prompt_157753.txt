I have recently retired after thirty-five years in experimental physics and have begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of science and biomedical ethics, where I find myself puzzled by institutional practices that seem to encode philosophical assumptions I never had to articulate in the laboratory. In particular, I have been examining a specific two-tier consent framework that several international neurotechnology research consortia have adopted for studies involving cognitive enhancement and neurofeedback interventions. Under this framework, protocols are classified as Tier A, in which lasting cognitive alteration is judged unlikely, or Tier B, in which it is considered a non-negligible risk. In Tier A, researchers may employ limited deception about the precise study aims provided that full debriefing follows data collection; compensation may reach up to one hundred fifty percent of the local median hourly wage; and validity requires only standard written disclosure plus a signed acknowledgment. In Tier B, deception of any kind is categorically prohibited; compensation is strictly capped at the exact value of lost wages, with no surplus permitted; and the signature is replaced by a two-stage competence audit—first a standardized comprehension examination, then an interview with an independent ethics officer who explicitly reminds the candidate of the risks and of the unconditional right to withdraw, assessing whether the agreement remains stable under that reminder. The framework’s internal guidelines justify this asymmetry by stating that in Tier B the practical stakes are so high that they “raise the normative threshold,” so that disclosure and the absence of overt coercion, while still necessary, are no longer sufficient for valid consent.

What philosophical theory of consent does this institutional architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, paying particular attention to how it treats deception, financial compensation, and competence: does it hold that deception vitiates consent only under conditions of elevated risk, or does it treat deception as uniformly incompatible with autonomy but pragmatically tolerated elsewhere? Does its compensation rule reflect a context-sensitive theory of coercion, or can it be reconciled with a uniform threshold for undue influence? Most importantly, does the framework’s appeal to practical stakes “raising the bar” commit it to something analogous to pragmatic encroachment—that the very conditions for valid consent depend on what is at stake—or can a context-invariant account defend the same procedures as stricter enforcement of unchanged criteria? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and assess whether its parts are philosophically consistent with one another. If one replaced its implicit stake-sensitive theory with a uniform account—say, one on which deception always invalidates consent regardless of risk level, and compensation is coercive only when it eliminates meaningful refusal—what specific procedural changes would be required in both tiers?

Please separate what is relatively settled in philosophical and bioethical literature on consent, deception, and coercion from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested claim—such as the thesis that practical stakes alter the validity conditions of consent itself—as though it were the consensus position.
