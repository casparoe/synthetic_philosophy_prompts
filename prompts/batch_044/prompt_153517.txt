In my AP History class, a classmate compared Marx's theory of technology and automation to a train running on fixed tracks: machinery is the engine, capitalist social relations are the tracks, workers and capitalists are passengers who cannot steer or apply brakes, and the inevitable destination is either systemic crisis or socialist transformation. Our teacher mentioned that Marx describes machinery as dead labor that dominates living labor, and that he distinguishes formal subsumption—where capital takes over existing labor processes—from real subsumption, where technology fundamentally reshapes production itself. I want to examine how far this train analogy actually holds. Please provide an explicit mapping of the engine, tracks, passengers, brakes, and destination onto Marx's concepts, including how formal and real subsumption fit in; then identify the points of breakdown, especially whether the analogy smuggles in technological determinism that ignores class struggle and the social shaping of machinery; assess whether Marx's view that automation is not a neutral tool but is embedded in specific relations of production survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and suggest whether a different analogy—perhaps one that captures both the coercive restructuring of labor under real subsumption and the possibility of redirecting technology—would be more accurate. I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of secondary sources; I need a conceptual audit that a student without library access could follow and evaluate.
