I'm trying to formalize an objection to nested sovereignty that keeps arising when I think through the cases of Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec. The argument seems to prove that any defensible right to secede collapses into either infinite territorial fragmentation or outright denial of self-determination, yet each premise looks hard to reject if one takes collective autonomy seriously. Here is the chain as I have constructed it.

(1) A territorially concentrated group possesses a remedial right to secede only when it suffers systematic exclusion from the larger state's governing institutions, such that its members are treated as a permanent minority with no effective voice.

(2) Any successful secession establishes a new state that contains its own internal minorities—anglophone and Indigenous communities within Quebec, Aranese and unionist municipalities within Catalonia, and distinct-interest regions such as Orkney and Shetland within Scotland—each capable of making structurally identical claims of exclusion against the new center.

(3) If the ground in (1) is genuinely moral rather than merely strategic, it applies with equal force to these subgroups; recognizing it for the first-level secession but denying it for the second treats sovereignty as a function of power, not principle.

(4) Consistent application therefore generates a recursive structure: every boundary that resolves one grievance creates a new governing center that produces further grievances, and there is no principled, non-arbitrary point at which the recursion can halt without arbitrarily privileging one group's autonomy over another's.

(5) A right that, if consistently exercised, dissolves all collective authority into atomized or endlessly fragmented units is not a workable political right; its consistent application destroys the very conditions—stable jurisdiction and collective agency—required for any group to exercise autonomy.

(6) Hence either we must accept that every subgroup with a grievance may secede, rendering statehood impossible, or we must deny that Scotland, Catalonia, or Quebec ever possessed a genuine secession right in the first place.

(7) Since the first disjunct is absurd, we should conclude that there is no principled remedial right to secede.

I want to resist (7) and preserve the idea that large-scale secession can be legitimate, but I feel pulled toward accepting (3) and (4). Where exactly does this argument fail? Is there a subtle slide—perhaps between the existence of a grievance and the entitlement to exit, or between theoretical recursion and practical impossibility—hidden in one of the steps? Please take a definite position: identify the specific premise that should be rejected, explain why it looked plausible, and state clearly what giving it up costs. Must I abandon universalism about self-determination, accept that sovereignty is ultimately conventional, or can there be a non-arbitrary stopping point that does not collapse into contradiction?
