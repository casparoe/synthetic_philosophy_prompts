After a long argument with a friend about whether Lucas and Penrose actually establish that human minds transcend every formal system, I want the full specification for what an adequate defense of that Gödelian anti-mechanist argument must achieve. Give me the desiderata: which phenomena and objections it must handle (the consistency problem, Benacerraf’s numbering challenge, the possibility that humans are merely inconsistent formal systems), the distinctions it must preserve—especially between being consistent and knowing one’s own consistency, and between individual cognition and distributed group reasoning—with the reason each is non-negotiable. Inject one further requirement that rarely comes up: the defense must either explain why the Gödelian limitation applies specifically to mathematical insight rather than to other apparently algorithm-transcending capacities, or justify that asymmetry. Flag any pair of desiderata you think are jointly unsatisfiable. Separate what is settled (the formal structure of Gödel’s theorems) from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of mind and logicians, and do not present any single reading of Lucas or Penrose as the consensus view.
