The philosophy-of-biology debate over whether DNA constitutes a literal code — with arbitrary symbol-to-referent mapping, semantic content, and a decoding mechanism — has been stalled for decades. One side, drawing on biosemiotics, argues that the triplet-to-amino-acid correspondence is genuinely arbitrary with respect to physical chemistry, that evolutionary selection establishes functional meaning, and that the ribosome operates as an interpreter translating syntax into phenotype. The other side, usually aligned with molecular biology and information theory, counters that Shannon information applies without semantics, that chemical affinity between codon and tRNA removes any need for interpretation, and that calling DNA a code is at best a heuristic borrowed from human engineering.

What makes the impasse frustrating is that both sides accept the same biochemical facts. The disagreement persists through every standard formulation: Crick's sequence hypothesis is uncontroversial, the genetic code is technically redundant and degenerate, and no one disputes that DNA lacks an intentional programmer. Yet in recent rationalist discussions of biological design — where genomes are routinely analogised to software repositories — the same arguments resurface without resolution.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but taxonomic: the two camps are answering different questions under one label. One camp asks whether evolutionary processes can generate semantic content in the absence of minds; the other asks whether the genome operates like engineered source code, with a compiler and runtime environment. If that is right, then the absence of a biological compiler — and the fact that translation is direct chemical catalysis rather than symbolic execution — should be the crux. But I also wonder whether a hidden conceptual disagreement is doing the work: whether "code" requires a triadic relation between representamen, object, and interpretant, or whether a dyadic causal correlation suffices.

There is also a more niche possibility that rarely enters the literature. Recent work on epigenetic error-correction — methylation patterns that restore sequence fidelity after damage — might provide a genuine error-correcting layer analogous to checksums in digital codes. If so, one might argue that the genome contains not just a message but a protocol for message integrity, which strengthens the literal-code reading. Conversely, if error-correction is itself chemically deterministic, it adds nothing semantic.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is it verbal, substantive, or a case of two disciplines using "code" for incompatible explanatory projects? Does the compiler/interpreter distinction actually separate the positions, or is it a red herring? And what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a formal model of biological interpretation that specifies what an interpretant must be, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the ambiguity, or an empirical finding about epigenetic regulation that forces one side to revise its framing?
