As a novelist who has spent the last two years drafting a multi-generational saga that moves from the interwar period to the present, I have become uncomfortably aware of how easily I slip into giving my earlier characters knowledge they could not possess—arranging their choices so that they appear to be stepping, however blindly, toward the world my readers and I already inhabit. This has led me to audit an informal course on historiography, where I encountered the critique of Whig history—the tendency to write the past as an inevitable progression toward modern institutions and values, judging earlier figures by standards they could not have known and treating contingent outcomes as if they were narrative destinations. I would like you to restate that historiographical critique entirely in the vocabulary of narrative craft: dramatic irony, anachronistic characterization, teleological plot architecture, the implied reader's retrospective vantage, narrative closure, prolepsis, and the distinction between diegetic time and the order of narration. Treat the historian's argument as if it were a theory of how historical narratives are constructed, and translate it point by point into these terms.

Then provide the residue report: which elements of the Whig tendency map cleanly onto these literary mechanisms, which can only be expressed if we distort either the historiographical claim or the narratological concept, and which assertions about historical progress have no counterpart whatsoever in the grammar of fiction. In particular, I want to know whether the novelist's intuition that "the ending writes the beginning" is structurally identical to the Whig fallacy, or whether there is a substantive difference that the vocabulary of narrative theory reveals but that historians usually overlook. Please do not offer a bibliography, suggest readings, or invoke specific scholars by citation; I am asking for conceptual translation only, and I want to see precisely where the framework of fiction breaks down when forced to carry a theory of historical knowledge.
