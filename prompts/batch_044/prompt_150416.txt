Write a retirement-dinner toast of no more than 450 words (roughly three minutes) for a moral philosopher who spent thirty years on moral uncertainty—how to act when you lack decisive knowledge of which normative framework is correct. The audience is split evenly: half are philosophers who will recognize references to inter-theoretic comparison, expected-value reasoning across moral theories, and the paralysis problem; half are family and colleagues from other fields who need an emotional arc that does not depend on technical vocabulary. The philosophical idea must shape the speech structurally: because her field holds that we rarely have proof that settles one moral theory over all rivals, you cannot praise her for having “defeated the opposition” or “delivered the final answer”; yet because she rejects simple relativism, you also may not retreat to “everyone has their own values.” Instead, build the toast around calibrated commitment—the practice of holding a normative position firmly enough to guide action, defend it in public, and build institutions around it, while preserving structural openness to revision should a more adequate framework emerge. This constraint should govern what can and cannot be said: avoid triumphalism, avoid subjectivist consolation, and articulate a specific intellectual virtue. Keep the tone warm and personal, do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific papers, and write so that both halves of the room feel directly addressed.
