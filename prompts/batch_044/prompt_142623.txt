As a journalist who covers the intersection of science and democratic theory, I have come to defend a precise version of Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry: democracy is not primarily a mechanism for aggregating preferences or protecting pre-political rights, but a form of cooperative, fallible social inquiry in which collective problems are treated as revisable hypotheses. On this view, citizens function less as sovereign choosers than as distributed sensors for unintended consequences, and expertise acquires democratic legitimacy only to the extent that it remains open to public correction rather than being delegated away. The view predicts that democratic health should be measured by the quality of shared problem-solving processes—how well a community tests, abandons, and revises its collective hypotheses—rather than by procedural fairness or outcome accuracy alone. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings about collective cognition, which institutional-design failures that resist interpretation as bad implementation, which philosophical arguments showing that inquiry and democratic equality are conceptually incompatible, and which results in other domains—say, organizational epistemology or the philosophy of science—would undermine the analogy between laboratory and polity. Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which would a committed Deweyan experimentalist concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be absorbed by refining the view? Does this position risk anything distinctive that preference-based or rights-based rivals avoid, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind?
