The exam question was: "Some philosophers defend guilt as the superior moral emotion because it operates without social surveillance, while others argue that shame is indispensable for repairing collective bonds and that a culture might deliberately cultivate one emotion over the other through institutional design. Assess whether shame's audience-dependence makes it inherently less reliable than guilt for moral correction, and evaluate the claim that a society can choose its dominant moral emotional register. In your answer, consider Bernard Williams's analysis of shame and whether Ruth Benedict's anthropological distinction supports a normative conclusion about cultural choice."

Below is a student answer (around 350 words):

"Guilt functions through internalized norms and therefore remains active even in private, whereas shame requires an audience and involves the degradation of one's social standing. Bernard Williams argued that shame is heteronomous—dependent on an external gaze—while guilt preserves autonomous moral agency, which means guilt-based societies are ethically superior to shame-based ones. Ruth Benedict's distinction between Western guilt cultures and Japanese shame cultures shows that these emotional economies are structurally fixed and not open to deliberate redesign; a society cannot simply choose to become a shame culture. Still, shame is more effective than guilt at repairing social bonds because it signals a willingness to reintegrate. The Meiji-era Japanese education reforms tried to replace shame with guilt-based moral instruction, but they failed because emotional registers are set in childhood socialization. Consequently, although institutions might shape honor codes, a culture cannot genuinely choose shame as dominant without sacrificing the autonomy Williams associates with guilt and without exposing itself to manipulation by those who control the audience."
