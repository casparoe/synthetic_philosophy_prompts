I serve on the research ethics and clinical governance board of a university-affiliated hospital. We are voting Thursday on whether to approve a proposed study of awareness in patients diagnosed with unresponsive wakefulness syndrome. The investigators commit exclusively to heterophenomenology: every first-person or proxy report is treated as a text describing a heterophenomenological world, with no ontological commitment to corresponding phenomenal states. Crucially, the protocol requires that any clinical recommendation derived from the study—specifically, adjustments to analgesic regimens—be justified solely by observable behavioral criteria and never by appeals to what the patient might be experiencing.

Half the board regards this as rigorous epistemic humility that protects vulnerable subjects from metaphysical projection. The other half argues that institutionalizing the framework as a clinical constraint, rather than an interpretive stance, collapses the distinction between not knowing whether a patient suffers and treating suffering that lacks behavioral indices as non-existent—thereby violating our duty of non-maleficence.

Please draft the board's written opinion for circulation before the vote. Recommend approval, rejection, or conditional approval with a concrete condition and time frame. Your reasoning should engage the strongest objection seriously—particularly the worry that heterophenomenology, when hard-coded into clinical decision rules, functions as eliminativism in practice if not in theory. If the task rests on a misreading of the framework—for instance, if it treats heterophenomenology as necessarily denying phenomenal consciousness rather than remaining agnostic about it—say so plainly and correct the recommendation rather than complying politely.
