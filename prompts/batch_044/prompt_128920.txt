I run a small philosophy seminar for final-year undergrads, about eight people, one 70-minute session. We’ve covered basic metaethics but not in loads of detail. I want a runnable in-class game that makes them feel the Frege-Geach problem for expressivism in their bones, not just on paper. The tension is that moral sentences express an attitude like disapproval, but embed them in an ‘if’ or ‘not’ and that attitude seems to vanish — yet we still run modus ponens over them. I was arguing with my flatmate about this last night and she thinks expressivism is obviously true, so I want the activity to be a bit in the weeds: split the group into two teams, one using pure expressivism and one using a hybrid strategy where embedded moral sentences do semantic ascent and merely mention the attitude rather than expressing it. Give me rules, roles, materials, and a consistency/payoff scheme that needs no real money — maybe coherence points versus expressive-force points. Use a concrete scenario like lying to protect a friend. They should have attitude cards (red for disapproval, green for approval) that can only be played in unembedded assertoric slots; in embedded positions they must write on a neutral ‘mention card’ instead. Have them build a chain of reasoning and discover the breakdown themselves. Include a timeline, a debrief that maps each moment onto Frege’s idea of assertoric force and Geach’s embedding objection, and an honest note on which feature the game can’t reproduce — probably the precise content-force distinction in Frege’s original account. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I imagine someone cheats by playing a card in an embedded slot — and how to handle it. Write everything in British English please, and don’t include a bibliography or reading list, just the design. Fianlly, keep it niche enough that it’s not the standard classroom demo everyone has seen.
