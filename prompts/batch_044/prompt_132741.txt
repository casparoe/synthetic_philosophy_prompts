I'm planning a single ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have just completed units on Locke's memory criterion and Parfit's psychological-continuity account, but who have no prior exposure to Buddhist thought. The goal is not to present anatta (no-self) in Theravada Abhidharma as merely a non-Western parallel to Humean bundle theory, but as a concept whose primary function is soteriological: the dissolution of upadana (clinging) rather than a straightforward metaphysical elimination of substance. Plan the teaching sequence rather than delivering the explanation itself: first, map the conceptual dependencies—what must be in place about conventional versus ultimate truth (sammuti-sacca and paramattha-sacca) before anatta can be introduced without being misread as empirical eliminativism about agency; second, identify the single carrying example or thought experiment that should anchor the session (it must not be the chariot analogy—explain precisely what that analogy fails to convey for students primed by Parfit, and propose an alternative that captures the skandha analysis and the absence of a controlling owner); third, list the misconceptions this specific audience will predictably generate given their background (for instance, that anatta denies moral responsibility, that it requires a mystical experience inaccessible to analysis, or that it collapses into Humean empiricism), and specify exactly where in the dependency sequence each misconception should be intercepted; fourth, provide one concrete diagnostic question or exercise I can use in the final ten minutes to determine whether they have grasped the soteriological dimension—release from clinging—rather than only the negative metaphysical claim; and finally, state explicitly which elements of Abhidharmic taxonomy or later Mahayana developments I should refuse to introduce at this level and say so outright.
