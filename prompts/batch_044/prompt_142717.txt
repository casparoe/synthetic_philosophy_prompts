I serve as the student representative on the District Secondary Education Ethics Board. Next Thursday we must vote on whether to approve a pilot program allowing eleventh-grade students to volunteer for an eight-week “cognitive restructuring and metacognitive immersion” module that combines guided neurofeedback with sustained phenomenological journaling. Its designers claim it produces lasting shifts in how students weigh long-term versus immediate rewards—changes some members compare to transformative experiences in philosophy, where a subject cannot rationally evaluate the outcome from her present epistemic position. Opponents argue this is merely an enhanced study-skills course and that invoking “transformative experience” is a category mistake used to bypass standard informed-consent protections for minors.

Please draft the formal opinion memo I will circulate before the meeting. I recommend conditional approval, but the memo must treat the opposing view—that the transformative framing is inapplicable here—seriously rather than dismissing it.

Structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. Executive Summary (about 100 words): state the concrete recommendation and identify the single non-negotiable condition.
2. The Case and Its Philosophical Stakes (about 180 words): explain whether and how the transformative-experience framework genuinely applies, using the distinction between epistemic and personal transformation, and avoid treating the framework as self-evident.
3. Recommendation with Conditions (about 200 words): specify the approval conditions—opt-in structure, parental disclosure format, and a review milestone—so they could be written directly into policy.
4. The Strongest Counter-Argument (about 150 words): present the best case that this is only skills training and that special consent protocols are unnecessary; then reply to it without simply reasserting the transformative label.
5. Implementation Timeline (about 100 words): propose concrete deadlines for disclosure drafting, enrollment opening, and the mid-pilot review.

Keep the reasoning precise enough for policy drafting but accessible to board members without philosophical training. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
