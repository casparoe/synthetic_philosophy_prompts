I'm trying to work out whether the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness actually illuminate real disputes or just solve toy models that collapse on contact with institutional reality. The episode I keep returning to is the 2016 ProPublica investigation into the COMPAS risk-assessment tool used in Broward County, Florida — specifically the finding that black defendants were falsely labeled high-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants, while white defendants were falsely labeled low-risk at roughly twice the rate of black defendants — and Northpointe's rebuttal that the scores are calibrated by race, meaning that among defendants assigned the same risk score, actual two-year re-arrest rates are similar across racial groups. I don't need the ecological backstory; I want a disciplined analysis of this controversy through the lens of the impossibility theorems, particularly the result that calibration across groups, equality of false-positive rates, and equality of false-negative rates cannot simultaneously hold unless base rates are equal or the predictor is perfect.

But I want to push past the usual gloss that "ProPublica demanded error-rate balance and Northpointe demanded calibration." Here is the framing I think is usually missing, and I want you to build the analysis around it. The formal results take a well-defined ground-truth label as exogenous — here, "recidivism within two years" — but in Broward County that label is constructed via re-arrest, which is itself a function of policing intensity and charging practices that vary systematically by race. So the "base rate" is not a raw statistical fact about human behavior; it is an institutional output. I want you to map the COMPAS dispute onto the impossibility framework with this contamination in view.

Specifically:
- Identify the precise fairness criteria each side was implicitly demanding, and show exactly how the impossibility result applies — or fails to apply — once the ground-truth label is treated as endogenous rather than given.
- Determine whether the debate stalled because the sides were demanding formally incompatible properties, or because they were answering different questions entirely: one about predictive accuracy conditional on the label-as-constructed, the other about the normative legitimacy of using that label as the target at all.
- Assess what the theory explains well. Does it account for why both sides could cite valid statistics and still talk past each other? Where is the framework silent — for instance, on which criterion ought to be prioritized when the underlying outcome is itself a matter of justice?
- Finally, tell me what the episode reveals about the formal results themselves. Does the institutional construction of the base rate mean the impossibility theorems miss the real problem? Or does it strengthen them, by showing that even the premises needed for compatibility are politically loaded?

Please do not survey the literature or list papers. I want the mapping disciplined: who plays the role of the predictor, what counts as the protected attribute, what counts as the outcome, and exactly where the analogy between the formal model and the Broward County pipeline breaks down. Be honest about where the framework is being forced to speak and where it genuinely illuminates the case.
