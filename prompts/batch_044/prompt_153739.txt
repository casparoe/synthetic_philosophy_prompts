I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level epistemology seminar (MA level, 90 minutes). I want one essay question on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia that rewards rigorous conceptual analysis rather than summary of the familiar literature. To keep it answerable without external sources but still genuinely demanding, please build the question around a distinction you define entirely within the prompt: distinguish process-level defeat (higher-order evidence that one’s reasoning mechanism in a domain is unreliable) from outcome-level defeat (higher-order evidence that one’s particular belief is false, without any claim about the mechanism that produced it).

Present this scenario: Dr. Aris, a historian, has carefully evaluated primary archives and formed the belief that Event E occurred in 1847. She then learns, from a declassified institutional review, that the archive-curation protocol she relied upon contained an undetected selection bias that systematically omitted counter-evidence for events of type E, though she has no way to know whether her specific case was affected. Separately, she learns that a rival historian, using a different method, has reached the opposite conclusion.

The question should ask students: (a) whether Aris’s continued belief in E is rationally permissible, epistemically irrational, or genuinely akratic; (b) whether process-level and outcome-level defeat differ in normative force and, if so, how; and (c) whether Aris can avoid akrasia by adopting a qualified doxastic attitude (for instance, a credence rather than full belief) without suspending judgement altogether.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, suited to a 90-minute essay (suggested length: 1000–1200 words).
2. A model answer of approximately 600 words that applies the distinction rigorously to the scenario rather than reciting standard positions.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 marks, listing five specific analytical moves an excellent answer must contain, the marks allocated to each, and a one-line note on the typical misconception that should cost marks on each point. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Use British spelling conventions.
