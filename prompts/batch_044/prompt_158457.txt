I've been trying to work out my own position on whether immoral art can be good art — specifically on separating the work from the artist — and I want a consistency audit before I finalize my philosophy essay. Here are my commitments:

(1) Aesthetic autonomism: the artistic value of a work is fixed by its internal formal and representational properties, not by the biographical moral status of its creator, unless the creator's moral failings are thematically embodied in the work itself.

(2) A distinction between representational contamination and causal contamination. Representational contamination occurs when the work invites the audience to adopt or endorse an immoral viewpoint; causal contamination occurs when harm was involved in the work's production but is invisible in the finished product. I hold that only representational contamination diminishes aesthetic value, while causal contamination gives a moral reason for boycott but not an aesthetic reason for devaluation.

(3) Moral realism: there are mind-independent moral facts, and some artists have committed genuinely wrong acts.

(4) A pro tanto moral duty not to promote, circulate, or financially support artists whose continued success depends on platforms that also amplify harmful behavior, even when the particular works I admire are causally uncontaminated.

(5) The claim that interpretation is never fully author-independent: knowing that a painter was a violent misogynist can change which patterns and intentions I legitimately perceive in the canvas, which means aesthetic judgment is partly indexed to my epistemic situation regarding authorship.

(6) The claim that if a work requires the audience to perform imaginative identification with an immoral agent in order to achieve its intended emotional effect, then the work is aesthetically defective — its failure is artistic, not merely moral.

My worry is that (1) and (5) pull in opposite directions: if interpretation is always mediated by knowledge of the author, then pure aesthetic autonomy seems impossible. Meanwhile, (2) relies on a clean boundary between what is "inside" the work and what lies outside it, but (5) suggests that boundary is epistemically porous. There also seems to be tension between (4) and (6): if I boycott an artist on moral grounds, am I implicitly treating their work as aesthetically tainted? Or can I consistently admire a work's internal properties while refusing to circulate it?

Please audit this specific package. Which commitments are genuinely incompatible, which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and how should they be ranked by severity? For each real tension, specify the least costly revision — whether I should weaken (1), refine (2), drop (5), or adjust (6) — and what I would lose by doing so. I want to know whether the problem lies in my ontology of the work, my epistemology of interpretation, or my moral psychology of the audience. I am not asking for a general lecture on the art-ethics debate; I need a targeted consistency check.
