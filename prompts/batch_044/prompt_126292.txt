As a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of history and science and technology studies, I am drafting an essay on whether Nietzsche's tripartite schema in his 1874 second Untimely Meditation, On the Advantage and Disadvantage of History for Life, can accommodate what I am tentatively calling "recursive monumentalism." By this I mean a historiographical condition—exemplified by continuously updated predictive archives in climate and epidemiological modeling—in which the past is not treated as a stable object to be preserved, emulated, or dissolved, but rather as a mutable parameter that is retroactively reweighted to validate projected futures. In such systems, a monumental function (the celebration of a progressive trajectory) and a critical function (the discarding of anomalous historical data points) appear to operate simultaneously through the same algorithmic mechanism. I am particularly interested in whether this collapses Nietzsche's insistence that the three modes—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—must remain distinct in order to serve life, or whether it reveals a fourth orientation that he did not anticipate.

In my notes so far, I argue that this is not reducible to antiquarian preservation, because the archive is not conserving a collective identity but optimizing for future states; nor is it purely critical, because the elimination of outliers serves to reinforce rather than liberate present life from the burden of the past. At the same time, I worry that applying the 1874 framework to iterative predictive systems risks anachronism, since Nietzsche was responding to documentary historicism rather than to feedback-loop historiography.

Could you comment on the coherence of this framing? Specifically, I would appreciate observations on whether "recursive monumentalism" genuinely introduces a conceptual tension within Nietzsche's schema or merely conflates mechanisms that he already distinguishes, and on whether the application to predictive modeling archives illuminates his warning about the paralysis of life through an excess of history, or whether it stretches the original argument beyond recognition. I am not seeking a literature review or source suggestions—only conceptual and interpretive feedback on the argument as sketched.
