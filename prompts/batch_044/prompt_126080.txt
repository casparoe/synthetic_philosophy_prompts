I run a philosophy of rationality workshop for about 9 grad students, one 90-min session. I want them to feel escalation of commitment in a way that isn't just the usual dollar-auction lecture they've all heard before. Design an in-class game — paper tokens only, no real cash — that uses a modified auction where you bid with "credibility points" you earn by defending a position in earlier rounds. So the sunk cost is epistemic/social, not financial. The rules need to make every individual bid feel rational but drive the group into an absured collective commitment they all see is wrong but can't back out of without losing face.

Specify: group roles or rules, round structure, payoff scheme with paper tokens, materials needed, and the exact win/loss conditions. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto Shubik's dollar auction mechanics and onto doxastic escalation — backing out costs credibility. Be frank about which real-world feature this can't reproduce (actual financial ruin? long-term reputation? identity fusion?). And tell me what will predictably go wrong first time — I bet people collude or quit too early — plus how to fix it.

One more thing: inject a niche framing that isn't in every undergrad text. Maybe tie the escalation dynamics to sequential consistency in peer disagreement, or to the idea that rational agents can be locked into shared error without anyone being individually irrational. Make sure the idea shows up in the rules/incentives, not just in the debrief I give afterwards.
