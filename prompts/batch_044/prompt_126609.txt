I'm moderating a podcast series on contested concepts in moral and political philosophy, and next week's guest is a philosopher—let's call her Dr. Elena Voss—who has developed an unconventional synthesis she terms mnemonic triage. Her view starts from Avishai Margalit's argument that societies have an ethical duty to remember certain collective wrongs, particularly those involving victims whose voices risk being erased. But she pairs this with Philip Rieff's claim that modern culture has replaced authoritative moral interdicts with therapeutic management, leaving societies without the cultural capacity to bear sustained collective guilt. Voss argues that in a therapeutic culture, universal demands to remember produce what she calls mnemonic inflation: the proliferation of commemorative obligations that dilute moral authority rather than preserve it. The ethical task is therefore not remembrance simpliciter, but a curatorial practice of strategic forgetting—ranking collective memories according to whether they can still sustain the kind of authoritative moral boundary that Rieff believed was disappearing. She applies this framework to debates about national monuments, school curricula, and transitional justice, insisting that forgetting can be ethically mandatory when remembrance would overwhelm a culture's diminished capacity for moral authority.

I want eight to ten interview questions that do not let her simply restate the synthesis. I need questions that press the internal tension: for instance, how she distinguishes legitimate curatorial forgetting from the kind of erasure Margalit condemns; whether her criterion for triage collapses into a majoritarian or state-controlled decision about which victims deserve remembrance; and how she responds to the objection that invoking Rieff's lost moral authority is nostalgic for a hierarchical culture that itself caused the wrongs now being remembered. For each question, include a brief note on the standard reply she is likely to give, and then draft the sharp follow-up that makes that reply harder to maintain. At least two questions should address the practical institutional mechanisms—who conducts the triage, and what happens when victim communities reject the ranking—without letting her retreat to the claim that these are mere implementation details. The tone should be rigorous but accessible to an audience familiar with philosophy of history and memory studies, but not with Voss's particular framing.
