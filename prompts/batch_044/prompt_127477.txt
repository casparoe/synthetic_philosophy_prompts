Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between an eliminative-reductionist interpreter of Buddhist anattā and a process-continuist interpreter of the same doctrine, both of whom take the Pali canonical framework seriously but disagree on its metaphysical implications for personal identity.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims—no more than eight—covering: (i) what anattā denies (a permanent, autonomous, unitary essence that could survive the dissolution of the aggregates); (ii) the standard analysis of the five skandhas and dependent origination; (iii) the conventional-versus-ultimate distinction as applied to persons; (iv) the persistence of first-person phenomenology despite the absence of a self; and (v) the fact that moral evaluation and karmic language remain operative in conventional discourse even if they lack an ultimate bearer.

This should be followed by a disagreements table. Each row must state the precise disagreement in wording that the side being represented would accept. Include rows on: (a) whether diachronic personal identity is a purely conventional fiction with zero normative weight, or whether a “thin” processual continuity—grounded in causal-psychological linkage and reflexive awareness (svasamvedanā)—suffices for moral responsibility and karmic transmission; (b) whether the “ownership-recurrence problem” (the difficulty that if no mental state has an owner, then the state that recognizes this absence also lacks an owner, threatening an infinite regress of unowned noticings) is a fatal paradox for eliminativism, or whether it dissolves once phenomenal presence is distinguished from metaphysical ownership; and (c) whether the reflexive structure of awareness can fully account for the “givenness” of experience without a minimal subject, or whether some non-substantive “center of orientation” is ineliminable.

Next, identify at least two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. The first crux should concern whether first-person pronouns in reports of experience function as indexicals that pick out a stream rather than a substance, or as pragmatic fictions carrying no referential content whatsoever. The second crux should concern whether the state of complete cessation of aggregates (nirodha-samāpatti) followed by resumption permits a non-conventional criterion of stream-identity, and whether karmic continuity can be reconstructed without any bearer of karma.

For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction written in advance. For the indexical crux, use the “Split-Mind Transplant Scenario”: a mental stream is divided into two equally continuous streams, each with full psychological continuity to the original. Both sides should state whether either successor is genuinely “the same person” in any non-conventional sense, and whether the original first-person pronoun could correctly be used by either, both, or neither. For the cessation crux, use the “Interruption of Aggregates Case” described above: both sides should predict whether the resuming stream stands in a relation of karmic inheritance to the prior stream that is stronger than the relation it bears to any contemporaneous stream, and whether that relation requires a bearer.

Conclude by stating whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—if so, specify which formulations turned out to be notational variants of the same claim, and which disagreements survive as genuinely metaphysical. Keep the tone formal and precise; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for source verification; and do not invoke obscure texts or figures that fall outside standard philosophical and Buddhist-studies training data. If you invoke specific technical terms (e.g., skandha, svasamvedanā, nirodha-samāpatti), treat them as conceptually available without attributing them to specific editions or commentators.
