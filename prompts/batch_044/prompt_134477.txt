Question: In Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments he draws a sharp line between sympathy and pity, and he uses the figure of the imparital spectator to explain how we judge our own actions. Explain (a) how sympathy actually works in Smith's account—what mental moves are involved and why it is not just feeling sorry for someone, and (b) what role the spectator plays specifically in overcoming self-deceit when we try to approve of our own conduct. Be precise about whether the spectator applies rules or shares sentiments.

Student answer:
Smith thinks sympathy starts when we imagine ourselves in another person's situation, almost like we're copying their feelings into our own mind. Its different from pity because pity is just feeling bad for someone from the outside, whereas sympathy means we actually share the sentiment. The impartial spectator is basically an internalized social audience—like imagining what reasonable people would think of you. We need this because we're naturally self-interested and will lie to ourselves about whether we're good people. So we split ourselves into two parts: the agent who acts, and the spectator who watches. The spectator applies general moral rules to check if the agent is being fair. If the spectator approves, that means our action was virtuous, and this approval is what makes us feel good about ourselves. The key point is that sympathy is the engine behind the spectator's judgments too—he feels with us—but the spectator himself is detached and doesn't have his own passions.
