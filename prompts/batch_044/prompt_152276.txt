I retired from a biology lab about three years ago and since then I've been sitting in on philosophy courses, mostly because I miss having hard problems to chew on. There's this study partner Maren—she's actually read the books, I'm just the old scientist trying to map moral psychology like I'd map a taxonomy. Anyway, we've been going through Nietzsche's genealogy and she's made four verdicts that she swears are consistant. One: medieval flagellation is slave morality, reactive life-denial born of weakness. Two: ancient Greek athletic competition for honor is master morality, active and self-affirming. Three: modern outrage mobs on social media are slave morality because they invent 'evil' in the powerful and feed on resentment. Four: a surgeon who works brutal hours out of pure craft-love, ignoring family and comfort but not resenting anyone, still counts as master morality even though it looks ascetic.

Now I've come up with this niche phenomenon I want to test her on—let's call it meritocratic asceticism. It's high-achievers who deliberately drop out of lucrative careers, live in vans or minimal apartments, and frame comfort, luxury, and even ambition itself as signs of moral rot. They don't seem envious of the wealthy; they seem contemptuous of them. But they also define 'good' negatively—as the absence of comfort-seeking—and they clearly derive energy from condemning the indulgent. So if Maren wants to stay consistent with her four earlier calls, should she classify this as master or slave morality? Which of her precedents is the closest analog—the surgeon or the flagellants? I think her rulings could hang together under two different rules: maybe one about whether the drive is reactive (born of weakness/envy) versus active (born of strength/overflow), and another about whether 'good' is defined positively by presence of noble traits or negatively by absence of evil ones. So under each rule, what does she have to say about meritocratic asceticism? What's her best guess if she picks one rule, and what principle is she committing to either way? And finally, what's the one hypothetical further case that would actually split those two rules apart, so we'd know which one she's really running?
