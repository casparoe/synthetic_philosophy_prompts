I've noticed a recurring asymmetry in how people attribute mental states to language models, and I want to know whether philosophy of mind has a precise name for it. In one case, a model produces a coherent, accurate explanation of a causal process, and observers comfortably say it "knows" or "understands" that explanation. In a second case, the very same model—same weights, same architecture—produces a coherent but false explanation of the identical process, and those same observers immediately say it "has no beliefs whatsoever" and is "just stitching tokens together." The mechanism hasn't changed; only the truth-value and practical usefulness of the particular output have changed. What's distinctive is that the attribution isn't withdrawn globally—people still speak of what "the model thinks" in other parts of the conversation—but it is withdrawn specifically for this unacceptable result, as if the system's epistemic status were tied to the sentence it just emitted rather than to any stable feature of its behavior or design. This is clearly near Dennett's intentional stance, but the standard debate asks whether the stance is ever appropriate for AI in general. The phenomenon I mean is the selective, output-contingent toggling of belief-attribution within a single discussion, driven by whether the result is normatively acceptable rather than by any architectural or behavioral change. Does this pattern have an established concept? If not, can you define it precisely—distinguishing it from ordinary context-sensitivity in mental-state attribution, from the general anti-realist argument that AI never has beliefs, and from mere conversational inconsistency—and specify what conditions should govern whether it applies?
