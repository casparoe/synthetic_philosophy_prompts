Consider the institutional practice, now standard in advanced economies, by which a central bank issues electronic reserves and sovereign debt instruments that carry no commodity backing—no gold, silver, or land claim—yet are treated as fully liquid money and priced purely by reference to the issuing state's creditworthiness and legal-tender status, with quantitative easing allowing reserve expansion that need not map onto immediate productive output. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory of value and signification that this practice presupposes: does it commit us to the view that value can inhere purely in a relational promise or token, or can a labor-grounded or materialist account accommodate it? In particular, examine whether the practice is compatible with George Berkeley's argument in The Querist that money is essentially a token conveying purchasing power whose legitimacy depends on its connection to real goods and productive labor rather than to the physical material of the token itself. If Berkeley holds that paper credit is sound only when tied to actual productive capacity, what does the decoupling of reserve growth from contemporaneous output—characteristic of modern quantitative easing—presuppose about the relation between sign and signified? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, state whether it is philosophically underdetermined among rival theories of value, and indicate what feature of the practice, if any, would have to change if we replaced its implicit metaphysics with Berkeley's token-based but labor-anchored view.
