I am preparing a lecture for an advanced seminar on Darwin’s views on morality and religion, and a persistent analogy has arisen in our discussions that I should like to examine with some rigour. A colleague maintains that Darwin’s explanation of moral conscience in The Descent of Man functions precisely like the pheromone-trail coordination observed in ant colonies: just as individual ants deposit chemical signals that regulate collective behaviour without any ant representing the good of the whole, so human moral sentiments emerge from social instincts that coordinate group survival without requiring rational deliberation or a genuinely normative grasp. She deploys this comparison to contend that Darwin ultimately reduces moral agency to a form of blind biological signalling, and that his account of religion—which she treats as an accidental by-product of the same instinctive machinery—suffers from an analogous reduction. I should be grateful if you could audit this analogy in detail: provide an explicit correspondence table (pheromone to moral sentiment, ant to individual agent, colony to social group, evolutionary pressure to selection for sociality), then indicate precisely where the mapping fractures, especially in light of Darwin’s insistence upon reflection, sympathy, and the gradual cultivation of higher mental powers in the formation of conscience. Does her reductive conclusion survive these disanalogies, or does the analogy surreptitiously import a model of insect sociality that Darwin himself distinguishes sharply from human moral development? Furthermore, might an alternative analogy—say, comparing the emergence of moral norms to the acquisition of language—better capture Darwin’s actual position without collapsing morality into chemistry?
