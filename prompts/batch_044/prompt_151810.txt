Draft the joint statement that a committed Hartian legal positivist and a committed Fullerian natural-law theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration focused specifically on algorithmic governance—particularly predictive-policing systems and machine-learning-generated regulatory norms that lack traditional legislative promulgation, general accessibility, and human-comprehensible formulation.

Structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. Separate what is genuinely settled from what remains contested among scholars who work on legal philosophy and technology governance; do not present a contested interpretive claim about Hart or Fuller as if it were the consensus view. The agreed claims should cover: the formal structure of Hart’s rule of recognition and its distinction between primary and secondary rules; Fuller’s eight principles of legality, including publicity, generality, and prospectivity, understood without attributing to them any specific moral content that is itself disputed; the empirical characteristics of current predictive-policing algorithms, including opacity of model weights, case-specific risk scoring, absence of legislative promulgation, and dependence on training data that may encode historical patterns; the conceptual distinction between legal validity and moral merit; and the shared acknowledgment that a system can possess social efficacy—being recognized and applied by officials—while still failing to meet normative conditions of the rule of law.

Second, a disagreements section, presented as a table or in parallel columns, stating the residual disagreements in wording that each side accepts for describing its own view. One disagreement should concern whether algorithmic norms that are technically present but not humanly intelligible to those governed violate Fuller’s principle of publicity, or whether publicity can be satisfied by technical accessibility of code and data regardless of cognitive accessibility. Another disagreement should concern whether Hart’s social-source thesis permits a rule of recognition that incorporates opaque, machine-generated outputs as binding legal norms provided that legal officials consistently treat them as authoritative, or whether such incorporation necessarily smuggles in moral criteria about the form law must take.

Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I take the central cruxes to be: (a) whether the principle of generality requires that legal rules be framed in terms that apply to classes of persons and actions in a way that is independently graspable by those subject to them, or whether case-specific algorithmic outputs can collectively constitute a general normative framework; and (b) whether the internal morality of law is a condition of legal validity itself, as Fuller maintains, or only a condition of efficacy and moral legitimacy that is conceptually separable from validity, as Hart maintains—specifically when the “law” in question is generated by statistical models rather than by intentional human promulgation.

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case, institutional arrangement, or empirical development that both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case. For crux (a), use the case of a jurisdiction that fully publishes the architecture, training data, and decision weights of its predictive-policing model, but retains case-specific risk scores that are not reducible to any human-readable rule—each side should state whether this satisfies generality and publicity. For crux (b), use the case of a judiciary that consistently applies an opaque risk-score threshold in bail and sentencing decisions, treating it as binding without legislative enactment—each side should predict whether this demonstrates the incorporation of algorithmic norms into the rule of recognition, and whether that incorporation undermines or confirms the separability of law and morality.

Finally, conclude by noting which parts of the dispute appear to be verbal—dissolving once terms such as “rule,” “validity,” “publicity,” or “internal morality” are specified with precision in the algorithmic context—and which parts remain substantive disagreements about the conditions under which a normative system counts as law. Throughout, maintain a formal, neutral tone, avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke highly specialized sub-theories that depend on recent technical literature inaccessible without search.
