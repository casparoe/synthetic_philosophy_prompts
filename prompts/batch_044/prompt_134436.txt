I direct the clinical ethics and assessment unit at a neurological rehabilitation center that specializes in patients with profound, long-term sensory isolation—complete locked-in syndrome, congenital absence of all sensory modalities, or extended anechoic deprivation maintained for research or therapeutic reasons. Our institutional mandate requires that we classify each patient, repeatedly and defensibly, into one of three states: (1) immediate, non-inferential self-awareness of the kind described in Avicenna’s flying-man scenario, in which a subject is aware of her own bare existence without any sensory mediation through bodily attributes; (2) reconstructed or narrative self-awareness mediated entirely by artificial feedback loops, caregiver language, or brain-computer interfaces that supply the vocabulary and temporal framing the patient then repeats; and (3) the absence of any core self-awareness, leaving only autonomic and reflexive functioning. I need this not as metaphysical commentary but as an operational decision tool that my evaluation team—neurologists, speech-language pathologists, and a clinical ethicist—can run at admission and at six-month intervals, with clear stopping rules and explicit handoffs to value judgment.

Please turn Avicenna’s distinction between awareness of the self (the bare, unmediated fact of one’s existence) and awareness of one’s attributes (bodily location, mental contents, temporal continuity) into a reusable clinical procedure. Present it as an ordered checklist: the specific tests, interview structures, or interface protocols to apply in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage to proceed; the precise termination points that yield a determination of state (1), (2), or (3); and the nodes where the question is irreducibly one of values—resource allocation, family preference, moral status—rather than clinical fact, and must be signed off by the ethicist rather than the neurologist. The procedure must handle hard cases: patients communicating only through single-channel brain-computer interfaces; patients giving contradictory responses across modalities; and caregivers or family members who have strong preferences about classification because it affects life-support decisions. Apply the procedure to three cases: (A) a locked-in patient who, via eye-tracking, consistently affirms first-person presence but denies all bodily location and temporal markers; (B) a patient in prolonged sensory deprivation who describes herself exclusively in the third person, using only vocabulary supplied by her artificial feedback loop; and (C) a case where the procedure indicates absence of core self-awareness, but the family insists on maintaining full support on the grounds that narrative reconstruction is morally equivalent to state (1). For each case, show the step-by-step run, flag where judgment cannot be eliminated, and note one predictable misfire.

Please structure your response in the following sections: The Procedure (approximately 600 words), presenting the ordered checklist with termination rules and value-handoffs; Worked Applications (approximately 500 words), running cases A, B, and C step by step; Judgment Nodes and Predictable Misfires (approximately 300 words), identifying the non-eliminable judgment calls and where the framework is likely to break; and Limits of the Framework (approximately 200 words), stating honestly what this Avicennian distinction cannot determine—particularly regarding moral status, quality of life, or rights—such that the team must supply those commitments externally. Include one case—specifically case C—where the procedure delivers a result that conflicts with what the family will want, and be explicit about where the procedure ends and human decision must begin.
