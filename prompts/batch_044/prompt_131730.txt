I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I keep noticing a recurring move in system-design discussions that I cannot name. The pattern involves two concrete cases.

In the first, a team proposes replacing a recommendation algorithm. They show that every current user receives an outcome at least as good as before, and they observe that users who would have joined only under the old algorithm do not exist in the new system, so no one is worse off; they therefore label the change a safe Pareto improvement and approve it. Their safety audit checks only the actual user database, not the counterfactual database of users who would have existed, and it treats a catastrophic feedback-loop risk as an unrelated tail issue rather than part of the Pareto comparison.

In the second, a compute-budget redistribution gives every active project equal or greater resources, while projects that would have been funded under the old budget are excluded from evaluation; the change is again called safe and Pareto-optimal, even though the excluded counterfactual set includes long-term initiatives whose absence shifts total welfare.

What I am looking for is not a bare label. I want to know whether philosophy has an established concept for this exact configuration: a Pareto evaluation restricted to an actual population, with a counterfactual population excluded because its membership depends on the choice, paired with a separation of immediate Pareto safety from systemic tail risk. How does it differ from the standard non-identity problem, from ordinary risk externalization, and from a simple framing or boundary error? If nothing established fits precisely, please propose a term with a sharp definition and boundary cases.

Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
