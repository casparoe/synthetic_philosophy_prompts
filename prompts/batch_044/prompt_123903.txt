Since my father was recently diagnosed with frontotemporal degeneration, I have been studying proposals for phased neural prosthesis, in which failing cortical regions are replaced by synthetic analogues over several years, with functional equivalence verified at each stage by behavioral and computational benchmarks. Philosophers often use a similar gradual-replacement scenario to argue that substrate-independent functionalism allows consciousness to survive a complete transition to silicon. Yet I find myself caught in what seems like a conceptual bind. If the process is genuinely continuous—verified step by step with no interruption in psychological life—then the endpoint is not an "upload" in the usual sense, which implies a discontinuous copying of information onto a new substrate; it is merely a repaired organism that eventually contains no original tissue. But if we deny that the endpoint is an upload, then the fact that its substrate is entirely artificial appears metaphysically irrelevant: the person never left, they were only maintained. Conversely, if we insist that the endpoint is indeed an uploaded mind, we seem to have introduced a hidden discontinuity—the moment the last biological component is replaced—precisely where the scenario promised none. Is the error in my reasoning that I am conflating the causal history of a system with its present metaphysical kind? Or does this tension show that "mind uploading" and "gradual neural replacement" are not two descriptions of one outcome, but categorically different processes that philosophers have wrongly treated as interchangeable?
