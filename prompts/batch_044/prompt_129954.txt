In a seminar on political economy, we have been debating whether Marx's analysis of machinery is ultimately pessimistic or emancipatory, and I find myself unable to adjudicate the disagreement. On one reading, drawn from Capital, machinery functions as dead labor embodied in fixed capital that dominates and displaces living labor; the factory system organizes workers as appendages to an automatic mechanism, and technological development under capitalism intensifies exploitation by cheapening labor-power and extending the working day through relative surplus value. On another reading, drawn from the Grundrisse notebooks—particularly the discussion of automation, the general intellect, and what is often called the fragment on machines—Marx seems to treat the same technical development as potentially undermining the labor-time basis of value itself, reducing necessary labor to a minimum and thereby opening the possibility of a society organized around disposable time rather than surplus extraction.

Please compare these two theoretical commitments: the view that machinery necessarily subordinates the worker within the value-form, versus the view that advanced automation renders direct labor redundant and destabilizes value production from within. Can they be reconciled by insisting that Marx distinguishes between the technical content of automation (its capacity to reduce labor time) and its social form (its subordination to capital's valorization requirements)? Or does the tension run deeper, such that the very increase in the organic composition of capital that machinery represents undermines the rate of profit and therefore the stability of the system that deploys it? I am especially interested in whether Marx's theory of value permits a technology that simultaneously maximizes surplus-value extraction and erodes the labor-time foundation upon which surplus value depends, and what this implies for his broader historical narrative.
