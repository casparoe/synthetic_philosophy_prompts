The exam question was: Some philosophers argue that the category of supererogation—acts that are morally good but not morally required—creates a tension: if such acts are genuinely heroic, then omitting them seems to indicate a moral shortcoming, yet by definition they are not obligatory, so omitting them should not be blameworthy. Assess whether this tension undermines the coherence of supererogation, with reference to deontological and virtue-ethical perspectives.

The student answer is as follows:

The problem arises because supererogatory acts, such as risking one’s life to save strangers from danger, appear both highly praiseworthy and non-obligatory. From a deontological angle, Urmson argued that Kantian ethics, which divides duties into perfect and imperfect ones, cannot accommodate acts that genuinely go beyond duty: either beneficence is an imperfect duty, in which case heroic rescue is merely an especially strong fulfilment of it, or it is not required at all. The answer correctly captures this framing, but then claims that Bernard Williams, in Moral Luck, resolved the tension by proposing a three-tier taxonomy—obligatory, permissible, and supererogatory. This is a misattribution: Williams did not construct such a taxonomy; he questioned whether morality ought to be conceived as a system of demands altogether, whereas the three-tier framing is closer to Urmson’s own proposal. Turning to virtue ethics, the student observes that virtue is a continuum rather than a binary of duty fulfilled or violated, so heroic generosity is a matter of being more excellent rather than meeting a fixed threshold. This is largely correct, but the student then infers that, because virtue is continuous, failing to perform a heroic act can never reveal any deficiency in one’s character. That does not follow: one can be less generous than the excellent person without being vicious, yet still fall short of a standard appropriate to one’s capacities. Finally, the student concludes that supererogation is coherent only within virtue ethics, overlooking that some deontologists—such as David Heyd—have defended the category by carefully separating moral worth from moral obligation.
