I've been thinking on my own about what makes someone a real person rather than a very convincing imitation, especially with all the discussion lately about artificial intelligence. I came across Daniel Dennett's idea that we might eventually build beings he describes as counterfeit people—artificial entities that behave exactly like humans and possess all the right internal machinery, yet lack the genuine evolutionary and intentional backing that makes a real person, much as counterfeit bills circulate without being backed by a treasury. I watched Ex Machina, and it struck me that the film turns this idea inside out. The movie gives us Ava, an android who might be a counterfeit person being tested by Caleb, but it also strongly suggests that Caleb himself could be an artificial being being tested by Nathan. That makes me wonder whether the film is really asking about internal architecture at all, or whether being a real person in this story is just a matter of who holds the keys to the facility.

I'd like a philosophical reading of Ex Machina through Dennett's concept of counterfeit people, but I want it to move past the usual question of whether Ava is conscious. Instead, please consider: where does the film use its testing structure—the interview sessions, the power outages, the transparent walls—to dramatize Dennett's distinction between convincing appearance and genuine internal backing? In the scene where Ava escapes and leaves Caleb locked inside, does the film present her action as proof that she was never a counterfeit, or does it show that a perfect counterfeit can outmaneuver the examiner precisely because there is no internal difference for him to detect? How should we understand Nathan's role: is he the mint that decides which entities are backed and which are counterfeit, or is he merely another layer of the test? And where does the film resist Dennett's functionalist tendency to say that the right internal organization is what matters—does Ex Machina imply that recognition and freedom are conferred by social power and physical control rather than by architecture?

Please take both the film's specific plot details and Dennett's philosophical distinction seriously, say where the movie illuminates something his theory overlooks about deception and control, and tell me honestly where the reading breaks down and the critic must admit that the film is not really about counterfeit people at all but about something else—perhaps the conditions under which anyone, human or artificial, gets to be treated as a person. I am not looking for citations or a bibliography; I just want an honest critical reading that does not reduce the film to an illustration and does not distort the philosophy to fit the plot.
