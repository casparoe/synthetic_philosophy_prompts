Hey — I retired from a chemistry lab a few years ago and lately I've been reading philospohy, mostly ethics. I got into Jaspers's idea that there's political guilt that sticks to citizens of a state for crimes the state did, even if the person did nothing. Then I started thinking about decay chains — you know, isotopes with half-lives — and I tried to model collective guilt like a physical quantity that either decays or doesn't. Now I have this argument that proves every current citizen of a long-lived state is permanently guilty for everything in its history, which seems like too much. Here's the chain:

(1) Jaspers allows political guilt: citizens share in guilt for state crimes because they belong to the political body.
(2) A nation-state is a continuing entity, not just the people alive now — like a corporation or a river, it keeps its identity through turnover.
(3) So the political body that was guilty in 1945 is numerically the same body today.
(4) If a body is guilty and I am a constituent part of it, then I share in that guilt.
(5) Citizenship makes me a constituent part of the state, not just an observer.
(6) Jaspers says moral guilt is strictly personal and dies with the agent, but he never gives political guilt an expiration date.
(7) So political guilt behaves like an isotope with an infinit half-life — it doesn't decay as generatons turn over.
(8) That means a child born in Berlin in 2024 inherits the full political guilt for crimes in 1945.
(9) By the same reasoning, every enduring state is permanently guilty for its entire history, and every citizen is trapped in it.
(10) But that turns political guilt into something like original sin, which Jaspers wanted to avoid.

Which step slips? I want to hang onto (1) and (6) — I like the distinction between political and moral guilt. Maybe I'm smuggling in something between (3) and (5) about parts and wholes, or maybe the problem is (7), treating guilt like a physical quantity with a half-life. I can't tell if there's an equivocation hiding in there or if it's actually valid and I just have to bite the bullet. Tell me which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs, especially if I want to keep the Jaspers distinction intact.
