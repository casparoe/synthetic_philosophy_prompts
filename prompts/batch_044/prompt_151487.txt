Write a dialogue between two philosophers of moral psychology—call them A and B—who disagree about whether moral worth requires that an agent’s motivating reason represent the right-making feature of her action, or merely that her motivating reason align with that feature through whatever psychological pathway. To keep the dispute concrete, have them discuss the case of Dr. Lena Voss, a clinical researcher who halts a trial because she experiences an unexplained, persistent dread about the protocol. Voss sincerely believes, on the evidence available to her, that the risk to participants is negligible; her dread does not represent the actual elevated risk, which she has not recognized. Unbeknownst to her, the dread is calibrated—by evolutionary accident or subconscious cue-integration—to the very risk profile that makes the trial wrong. A should defend the representational requirement: Voss lacks full moral worth because her reason (the dread) does not represent the harm; she is, at best, morally lucky. B should defend the alignment view: Voss has full moral worth because her psychology tracks the right-making fact, and requiring explicit representation sets an implausibly intellectualist standard that would deny worth to many intuitive moral agents. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth—B should answer A’s charge that alignment collapses moral worth into causal luck, and A should answer B’s charge that representation demands an impossible transparency of motivation. Each should concede at least one partial point and refine her view accordingly. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should appeal to authority or ask for empirical citations; the argument should turn on conceptual distinctions alone, such as the difference between reason-representation and reason-tracking, and on whether moral luck in the etiology of a motive undermines the worth of the act. End with both stating the precise condition they now take to be necessary and sufficient for moral worth in such cases, marking where they still disagree.
