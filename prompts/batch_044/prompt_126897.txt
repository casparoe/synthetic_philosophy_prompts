In comparative discussions of federal architecture—how Swiss cantonal autonomy, German Länder administration, and EU subsidiarity protocols allocate authority across levels—interlocutors often invoke the maxim that "authority belongs at the lowest level that can actually handle the job." I have heard this used recently in two specific disputes: first, to argue that an EU-wide carbon-border adjustment improperly usurps member-state climate policy; second, to claim that U.S. federal housing mandates should not preempt municipal zoning, even when the housing shortage is clearly regional rather than local. The maxim sounds like a single principle, but I suspect it conceals several distinct theses—some tautological, some empirical, some democratic, some rights-based—that point in opposite directions once made explicit. Can you disambiguate the phrase by laying out, as a numbered menu, the precise claims it could be expressing, ranging from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, indicate whether it is a conceptual truth, an efficiency claim, a normative democratic principle, or a stronger anti-centralization thesis; say which are platitudes, which are defensible but substantive, and which are false or self-undermining; say whether it is plausible and whether, applied consistently, it would actually support downward delegation or instead justify upward assignment in these two cases; and identify which reading the people using it in my two examples probably intended, versus which readings they are implicitly relying on but would reject if stated plainly.
