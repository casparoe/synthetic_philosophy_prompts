I am a doctoral student in philosophy of decision theory preparing a briefing for an institutional ethics board. The board must decide whether to endorse a proposal from a geoengineering collective: the collective seeks regulatory approval for an intervention whose success is estimated at less than one in a trillion, but which—if successful—would prevent catastrophic climate damage and preserve welfare for an astronomically large number of future generations. In return, the collective offers an immediate, guaranteed allocation of funds to the board's present priorities, together with a contingent commitment to deploy unbounded resources for global adaptation should the intervention succeed. One board member argues that standard rational-choice principles require endorsement: multiplying any non-zero probability by a sufficiently large welfare gain must dominate any finite present cost, irrespective of the mechanism's empirical implausibility. Several other members feel that allowing this single tail-risk scenario to dictate the decision is conceptually coercive, but they cannot determine whether the error lies in the probability assessment, in the linear aggregation of value across scales, or in an implicit metaphysical commitment to treating all logical possibilities as decision-relevant regardless of evidential status. I need to present the board with a precise philosophical diagnosis: is the first member's argument a direct application of expected-utility maximization, or does it smuggle in a controversial premise about how rational agents ought to weigh outcomes that lie outside any historical base rate? Furthermore, what principled considerations—short of flatly rejecting low-probability, high-stakes reasoning—should guide the board's decision? I would appreciate a rigorous but accessible analysis of the conceptual structure underlying this negotiation, with particular attention to whether there is a coherent middle position between naive expected-value calculation and outright dismissal of extreme tail risks.
