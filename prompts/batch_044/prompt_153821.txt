ok so in just war theory the moral equality of combatants thing has been deadlocked since basically walzer vs the revisionists, and in my seminar we've hit the same wall for like a year. every argument is familiar — collective responsibility, the epistemic excuse for soldiers who dont know the war is unjust, the seperation of jus ad bellum and jus in bello. but nobody moves. my hunch is that it's not one disagreement but two crammed under the same label: one about whether its permissible to kill an enemy soldier, and one about whether that soldier is morally blameworthy for participating. the traditionalists might be answering the permission question while the revisionists answer the blame question, which is why they talk past each other.

but then there's also this weird asymmetry i've been chewing on: soldiers fighting for the unjust cause might actually have stronger internal reasons to believe their cause is just — state propaganda, cohesion, whatever — so the standard epistemic excuse argument (that soldiers on both sides are equally ignorant) might not be symmetric at all. is that a hidden empirical claim doing work, or just a red herring?

so can you diagnose the stall? is it verbal, substantive, are the sides answering different questions, is there a hidden formal or empirical issue? and given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a conceptual split, a model of doxastic responsibility, an experiment, or is it just bedrock intuition? i dont need a bibliography, just the structure of the disagreement and whether there's any way out.
