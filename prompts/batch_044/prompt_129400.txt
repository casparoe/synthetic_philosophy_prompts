In my role as a reporter who covers scientific and philosophical disputes for a general-interest publication, I need a reusable decision procedure—one I can run in about fifteen minutes before filing—to classify expert disagreements I encounter. Ground it specifically in Aumann's agreement theorem. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the precise termination points (e.g., classify as rational disagreement with genuinely divergent priors; classify as a failure of common-knowledge reasoning that should be flagged; classify as outside the theorem's domain and hand the evaluation back to value judgment), and the explicit points where judgment cannot be eliminated and a reporter must decide. The procedure must handle hard cases: disagreement that persists despite shared evidence because priors genuinely differ; disagreement that appears substantive but collapses once reasoning is actually exchanged to common knowledge; and disputes in which policy recommendations or normative commitments, not probabilistic posteriors, drive the split. Apply it to three scenarios: (1) two epidemiologists assigning different probabilities to a laboratory-origin hypothesis for a novel pathogen; (2) two philosophers of mind disagreeing over whether phenomenal consciousness is physically reducible; and (3) two AI safety researchers with divergent timelines for transformative artificial intelligence. For each, show where the procedure lands, and include at least one case where the procedure classifies the disagreement as non-substantive or outside the theorem's domain despite public framing as a deep scientific split, stating what that implies for the reporting. Throughout, separate what Aumann's result actually proves—about common priors, common knowledge of rationality, and convergence—from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among decision theorists, particularly regarding bounded rationality, the treatment of non-probabilistic beliefs, and whether the theorem applies to value-laden disputes, and do not present any contested reading as the settled consensus.
