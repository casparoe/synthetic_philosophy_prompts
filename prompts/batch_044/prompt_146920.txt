I recently read a long forum post that summarizes Molyneux's problem in a somewhat unusual way, and I am trying to determine how much of it to trust. The author claims that William Molyneux posed the question to John Locke in the late seventeenth century specifically to test whether empirical knowledge has a unified structure across the senses, and that Locke's negative answer—that a man born blind who suddenly gained sight could not immediately distinguish a cube from a sphere—was meant to show that tactile and visual ideas have no intrinsic qualitative overlap. According to the summary, this proves that empiricism must be modular: each sense produces its own proprietary conceptual vocabulary, and recognizing an object across modalities requires an explicit learned translation rather than direct conceptual application. The post then argues that modern research with sensory-substitution devices, in which blind individuals learn to navigate visual scenes through tactile feedback, confirms Locke's modular view, because subjects only achieve recognition after acquiring a decoding protocol rather than through any spontaneous cross-modal insight. From this, the author concludes that empiricism should be abandoned in favor of what he calls constructivist modularism—the brain does not extract ideas from raw sensation, but actively constructs modality-specific representations that must be artificially integrated.

I am not sure whether Locke really held such a modular view, whether the neuroscience actually supports it, or whether the conclusion is being smuggled in. Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts are accurate representations of Locke's position and the modern evidence, which parts conflate Locke with later philosophers or overstate the science, and whether the move to constructivist modularism actually follows from the premises the author lays out?
