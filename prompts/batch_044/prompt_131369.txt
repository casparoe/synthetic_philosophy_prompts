After a long argument with a friend about whether absurdism genuinely allows for meaningful lives, I keep noticing a specific pattern I do not have a name for. Interlocutors defend local, human-scale meaning—loving relationships, artistic creation, moral commitment—by saying these remain valuable even if the universe is indifferent. But when I offer a structurally parallel case, such as someone who spends decades constructing a rigorous taxonomy of micro-decay patterns in decommissioned industrial concrete, building shared knowledge and experiencing deep emotional investment within a small community, the same people dismiss it as “merely coping,” “just a hobby,” or “not the right kind of meaning.” The formal features they praised in the canonical examples—complexity, self-transcendence, relational value—are all present, yet they invoke an unstated cultural canon to exclude the unfamiliar case. Is there an established concept for this precise move: using the rhetoric of scale-independent local meaning to defend a pre-selected list of activities, then treating that list as if it were justified by the rejection of cosmic-scale criteria? If there is no specific term, how should it be carved—what exactly is it, what are its boundary cases, and how does it differ from ordinary parochialism, from equivocation between subjective and objective meaning, and from the defensive retreat characteristic of motte-and-bailey arguments? I want the phenomenon precisely mapped, not merely labeled.
