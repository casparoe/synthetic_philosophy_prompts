I am a novelist working on a manuscript in which artificial companions and various non-human presences share narrative space with human characters, and I wish to test whether Descartes' mechanistic view of animals—his claim that they lack articulated language, flexible reason, and therefore any immaterial conscious soul—can be translated into the conceptual vocabulary of fiction craft without losing its force or its coherence. Restate Descartes' core argument, particularly his inference from observable behavior and the absence of syntactic language to the conclusion that an animal is a bête-machine with no interiority, using only the terms of narrative construction: focalization, free indirect discourse, round and flat characterization, plot function, narrative agency, and point-of-view mechanics. For each philosophical claim in the argument, report whether it maps cleanly onto these literary categories, whether it must be distorted to fit within them, or whether it has no counterpart whatsoever in how fiction organizes non-human presence. Then provide the residue report: does fiction's routine ability to sustain an animal's point of view across many pages without logical contradiction expose a genuine weakness in Descartes' reasoning from behavior to mechanism, or does it merely demonstrate that narrative is an ontologically permissive medium that imposes no commitments about real minds? Reach a reasoned conclusion on that question and justify it. Write everything as continuous prose in the manner of a formal letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
