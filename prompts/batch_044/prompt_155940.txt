I have spent some time thinking on my own about what philosophers call the experience machine, and I have worked myself into a particular confusion that I hope can be cleared up conceptually. The usual version of the thought experiment, as I understand it, asks whether we would plug into a machine that gives us only pleasant illusions, and it is meant to show that well-being depends on more than just how life feels from the inside. But I want to press a variation that seems less discussed: imagine a machine that does not feed you passive pleasure, but instead constructs an environment in which you genuinely struggle with hard problems, develop real skills, and experience the fatigue and satisfaction of actual mental effort, except that all your achievements occur inside the simulation and have no effect on the real world, and you never learn this while inside. Now, if we also assume that you hold a true second-order desire that your efforts contribute to a meaningful life story, even though they do not reach outside the tank, does this internal reality of effort salvage the life from the usual objections? And specifically, how does this scenario bear on the three broad accounts of well-being—hedonism, desire-fulfillment, and objective-list views—when we ask not just whether the life feels good, but whether the absence of external causal impact undermines the value of the internal achievements?
