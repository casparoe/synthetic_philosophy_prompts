Here is an argument I have been turning over in a workshop on population axiology, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion is far more destructive than anything I meant to defend.

(1) Dominance: if world W contains exactly the same individuals as world V, and each individual in W has well-being at least as high as in V with at least one strictly higher, then W is better than V.

(2) To block the repugnant conclusion—that a vast world of lives barely worth living is better than a smaller world of excellent lives—we must reject total-utilitarian aggregation and adopt some form of averaging across individuals.

(3) Averaging is not essentially about worlds; it applies to any closed collection of well-being-bearing entities over which we can compute a mean.

(4) A human life, on a standard four-dimensionalist metaphysics, is not a point but a spatiotemporally extended object composed of temporal stages, each stage carrying some well-being value.

(5) Therefore, if extending life L with n additional years of welfare just barely above zero lowers the mean well-being across all stages of L, then the extended life L+ is worse than L by the very averaging standard in (2).

(6) For any life whose current mean welfare is only modestly positive—say, because suffering and flourishing roughly cancel, leaving a thin positive residue—there exists such a destructive extension: add enough barely-positive years and the life-mean falls.

(7) Most actual human lives satisfy the condition in (6).

(8) Hence, by (5)-(7), extending most lives with ordinary, barely-pleasant years makes those lives worse.

(9) The same structure applies to reproduction: adding a child with barely-positive welfare lowers the world-mean of well-being, so by (2) we should reproduce only if the child’s welfare would be excellent.

(10) But if we must reject (9)—and indeed (8)—while retaining (2), then we need a principled reason why averaging applies across individuals within a world but not across stages within a life, and that reason cannot be ad hoc.

If I have to choose, I would keep (1) and (2). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is the flaw a hidden equivocation in (3) or (5), an unjustified empirical slide in (6) or (7), or is the argument actually valid, in which case the real price is paid by abandoning either averaging or four-dimensionalism about lives?
