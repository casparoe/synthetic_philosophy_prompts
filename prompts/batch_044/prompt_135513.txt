I'm studying comparative economic history and keep encountering a particular argumentative loop in discussions of the Great Divergence and the Needham question. The pattern unfolds like this: a scholar asks why sustained industrial growth emerged in Britain rather than in Qing China, treating the two cases as comparable trajectories that might have diverged at some identifiable point. A critic responds that this framing presupposes a false equivalence—Britain and China operated with different ecological constraints, different state capacities, and even different conceptual categories for wealth, productivity, and technological change—so measuring both against a single axis of "industrialization" imposes a teleological metric drawn from the very outcome being explained. Rather than denying these asymmetries, however, the original scholar often concedes them and then redeploys them as a methodological virtue: they argue that comparing two profoundly different cases is valuable precisely because it reveals where paths diverged, and that doing so requires a shared baseline even if that baseline is artificial or anachronistic. The critic then observes that the baseline—typically "potential for sustained growth" or "capacity for mechanized production"—is usually constructed post hoc from indicators that are only identifiable as causally relevant because they preceded industrialization in Britain. At this point, some defenders retreat further, insisting that they are not comparing outcomes but rather testing counterfactual resilience, yet the counterfactuals they construct tend to hold British institutional configurations constant while varying only Chinese ones, which silently preserves the asymmetry they claimed to have bracketed.

I do not have a name for this specific dialectical structure, and I am unsure whether it should be classified as a malformed comparative question that survives through methodological self-defense, or whether it is a legitimate form of counterfactual reasoning that merely needs stricter boundary conditions. Is there an established concept in the philosophy of history or in comparative historical methodology—perhaps related to framing effects, circular explanation, or counterfactual asymmetry—that captures this exact move, where the acknowledged incommensurability of two cases is acknowledged and then converted into a justification for retaining the comparison? If there is no precise term, how should one distinguish this loop from ordinary false equivalence on the one hand and from well-formed contrastive explanation on the other, and what logical structure best describes the way the baseline gets reconstructed from the outcome it is meant to explain?
