I cover the philosophy of biology and synthetic genomics for a science magazine, and I have become stuck on a conceptual point that seems to split the researchers I interview. Everyone calls DNA a "code," yet when I press them on what kind of code it is, they slide between the technical genetic code—the codon-to-amino-acid mapping established in the early 1960s—and a much stronger claim that the genome is literally a program written in a four-letter language with syntax, semantics, and an interpreter. The confusion I cannot shake is this: in every formal system I know, from Morse to high-level programming languages, a code requires an arbitrary convention shared between an encoder and a decoder that are functionally distinct. But in cellular translation, the ribosome and the tRNAs are chemically coupled to the mRNA; the "decoder" is not interpreting an arbitrary symbol but recognizing a physical shape through molecular complementarity. If there is no true arbitrariness and no separation of interpreter from message, then is calling DNA a "code" actually a category mistake—closer to a causal template or a lock-and-key mechanism? And if the analogy collapses, what happens to synthetic biology's framing of genes as rewritable software?

A few researchers mentioned mitochondrial code variants and the so-called histone code as evidence of conventional, dialect-like layers nested inside the genome, almost like nested programming environments running on shared biochemical hardware. Does that rescue the programming metaphor by showing that evolution can indeed maintain arbitrary conventions across distinct molecular contexts, or does it merely multiply the confusion by positing interpreter modules—virtual-machine-like layers—that natural selection has no clear mechanism to design? I am trying to understand whether the "code" language in biology is a disciplined metaphor with precise limits, or whether these nested variants reveal that life is genuinely running something like stacked interpreters, and if so, whether that forces us to abandon the idea that DNA is just chemistry with informational decoration.
