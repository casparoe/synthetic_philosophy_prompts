Exam question: Contemporary discussions of weakness of will (akrasia) sometimes appeal to hyperbolic discounting—the tendency to value near-future rewards disproportionately more than distant ones—to explain why agents reverse preferences as rewards become imminent. Explain how this model is supposed to account for incontinent action without requiring that the agent literally acts against her best judgment. Then evaluate whether it genuinely explains akrasia or merely redescribes preference change. In your answer, address how this proposal interacts with Davidson’s distinction between all-things-considered and unconditional judgments, and with the claim that diachronic planning requires stable cross-temporal preferences.

Student answer: The hyperbolic discounting model treats weakness of will as a predictable distortion in temporal valuation rather than as a failure of executive control. Unlike exponential discounting, which assumes a constant rate of time-preference, hyperbolic curves predict that an agent will prefer two units of reward in ten days over one unit in nine days, yet prefer one unit now over two units tomorrow. Applied to akrasia, this means the agent does not act against an unchanged best judgment; instead, the judgment itself is time-relative, so that the same option is evaluated differently as it becomes imminent. This avoids Davidson’s problem of explaining how an unconditional judgment can be overridden by a conditional one, because there is no single all-things-considered judgment that persists across the temporal gap. The agent is rational relative to her present discount rate.

However, this raises the problem of preference change: if judgments shift with proximity, then so-called incontinent actions might just be rational updates rather than failures. Ainslie’s distinction between occurrent and dispositional preference was meant to block this, but applied to hyperbolic discounting it fails, because both types of preference are equally subject to temporal proximity effects. Furthermore, Bratman has argued that hyperbolic discounting collapses diachronic rationality into synchronic rationality, which undermines planning agency altogether. Since hyperbolic discounting is also evolutionarily adaptive for foraging, it follows that it cannot constitute a failure of rationality in humans. Therefore, the hyperbolic model explains the phenomenon but dissolves the normative problem: there is no akrasia, only temporal preference reversal.
