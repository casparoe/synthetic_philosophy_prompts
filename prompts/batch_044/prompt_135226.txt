I serve on the board of our neighborhood community garden, and we just ran into a deadlock that feels philosophically strange, though I can't name the underlying problem. We were deciding whether to approve a composting partnership with a local restaurant. The partnership should go through only if two conditions are met: our liability insurance covers it, and it actually lowers our waste-hauling costs. We voted separately on each condition and then on the proposal itself. By simple majority, both conditions passed. Yet the final proposal failed, four to three. Three members who had voted yes on both conditions voted no on the proposal, saying they had holistic reservations they couldn't break into parts.

Now the board is split. One faction argues that if the group has collectively affirmed both premises, consistency demands that we approve the conclusion; anyone who voted yes-yes-no is being irrational, and we should adopt a binding rule that makes the premise votes determine the outcome automatically. The other faction says that forcing consistency disrespects members' autonomy—perhaps the premises miss something important about risk or community values that only emerges when you face the actual decision, so the conclusion vote should stand on its own. I was discussing this with a friend who studies collective decision-making, and she mentioned that groups can reason in ways individuals don't, but she didn't resolve the tension.

Here's the twist: our bylaws require that every vote be accompanied by a brief written justification, posted in public minutes. If we keep that transparency rule, does it make the inconsistency more blameworthy—because members must defend contradictory positions in writing—or does it actually justify allowing the contradiction, since it reveals that people are weighing something the premises don't capture? I want a definite stance, not a survey of options: should our board bind itself to logical consistency between premise and conclusion votes, or should we protect the right to vote inconsistently? And is there a principled middle path—perhaps restructuring how we frame the premises—that respects both collective reasoning and individual judgment? Please take a clear position, defend it with reasons, and say whether the public-justification requirement should push us toward consistency or away from it.
