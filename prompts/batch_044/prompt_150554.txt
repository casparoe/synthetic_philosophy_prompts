I spent much of last evening arguing with a close friend about Nietzsche’s account of noble and slave morality, and together we constructed what feels like a self-defeating trap. The reasoning runs as follows. (1) Noble morality begins with spontaneous self-affirmation: it values itself as good and only secondarily assigns the label bad to outsiders. (2) Slave morality is essentially reactive: its concept of evil is not independently generated but is produced by inverting the nobles’ prior valuation of good, so the judgment’s content is constituted by negation of another. (3) Nietzsche’s own critique describes slave morality as life-denying and decadent, which means it characterizes slave morality negatively by reference to the absence of the vitality and strength that define the noble type. (4) Therefore, Nietzsche’s critique is structurally reactive — its negative valuation is derived by inverting an affirmative standard. (5) If structural reactivity is sufficient to make a moral stance an instance of slave morality, then Nietzsche’s own position collapses into the very phenomenon he condemns. (6) One might reply by distinguishing genetic reactivity, the psychological origin of a judgment in resentment, from structural reactivity, the logical form of deriving one evaluation by inverting another; yet if that distinction is legitimate, then slave morality could be structurally reactive without being genetically reactive, severing the link between formal judgment-structure and genealogical origin that the account requires.

My friend thinks the error hides somewhere between steps (4) and (5), but I suspect the tension is deeper and concerns the distinction introduced in (6). I want to preserve both that slave morality is genuinely reactive in a genealogically significant sense and that Nietzsche’s critique is not merely another expression of slave morality. Which step should I reject, and what does abandoning it cost? Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introductory or concluding remarks.
