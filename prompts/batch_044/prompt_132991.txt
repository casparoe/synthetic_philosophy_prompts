i run a seminar on nationalism and history of ideas, about 14 people, 90 mins. design a classroom game that makes them feel the force of hobsbawm's invented traditions and anderson's imagined communities - specifically the weirdness that nations feel ancient even though they're structurally modern. split them into three 'founding committees' for fictional post-colonial states circa 1920. each gets a packet of 8 cultural practices with hidden origin notes (some genuinely old, some standardized in 1890 by a colonial bureaucrat, some hybridized in the 1910s). they have 30 mins to pick 3 traditions to present to an 'international recognition board' (me) as proof of continuous ancient nationhood. scoring: internal cohesion points + recognition points - penalty if they accidentally pick a recently-invented one and defend it as medieval. they don't see the hidden origins until after pitches.

include rules, materials, payoff scheme (no real money - use prestige tokens or something), a timeline, and a debrief that maps their justifications onto hobsbawm's taxonomy and anderson's census-map-museum idea. also include the predictable failure mode (one group always grabs the 'official' ritual that was actually invented by a colonial administrator) and be frank about what the activity can't reproduce - probably the emotional pull of national symbols once they're adopted, which you'll have to supply in discussion.

oh and build in a specific twist: two of the practices in each packet come from ethnic groups that were merged only by the colonial census, so committees have to paper over that contradiction in their national narrative. that should make the 'imagined' part visceral without me lecturing about it. also, they should recieve the hidden origin sheet only after the board has questioned them, so the reveal hits during the debrief. keep it runnable without outside research.
