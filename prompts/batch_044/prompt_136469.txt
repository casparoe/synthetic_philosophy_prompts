I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology drafting a dissertation chapter on functional explanation in non-model organisms. I am trying to move beyond the standard textbook cases—hearts pumping blood, kidneys filtering waste—to see whether the classic dispute between etiological (selected-effects) and causal-role accounts of biological function can be resolved, or at least refined, by examining a more complex case. Specifically, I am interested in chromatin-remodeling complexes in eusocial insects that alter gene expression in response to colony density, thereby mediating caste plasticity. These complexes appear to have been conserved for general stress-response pathways, yet in certain ant and termite lineages they have been co-opted to regulate reproductive division of labor. Write an essay that addresses the following. First, precisely characterize the core commitments of the etiological account (along the lines of Larry Wright) and the causal-role account (along the lines of Robert Cummins), emphasizing where they diverge on the criteria for attributing a function to a present trait. Second, apply both frameworks to the chromatin-remodeling case: on the etiological view, is the current caste-regulating role the proper function, or merely an exaptation of an ancestral stress-response function? On the causal-role view, does the present systemic contribution to colony-level organization suffice to ground functional ascription, regardless of selection history? Third, evaluate whether this case supports a hybrid or pluralist position—perhaps distinguishing proper function from systemic contribution—or whether it instead shows that one account absorbs the other. Finally, reach and justify a definite conclusion: either defend the superiority of one account for handling evolutionary novelty and co-option, or argue that functional biology requires both concepts operating in distinct explanatory contexts. Do not conclude with an unresolved summary; defend a specific verdict and explain what practical difference it makes for how biologists should describe these mechanisms.
