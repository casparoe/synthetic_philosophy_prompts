As part of a dissertation chapter on speech-act theory and synthetic testimony, I am working through a counterfactual exercise: imagine J. L. Austin survived into the present and was confronted with a now-common discursive practice in which human agents publicly assert complex empirical claims generated by large language models. In these cases, the speaker typically lacks both the belief that the claim is true and the first-person epistemic access that would constitute knowledge; yet the claim is often true and calibrated to the model's training distribution, the speaker intends to inform an audience, and that audience treats the utterance as a standard assertion. Given Austin's framework—his distinctions among locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts, and his analyses of felicity conditions, misfires, and abuses—how would he likely classify such an utterance? Would he regard it as a misfire (failure of the conventional procedure), an abuse (failure of sincerity or authority conditions), or as the emergence of a novel illocutionary category requiring its own felicity conditions? More specifically, defend a clear verdict on whether Austin would preserve a belief-based norm of assertion that renders synthetic testimony systematically infelicitous, or whether he would adapt his framework to accommodate assertions whose epistemic authority derives from statistical calibration rather than speaker cognition. The analysis should remain internal to Austin's conceptual apparatus; do not invoke specific page references, bibliographies, or recent secondary literature by citation. Aim for roughly 900 words in formal philosophical prose, and reach a reasoned conclusion rather than ending on an agnostic note.
