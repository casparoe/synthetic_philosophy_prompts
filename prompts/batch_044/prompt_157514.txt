I'm preparing the opening address for tomorrow's interdisciplinary workshop, 'The Limits of First-Person Authority', held in honour of Dr Miriam Cole's retirement from the Institute for Introspective Studies, where she spent three decades investigating clinical self-reporting. The room will be split evenly between researchers in philosophy of mind and clinical psychology, who will catch every reference to confabulation and transparency, and family members, students, and clinicians from adjacent fields who will not. I need a fifteen-minute speech in which the reliability of introspection does real structural work: it should determine what I can praise about Dr Cole's legacy without overstating certainty, what I must leave unsaid about the patients whose reports she trusted, and how I address an audience that includes both experts and people for whom this is entirely personal. The philosophical idea should shape the boundaries of the speech, not merely ornament it.

The subject has become urgently personal over the last three months, since I was required to produce a detailed introspective account of my own stress levels for an occupational-health evaluation and discovered that my report was far more a coherent narrative constructed for the assessor than a direct observation of my mental state. That experience has left me genuinely uncertain whether the first-person authority that underwrote Dr Cole's clinical method is a resource to celebrate or a fiction we politely maintain. I want the address to navigate that tension honestly: to honour her work without pretending that introspection is either infallible or entirely useless, and to acknowledge that for the non-specialists in the room—including her family—the question is not academic but lived.

Please write the address in American English, with British spelling and punctuation in this prompt. I do not want a bibliography, reading list, or citations. To keep the framing from collapsing into familiar slogans about the brain deceiving itself, please treat introspective reports not as direct observations or pure theoretical inventions, but as calibrated performances shaped by an implicit audience and by normative expectations about what counts as a credible emotional narrative. Use that framing to decide what can be said about Dr Cole's contribution, what must remain carefully qualified, and how the mixed audience should understand the value of her career.
