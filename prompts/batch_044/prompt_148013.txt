Write a formal dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors, Althea and Balthasar, debating the character of democracy in Spinoza’s Tractatus Politicus and related political writings. Althea defends the interpretation that Spinoza’s democracy is fundamentally direct and affective: democratic sovereignty resides in the continuous collective conatus of the multitude, and any council or assembly is merely a temporary, revocable crystallization of that power rather than a genuine delegation. Balthasar defends the interpretation that Spinoza’s democracy is structurally mediated and constitutional: the text describes a specific governing council with explicit membership rules and exclusions—of women, children, foreigners, and those under another’s power—which shows that democratic sovereignty operates through durable juridical representation and stable rules, not through perpetual direct participation.

Have them engage in real back-and-forth on the central tension: Spinoza presents democracy as the form in which natural equality is best preserved and sovereignty is absolute, yet his democratic constitution assigns authority to a council and excludes broad categories of people. Althea must account for why these institutional details and exclusions do not undermine her claim of direct, unmediated sovereignty; Balthasar must account for why Spinoza insists that democracy alone preserves equality if its practical form resembles an aristocratic body. Each should concede minor points where warranted but never abandon their core reading, and neither should be reduced to a strawman.
