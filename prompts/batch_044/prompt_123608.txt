I'm giving the toast at my sister's retirement dinner after thirty years designing speech-generating assistive devices for people with locked-in syndrome. Half the room are engineers and clinical neurologists who understand exactly how the boxes work; the other half are family and neighbors who only know she built machines that let people talk again. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've spent years turning over one question that won't leave me alone: when a patient selects "water" on her screen and the machine says it aloud, is that word carrying real meaning—what philosophers call original intentionality—or is it only derived, like ink on a map that means something only because a human mind gave it meaning? I want the toast to honor her without pretending the devices have souls, and without reducing her patients' voices to mechanical noise. To do that, I need the speech to navigate a knot I've never seen named well in anything I've read: what I'm calling a "double derivation" loop, where the patient's original intention gets reshaped by the device's fixed vocabulary, and then the device derives its meaning back from that already-altered intention, so the boundary between user and tool blurs in both directions. The idea should do real structural work—shaping what I can and can't claim about her life's work—rather than serving as decoration. It needs to land for the experts without losing the family. Please write the toast, about four minutes long, spoken standing with a glass raised, and begin your response immediately with the opening line of the speech itself: no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of the question.
