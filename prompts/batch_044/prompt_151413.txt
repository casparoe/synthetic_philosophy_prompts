They say engineered ambiguity is basically the same as misleading, which is different from lying because lying requires saying something false. In these transparency cases, the institution never actually asserts the false claim—say, that safety incidents went down—because every sentence is technically true. They just leave out the baseline data. So it's misleading, not lying.

This matters morally because lying violates the hearer's right to be told the truth, which Kant said is absolute. Misleading doesn't violate that right because you're not giving them false information; you're just letting them draw their own wrong conclusion. The speaker controls the framework, but that doesn't change the fact that no false assertion was made, so the moral status stays closer to omission.

Also, Grice's Cooperative Principle says that misleading breaks the maxim of quantity because you withhold information. But since Grice thinks all conversation should be cooperative, this means misleading is actually a worse violation than lying, because lying only breaks quality while misleading breaks both quality and quantity. So there's no moral asymmetry—both are bad, but misleading is worse because it exploits the listener's own reasoning.
