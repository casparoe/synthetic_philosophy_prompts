Two people I know—Maya and Leo—got into an argument after a seminar on George Berkeley’s economic writings, specifically the line of questioning in *The Querist* about what gives money its power to command labor and goods. Maya said Berkeley treats coin and paper as mere “tickets” or counters that direct the mind to future commodities, which makes his view essentially a conventionalist, functionalist theory of value: money works like a social language, and its worth comes entirely from the collective agreement to treat a token as exchangeable for labor, not from any intrinsic property of gold or silver. Leo replied that this reading ignores Berkeley’s immaterialism: if physical coins are just sensory ideas ordered by divine law, and the goods they represent are also just patterns of ideas, then calling money a “ticket to a show” collapses the distinction between the sign and the thing signified. A bank run, Leo said, becomes nothing more than people altering mental associations, which means Berkeley can describe a financial panic but cannot explain why it correlates with real failures of harvest or production unless he smuggles in some non-mental reality of labor and scarcity. Maya countered that this is not smuggling but consistency: the “real” shortage is itself a sustained pattern of ideas (empty fields, failed crops), and the monetary crisis is a parallel breakdown in the sign-system that connects tokens to those patterns; Berkeley distinguishes the two by the content of the ideas involved, not by invoking matter. Leo then pressed that if everything reduces to mental content, Berkeley’s economic analysis loses all independent explanatory power—every market event becomes a footnote to metaphysics, and the “ticket” metaphor does no work if both the ticket and the admission are just ideas. Maya answered that coherence is not redundancy: Berkeley treats labor as genuinely real in the only sense available to him—ideas sustained by God—and the point is precisely that money’s value is not in the metal but in the conventional link between token and sustained idea-pattern, which makes him a conventionalist about representation rather than a materialist labor-theorist. Leo’s final move was that if the agreement is purely mental, Berkeley still owes an account of why monetary panics spread like contagion across minds rather than affecting isolated individuals; his individualist ontology of ideas seems ill-equipped to explain collective, systemic failure without appealing to something like shared mental causation that his system does not clearly provide.
