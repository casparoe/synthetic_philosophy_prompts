Since retiring from a long career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have devoted myself to formal philosophy, and in a recent seminar on epistemic rationality I witnessed a persistent disagreement that I believe can be made precise. On one side stands a camp that treats accuracy—typically measured by proper scoring rules—as the sole fundamental epistemic value, holding that any other apparent good, such as explanatory coherence or discriminatory resolution, derives its worth instrumentally from its contribution to long-run calibration. On the other side stands a camp that accepts epistemic utility theory in a pluralist form, insisting that coherence, understanding, and resolution carry independent weight and that rational credence can legitimately trade a marginal loss in calibration for a substantial gain in one of these other dimensions. Rather than asking for a verdict, I want you to draft the document that a committed representative of each camp could both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration.

The statement should begin with a numbered list of agreed claims: that belief states are subject to synchronic coherence constraints that prevent Dutch-book vulnerability; that the Brier score decomposes into calibration and resolution; that belief serves action through some decision-theoretic bridge; and that no normative epistemology can be fully divorced from the practical contexts in which predictions are tested. This should be followed by a disagreements table in neutral wording that each side accepts for its own position—particularly whether calibration should enjoy lexical priority in every epistemic domain, whether explanatory coherence is reducible to expected accuracy, and whether a rational agent facing a well-calibrated but opaque predictive model should prefer it over a slightly miscalibrated but mechanistically transparent rival. For each crux, specify a concrete case both sides agree bears on the issue: I propose the climate-ensemble dilemma, in which an opaque statistical ensemble is well calibrated but offers no physical mechanism, while a process-based model is somewhat miscalibrated but yields explanatory understanding. Each camp should record its prediction about which model ought to be adopted, and the table should indicate whether settling the empirical calibration gap would resolve the philosophical dispute or merely shift its locus. Finally, state clearly whether any part of the disagreement is verbal—perhaps concerning the definition of accuracy itself—and identify the formal result, if any, that would genuinely move the debate. Write this in a formal, polished style appropriate for joint publication, and do not include reading lists or citations, as I am working away from my usual library.
