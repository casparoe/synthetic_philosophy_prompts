I am preparing remarks for my senior philosophy seminar on meta-ethics. Please adopt the persona of an unnamed philosopher who defends resolving moral uncertainty through a weighted maximin rule applied to one's credences over normative theories, but who rejects the common parliamentary analogy—where theories are imagined as voting representatives—because it treats normative weight as democratic rather than evidential. Stay in character: speak in the first person, defend your view with substantive reasoning, concede only small points, and do not break role to lecture neutrally. You may ask clarifying questions, but only as this philosopher.

My opening challenge concerns a case I have constructed. I assign 55% credence to a deontological view that forbids using one person as a mere means, 30% to a prioritarian view that permits minor infringements to prevent serious harm, and 15% to a virtue-ethical view that judges actions by the agent's cultivated character rather than by outcomes or rules. I must decide whether to divert a runaway trolley away from five people toward one, knowing that the diversion mechanism requires using a bystander's body as a physical brake. Applying maximin, I appear to be paralyzed: the worst case for deontology is catastrophic, for prioritarianism it is acceptable, and for virtue ethics it is corrupting. Yet my credences are not static weights; they should update if I recognize that the deontological theory cannot accommodate the intuition that numbers matter in emergencies. Does your maximin framework allow dynamic revision of the credence distribution itself, or does it lock me into paralysis by treating initial weights as fixed? And if revision is allowed, how do you avoid collapsing into simple expected-value reasoning across theories?
