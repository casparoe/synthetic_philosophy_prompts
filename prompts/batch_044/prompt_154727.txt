I was arguing with a friend about whether contradictions can actually be true, not just seem that way because we're confused. I want to check my instincts against a few cases, because they feel all over the place.

Take physical boundaries: a fence that sits exactly on the property line between two yards. I'd say it's genuinely in yard A and genuinely not in yard A, and that's not a mistake — the world just doesn't slice neatly there. Same with a sound that's right at the threshold of human hearing: it's both audible and not audible to a normal ear. Those feel like real, unavoidable contradictions. Then there's institutional overlap. A refugee who holds citizenship papers from country X but X's law says she never recieved them, while country Y's law says she did. So she's both a citizen and not one, according to the actual legal codes. That also feels like a true contradiction in the social world, not just an ambiguity we could clear up with a phone call.

But now compare a biologist saying 'this cell is both cancerous and not cancerous' because the test results are contradictory. That feels wrong — like the contradiction shows our ignorance, not a feature of the cell. Or a philosopher declaring 'morality is both objective and not objective' as if that's deep. That feels like a cop-out. So my pattern seems to be: contradictions are okay when they come from boundary conditions or overlapping systems, but not when they come from bad evidence or conceptual laziness. Except I'm stuck on formal logic. In some paraconsistent systems you can derive both P and not-P without the system exploding, and people call that a true contradiction. Sometimes I think that's just like the fence — the formal system has overlapping rules. Other times I think it's just a language game, and '=' means different things in the two derivations. So my principle might break here.

Can you lay out a precise principle — or maybe two competing ones — that captures which contradictions I should accept? I need it to handle the fence and the refugee case, reject the cancer and morality ones, and tell me exactly what to think about the formal logic example. Also, what does it say about a case I haven't mentioned: a sentence that's grammatically correct in dialect A and grammatically incorrect in dialect B, spoken by someone who genuinely belongs to both dialects?
