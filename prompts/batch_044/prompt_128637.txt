I am working through past feedback from Dr. Miriam Holroyd, who supervises our philosophy of history module and has rather idiosyncratic views on historical explanation. Over the past term she has ruled on several student proposals as follows. She marked adequate a structural explanation of the Meiji Restoration that appealed to foreign gunboat diplomacy, the Tokugawa fiscal crisis, and the collapse of the shogunate’s monopoly on violence, without requiring any chronology of the 1868 events themselves. She marked inadequate a proposal that explained the same restoration purely through the narrative of Saigō Takamori’s rebellion, the Chōshū-Satsuma alliance, and the Emperor’s declaration, saying it left the structural preconditions unexplained. She marked adequate a narrative explanation of the fall of the Western Roman Empire that traced the specific sequence of barbarian migrations, military usurpations, and the division of 395, without invoking any general law of imperial decline. By contrast, she marked inadequate a proposal that explained Rome’s fall solely through a covering law of imperial overreach leading to systemic collapse, complaining that it erased the particularity of the Western experience. She also marked adequate a proposal explaining the rapid diffusion of the printing press across European cities that combined literacy-rate data with the institutional demand generated by the Council of Basel, but marked inadequate one that cited only urban density and mercantile networks with no mention of Gutenberg’s workshop or specific ecclesiastical needs.

Next term she will examine a proposal that explains the dissolution of Yugoslavia between 1991 and 1995. The proposal offers a narrative tracing the specific sequence of Slovenia’s secession, the Croatian War of Independence, Milošević’s centralising rhetoric, and the Srebrenica massacre, without appealing to any general law of multi-ethnic state fragmentation. I am trying to predict her verdict, but I can see at least two plausible rules running through her earlier judgements. One rule distinguishes state consolidation from state collapse, demanding structural conditions for the former and allowing particularist narrative for the latter. Another distinguishes events with concentrated modern state agency from those with dispersed pre-modern authority, requiring structural framing where bureaucratic institutions dominate and narrative framing where authority is fragmented. A third possibility is that she applies a principle of explanatory proportionality, accepting covering-law explanations only when embedded in specific institutional contexts and accepting pure narrative only when no modern structural analogue exists.

Please predict her verdict on the Yugoslavia proposal, set out the candidate rules her track record genuinely supports, say which one you think she is implicitly running and why, and indicate which further case—perhaps the unification of Italy in 1861 or the partition of India in 1947—would best distinguish between them. I am less interested in the answer itself than in how her earlier rulings underdetermine it and what principle she would implicitly be adopting whichever way she rules.
