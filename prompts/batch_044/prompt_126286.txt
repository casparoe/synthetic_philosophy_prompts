I work as a software engineer in distributed systems, with no formal background in philosophy, and I am trying to think through a conceptual problem that sits at the boundary of metaphysics and modern neuroscience. Kurt Gödel died in 1978, so he never encountered two debates that have only matured since then. The first is the empirical literature on terminal lucidity—brief, unexpected recoveries of mental clarity in patients with severe neurodegeneration shortly before death—which some philosophers of mind treat as potential evidence that consciousness is not fully reducible to intact neural architecture. The second is what I will call the substrate-independence thesis applied to personal survival: the proposal that if consciousness is essentially information processing, then a sufficiently precise computational reconstruction of a brain’s functional architecture could preserve personal identity after biological death, functioning as a technologically mediated form of afterlife.

Given Gödel’s documented commitments—his rationalist theism, his ontological argument for a necessary being, his Platonist conviction that mathematical objects exist objectively in a non-physical realm, his belief in an actual afterlife, and his later interest in phenomenology—how might he have assessed these two modern ideas?

I would like the answer laid out as a menu of distinct readings rather than a single verdict:

1. Would he have interpreted terminal lucidity as evidence for a non-physical soul or monad temporarily decoupling from a failing brain, consistent with his rationalist metaphysics?

2. Or would he have treated it as an unexplained but ultimately physical neurochemical event that offers no genuine metaphysical purchase on the afterlife?

3. Regarding substrate-independent reconstruction: would he have rejected digital resurrection on the grounds that personal identity requires a non-physical substance that cannot be instantiated in silicon?

4. Or might he have accepted a reconstructed informational pattern as a genuine continuation of the self, provided that the underlying rational structure—the “monad” in a logical rather than biological sense—remains intact?

For each reading, please indicate whether it is strongly consistent with Gödel’s known views, whether it requires a significant extension of them, and whether it conflicts with his Platonism or his theism.

I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want conceptual analysis only. I also do not need a full exposition of Gödel’s ontological proof—assume familiarity with its basic rationalist structure. Please treat the question as a genuine exercise in philosophical prediction rather than a historical report, and feel free to note where Gödel’s own writings leave the answer genuinely underdetermined.
