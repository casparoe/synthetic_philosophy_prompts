For an upper-level undergraduate exam in metaphysics and philosophy of religion, write a single 45-minute essay question (target response length approximately 800 words) that requires students to evaluate a constructed, non-standard distributive challenge to Leibniz's claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. Frame the challenge precisely: suppose world W1 and world W2 contain exactly the same multiset of goods and evils and exhibit the same total degree of compossible perfection, but differ only in distributive pattern—W1 concentrates suffering within a small minority while W2 disperses it evenly across all individuals. The question should ask whether Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason, together with his doctrine of compossibility, can adjudicate between W1 and W2 without covertly importing an independent principle of distributive justice, and whether any attempt to rescue the best-world thesis by adding such a principle collapses Leibniz's view into a form of consequentialism he explicitly rejects. The prompt must be unanswerable by merely reciting a standard summary of Leibniz's essays on divine justice and the origin of evil. Provide a model answer of about 700 words that reconstructs the argument from sufficient reason, isolates the hidden premise that global optimization is exhaustive, demonstrates why distributive invariance undermines that premise, and assesses the charge of consequentialist collapse. Then provide a marking scheme that distinguishes an A-grade answer—which must explicitly identify the compossibility constraint, rigorously separate global evaluation from distributive evaluation, and show that the distributive premise is not derivable from sufficient reason alone—from a B-grade answer, which correctly summarizes Leibniz's position but either fails to register the distributive insertion or misses the consequentialist-collapse argument. List the specific errors that should cost marks, including conflating metaphysical evil with moral evil, treating compossibility as a merely quantitative aggregation, and ignoring the distinction between optimizing total perfection and optimizing its distribution.
