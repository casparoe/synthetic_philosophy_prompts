Hey—been thinking about corruption and culture for a seminar I'm prepping, and there's a pattern I keep noticing that I don't have a good word for. Imagine a small-scale bureaucratic setting: a farmer brings the local official a crate of produce after harvest, or a family invites him to a wedding with a envelope. There's no specific permit being requested that day, so it's not a classic quid-pro-quo bribe. The gift is too small to buy a particular decision. But everyone understands that if that farmer needs help with a land dispute next year, the official will remember the crate; if he never gets crates, he might be less responsive. Meanwhile, in that community, refusing the gift is seen as insulting—like breaking social trust—rather than as preserving imparitality. So you get this accumulation of small, asymetrical exchanges that aren't part of a formal patron-client machine (no vote-blocking, no explicit loyalty contract), but they still build a kind of standing social credit that shapes access over time. Anthropologists often file this under reciprocity or gift-giving; legal reformers file it under corruption or clientelism. But both labels feel wrong for this specific middle space. Does this exact configuration—non-transactional gifts that slowly accumulate into preferential access without ever being bargained—have an established concept? If so, how is it distinguished from ordinary reciprocity and from full-blown clientelism? And if not, what's the sharpest way to draw its edges? I'm not after a bibliography, just the conceptual carving.
