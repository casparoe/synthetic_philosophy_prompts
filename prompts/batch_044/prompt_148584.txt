I am preparing a seventy-five-minute session for an advanced philosophy-of-science workshop with fourteen graduate students, focused on the demarcation problem and falsificationism. In a recent discussion with peers, I found myself drawn to a pedagogical construct I am calling the "falsification budget": the idea that a research program is not simply falsified or not, but begins with a limited pool of permissible immunization strategies—say, three tokens—each allowing one revision to an auxiliary hypothesis that protects the program's hard core from an anomaly. Once that pool is depleted, further defense requires either abandoning the core claim or accepting classification as a degenerating program. I want you to design a runnable classroom simulation that makes this structural pressure visceral, not theoretical. The activity should divide participants into rotating roles—core theorists, anomaly generators, and a funding panel with discretionary power over token replenishment—governed by explicit rules and a point-based payoff scheme that requires no real money. The dynamics should be arranged so that participants feel the pull of strategic immunization, the ambiguity of the cutoff, and the social pressure to keep a failing program alive, all before the facilitator names the philosophical analogues. Include a precise timeline, the rules of interaction, the payoff structure, and a structured deliberation protocol. Then provide a debrief that maps the simulation's key moments onto the actual philosophical landscape—Popper's asymmetry of verification and falsification, Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes, and the problem of ad hoc adjustments—while clearly flagging which feature of the token-budget model is an artificial pedagogical device that does not correspond to any real mechanism in scientific practice and must be supplied by the facilitator's commentary. Finally, anticipate what will predictably malfunction during a first run—such as informal collusion between theorists and panelists, or participants interpreting point loss as personal failure—and specify how to correct for it in the moment. Do not ask participants to produce bibliographies, citation lists, or specific textual references; the debrief should treat the philosophical ideas in general conceptual terms only.
