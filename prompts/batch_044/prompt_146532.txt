As a novelist drafting literary fiction that engages with philosophical psychology, I want a short story that takes Daniel Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity and pushes it into less charted territory. Write the story in American English. Rather than depicting a standard, unitary self constructed around its own memories, imagine a protagonist who gradually realizes they are a derivative, satellite center of narrative gravity—spun off through years of deep empathetic absorption into another person's life. The revelation should arrive not through technology or external revelation but through small, accumulating inconsistencies: autobiographical memories that do not align with physical timelines, emotional responses that belong to a different history, and a sense of agency that feels borrowed rather than generated. Structure the plot around the protagonist's attempt to establish an independent narrative center, and show that failure arising not from coercion but from the internal logic of narrative dependency—once a center of gravity has been established, it exerts a structural pull that makes true orbital independence conceptually unstable. Treat this as an introspective, literary exploration rather than a philosophical treatise: let the concept shape the imagery and the character's internal experience without relying on technical jargon or didactic explanation. Do not include bibliographies, citations, references to specific texts, or any framing that asks for scholarly apparatus; the story must stand entirely on its own conceptual architecture. Keep the tone restrained and precise, with the philosophical strangeness emerging organically from the protagonist's ordinary attempts to account for their own life.
