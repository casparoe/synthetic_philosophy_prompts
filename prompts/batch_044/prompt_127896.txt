I know the broad chronology—crop failures, price spikes, food riots, and the spread of disease—but I am struggling to assign each layer of this sequence to Braudel’s temporal scales.

Please analyse the episode through his tripartite division. Which elements belong to the geographical or climatic long term, such as agricultural dependence on particular grain cycles or regional weather patterns? Which belong to intermediate social structures—market networks, poor-law administration, state capacity? And which genuinely fall within the domain of individual contingency, such as the decisions of particular ministers, local magistrates, or migrating families? Then, please identify where Braudel’s framework reaches its limits: does classifying the Tambora eruption itself as a discrete “event” obscure the fact that volcanic forcing operates across multi-decadal climatic scales? And does his emphasis on slow structures risk downplaying the immediate bodily suffering produced by a single winter?

Should cholera be interpreted as an extension of the same long-duration climatic structure, as an intermediate phenomenon shaped by trade routes and urban density, or as an autonomous event that sits awkwardly outside Braudel’s scheme? Finally, what does this entire sequence—volcano, climate anomaly, famine, disease—indicate about Braudel’s theory? Does it confirm that geography ultimately governs human affairs, or does it reveal that the boundaries between his three temporal scales collapse when climate itself acts as a historical agent?
