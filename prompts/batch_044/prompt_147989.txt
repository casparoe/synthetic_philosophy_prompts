I have just spent the evening arguing with a close friend about whether introspection can ever be authoritative, and our disagreement turned on concrete cases rather than broad slogans. I want to know whether there is a single principle that captures my own verdicts, or whether consistency requires me to overturn some of them.

Here are five judgments I stand by, though I admit they pull in different directions.

First, when I experience a sudden sharp migraine and immediately report to myself, "this is unbearable," I treat that introspective report as nearly authoritative; the phenomenal intensity seems directly available.

Second, when I try to determine, by inward attention alone, whether my belief that current climate policy is inadequate is driven mainly by evidence or mainly by my anxiety about economic insecurity, my introspective answer feels unstable and mood-dependent; I distrust it.

Third, during our argument tonight my friend pointed to my tense posture and said I was clearly angry. I introspected and felt genuinely calm—only intellectually engaged. I think my introspection was correct and the bodily cue was misleading. So here introspection corrects an external signal.

Fourth, yet in the very same conversation I reported to myself, "I'm not trying to win; I'm genuinely seeking clarity," and in hindsight that was obvious rationalization. My introspection failed to detect the competitive framing of my own speech.

Fifth, I can reliably report that I am now thinking about the concept of monadic perception, but when I try to determine whether that thought takes the form of an inner verbal sentence or a spatial diagram, any answer I produce feels like confabulation.

The tension is especially sharp between the third and fourth cases: in one moment introspection seems to penetrate deeper than behavioral evidence, in the next it seems blind to its own framing conditions.

So I ask: is there one principle—perhaps involving what we might call the "architectural transparency gradient," distinguishing access to phenomenal profile and intentional target from access to causal ancestry, representational vehicle, and pragmatic framing—that delivers all five verdicts? Or must I abandon one?

Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause handles which case, predict what it says about two further cases I have not mentioned—namely, introspecting whether one's current desire is authentic versus socially implanted, and introspecting whether one's visual field has a left-to-right gradient in its phenomenal structure—and then honestly say which of my five verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether overturning that verdict is more plausible than complicating the principle.
