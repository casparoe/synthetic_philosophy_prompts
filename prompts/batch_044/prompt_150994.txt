Here is an argument I drafted for a philosophy-of-law seminar that now strikes me as proving far too much. (1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem applies to any consistent formal system that is recursively axiomatizable and can express elementary arithmetic. (2) The body of law in a modern constitutional democracy—statutes, sentencing guidelines with explicit arithmetic, and recursive rules of precedent—constitutes just such a system, call it L. (3) Therefore there exists a legal proposition P that is true in L yet neither provable nor disprovable within L. (4) Democratic legitimacy requires that citizens be able, in principle, to derive all of their rights and obligations from publicly accessible legal sources. (5) Consequently every democracy necessarily harbors real but permanently inaccessible rights and duties, undermining its legitimacy. (6) This applies to any jurisdiction with numerical provisions in its code, so universal democratic failure follows. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (4) if I can, but I need to know which step fails and what giving it up costs. Reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
