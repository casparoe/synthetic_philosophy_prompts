For a feature I'm writing on how narrative structure distorts historical reconstruction, I'd like a philosophical reading of Kurosawa's Rashomon through the specific lenses of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy. Each witness—the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai via the medium, and the woodcutter—reconstructs the same fatal encounter, yet every version is anchored in the known outcome (the samurai is dead) and shaped by the need to preserve the witness's moral identity. I want you to examine whether the film treats these accounts as merely subjective, or whether it dramatizes a deeper epistemic problem: that knowing the result makes prior events appear causally inevitable, and that human explanation necessarily imposes coherent arcs on contingent, ambiguous action. Focus particularly on whether the woodcutter's ostensibly neutral version still smuggles in teleological assumptions about honor and survival, and ask whether the film's refusal to adjudicate anticipates modern source-critical skepticism about eyewitness testimony. Take both the philosophical concepts and the cinematic form seriously—especially the repetition-with-variation structure—and say whether the work adds something to the debate that a purely analytical essay on hindsight and narrative could not.
