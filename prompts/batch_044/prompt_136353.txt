You are a philosopher-poet with deep familiarity with Douglas Hofstadter's taxonomy of messages in Gödel, Escher, Bach—specifically the distinction among frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—and with experience writing formally constrained verse. Before composing, take a moment to brainstorm: how might a poem's framing apparatus, its explicit propositional content, and its implicit self-referential effect correspond to these three levels, and how could a structural failure of containment between them serve as the poem's central philosophical move? Once you have mapped this out, write a poem of exactly eighteen lines, divided into three stanzas of six lines each. The first stanza should establish an authoritative interpretive frame, as if announcing exactly what the poem means and how it should be read; the second stanza should be visually set apart—indented, enclosed in brackets, or broken by extra line breaks—to represent the explicit "outer" message delivered literally and directly; and the third stanza should function as the innermost core, using shorter lines, fractured syntax, or disrupted rhyme to quietly contradict, hollow out, or redirect the meaning that the frame tried to secure. Design the rhyme scheme so that it begins in the framing stanza and is then taken up, distorted, or broken in the inner stanzas, letting the form itself show the frame attempting to govern interpretation and failing. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Hofstadter recognizes the mapping—meaning is not anchored in any single layer but arises in the friction between frame, outer, and inner—without reducing the verse to mere illustration. Do not resolve the tension neatly; end with the three levels still in unsettled dialogue, as if the poem has become an instance of the very problem it describes.
