As a retired physicist recently drawn into philosophy of mind, I am drafting a proposal for a six-month part-time research fellowship with my university's philosophy department. The question I want to pursue grows out of Douglas Hofstadter's dialogue known as the Ant Fugue and the figure of Aunt Hillary: can an ant colony genuinely possess a mind? Existing answers seem to me trapped in a false dichotomy — either the colony is a unified conscious subject, as emergentists claim, or it is merely a collection of mindless insects, as eliminativists hold. I wish to test a different framing: treating mindedness not as a single binary property but as a multi-dimensional, scale-dependent feature that can be present to varying degrees across different cognitive capacities — integration, adaptive representation, and goal-directed coordination — without requiring a single point of view or a central nervous system. The proposal should set out the precise research question; explain why both emergentist and reductionist positions, together with the standard organism analogy, fail to address this graded, multi-scale possibility; describe a method that combines conceptual analysis of philosophical notions of subjecthood with a structured survey of existing empirical literature on colony-level decision-making, with no original fieldwork required; specify what would count as a successful outcome — for instance, a coherent taxonomy of colony capacities mapped onto philosophical criteria for mindedness — and what would constitute failure, such as the framework collapsing into either pure metaphor or unanalysed behaviourism; and demonstrate that this is achievable within six months of part-time study. Please write the proposal in polished, formal British English, approximately two pages, without including any bibliography or citation list, which I shall supply later. The argument should be accessible to someone with a general background in philosophy and the natural sciences, and should not rely on obscure technical sources.
