I have been thinking on my own about what philosophers mean when they say one thing “grounds” another, and whether reality has to bottom out somewhere. In ordinary conversation we say a table is “just” atoms arranged a certain way, as if the atoms are the real foundation and the table is only derivative. But I recently came across the idea that in physics there is no single correct scale of reality: chemists treat atoms as basic, particle physicists treat quarks and fields as basic, and neither group is wrong—they are just working at different resolutions for different questions. That made me wonder whether metaphysical grounding might be like that too. Perhaps there is no absolute, once-and-for-all “fundamental” layer, only layers that count as fundamental relative to the explanatory task at hand. I would like you to argue for that position. Specifically, defend the claim that fundamentality is resolution-dependent: that saying something is fundamental is more like setting a magnification level than like discovering an ultimate, inviolate foundation. Address the obvious objection that if there is no absolute bottom, explanations never truly finish but only drift downward in an endless regress or loop back on themselves. And say whether, on your view, grounding relations become circular, or whether they remain directional and layered even though there is no single level that is privileged for every purpose. Please make the case directly rather than surveying what others have said.
