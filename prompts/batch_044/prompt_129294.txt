As a novelist working on a long-form narrative about institutional life, I am applying for a six-month interdisciplinary research fellowship to develop a philosophical framework that my fiction keeps encountering but cannot resolve on its own. The precise question is this: do the rehabilitative practices at Norway's Halden prison and the punitive architecture of American mass incarceration represent two genuinely different normative conventions—stabilized by distinct coordination points and common-knowledge structures—or are they merely surface variations of the same underlying carceral logic? I want to examine whether rehabilitative norms depend on what I will call anticipatory recognition, a convention in which staff and inmates coordinate on a shared expectation of future social reintegration, and whether this convention is structurally impossible in systems where exit is permanently foreclosed and narrative identity is institutionally frozen.

Existing philosophical and comparative accounts leave this open because they treat rehabilitation and punishment as competing moral justifications or as policy choices, rather than asking whether they are stable conventions that require specific conditions—mutual expectation, arbitrary alternatives, common knowledge—to persist. The obvious alternative, evolutionary game theory, explains norm stability through payoff structures but cannot account for the role of physical space and shared narrative in sustaining or undermining coordination in closed institutions.

The method will be conceptual and comparative, not empirical. In months one and two, I will map Halden's documented practices—informal staff-inmate interaction, vocational training embedded in domestic architecture, privacy norms—against the conditions for convention stability, asking whether these practices meet the criteria for genuine coordination rather than mere compliance. In months three and four, I will apply the same framework to American mass incarceration, identifying the competing salience points—security classification, risk assessment, perpetual surveillance—that may prevent the emergence of anticipatory recognition. Month five will test whether rehabilitative conventions are parasitic on broader societal norms of reciprocity that American carceral systems deliberately suppress. Month six will produce the deliverable: a philosophical monograph arguing that rehabilitation is a fragile, context-bound convention that collapses when its narrative and spatial conditions are removed, with clear failure conditions specified—specifically, if both systems turn out to share identical equilibrium structures despite their surface differences.

This scope is realistic because it relies on published institutional descriptions and philosophical analysis rather than fieldwork, statistical modeling, or access to restricted sites. If the framework fails—if rehabilitative norms prove reducible to coercive compliance in every case—the negative result is still philosophically determinate and useful for the narrative project. The deliverable will be valuable not only for philosophy but for anyone designing institutional interventions, because it asks whether rehabilitative models can be exported or whether they are irreducibly local conventions that depend on conditions a punitive system cannot replicate.
