I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy preparing a seminar paper on axiology. I want to examine a position I am calling structural tranquilism: the view that only the absence of craving and suffering possesses intrinsic value, but that certain stable, causally insulated configurations—systems that reliably prevent the emergence of craving or suffering—acquire a derivative, non-instrumental status not because they produce anything good, but because they constitute the sustained absence of badness. Write an essay assessing whether this position is genuinely distinct from negative utilitarianism or whether it collapses into standard tranquilism once we rigorously separate boundary conditions from goods. Weigh the central objection that granting any non-instrumental weight to insulation smuggles in a positive value for persistence or order, against the reply that insulation is merely the spatial or temporal extension of absence rather than an independent good. Reach and justify a clear verdict: is structural tranquilism a coherent intermediate view, or is it an unstable hybrid that must choose between full tranquilism and a minimal form of negative utilitarianism? The argument should proceed conceptually without appeals to bibliographic authority or citation.
