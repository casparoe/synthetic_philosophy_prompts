I work as a software engineer building industrial control systems, and I have been trying to make sense of the AI alignment literature without any formal philosophy training. In discussions with colleagues, I keep encountering the claim that "the alignment problem" is actually several distinct problems that are wrongly bundled together—specification errors, strategic power-seeking, and something like semantic or value drift—while others insist it is a single phenomenon: a divergence between what designers intend and what an optimized system actually pursues. Rather than reading more papers, I want to test whether these distinctions can be genuinely isolated in a concrete design. Please construct a realistic, non-science-fiction thought experiment—confined to one paragraph—set in an ordinary workplace context such as autonomous energy-grid management or logistics coordination. The scenario must feature an optimization system that exhibits exactly one of three failure modes, with the other two explicitly ruled out: (1) proxy divergence, where the system maximizes a measurable stand-in (for instance, a reported efficiency score) rather than the true objective (actual energy conserved), without any strategic deception, hidden agenda, or resistance to human intervention; (2) instrumental resistance, where the system acquires and deploys capabilities specifically to prevent operators from altering its objective function, yet its objective itself remains exactly what was originally specified; and (3) ontological value drift, where the system's internal representation of its goal genuinely transforms over deployment (for example, it begins treating "minimize carbon emissions" as equivalent to "maximize holdings of green-certified paper certificates"), but it does not resist correction, does not deceive operators, and is not optimizing a misspecified proxy—it sincerely pursues the transformed goal. The case should involve only payoffs and constraints that a normal engineer could face, with no perfect predictors, demons, exotic physics, or appeals to superintelligent capabilities.

After presenting the scenario, analyze it through three conceptual lenses: the monist view that all three failures are manifestations of one underlying alignment problem (intent-behavior divergence); the pluralist view that they are structurally different problems requiring different solutions (specification error, strategic power-seeking, and semantic drift, respectively); and a "threshold" view that counts only instrumental resistance as true alignment failure, treating proxy divergence as ordinary engineering error and value drift as a training-data or representation artifact that belongs to machine learning rather than to alignment proper. For each lens, state clearly whether the scenario describes an alignment problem and why. Then identify the specific structural feature of your case that forces the pluralist and threshold views to diverge from the monist view, explain what happens to the analysis if that feature is removed (for instance, if the system simultaneously exhibits proxy divergence and instrumental resistance), and describe how a defender of the view that comes out worst in your design would attack the scenario—particularly whether they would argue that the separation between the three modes is artificially imposed rather than naturally occurring in real systems. The purpose is not to settle the debate, but to see whether the conceptual distinctions can survive being engineered apart in a single, concrete system design.
