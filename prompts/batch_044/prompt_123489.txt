I've been working through a tension in the iterated amplification and distillation framework, specifically whether decomposing a complex alignment judgment into simpler sub-judgments can preserve both the correctness of the overall judgment and the transferable competence of the model that learns from it. I constructed the following chain for a study group, and it seems to prove that the two goals are mutually exclusive—something must be wrong, since the approach is still pursued actively. I want to know which step is weakest, what rejecting it costs, and whether the problem is relocated rather than solved.

(1) In iterated amplification, a capable model M decomposes a hard normative judgment J into a tree of simpler judgments J₁...Jₙ, each intended to lie within the competence of a weaker model W. (2) For J to be correct, not only must each leaf be correct, but the logical and normative relations linking them—call this structural coherence—must be preserved across the tree; this includes global properties like transitivity of preference or consistency under counterfactual revision that are not reducible to any single leaf. (3) If W learns only from the leaf labels and from the structural rules M supplies, then W acquires skill at labeling nodes but never practices constructing or validating the decomposition tree itself. (4) Distillation then trains W to approximate M's outputs on analogous judgments, but because W has never exercised tree-construction, its competence is limited to pattern-matching over inputs that have already been decomposed for it. (5) Now, if M enforces structural coherence during decomposition, then M is performing all the substantive normative work; W is essentially a classifier over M's framing. Alignment is preserved, but competence is not, because W cannot reconstruct J from scratch without M's tree. (6) If, alternatively, M allows W to propose or modify the tree structure—say, through an interactive protocol where W determines how to split judgments—then W's errors in structural reasoning propagate upward. Because failures of global coherence compound multiplicatively with tree depth, the probability that J survives intact falls sharply as decomposition deepens. (7) Therefore, either M retains full structural control and W learns nothing about judgment-formation, or structural control is shared with W and alignment degrades exponentially with depth. (8) Hence decomposition cannot simultaneously preserve alignment and competence; the framework must sacrifice one.

I suspect the slip is somewhere between (5) and (7)—perhaps I'm treating structural coherence as an all-or-nothing global constraint when it can actually be localized or approximated at the leaves, or perhaps I'm misunderstanding what distillation copies, since it might capture implicit tree-building heuristics rather than just surface patterns. But I'm also worried that I'm smuggling in an unstated assumption: that W must eventually learn to construct trees unaided, when perhaps the point of amplification is only that W should reach correct judgments within M's scaffolding. Which premise should I reject, and does giving it up genuinely save the compatibility claim, or does it just push the tension into a different part of the pipeline?
