I have recently taken in a severely disabled rescue cat whose chronic pain requires constant management, and this experience has made Kant's scattered remarks on non-rational beings unexpectedly urgent. From the Metaphysics of Morals and the associated lecture notes, I understand that Kant denies direct duties to animals because they lack rational autonomy and therefore cannot be ends in themselves; yet he also condemns cruelty and insists that we avoid causing unnecessary suffering. The received explanation is the indirect-duty view: our obligations regarding animals are mediated entirely by our duties to rational beings, particularly the duty to preserve and cultivate our own moral feelings so that we do not become hardened toward our fellow human beings. But I am troubled by what seems like an unavoidable dilemma in this reading. If the wrongness of cruelty is fully explained by its psychological spillover to human relations—what I have seen called the spillover thesis—then Kant ought to be morally indifferent to suffering that occurs in complete isolation, such as painful procedures performed on animals in settings where no human observer is present to have her sympathies dulled. Conversely, if he condemns even such isolated suffering, then he appears to be treating the animal's pain itself as providing a reason for action, which amounts to granting animals a derivative moral status he explicitly rejects. Is there a coherent third interpretation—perhaps grounded not in empirical psychology but in Kant's doctrine of perfect duties to oneself, or in the idea that cruelty corrupts one's rational nature regardless of external effects—that allows him to condemn isolated cruelty without either smuggling in direct moral consideration or relying on a contingent generalization about human sentiment? Or does his position genuinely contain the tension I have described?
