I've been revisiting Lewis-style modal realism after a recent forum thread about whether possible worlds are "real" in any meaningful sense, and I keep noticing a particular argumentative oscillation that I can't quite place. A defender will invoke concrete, spatiotemporally isolated worlds to ground counterfactuals or modal knowledge—saying that what could have happened is literally what happens in some other universe—but then, when pressed on how we could ever know about these causally disconnected realms, or why we shouldn't treat them as an extravagant ontological multiplication, they'll retreat to describing worlds as "representational devices" or "theoretical posits" whose concreteness is just a heuristic. Once the objection is addressed, they return to treating worlds as genuinely existing entities that make modal statements literally true. This isn't exactly the motte-and-bailey, because both the concrete-ontology reading and the deflationary-representational reading are presented as serious positions rather than as a defensible core and an indefensible extension; and it isn't plain equivocation, since the speaker isn't merely shifting the meaning of "world" but actively toggling the ontological weight assigned to the same construct within a single defense. Is there an established name for this specific dialectical pattern—the strategic modulation of ontological commitment to a theoretical entity within a single argumentative arc—and if not, could it be defined precisely enough to distinguish it from both motte-and-bailey and from ordinary theoretical pluralism? I'd like the carving, not just a label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

To ground it in concrete instances: in one discussion, someone used Lewisian worlds to explain why "I could have become a musician" is true because a concrete world-mate does so, but when asked why we should believe in those worlds given our lack of causal access, they said the worlds are just "models of possibility" no more mysterious than mathematical structures. Once the epistemic worry was set aside, they went back to insisting that counterfactual dependence is literally causal dependence across worlds. In another case, a speaker argued that modal realism solves the problem of essential properties by distributing them across worlds, but then, when challenged on whether this requires quantifying over unobservable entities, they said the worlds are only "ways things could be" described concretely for convenience. The pattern seems to involve using ontological concreteness for explanatory work and representational abstraction for defensive work, with the toggle tied specifically to whether the current objection targets explanatory power or parsimony.

If there is no established term, I'd be satisfied with a coined concept that captures the structure: an argumentative strategy in which a single theoretical posited entity is assigned full ontological weight when it serves an explanatory or reductive function, and then assigned a lighter, representational, or heuristic weight when it serves to deflect an ontological or epistemic cost, with the assignment shifting within one continuous defense rather than between separate contexts. Please distinguish this sharply from related failure modes, and say whether you think it is genuinely distinct or collapses into one of them. Also, please state how confident you are in this characterization and single out the point you are least sure about—in particular, whether I have misidentified the pattern as a single phenomenon when it might actually be two separate argumentative moves that only look connected.
