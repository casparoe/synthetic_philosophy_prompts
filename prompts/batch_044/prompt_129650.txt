I'm presenting next Thursday in our philosophy of mind colloquium, and I'm defending a view that self-deception is structurally possible but only if we reject what I call the Cartesian spectator model—the assumption that belief-formation must be supervised by a single, unified subject that has transparent access to all its own mental contents. On my account, belief-formation is distributed across temporally indexed, functionally isolated cognitive modules that can maintain contradictory representations without central arbitration; I draw analogies from divided-attention research and from clinical descriptions of compartmentalized trauma responses, but I treat these as conceptual illustrations rather than empirical proofs, since my core claim is about the architecture of belief, not about pathology. The audience is about fifteen people: two Kant-influenced moral philosophers for whom self-transparency is a precondition of autonomous agency and moral responsibility; a cognitive psychologist who studies motivated reasoning and regards the paradox of self-deception as a pseudo-problem already dissolved by bias literature; a philosopher of psychiatry sympathetic to fragmentation models but concerned that they pathologize ordinary cases; and several mainstream philosophers of action broadly committed to Davidsonian rational unity, who are suspicious of divided-self metaphors that sound like literature rather than rigorous philosophy. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis itself. Give me the objections I will actually face, ordered from the most damaging to the least, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would actually use—the Kantian worry about moral agency, the psychologist's charge of conceptual redundancy, and the Davidsonians' charge of metaphorical obscurantism. For each, provide a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, a frank note on where that reply is weakest, and the specific concession I should make up front rather than defend later. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks: how modular beliefs can still count as mine in a way that sustains moral responsibility without collapsing back into the unitary-self model I just rejected, and suggest how I should respond if it arises, specifying what to concede immediately and what I must hold at all costs.
