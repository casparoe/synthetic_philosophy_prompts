Below is an exam prompt and a student response for a course on early American moral philosophy. The question asks students to interpret Benjamin Franklin's "project for moral perfection" in his Autobiography—specifically his list of thirteen virtues (Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and Humility), his weekly tracking ledger, and his admission that he never achieved perfection, especially with Order. The student should consider whether the scheme is best understood as virtue-ethical habituation, a rationalist technology of self-formation, or a utilitarian optimization of reputation and happiness, with special attention to Franklin's gloss of Humility as imitating Jesus and Socrates.

Student answer:

Franklin's project is best read as a virtue-ethical practice of habituation. By selecting thirteen virtues and tracking them in a weekly ledger, he treats moral improvement as repeated practice rather than rule-following. The virtue of Humility, which Franklin glosses as imitating Jesus and Socrates, confirms this: it requires the agent to recognize his own imperfections, a posture necessary for genuine habituation. Franklin's Enlightenment rationalism modifies the Aristotelian framework only in method, not in aim; he uses reason to systematize virtue, but the end remains the formation of character.

That said, Franklin's admission that he never reached perfection, and that Order in particular remained intractable, actually weakens the virtue-ethical interpretation. In a proper virtue ethics, sustained practice should eventually produce a stable disposition; Franklin's persistent failure with Order suggests that his method is closer to a utilitarian calculus in which he weighs the personal and social benefits of each virtue and quietly abandons those with low returns. The ledger supports this instrumental reading: he records violations like an accountant tracking losses, measuring cost rather than shaping soul. Even Humility serves an instrumental function—Franklin includes it because he recognizes that displaying modesty improves his reputation among others, which is a classic optimization of social utility. Thus, despite the virtue-language, the project is fundamentally a technology of rational self-interest.
