I direct a small, self-funded research collective in which I also apply for internal grants. To avoid conflicts of interest, I constructed an anonymized review protocol: I assign random identifiers to every proposal—including my own—before passing them to a junior colleague who evaluates them blindly. Funding is then allocated strictly according to the resulting scores. Over the past year, I have observed that my submissions consistently fall near the funding cutoff, and I have gradually revised the scoring rubric in directions that are individually defensible—tightening criteria in areas where my projects are weak and relaxing them where they are strong—without ever consciously deciding to privilege myself. When I reflect on my motives, I do not discover an explicit belief that my work is superior; instead, any comparative judgment seems to be diffused across the rules and delegated roles I have instituted. The experience is not that of telling myself a lie, but of having built a structured opacity: the mechanism that should reveal bias is itself arranged so that the comparison never reaches me in a form that requires acknowledgment. I want to understand whether this can still be described as self-deception, given that no single mental state seems to harbor the false content—the misleading structure resides in the architecture of the procedure rather than in a hidden belief. Does a standard philosophical account of self-deception require a partitioned mind in which one part believes and another suppresses, or can deception operate entirely at the level of institutional design? Furthermore, if responsibility for self-deception normally attaches to the failure to correct a false belief one harbors, what form does responsibility take when the error is embedded in a system one created and continues to administer?
