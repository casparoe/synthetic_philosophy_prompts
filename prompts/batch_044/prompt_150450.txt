I'm working through the moral logic of conditional welfare for a course on social policy, and I've constructed an argument that seems to justify far more state intrusion than defenders of workfare intend, without my being able to locate the exact leak. Here it is, step by step. (1) The standard normative defense of workfare holds that unconditional transfers violate reciprocity: those who can labor but do not should not receive the same support as those who genuinely cannot work. (2) To prevent moral desert from collapsing into macroeconomic luck, "can labor" must be interpreted as a capacity claim—possessing the physical and cognitive ability to perform some task that exists—rather than as a market claim about actually securing employment. (3) So anyone with such capacity who receives unconditional support is free-riding on the cooperative scheme sustained by those who do labor. (4) Apply the same reciprocity principle consistently: anyone holding capital that could be deployed to generate taxable employment but is instead kept in low-risk, low-productivity instruments is also withholding a form of contribution that they have the capacity to make; by (2) and (3), they too are free-riding on the legal and infrastructural framework that secures their holdings. (5) Therefore either the reciprocity principle justifies conditioning not only welfare but also investment allocations, inheritance access, and consumption patterns on continuous state-verified productivity metrics; or there is a hidden asymmetry that restricts (3) to labor markets alone. (6) Any such asymmetry looks ad hoc, since labor capacity and capital capacity both depend on socially constructed institutions, so privileging one over the other requires a justification that does not already presuppose the workfare conclusion. (7) The universal extension in (5) is plainly illiberal and administratively impossible; hence (2) must be rejected, and "can labor" must be interpreted as market-relative after all. But (8) if desert is market-relative, then structural unemployment eliminates the very distinction between deserving and undeserving poor that workfare was designed to enforce, which collapses the argument back into unconditional support. I want to keep both that reciprocity carries genuine moral weight and that not all dependency is undeserved, yet I cannot see how to contain the reasoning without either an arbitrary boundary or outright self-defeat. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me?
