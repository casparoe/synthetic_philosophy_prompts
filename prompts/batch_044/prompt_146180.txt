I am working through what makes consent valid, with particular attention to how deception, coercion, and competence interact, and I want to test my intuitive verdicts rather than reconstruct a literature. Here are my judgments on eight concrete cases, followed by what I need from an analysis.

First, a fully literate patient consents to surgery after an honest statistical disclosure of risks; this seems clearly valid. Second, a researcher describes a study as involving only “mild discomfort” while deliberately omitting a documented risk of severe anxiety that is central to the protocol; I judge this invalid, because the deception concerns the very harm to which consent is given. Third, a tenant signs a lease after the landlord falsely claims another applicant will take the unit within hours, a claim fabricated solely to pressure agreement; I take this as invalid, though I am unsure whether the defect is coercion proper or the deception that manufactures urgency. Fourth, a patient with diagnosed mild cognitive impairment consents to an elective procedure after demonstrating accurate recall and reasoning tailored to that specific decision; despite the diagnosis, I consider the consent valid. Fifth, a graduate student agrees to an unpaid, open-ended research commitment after being told that “this level of dedication is the norm in the field,” with no explicit threat but with obvious implicit career consequences; here my judgment wavers between treating it as valid institutional consent and treating it as structurally coerced. Sixth, an individual agrees to biometric data collection after being presented with technically accurate terms of service in dense legal language they do not command; comprehension is practically impossible, yet nothing is misrepresented. I am inclined to call this invalid, but it feels structurally close to the first case, with linguistic competence substituting for medical literacy. Seventh, a person with severe but stable anxiety consents to exposure therapy requiring direct confrontation of a phobia; they understand the protocol precisely, though their anxiety systematically distorts risk assessment. I take this as valid, since the distortion does not eliminate autonomous choice. Eighth, and this is the case I keep returning to: a parent gives consent on behalf of an adolescent child for an irreversible cosmetic procedure that the child requests but that the parent suspects the child will deeply regret upon reaching adulthood. The parent understands all risks, faces no deception or coercion, and acts in good faith; yet the consent seems to bind a future self whose values may differ radically. I feel this should be invalid, but I cannot fix whether the defect lies in the parent’s authority to bind another, in the irreversibility of the act, or in the child’s present incompetence to authorize such a proleptic commitment.

I have tentatively wondered whether the distinction between transactional consent—to a discrete, bounded act—and proleptic consent—binding a future self across a change in values or capacities—can organize these cases. But I am not committed to it.

What I want is a precise principle, or a small competing family, that captures these verdicts. For each candidate, please specify: (i) the clause governing deception and the case that motivates it; (ii) the clause governing coercion and structural pressure, with its motivating case; (iii) the clause governing competence, stating clearly whether it is decision-relative or global, and with its motivating case; (iv) its verdict on the graduate-student case and on the biometric-data case, which I have left unresolved; (v) its verdict on the proleptic-consent case; and (vi) an honest statement of which of my eight verdicts the principle must overturn to maintain coherence. If no single principle accommodates all eight without excessive complexity, present the leading candidates, state the crux that divides them, and indicate what further case or distinction would adjudicate between them. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need the reasoning, not the literature map.
