Please comment on the following notes for a short essay on vagueness and the sorites paradox.

The essay argues that the sorites paradox is best understood not as revealing hidden sharp boundaries (the epistemicist response) nor as justifying a rejection of classical bivalence (the supervaluationist response), but as exposing a structural mismatch between continuous physical change and discrete categorical labeling. Specifically, it claims that the tolerance principle—if n grains make a heap, then n-1 grains make a heap—fails not because there is an imperceptible cutoff, but because "heap" functions as a comparative, context-sensitive grading term rather than an absolute predicate. To support this, the essay draws on a distinction between "gradual properties" (like temperature) and "threshold-governed classifications" (like legal adulthood), proposing that sorites-sensitive predicates belong to a hybrid category: they have continuous underlying dimensions but are deployed with socially negotiated "zones of application" that are deliberately left fuzzy. The essay then considers an objection: if zones of application are socially negotiated, does vagueness collapse into a pragmatic contextualism? It responds by distinguishing "negotiated application zones" from speaker-relative context, arguing that the former are stable, intersubjective conventions that still lack precise borders, thereby preserving ontic indeterminacy without invoking mysterious metaphysical vagueness. It concludes that recognizing this hybrid status lets us accept classical logic for the underlying dimensions while treating the categorical predicate itself as a useful fiction with intentionally porous boundaries—avoiding both the counterintuitive sharp lines of epistemicism and the logical revisions required by supervaluationism.

Please evaluate: (1) whether this framing genuinely differs from standard contextualist or degree-theoretic accounts, or whether it collapses into one of them; (2) whether the distinction between gradual properties and threshold-governed classifications holds up under scrutiny, particularly for cases like "baldness" where there is no obvious social negotiation; and (3) whether the conclusion—that categorical predicates are useful fictions with intentionally porous boundaries—is philosophically substantive or merely relabels the problem.
