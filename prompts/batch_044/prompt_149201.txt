Consider Avicenna's flying-man (al-insan al-ta'ir) thought experiment. Some interpreters read it as demonstrating that the soul enjoys an immediate, non-relational presence (hudur) to itself that requires no bodily mediation whatsoever, while others argue it only shows self-awareness is independent of external sensation and may still involve an internal common sense (al-hiss al-mushtarak). Explain the experiment and evaluate whether it succeeds in establishing the soul's self-awareness on the stronger, non-relational reading.

A student submitted the following answer:

The flying man is suspended in a void, blindfolded and with limbs separated, so that no tactile, visual, or auditory data reach him. Avicenna asks whether such a person would still affirm that he exists. He answers yes: the suspended subject knows he is, even though he knows nothing about his body or the world. This shows that self-awareness is not mediated by any bodily organ, including the internal common sense. Consequently, the soul is immediately present to itself in a direct, non-relational way—what is called hudur—without any representational or sensory intermediary. Because this awareness persists even when every empirical faculty is disabled, Avicenna can conclude that the soul is an immaterial substance that is essentially self-conscious and separable from the body in the manner of a Cartesian ego. The argument succeeds on the stronger reading, since if an internal sense were still operating, the flying man would need some bodily basis for that faculty, which the conditions of suspension explicitly deny.
