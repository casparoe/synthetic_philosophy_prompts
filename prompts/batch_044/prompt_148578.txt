I've been preparing material for a small rationalist discussion group on the doing/allowing distinction, and I want to move past standard hospital-triage tropes that feel like trolley problems in clinical clothing. Could you construct a single-paragraph, realistic scenario—no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no demons—set in a workplace or hospital, involving a scarce life-saving resource (for example, a single dialysis slot or a limited antidote batch) where an institutional gatekeeper must decide between actively redirecting the resource from Patient A to Patient B and passively allowing the current allocation to stand?

Please engineer the case so that three specific theoretical positions generate clearly different verdicts, with the reversal falling out of the theories rather than being stipulated by malicious intent or an obviously defective rule. First, a strict deontological view that holds killing intrinsically worse than letting die regardless of outcome or institutional framing: this should condemn the active redirection as impermissible and permit the passive course. Second, a pure consequentialist view that denies any intrinsic moral relevance to the causal mechanism: since the outcome is one life saved either way—assume equal prognosis and no side effects—this view should treat both options as morally equivalent, selecting on non-moral grounds such as a fair lottery, and should reject the idea that physical pathway changes the moral calculus. Third, inject a niche intermediate position I'll call "threshold contractualism about institutional omission": the gatekeeper occupies a designated triage role, and the current allocation was produced by an automated or non-authoritative protocol rather than by a legitimate prior decision. On this view, actively correcting the misallocation fulfills her role-based duty and is permissible, whereas passively allowing the erroneous default to persist crosses an institutional complicity threshold—making her omission structurally equivalent to an abdication—so the passive course is impermissible. The reversal—active permitted, passive condemned—must emerge from the role-structure and authority conditions, not from a bad rule adopted for the wrong reasons.

After presenting the scenario, walk through what each view says and why, identify the precise feature (the combination of a designated gatekeeper and a non-authoritative default) that does the work, and explain honestly what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed—for instance, if the initial allocation was made by a legitimate, fully informed prior decision. Also include a brief note on how a defender of the strict deontological view—which comes out badly here—would likely attack the case, probably by insisting that institutional framing cannot dissolve the physical-causal distinction between acting and refraining. Throughout, please separate what is settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested among people who understand the topic, and do not present the invented "threshold contractualism" framing, or any other contested interpretation, as the consensus view. This is for pedagogical use with people who think in rationalist-style terms but are not necessarily immersed in the academic literature, so being explicit about where the case design loads the dice is more important than being exhaustive.
