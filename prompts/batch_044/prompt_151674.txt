In museums and official memorial boards across several post-conflict societies, a curatorial protocol has become common: when archival records conflict with the oral testimony or collective memory of a local community, the institution is expected to present both accounts without ranking them, and in some frameworks to grant the community final editorial authority over the public framing, even when professional historians judge the documentary record more reliable. Defenders often claim this is not mere politics but epistemology—that lived experience carries weight documents cannot capture, or that a group's self-understanding partly constitutes the historical fact in question. What philosophical commitments about evidence, collective identity, and the purpose of public history does that practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it depend on a special epistemic status for testimony, a non-documentary theory of historical knowledge, or a normative claim about group authority over narrative? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, say whether these commitments are mutually consistent, separate what is broadly accepted about the role of testimony from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and historians, and describe what museum and memorial practice would have to change if its underlying framework were replaced by one that treats archival accuracy as always decisive.
