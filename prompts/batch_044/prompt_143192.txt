I have been trying to understand why biologists still seek a single definition of life when the borderline cases seem to generate direct contradictions. The NASA working definition requires a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution, yet synthetic protocells can meet both conditions using entirely abiotic scaffolding without nucleic acids, whereas viroids and prions replicate and evolve within hosts but possess no autonomous metabolism. Since vitalism has been rejected, there is apparently no underlying essence that could resolve these conflicts; the category seems to depend on which criterion one chooses to privilege. In ordinary language, this might just be vagueness, but the conflict appears stronger: under the metabolic criterion a viroid is not alive, under the evolutionary criterion it is, and there is no deeper fact of the matter to break the tie. That suggests the concept is not merely fuzzy but genuinely dialetheic — accommodating true contradictions rather than lacking sharp boundaries. If this is correct, should biologists abandon the expectation of a unified, consistent definition and instead accept that 'life' functions as a paraconsistent concept? Or is there an error in assuming that the absence of vitalism forces us into logical contradiction rather than into a family-resemblance or pluralist account?
