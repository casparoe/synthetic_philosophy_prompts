Exam question: Some philosophers draw a line between guilt (autogenic—tied to internal norms, repaired through private restitution) and shame (allocentric—needs a social mirror, repaired through reintegration). Others say this collapses cross-culturally. Then there's the hypothetical case of the Vorn, a society that institutionalizes shame-first education: kids learn to feel shame about being the kind of person who'd do wrong, not just about the act, and the culture offers public rituals of reintegration instead of private penance. Critics say shame targets the self so it's destructive; defenders say it builds cohesion.

Question parts:
(1) Does the autogenic/allocentric distinction hold up in cross-cultural analysis?
(2) Does the Vorn design avoid the classic objection that shame is destructive to the self?
(3) Can a culture genuinely choose its dominant moral emotion, or is emotional ecology too path-dependent to be engineered?

Student answer (typed up):

The autogenic/allocentric split is pretty solid. Guilt is internal, you feel bad even if nobody knows, like when you cheat on a test alone in your room. Shame needs an audience, even if its imaginary—like feeling exposed. So cross-culturally, guilt should be more private and shame more public. In collectivist cultures people talk about losing face, which is just shame with a different name, but the structure is the same: there's a social mirror.

For the Vorn, they teach shame about character, not acts. Critics say this is destructive because if you think I'm bad instead of I did bad, you can't fix it. But the Vorn gets around this by having public rituals where the community reintegrates you. That means the shame is repaired socially, which is actually how shame works anyway—so they're not avoiding the objection, they're just using shame's natural repair mechanism. Wait, actually they do avoid it, because the ritual shows you're not permanently bad, just that you need to realign with the group. So the self-targeting isn't destructive if there's a path back.

On whether culture can choose: emotional ecology is path-dependent, meaning once you're on a guilt-track it's hard to switch because institutions reinforce it. The Vorn shows it can be done if you redesign education and ritual together. But I think it's not really a free choice—it's more like engineering within constraints. So yes and no: you can't just flip a switch, but you can steer over generations.
