Here is the student answer: The heterogeneity problem arises because pleasures seem too diverse to share one quality. Eating chocolate feels different from finishing a proof, so they cannot both contain the same pleasantness sensation. This threatens hedonism, which says pleasure is the only good. Sidgwick tried to solve this by saying all pleasures have a favorable consciousness—a tone of agreeableness that accompanies any content. But this still sounds like a specific feeling, just an abstract one. Ryle objected that pleasure is not a feeling at all but a disposition to seek repetition; he also argued that pleasures are related only by family resemblance, like Wittgenstein’s games, with no common feature. This pluralist view handles diversity well. Mixed pleasures, like enjoying a sad movie, show that pleasure and pain can occur together. If they can coexist in one state, then pleasure cannot be defined by opposition to pain, and the hedonist must admit there is no single common quality—only overlapping similarities. So the family-resemblance view is better.
