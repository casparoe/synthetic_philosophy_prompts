Read Sophocles's Oedipus Rex through the lens of inside-view versus outside-view reasoning and the demand for epistemic modesty. The audience possesses an outside view: they know the prophecy, the parricide, and the incest before Oedipus does. Oedipus, by contrast, reasons entirely from an inside view—his own intentions, his evidence, and his self-image as the savior who solved the Sphinx's riddle. Ask whether the play is not merely a tragedy of fate but specifically a tragedy of failed epistemic modesty: Oedipus treats his inside view as authoritative and dismisses counter-evidence (Tiresias's accusation, Jocasta's attempt to stop the investigation) because accepting it would require admitting that his reasoning is structurally blind to causal chains he cannot observe. To push the reading further, treat the chorus not as neutral commentators but as holders of an intermediate epistemic position—partially informed, yet reasoning from within Theban norms and loyalty—so that the play stages three levels of knowledge rather than a simple binary. Does Sophocles suggest that true epistemic modesty requires recognizing not just that one's beliefs are fallible, but that one's inside view is systematically blind to the "outside" facts of biology and fate that determine outcomes? I want a reading that takes both the dramatic irony of the text and the philosophical literature on forecasting and self-assessment seriously, that asks where the metaphor of physical blindness maps precisely onto epistemic humility and where it misleads, and that ends by saying whether the tragedy adds a critique of rationalist self-confidence that formal arguments about inside versus outside view tend to miss.
