As a journalist covering the intersection of aesthetics and public life, I am preparing a long-form feature on W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay Criteria of Negro Art, in which he argued that Black artists should treat beauty as a vehicle for racial justice rather than pursue art for art's sake. Rather than rehearse the standard biography of that claim, I want to treat it as a living philosophical thesis and ask which open questions or practical implications should actually command sustained attention now.

Specifically, I see three competing lines of work that could occupy a researcher or a small cultural funder for roughly three years:

First, there is an interpretive problem about the target of Du Bois's propaganda thesis: is it primarily about representational content—what images and narratives circulate—or about formal structure—how rhythm, color, and compositional choice themselves carry political meaning? If the thesis targets form rather than content, then the standard liberal critiques about censorship and artistic freedom may miss the point entirely.

Second, there is the institutional question: if we accept that all art propagandizes in some sense, how should that reshape the design of public funding bodies, museum acquisition committees, or algorithmic recommendation systems that now determine which works reach an audience? This line requires conceptual work but also engagement with governance structures.

Third, there is a comparative philosophical tension: Du Bois's instrumental view sits uneasily with later theories of aesthetic autonomy—particularly the idea, associated with Theodor Adorno, that art resists instrumentalization precisely through its formal independence. Is Du Bois's propaganda thesis compatible with a genuinely resistant art, or does it collapse into pure instrumentalism?
