I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether any belief can be genuinely self-certifying. She insisted that allowing even one "easy" knowledge claim lets you bootstrap your way to knowing everything, which proves we need foundational beliefs. I want to write an essay pushing back, but from an angle I have not seen discussed: treating bootstrapping not as an individual epistemic failure but as a structural feature of distributed verification networks—specifically, open-source software supply chains, where components are validated partly by tools that themselves depend on those components. I want to argue that what looks like vicious circularity at the individual level becomes non-vicious coherence at the system level, and that the "too easy" objection confuses local dependency with global justification. Before I commit to the full draft, I would appreciate comments on whether this framing misrepresents the original bootstrapping problem by displacing the individual knower, whether the software analogy is philosophically illuminating or a category error, and whether my claim that these networks lack a privileged foundational layer concedes too much to coherentism. I am particularly unsure about the last point: does abandoning a foundation abandon the internalist intuition that drives the puzzle?
