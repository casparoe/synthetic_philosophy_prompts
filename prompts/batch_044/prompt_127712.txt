I am working through an independent study of Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, and I have become stuck on the precise logical function of the catuṣkoṭi in his argument against svabhāva. The reasoning runs roughly as follows: if entities possessed an intrinsic nature, they could not be produced by conditions; but since all phenomena arise dependently through pratītyasamutpāda, none can possess such a nature. Consequently, inherent existence is negated through all four corners—entities are neither existent with svabhāva, nor absolutely non-existent, nor both, nor neither.

My difficulty concerns the reflexive application of this pattern. If the relation of dependence itself lacks svabhāva—since it too is dependently arisen—then the very ground of the argument seems to dissolve into the same emptiness. Moreover, if emptiness (śūnyatā) is defined as the absence of svabhāva, and emptiness itself lacks svabhāva (the doctrine sometimes called śūnyatāśūnyatā), then emptiness appears to be just another conventional phenomenon rather than an ultimate metaphysical insight. This leads me to wonder whether the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth is itself subjected to the tetralemma, or whether it enjoys a special logical status that blocks regress. I have considered whether the framework resembles a stratified semantics—where conventional discourse operates with one set of commitments and ultimate discourse operates with none—but I cannot see whether this stratification is itself a conventional construct or something more.

Could you help me understand the passage's reasoning step by step? In particular, I want to see exactly how the argument avoids self-refutation when applied to its own premises, and whether the conventional/ultimate distinction survives only as a pragmatic, soteriological device rather than as a metaphysical thesis. Please avoid simply saying that the point lies beyond rational analysis; I am trying to grasp the internal logical architecture that allows the negation to turn back upon itself without collapsing into contradiction.
