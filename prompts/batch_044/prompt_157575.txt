I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about corruption, going by my gut on cases. A mayor in Brazil hires his fully qualified cousin for a city job, no open search at all — that feels corrupt. In Japan a contractor sends expensive seasonal gifts to the bureaucrat who just approved their bid; it's expected, documented, kind of recipricol. That doesn't feel corrupt to me, at least not in the same bucket. A clerk in Lagos asks for dash to stamp your passport faster — corrupt. A US senator's kid gets a board seat at a foreign energy firm right after dad pushes policy helping them; no explicit deal, but it stinks. Corrupt. But in some Pacific island societies, distributing disaster aid first to your own clan is seen as kin obligation, not theft — and I want to say that's not corruption either. So what's separating the clan case from the cousin hire? Both are kin-based preference in resource allocation. Is there one principle that saves all five verdicts, or am I just sneaking in Western legal norms? Which verdict should I drop if I want simplicity? And what does the winning principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a Western NGO paying facilitation fees to local customs officials so medical supplies don't sit in a warehouse, where locals say it's just the price of doing business?
