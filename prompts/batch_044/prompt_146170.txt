In preparing materials for my graduate seminar on philosophical theology and modal metaphysics, I have drafted a short argumentative essay that I intend to present next week, and I would appreciate your critical commentary on its structure and philosophical coherence. I have been discussing these issues with colleagues in our department, particularly the tension between divine omniscience and libertarian freedom, and I find myself dissatisfied with the standard framing of the grounding objection against Molinism; rather than rehearsing familiar defenses, I want to propose a more granular taxonomy of counterfactual knowledge that may shift where the metaphysical burden lies.

The essay argues that Molinist accounts of middle knowledge typically presuppose what I call the "uniform accessibility thesis"—the claim that if God knows a counterfactual of creaturely freedom in one possible world, then that same counterfactual is epistemically available to God across all worlds in which the relevant creature exists. Against this, I introduce a distinction between "core counterfactuals," which remain stable within a defined modal neighborhood around the actual world—where initial conditions and natural laws remain within a certain threshold of divergence—and "peripheral counterfactuals," which concern worlds so distant in modal space that the creature's intrinsic dispositions no longer determinately ground a specific choice. On this view, God possesses middle knowledge of core counterfactuals in a robust, world-independent manner, but peripheral counterfactuals are not false; rather, they are epistemically indeterminate for God, not because of any cognitive limitation, but because the metaphysical conditions required to ground a specific counterfactual dissolve once modal perturbation exceeds the stability threshold. I suggest that this allows the Molinist to concede that there are worlds in which God cannot precisely foresee a particular free choice without thereby surrendering providential control over the actual world or collapsing into open theism, since providence requires only knowledge of core counterfactuals within the relevant modal range. I also briefly explore whether this "threshold of modal perturbation" has a structural analogue in the treatment of distant logical truths—where reasoning becomes initially indeterminate and converges only as one approaches a well-defined domain—though I do not develop that comparison into a full technical account.

I am particularly interested in your assessment of whether this distinction genuinely addresses the grounding objection or merely relocates it, whether the notion of a "modal neighborhood" can be made precise without circular reliance on the very counterfactuals it is meant to organize, and whether the analogy to epistemic indeterminacy in distant modal space is philosophically illuminating or merely metaphorical. Please do not provide a bibliography or citation list; I am seeking conceptual critique and suggestions for sharpening the argument's internal consistency. If you notice any mischaracterizations of standard Molinist commitments—especially regarding the relationship between middle knowledge and natural knowledge—or if you see a more elegant way to frame the stability threshold without invoking unnecessary metaphysical machinery, I would welcome those observations as well.
