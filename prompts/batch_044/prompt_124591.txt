I've been trying to make sense of a tension in how institutions treat past investment. Adaptive clinical-trial protocols and large-scale capital-project gate reviews both embed explicit decision rules that treat prior expenditure as normatively irrelevant—evaluators are instructed to weigh only future costs and benefits, and honoring sunk costs is classified as a cognitive bias to be engineered away. Yet the same institutions structurally reward persistence: a trial that has already enrolled hundreds of subjects faces strong organizational pressure to reach its endpoint even when conditional power is near zero, and a capital project that has consumed years of engineering labor survives a gate review far more easily than a greenfield proposal with an identical net present value. What philosophical conception of practical rationality does the formal stopping-rule framework presuppose—does it require that only future-oriented states carry normative weight, or is it compatible with views on which past commitments generate present obligations? Conversely, what view of value or agency does the persistence-rewarding structure presuppose—perhaps that rationality includes maintaining diachronic or narrative coherence, that prior effort can partly constitute present value, or that abandoning a course of action can wrong the prior self who initiated it? Are these two institutional logics genuinely in tension, or can a unified account—say, one that distinguishes individual from collective rationality, or first-person from third-person evaluation—reconcile them? And if we took the persistence logic seriously rather than treating it purely as a failure mode, what concrete changes would have to follow in how we write futility-stopping rules, allocate scientific credit, or design review-board incentives?
