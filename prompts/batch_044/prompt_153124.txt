I am delivering the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Vasquez at her university dinner next month. She spent thirty-five years studying escalation of commitment; her classroom demonstration of the dollar auction was legendary, and her research on sunk-cost dynamics in organizational behavior is well known to specialists. The audience is split: roughly half are behavioral economists, organizational theorists, and former doctoral students who understand the formal structure of the auction; the other half are family, university administrators, and colleagues from other fields who do not.

Write a five-minute toast that uses the logic of the dollar auction as its actual structural frame, not as decorative color. The idea should constrain what I can honestly say: I must praise her lifelong persistence and her refusal to abandon hard problems, yet I must avoid praising the very pathology she analyzed — throwing good resources after bad. The specialists should hear that I am navigating the precise distinction between rational persistence and the self-defeating escalation trap; the non-specialists should grasp the emotional and moral point without encountering unexplained jargon.

Do not cite specific papers, list publications, or provide a bibliography. Do not ask the audience to recall particular studies. Instead, mirror the auction's dynamics — the point at which bidding one more dollar feels locally reasonable but is globally destructive — against the arc of her career. The closing line should land for both halves of the room simultaneously. Keep the tone warm, affectionate, and intellectually honest; do not resolve the tension with false comfort.
