I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of computation and epistemology in earnest. Next month I am leading a single ninety-minute session for a discussion group of fellow retired scientists—mostly engineers and biologists, with one mathematician—none of whom have formal philosophical training but all of whom are comfortable with calculus, basic logic, and the idea of an algorithm. I want them to leave with one precise idea: that asymptotic worst-case complexity is the wrong conceptual tool for philosophical arguments about what is knowable or doable “in principle,” and that the gap between “computable” and “feasibly computable” specifically undermines classical thought experiments such as Laplace’s demon or perfect predictive verificationism.

Please do not explain the concept to me. Instead, draft the teaching plan for that session. I need the following, in this order:

1. The dependency-ordered explanatory arc: which conceptual component must come first, what it makes possible, and the precise point at which to introduce the distinction between worst-case asymptotic growth and physical-time feasibility. Show how each step depends on the previous one.

2. The carrying example: identify one concrete case—algorithmic, physical, or conceptual—that should carry the entire session. Specify exactly what properties it must possess (for instance, a formal worst-case result that sounds catastrophic paired with routine practical success) and what it must lack (for instance, dependence on quantum mechanics or on an unsolved conjecture). If you propose the simplex method, the traveling salesman problem, or another familiar case, justify that choice against the session’s philosophical goal; if you reject them, propose an alternative and defend it.

3. The misconception map: list the misconceptions this audience will likely import from their scientific training—such as conflating exponential scaling with physical impossibility, or assuming that “in principle computability” licenses practical predictions—and specify the exact moment in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off.

4. A single end-of-session check for understanding: design one question or brief task that reveals whether they have grasped the philosophical point (the in-principle versus in-practice distinction as applied to knowledge claims) rather than merely recalling Big-O notation or an algorithm’s name.

5. What must be left out: explicitly state which related topics—formal models of computation, specific open problems such as P versus NP, quantum speedups, or the Cobham-Edmonds thesis—should be omitted at this level, and why each omission protects the session’s focus.

6. One niche injection: incorporate a specific, non-obvious point about average-case versus worst-case analysis, or about oracle-relative computation, that prevents the session from being a generic “complexity for philosophers” overview. The point should be accurate enough that a specialist in computational complexity would recognize it, yet your plan must show exactly how to present it without invoking formal machinery beyond what this audience possesses.

Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a retired scientist addressing an educated peer group. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
