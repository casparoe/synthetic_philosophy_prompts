I am currently taking a graduate seminar on personal identity and clinical ethics, and I have been asked to deliver the closing remarks at our symposium's dinner tomorrow evening. The audience is split between philosophers who work on diachronic identity and hospice clinicians and social workers who encounter these situations daily but have no background in metaphysics. Our discussions have centered on whether an advance directive signed by a competent earlier self retains binding authority over a later self with severe dementia who nevertheless appears to experience sustained well-being, particularly in cases where the later self retains what clinicians sometimes call vocational presence—recognizing sheet music, responding to ritual, or engaging in familiar craft—even though narrative memory and formal decision-making capacity are largely lost. I need these remarks to treat that tension as structurally decisive rather than decorative: the conflict between honoring the document and honoring the present experiential self should constrain what I am permitted to say. Specifically, I must not resolve the matter by a simple appeal to either precedent autonomy or present welfare, and I need to address the possibility that the demented state harbors a form of practical rationality—directed at immediate, embodied engagement—that neither preference-satisfaction nor narrative-continuity theories fully capture, which complicates the claim that the earlier self was uniquely authoritative about what this life should become. At the same time, the speech must remain fully intelligible to the clinicians without philosophical condescension, and it must avoid offering false comfort or a tidy conclusion, since our symposium has deliberately not reached consensus. Please write a continuous prose address of approximately four minutes—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, formatted as a single flowing spoken piece or letter—that navigates this specific problem, respects both halves of the room, and ends with an open question rather than a prescription.
