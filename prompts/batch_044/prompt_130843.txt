So I was arguing with two friends from my alignment reading group — we meet every other tuesday, mostly to complain about papers we didn't finish — and they got stuck on whether the alignment tax is actually structural or just a measuremnt error. I want an outsider's take because I honestly can't tell who's dodging.

Friend 1 (call him J) said the competitiveness constraint is basically unavoidable: if you're training a frontier model and apply RLHF or some other safety filter, you're literally pulling the optimizer away from the pure predictive-loss minimum. He said even a 2-3% drop in benchmark scores means death in a competitive open-weights market, and history shows safety regulations always get outcompeted unless they're universal. So the tax isn't just real, it's fatal if you don't have monopoly power.

Friend 2 (M) pushed back that this assumes the "pure" optimum is both reachable and actually good. She said unconstrained models overfit to spurious correlations in the training distribtion, and alignment techniques often work like regularization — they force the model into a smoother region of the loss landscape that generalizes better out of distribtion. So in many real evaluations the "tax" is actually negative; you're measuring against the wrong baseline. She also said comparing RLHF'd models to base models is unfair because the base model hasn't seen the same post-training data budget.

Then J came back with this more geometric framing he picked up somewhere — he said the tax isn't about loss at all, it's about representational capacity. Alignment forces representations to stay human-legible, which collapses high-dimensional manifolds that carry task-relevant information. So even if your loss looks fine, you've destroyed internal structure that'd be useful for weird out-of-distribution coding problems. He thinks this explains why RLHF'd models choke on novel algorithmic tasks.

M said that's smuggling in an unproven claim about legibility requiring collapse. She pointed out that sparse autoencoder work shows you can have interpretable features without destroying dimensionality — you just need more compute to maintain both. So the "geometric tax" is really just a compute tax, and compute gets cheaper every year. Therefore the constraint isn't a fundamental law of competition; it's an engineering budget problem that Moore-adjacent progress solves.

They went back and forth a bit more but those are the four main moves. Can you go through them step by step — rate each move from clearly wrong to clearly right, and say who's missing what? I don't need a final winner, just an honest adjudication of where each argument holds up and where it cheats.
