I'm preparing discussion notes for a graduate seminar on self-cultivation and virtue ethics, and I've become stuck on an internal tension in Benjamin Franklin's well-known project of moral perfection. In his autobiographical account, Franklin describes tracking thirteen virtues through a weekly ledger, marking black spots for each lapse so that the record resembles a quantitative account book—moral progress measured by the progressive reduction of debits. Yet in the same body of reflections he compares enduring moral faults to the errata of a printed book: errors that, once the impression is fixed, can never be fully corrected, only acknowledged as permanent features of the volume. These two images—the clearable ledger and the irreparable erratum—seem to presuppose very different ontologies of character and improvement.

What philosophical commitments about moral agency, progress, and the nature of virtue must be in place for Franklin to maintain both practices simultaneously? Does the ledger implicitly commit him to a behavioral-accounting model in which failures are discrete, countable events amenable to elimination through disciplined repetition, while the errata analogy requires a view of character as partly sedimented and historically fixed? Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral self-cultivation that holds the two together, carefully distinguishing what the combined practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it. Is the framework coherent, or does it quietly rule out rival conceptions—such as a purely aretaic view in which virtue is a holistic state rather than a summed behavior, or a strictly deontological account in which duty is independent of quantitative self-monitoring—without explicit argument? And if one replaced Franklin's accounting-and-errata model with either of those rival frameworks, what concrete changes would be necessary in the actual practice of self-surveillance?
