Draft the joint statement that a “Memorial Continuity” party and a “Reparative Amnesia” party could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on collective memory, moral repair, and the ethics of forgetting, engaging with but not confined to the positions associated with Avishai Margalit and Philip Rieff.

The Memorial Continuity party holds that present institutions bear an asymmetrical relational duty of remembrance toward past collective wrongs. This duty is not merely epistemic or symbolic; it binds institutional successors and descendants of affected communities to maintain structured practices of acknowledgment. They maintain that the obligation weakens only when the structural conditions that enabled the wrong have been fully remediated, not simply when direct survivors have died or when time has passed naturally.

The Reparative Amnesia party holds that there exists a positive moral practice of collective forgetting—what we will call architectural forgetting—distinct from organic forgetting (the natural decay of memory over generations). Architectural forgetting involves the deliberate redesign of commemorative institutions: removing ritualized markers, dissolving fixed victim-perpetrator binaries in public space, and replacing monuments with curated absence, while preserving full archival access. They argue that when remembrance becomes a mechanism for present-day identity consolidation rather than for genuine structural repair, there is a duty to enact this structured absence.

Both parties reject the simplistic framing that remembrance is always virtuous and forgetting is always denial. They agree that the public debate has been obscured by three conflations: between organic and architectural forgetting; between remembrance as accountability and remembrance as identity performance; and between the death of direct victims and the expiration of institutional obligations. They further agree that any evaluation must distinguish what a society knows (archival fact) from what it ritually enacts (institutional memory).

Your deliverable should contain exactly these sections, in this order, with approximate lengths indicated:

1. Agreed Claims (numbered list, roughly 350–450 words). Include precise, non-vacuous claims both sides accept: about the relational nature of memorial duties; about the conditions under which remembrance serves justice versus when it fossilizes present identities; about the distinction between organic forgetting and architectural forgetting; about the relevance of structural remediation versus temporal distance; and about a concrete shared reference case—a municipality that retains complete archival records of an atrocity but removes its central public monument and replaces it with an unmarked garden designed for silence rather than ritual, leaving no interpretive plaque. Both sides should agree this case is genuinely relevant and should state what they jointly observe about it without resolving whether the redesign is permissible or required.

2. Residual Disagreements (table format, roughly 300 words). Use two columns labeled “Memorial Continuity formulation” and “Reparative Amnesia formulation,” with three rows: (a) whether the duty of remembrance extends to generations with no direct lived connection to the wrong, and if so whether it takes the form of a transmitted mnemonic inheritance or a contingent institutional responsibility; (b) whether architectural forgetting can ever be morally required rather than merely permitted, and whether its permissibility depends on prior achievement of full structural repair; (c) whether remembrance that no longer serves structural repair retains independent value, and whether that value is intrinsic or instrumental. Each cell should contain the formulation its own side accepts and that the opposing side recognizes as an accurate statement of the disagreement, not a strawman.

3. Cruxes and Predictive Tests (roughly 450 words). Identify three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. One crux should concern whether naturalness or continuity of commemorative practice carries independent moral weight. Another should concern how far sociological and psychological predictions about identity consolidation, intergroup trust, and moral repair can be trusted in post-conflict institutional redesign. The third should concern whether there exists a genuine mnemonic inheritance—a transmitted obligation that survives the death of direct victims and binds descendants independently of present structural harm. For each crux, provide: (i) a neutral framing both sides accept; (ii) a concrete case, comparative study, or institutional experiment both agree bears on it (for example, long-term comparative outcomes in societies that adopted structured absence versus sustained monumental presence, or a specific documented redesign of a commemorative institution); and (iii) each side’s explicit prediction about what that case or evidence would show, written in advance so that either side could be surprised.

4. Epistemic Reflection (roughly 150–200 words). State clearly what specific argument, empirical finding, or conceptual distinction would cause the synthesizer to revise the framing of one of the cruxes or to conclude that the two positions are not genuinely adversarial but merely verbal or rhetorical variants. Identify the point in the reasoning most likely to fail—whether the distinction between organic and architectural forgetting collapses under scrutiny, whether both sides in practice recommend identical institutional arrangements and differ only in normative vocabulary, or whether the concept of mnemonic inheritance can be shown to be incoherent. Do not present a verdict on who is correct; present the conditions of potential error.

Keep the tone formal, analytical, and synthetic. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not invoke specific texts, page numbers, or little-known theories outside the broad association of Margalit with an ethics of collective memory and Rieff with critical reflections on cultural trauma and therapeutic forgetting. Do not place any real textual passage inside quotation marks; paraphrase all attributed ideas. In the Epistemic Reflection section, explicitly state what would change your mind about the structure of this adversarial collaboration.
