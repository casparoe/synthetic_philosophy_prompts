As a retired physicist now working through metaphysics of science, I have been trying to apply the idea of renormalization—where lower-scale variables are integrated out and become descriptively irrelevant to autonomous higher-scale dynamics—to the metaphysical relation of grounding. I worry that treating grounding as cross-scale ontological dependence produces an argument that eliminates every non-fundamental domain. Here is the chain:

(1) If A grounds B, then B is ontologically redundant: all that is true of B is already true in virtue of A, and B adds no independent reality.
(2) Grounding is transitive: if physical facts ground chemical facts, and chemical facts ground biological facts, then physical facts ground biological facts.
(3) Biological, psychological, and social facts are therefore fully redundant with respect to the fundamental quantum fields.
(4) Yet in physics, integrating out lower scales does not render higher-scale phenomena redundant; it grants them autonomous causal and explanatory powers.
(5) So if grounding parallels physical scale-dependence, (3) should be false, because biological autonomy demonstrates that biology is not redundant.
(6) Hence either grounding is not transitive across scales, or it does not imply ontological redundancy, or biological autonomy is illusory.
(7) Denying the first two options forces us to treat biology, psychology, and social reality as illusions, which seems absurd.
(8) Therefore something in (1)–(3) must fail, yet I want to preserve the intuition that quantum fields are more fundamental than organisms.

Which step should I reject? If I give up (1), do I lose the very idea of fundamentality? If I reject (2), must I treat biological facts as ungrounded and therefore fundamental? And how should I understand the relation between physical scale autonomy and metaphysical grounding without collapsing one into the other?
