Write a deadpan parody of an internal corporate human-resources policy manual—specifically, the “Moral Luck-Adjusted Accountability Framework” section for the fictional multinational firm Axiomatic Holdings—formatted as numbered clauses with subheadings and a short appendix. Aim for about 700 words for the memo, plus a separate author’s note of roughly 100 words.

The voice should be that of an earnest, jargon-heavy compliance document: it takes the philosophical problem of moral luck with complete institutional seriousness and applies it to quarterly performance reviews, bonus allocations, and disciplinary proceedings. The satire should target the practice of translating philosophical distinctions into bureaucratic metrics, and the contradictions that emerge when an organization tries to correct for “luck” without dissolving responsibility altogether.

In the memo, translate the standard four-part taxonomy of moral luck—constitutive, circumstantial, causal, and resultant—into HR terminology, but do so precisely enough that a reader familiar with the literature recognizes the references. For example:

- Clause 2 should define “Type-C Circumstantial Fortune Variables” and explain how they are factored into “Situational Blame Ratings.”
- Clause 4 should address “Constitutive Fortune Adjustments” (the kind of person an employee is, shaped by temperament and upbringing) and specify whether “Character-Based Evaluations” are discounted when those traits lie outside the employee’s control.
- Clause 6 should establish a “Counterfactual Normalization Protocol” for “Resultant Luck Events”—outcomes that depend on unforeseeable factors—and state whether bonuses are forfeited when success is judged “fortune-contaminated.”
- Clause 8 should outline the appeals process: an employee may petition the “Ethics Adjustment Board” if they believe a blame rating ignores circumstantial or causal luck, but the board’s own decision should be noted as subject to “Board Constitutive Luck.”

The memo should include a brief embedded objection from a fictional internal critic—name the character yourself and invent the wording—showing that even dissenters remain trapped inside the framework. Do not quote any real philosopher verbatim; paraphrase standard positions without quotation marks.

Keep the irony sustained and deadpan: the document should never wink. The philosophical objections should emerge from the bureaucratic language itself—e.g., the more precisely the protocol tries to isolate “pure agency,” the more it reveals that nothing remains to evaluate. Do not slip into compatibilism or straightforward retributivism for comic relief unless that slip is itself the punchline of a specific clause.

The target is the institutional discourse that applies moral-luck reasoning to real-world accountability, not any named living person.

After the memo, include the author’s note. In it, identify which elements of the framework correspond to settled distinctions in the moral-luck literature and which are speculative institutional extensions invented for the satire. Explicitly flag any point where the piece takes liberties with a genuinely contested interpretation—for instance, whether constitutive luck undermines all moral judgment or only certain forms of it, and whether resultant luck can ever be fully “normalized” without collapsing evaluation into arbitrariness. In both the memo and the note, separate what is philosophically settled from what remains contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view as the consensus. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
