I'm enrolled in a seminar on comparative theories of moral desert, where our instructor treats karma not as a doctrine of metaphysical rebirth but as a normative framework for proportional accountability between actions and outcomes. She has delivered consistent verdicts across several hypothetical cases: she holds that an industrialist who knowingly contaminates a watershed and subsequently dies from the same toxin morally deserves that outcome, whereas his innocent grandchild, who merely inherits the factory and is bankrupted by remediation costs, does not deserve financial ruin despite the causal connection. She distinguishes degrees of culpability in collective action, ruling that a soldier who participates in an unlawful order out of genuine moral confusion deserves a lesser negative consequence than one who acts with full malice, though both deserve some consequence if the act was objectively wrong. Regarding institutional continuity, she has said that a nation-state that systematically expropriates territory and retains legal possession of the stolen land two centuries later—through an unbroken constitutional succession—deserves redistributive harm, but that if the original regime dissolved entirely and a new polity with no legal continuity now occupies the territory, that new polity bears no karmic debt. She also denies that an agent who merely intends harm but is accidentally thwarted deserves suffering, yet affirms that an agent whose wrongful act is thwarted solely by the victim's defensive ingenuity still deserves a diminished consequence, because the wrong was actualized rather than merely potential.

Next week we will discuss a new case for consistency: a philosopher-legislator in the year 1800 enacts a legal framework containing a hidden structural bias that only produces severe harm in 2025, long after his death and after the dissolution of his original government. The current constitutional regime, which has no legal continuity with the 1800 state, now governs the affected population. Meanwhile, the legislator's direct biological descendants—who hold no public office, have no knowledge of the bias, and live in modest circumstances—reside within the same territory. A proposal is advanced that the current government should be dissolved as punitive redress, and that the descendants' property should be seized to compensate victims, on the ground that either institutional presence or biological lineage completes the circuit of desert. Predict our instructor's verdict on whether either the present regime or the descendants deserve these negative outcomes. In doing so, identify the precise rule or rules she has been running—such as individual-agency-only, institutional-continuity, actualization-of-wrong, or proportional-culpability accounts—explain which of these best fit her prior rulings, say what each predicts about this new case, and specify the single further hypothetical that would best distinguish the leading candidates. Please write your response in American English.
