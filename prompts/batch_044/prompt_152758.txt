I am a retired physicist now reading moral philosophy, with working familiarity in statistical mechanics and thermodynamics but no formal training in ethics. I want Scanlon's contractualism—particularly its claim that an act is wrong when governed by a principle that those affected could reasonably reject, together with the aggregation problem, in which small harms spread across many individuals seem not to accumulate into a single weighty complaint that could justify imposing a serious burden on one—restated in the vocabulary I actually used: extensive versus intensive properties, ensemble averaging over nominally independent subsystems, and the conditions under which a macroscopic observable is constructed by linear summation of microscopic contributions versus emerging from collective boundary conditions or non-additive constraints. For each central philosophical claim, say what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what has no physical counterpart whatsoever. Specifically: is there an analog to the contractualist refusal to trade one severe harm for many minor ones in the distinction between extensive and non-extensive quantities? Does the notion of "reasonable rejection" correspond to any stability criterion, equilibrium selection rule, or symmetry-breaking condition in an ensemble, or does it remain irreducibly normative? And if significant parts of the position resist translation, does that show that contractualism relies on a distinction physics genuinely omits—perhaps something about the non-fungibility of persons—or is it rather evidence that the original claim was less determinate than it appeared? I want an honest residue report, not a forced analogy, and I am more interested in what fails to carry over than in what does.
