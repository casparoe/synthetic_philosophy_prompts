Write the opening remarks for the twentieth-anniversary session of the workshop series you founded and are now leaving to direct, titled "Institutional Boundaries." The audience is split evenly between professional moral philosophers and practicing architects, civil engineers, and public-health officials with no formal philosophical training. Your remarks must be structured around a conceptual tension you have observed over two decades rather than around biography or gratitude: deontological constraints in institutional design claim authority precisely because they are outcome-independent, yet an institution can preserve those constraints only by continually measuring, displaying, and celebrating the disasters they prevent. This creates what you term the display paradox — each successful prevention becomes evidence that the rule was never truly costly, which gradually erodes the institutional will to uphold the constraint exactly when testing it is most expensive. Do not resolve the tension with a synthesis; let the architecture of the speech mirror the instability, so that philosophers see why a tidy reconciliation is conceptually impossible (the rule's authority and its institutional survival pull in opposite directions) while practitioners see why they cannot reduce the rule to a merely prudent heuristic without destroying the protection it offers. Speak without citing specific philosophers, texts, or technical literature; do not include a bibliography, reading list, or named citations; and avoid vocabulary that requires philosophical training to parse. Aim for approximately five minutes, and ensure that both halves of the room leave understanding the same unavoidable problem for opposite reasons.
