I have recently assumed primary care for a parent with advancing dementia, and the practical necessity of deciding whether to authorize interventions that prolong biological life while apparently increasing suffering has led me back to Buddhist ethical theory—not for comfort, but for conceptual clarity. I am specifically trying to understand the structural relationship between consequentialist evaluation in Buddhist moral thought, where the kammic weight of an action is partly determined by its effects across future existences, and the anattā doctrine, which denies any persisting self that could serve as the subject of those long-range outcomes. Please explain this conceptual issue in depth: is the tension between rebirth-spanning consequentialism and the absence of a self merely apparent—perhaps dissolved by the continuity of the mental stream (citta-santāna) or by the impersonal transmission of kamma—or is it genuinely structural? In your explanation, address how upāya-kauśalya (skillful means) functions when the agent lacks enduring identity, whether the kusala/akusala distinction operates as a deontic constraint independent of outcome or as an instrumental heuristic oriented toward cessation, and whether anattā collapses the distinction between harming and failing to benefit when there is no enduring subject to bear the harm. I want a rigorous conceptual analysis rather than pastoral guidance or a survey of texts.
