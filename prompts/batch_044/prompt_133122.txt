Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—A, a structural historian, and B, a biographical historian—debating whether a Nazi Germany could have existed without Adolf Hitler. A should defend a position she calls modal robustness of structural convergence: the claim that Weimar’s collapsed parliamentary legitimacy, the reparations crisis, and the Great Depression created a narrow corridor of possible regimes, all of which would have required a racialized scapegoat and an autarkic, expansionist economy to stabilize; on her account, Hitler supplied specific rhetoric and bureaucratic fusion, but the institutional form—an authoritarian one-party state with a racial hierarchy and aggressive Lebensraum policy—was structurally overdetermined. B should defend counterfactual identity fragility: the claim that Nazi Germany denotes not merely an authoritarian outcome but a specific causal pathway in which Hitler’s personal conspiracy ideology was the necessary link between bureaucratic rationality and genocidal execution; without that link, B argues, the likely alternative was a conservative-military dictatorship (perhaps under Papen or Schleicher’s successors) that might have persecuted minorities but would not have produced the SS-state fusion, the racial-biological legislation of the Nuremberg Laws, or the Four-Year Plan’s particular war-economy logic. The exchange must contain real back-and-forth: B must respond directly to A’s claim that structural conditions overdetermine outcomes regardless of personality, and A must respond directly to B’s claim that functional equivalents do not preserve historical identity. Both should appeal to concrete institutional mechanisms—the passage of the Enabling Act, the coordination of the Länder, the integration of racial policy into economic planning—without reducing the other side to a strawman. The dialogue should proceed entirely from conceptual and historical reasoning; do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or verification of specific dates.
