I am taking a course that pairs philosophy with modern literature, and after discussing Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander's Surfaces and Essences with my study group, I have become convinced that analogy-making—not formal logic—is the fundamental mechanism of cognition. They argue that human reasoning constantly moves between surface resemblances and deeper structural essences, often without our noticing the shift. For my final essay, I want to examine Franz Kafka's The Metamorphosis through this framework, but I want to avoid reducing the story to a simple illustration.

Gregor Samsa's physical transformation into an insect is usually read as an allegory for alienation, yet I am more interested in how the narrative itself stages analogical cognition. The Samsa family repeatedly analogizes Gregor's condition to illness, economic burden, and animality—mappings that stay at the surface of his changed body and fail to reach his enduring humanity—while Gregor himself analogizes his new form back to human practices, climbing walls as if they were stairs and hoarding food with human anxiety. Read the novella as a study in analogical reasoning: where do these mappings penetrate to essence, and where do they collapse by remaining stuck at surface resemblance? I also want to know whether Kafka ultimately supports Hofstadter and Sander's claim that analogy is structurally central and unavoidable, or whether the story dramatizes a tragic failure of analogy—an inability to map surface change onto stable essence—that adds a darker qualification to their view. Please take both the philosophical framework and Kafka's text seriously, identifying specific scenes where analogical reasoning succeeds or breaks down, and be honest about where the literary work resists being fully captured by the theory.
