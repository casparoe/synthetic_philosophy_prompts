I'm prepping a lecture for my grad seminar on comparative ethics and I keep circling back to wether Buddhist ethics can be reconstructed as a form of consequentialism without totally betraying its core commitments. I want to compare two specific framings: on one side, the familiar Western welfare-consequentialist view where rightness tracks aggregate well-being across all affected beings, usually evaluated within a single life-frame; on the other side, a view I've been sketching out — call it 'karmic-trajectory consequentialism' — where an act is evaluated not by its immediate hedonic output but by whether it shifts the agent's (and releated beings') mental-continuum away from greed, aversion, and delusion over long temporal arcs, possibly across rebirths, with the 'good' being a kind of dynamic stability in intentional structure rather than summed pleasure.

My question is how these two actually differ in practice, especially regarding wild-animal suffering and the problem of moral luck. Does the trajectory framing avoid the aggregation headaches that make standard consequentialism demand implausible levels of sacrifice? Or does it just push the evaluation into an opaque future-state space where we can't compute anything? And which framework is more vulnerable to the charge that it asks ordinary moral agents to be long-range optimizers of mental states they can barely introspect?

Sorry, I know that's a mouthful — I was up late reading some Theravada commentaries and probably over-cooked the idea.
