I'm trying to lock down my position on pain and harm before drafting a longer post, and I'd appreciate a consistency audit from people who've thought about the clinical literature and the metaphysics of well-being. Here is roughly where I've landed, partly shaped by reports of pain asymbolia and partly by a conceptual distinction I keep returning to—between sensory-discriminative, affective-motivational, and cognitive-evaluative dimensions of pain, applied specifically to argue that the phenomenal and practical pathways can come apart without either being reducible to the other.

(1) I hold phenomenal essentialism: pain is essentially a conscious state with a negative qualitative character, and that qualitative badness is the primary source of its disvalue. (2) Pain asymbolia demonstrates that the phenomenal and motivational dimensions are genuinely dissociable—patients report an unpleasant sensory experience but show no avoidance behavior or emotional distress, which suggests the affective-motivational pathway can be selectively disrupted. (3) From this I draw an anti-reductionist conclusion: the badness of pain is not reducible to its functional role in producing avoidance or its contribution to evolutionary fitness. (4) At the same time, I hold an internalist practical-integration condition on harm: for a state to be genuinely harmful to a particular subject, it must be capable of undermining that subject's practical projects or well-being from within their first-person perspective—something they can register as an obstacle or reason, not merely a biological event happening to them. (5) I reject purely objective, biological-damage accounts of harm, but I also reject what I call phenomenal sufficiency—the view that phenomenal badness automatically constitutes harm regardless of how the subject practically relates to it. (6) I think evolutionary debunking arguments that target inferred normative judgments don't apply to pain's badness, because the negative valence is phenomenologically immediate rather than theoretically mediated. (7) Despite (5), I want to maintain that asymbolic patients are still genuinely harmed: the unpleasant phenomenal character itself constitutes a negative experiential fact in their lives, even though it doesn't integrate into their practical economy or motivate avoidance.

Do these hang together? I'm specifically worried that my practical-integration condition in (4), combined with my explicit rejection of phenomenal sufficiency in (5), pulls the rug out from under my verdict in (7). If harm requires being registered as undermining one's projects, and these patients lack the affective-motivational link that would do that integrating work, then on what grounds can I insist they're harmed rather than merely undergoing an unpleasant but practically inert sensation? Is there a hidden tension between (3) and (4)—that is, does defending phenomenal essentialism and anti-reductionism commit me to treating the phenomenal quality as sufficient for harm, which conflicts with (5)? And is there only an apparent tension between (2) and (6)—perhaps someone thinks that if evolutionary pressures shaped the motivational pathway separately from the sensory one, then the phenomenal quality's badness is itself an evolutionary artifact that debunking could target, when in fact debunking applies to inferred beliefs rather than to immediate qualitative character? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which single commitment is the least costly to revise if consistency demands it, and tell me which pairs are genuinely in conflict versus which ones only look like it. I don't want a lecture on pain theory; I want an audit of my package.
