hey, longtime lurker on the rationalist forums, recently got sidetracked into french revolution historiography and theres a pattern i keep seeing that i dont have a word for. you know the three big frames — class struggle, ideology, contingency — and the old line that revolutions devour their children. well, heres the specific thing: historians who think the revolution was structurally inevitable (grain prices, class conflict, the fiscal crisis, etc.) usually also say the terror was inevitable, part of the same deep forces. but historians who think it was contingent — that it could have stayed moderate — always assign the violence to specific accidents: Robespierre's paranoia, the war panic, ideological fanaticism. so the causal framework shifts based on whether the outcome is normatively "good" (the constitutional break, abolition of feudalism) or "bad" (the guillotine, the september massacres). its like people use structure for the parts they want to defend and contingency or ideology for the parts they want to distance themselves from. i can see this is related to the class-struggle vs ideology debate, but this feels more specific — an asymmetry in causal bookkeeping. does it have an established name? if not, how is it different from just motivated reasoning or the fundamental attribution error applied to history? also, what kind of historical case — maybe one where structural forces clearly produced both liberal reform and mass violence together, with no contingent trigger that you could seperate out — would change my mind and make me think this framing is wrong? maybe im overfitting, but i cant shake the feeling theres a sharper concept hiding here.
