Having spent my career in molecular biology before turning to philosophy in retirement, I find myself returning often to Douglas Hofstadter's image of the careenium—the substrate of elementary simmballs out of which high-level symbolic patterns, or simms, emerge—and to the old question of downward causation: do those symbol-level structures genuinely push the substrate particles around, or is the appearance of such influence merely an artifact of our descriptive level? Recently I came across a related notion of the dormant careenium—patterns that lie inactive in substrate until some triggering configuration revives them—and I wonder whether this might illuminate fictional representations of artificial consciousness. I was discussing this with friends over dinner, and we found ourselves arguing about Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982), since the replicants there seem to occupy precisely this ambiguous zone: they possess implanted memories and emotional responses that behave like active simms, yet they are also engineered biological substrates whose every reaction might in principle be traced to simmball mechanics. I would like a philosophical reading of the film through this specific lens.

Please treat the film not as a mere illustration of the idea, but as a work that can complicate it. I am especially interested in three moments. First, the Voight-Kampff test: it treats pupil dilation and emotional response as indicators of a symbol-level self-model, yet the replicants' reactions could also be read as purely substrate-level reflexes. Does the film present this measurement as detecting genuine downward causation, or as mistaking correlation for cause? Second, Rachael's discovery that her childhood memories are implanted: when she realizes that the symbol-pattern she took to be her own history is in fact an imported file, does the film suggest that her subsequent grief and identity-crisis are causally driven by the symbol—the belief in a past—or that they collapse back into substrate-level programming? Third, Roy Batty's final monologue about lost experiences and the dissolution of his memories: here a dying substrate seems to generate, or perhaps to be generated by, a symbol-level narrative of transcendence. Would the careenium framework read this as the resumption of a dormant pattern, or as the final dissolution of a simm into mere simmball noise? I want a reading that takes both Hofstadter's conceptual architecture and Scott's cinematic choices seriously, noting where the metaphor holds, where it risks conflating epistemic uncertainty with substrate indeterminacy, and what the film adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not capture. I am preparing this for a small discussion group of scientists who have begun reading philosophy, so I need no bibliography or citations—only an honest critical interpretation that does not reduce the film to a diagram or distort the physics of causation to fit a scene.
