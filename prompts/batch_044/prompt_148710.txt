I'm in a seminar on African metaphysics and we got into ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood, and my study group built this argument that seems to show the view eats itself. I think there's a hidden slide somewhere but I can't name it.

(1) In the Bantu framework, a muntu (a person) is defined not by some intrinsic substance but by standing in reciprocal recognition relations with other bantu; a kintu (a thing) lacks those relations.
(2) If personhood is purely relational, then the boundary between muntu and kintu depends entirely on the presence or absence of those relations, not on any internal feature.
(3) Now these relations are transitve across social networks: if A recognizes B and B recognizes C, then A and C are both embedded in the same web of recognition that constitutes B.
(4) So the constitutive relation doesn't stop at pairs; it scales up to the whole community, meaning the village jointly constitutes each individual as a muntu.
(5) But if the whole village jointly constitutes every individual, then there's no metaphysical difference between "one person with many limbs" and "many people in one village" — both are just one distributed relational entity.
(6) That collapses ubuntu into a kind of collective monism where individual persons don't really exist, which is exactly what ubuntu scholars want to avoid.
(7) Therefore the relational conception is self-defeating.
