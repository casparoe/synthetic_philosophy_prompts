In my philosophy of history seminar we have been comparing Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men with Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners, and after weeks of discussion the debate seems frozen: Browning treats Reserve Police Battalion 101 as a case study in situational dynamics—peer pressure, incremental authorization, and the suspension of moral judgment—while Goldhagen reads the same killings as the expression of a culturally embedded eliminationist antisemitism that made ordinary Germans eager participants rather than reluctant conscripts. Both sides cite the same postwar interrogations and demographic records, yet neither treats the other's interpretation as even relevant to the question at hand. Is this stalemate best understood as a substantive causal disagreement that awaits better archival evidence, or is it a case of explanatory pluralism in disguise—Browning offering a mechanism sketch of how these particular policemen came to kill, Goldhagen offering a dispositional account of why the social reservoir existed—such that they are not competing answers to one question but answers to two different questions concealed under the phrase explaining perpetrator behavior? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure rather than a verdict on the Holocaust historiography: do the sides differ about facts, about standards of explanatory adequacy, or about which cases should count as data? And if the stall is conceptual rather than empirical, what kind of clarification—a distinction between explanation-types, a reframing of the explanatory target, or an explicit partition of the question—would actually allow progress, or should we conclude that the framing itself is irreducibly ambiguous?
