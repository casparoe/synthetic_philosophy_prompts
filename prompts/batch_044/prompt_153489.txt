Draft the joint statement that a committed Kuhnian incommensurabilist—who maintains that paradigm shifts involve genuine semantic discontinuity, the theory-ladenness of all observation, and the principled impossibility of neutral arbitration between competing frameworks—and a committed rational-continuist—who maintains that cross-paradigm comparison is always available through shared predictive, structural, and explanatory criteria, and that Kuhn’s semantic rupture is an overstatement of local pragmatic friction—could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration on the nature of scientific change. Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: specify what both sides accept about the historical dynamics of major revolutions (for instance, the Copernican and relativistic transitions), about the role of accumulated anomalies in destabilizing a dominant framework, about the existence of local translation difficulties between theoretical vocabularies, and about the formal limits of pure logical comparison between axiom systems embedded in different empirical interpretations. Second, precise formulations of the residual disagreements, with each formulation phrased so that the camp it describes would accept it as an accurate statement of its own view; in particular, distinguish sharply between ontological incommensurability (the absence of shared referents or entities across paradigms) and normative incommensurability (the absence of shared standards for explanatory adequacy, simplicity, or predictive success), and state whether either side regards the other distinction as substantive or as a terminological redescription. Third, identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would shift at least one party’s confidence—including whether a non-circular metric for “translation failure” can be constructed without presupposing one paradigm’s ontology; whether persistent predictive equivalence in cases of radical underdetermination supports continuity or merely masks deeper semantic divergence; and whether the retention of mathematical formalism across a revolution counts as evidence of shared conceptual structure or only of syntactic coincidence. Fourth, for each crux, present one concrete historical episode or formal argument that both sides agree is genuinely relevant, record in advance what each side predicts that case demonstrates, and note whether the two predictions are empirically distinguishable. Fifth, conclude with a brief joint assessment of whether any portion of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, supplying explicit criteria—such as inter-translatability of core predictions and mutual recognizability of explanatory successes—that both sides accept for distinguishing verbal from substantive disagreement. Write throughout in a formal register appropriate to a philosophy-of-science journal’s collaborative-inquiry format, avoiding bibliographic appeals and framing every claim so that it could be evaluated by a reader without access to external sources.
