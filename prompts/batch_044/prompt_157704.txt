I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy working on intergenerational justice, and I'm preparing an essay that uses literary cases to test whether abstract arguments about inherited capital miss something that narrative form can reveal. Read Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice through the lens of dynastic wealth, inheritance taxation, and equal opportunity—but do so through a specific conceptual distinction I want to hold in view: the difference between the inheritance of liquid wealth and the inheritance of "status capital" (the transmission of social positioning, relational networks, and class closure that reproduces advantage even when estates are taxed or divided). The entailment of Longbourn is the obvious entry point, but I'm especially interested in whether the novel treats the marriage market—Charlotte Lucas's acceptance of Mr. Collins, Jane Bennet's match with Bingley, Elizabeth's refusal—as a functional substitute for inheritance rights for women blocked by dynastic law. Does Austen present marital strategy as an adequate private remedy for a structural failure of equal opportunity, or does the irony with which she frames Mrs. Bennet's panic and Mr. Bennet's detachment suggest that the harm is not merely distributive but also relational and emotional? Where does the novel illuminate the claim that breaking the "dynastic loop" requires legal and fiscal intervention rather than individual negotiation? And where does it resist that framing—perhaps by showing that Mr. Collins's acquisition of the living is itself a form of meritocratic entry that complicates any simple anti-inheritance story? I want a reading faithful in both directions: one that doesn't reduce Austen to a parable about patrimonial capitalism, and that doesn't distort the philosophical debate about inheritance taxes and opportunity to fit the plot. End by saying what Pride and Prejudice adds to the argument about dynastic wealth and equal opportunity that philosophical abstraction alone tends to miss.
