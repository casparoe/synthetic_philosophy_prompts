I'm writing this for my upper-level seminar on ethics and collective action, and I want a rigorous conceptual analysis rather than generic practical advice. Here is the concrete situation I'm struggling with.

I serve as the procurement delegate for a 40-person student housing cooperative. Each month I order shared groceries, including protein. Our usual supplier is inexpensive but sources chicken from large-scale industrial facilities with poor welfare conditions. There is an alternative supplier with somewhat better welfare practices, but it costs roughly thirty percent more. I have done the arithmetic: if I switch, the cooperative's budget will not cover the cost for everyone, and at least two members have said they would leave the co-op and purchase cheaper meat on their own. I also spoke informally with a representative of the usual supplier, who indicated that they adjust orders to demand; if I stop buying, they will not reduce the number of birds raised, but will redirect that same quantity to other buyers. So the direct causal effect of my order on the total number of animals affected appears to be zero.

Separately, I bike to campus every day. A roommate recently told me this is irrational: my individual carbon savings are negligible, and whether I bike or drive, aggregate emissions do not change. He applies the same reasoning to the chicken order: since I am one buyer among millions, and my choice does not cross any threshold that changes total production or welfare outcomes, there is no moral reason for me to bear the extra cost or inconvenience.

I feel strongly that the chicken case and the biking case are not morally equivalent, but I cannot defend the asymmetry. One possibility I've considered is that in the cooperative I am acting in an institutional role—representing a collective decision—whereas biking is purely private consumption. Another is that the relevant factor is not causal efficacy but something like expressive alignment: by ordering the cheap chicken, I endorse a practice I reject, even if my endorsement changes nothing in the world. But I'm unsure whether either distinction survives scrutiny, especially since switching suppliers might actually increase harm by pushing departing members toward worse individual options, or whether I'm simply rationalizing an emotional preference.

Can you lay out whether there is a coherent philosophical distinction between these two cases—one involving institutional procurement with broken causal chains and potential displacement effects, the other involving private consumption with negligible marginal impact? Apply the relevant theoretical framework rather than offering encouragement. Please also state how confident you are in the claims you make, and specifically identify the point about which you are least certain.
