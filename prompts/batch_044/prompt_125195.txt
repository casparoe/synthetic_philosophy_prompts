I'm a graduate student in a seminar on practical rationality and collective decision-making, and I've been assigned to help a university governance committee that approves binding institutional commitments—multi-decade contracts, endowment policies, and core-curriculum requirements—that will constrain future students who are not yet present to vote. The committee currently relies on surveys of current students, but I want to translate a framework closer to coherent extrapolated volition into an operational tool: the normative standard should not be what students happen to want now, but what they would want after coherence, full information, and reflective equilibrium. Because this must be reusable rather than a one-time philosophical defense, I need it rendered as an actual decision procedure.

Please design an ordered checklist or flowchart-in-prose that the committee can run on any proposal for a long-term binding commitment. Specify how to identify the stakeholder preference set without treating every expressed opinion as fixed; the coherence tests—transitivity, framing independence, consistency across time-horizons—that must be satisfied before extrapolation; how to model "better-informed" preferences without substituting the committee's own values; the points that must be handed to a deliberative body rather than solved analytically; and explicit stopping conditions—thresholds of divergence or ambiguity—where the procedure returns "do not decide" rather than imposing a constructed preference. Mark every unavoidable judgment call.

Apply this procedure to three proposals: a thirty-year fossil-fuel-free campus energy contract that raises tuition by 2% annually; a mandatory two-year foreign-language core requirement that current students oppose by about seventy percent; and an endowment divestment excluding both fossil fuels and nuclear energy, which satisfies activists but reduces projected returns affecting future financial aid. Include one case where the procedure yields a recommendation the committee will strongly resist, and explain why it is forced. Then state clearly what the procedure cannot do—especially where values are constructed rather than discovered, and where coherence permits incompatible orderings. Organize your response in these sections with approximate lengths: (1) Role, Constraints, and Scope (~150 words); (2) The Procedure: Steps, Tests, and Stopping Rules (~450 words); (3) Worked Applications (~550 words); (4) Judgment Points and Hard Limits (~250 words).
