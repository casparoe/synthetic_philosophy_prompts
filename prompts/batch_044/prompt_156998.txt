I'm drafting an argument for a graduate seminar on democratic theory that tries to show the concept of democratic backsliding either proves too much or collapses into partisan labeling, and I need help finding where the reasoning slips. The cases I'm thinking about are Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States, but I want to avoid just listing examples and instead test the logic of the concept itself. Here is the reconstructed chain.

(1) Democratic backsliding should be defined structurally, not by outcomes: it occurs whenever an elected government systematically reduces institutional veto points—judicial independence, media pluralism, civil-service neutrality, or inter-branch checks—that previously constrained executive discretion, regardless of whether those constraints were themselves directly authorized by popular vote.

(2) If structural reduction of veto points is sufficient for backsliding, then any democratic consolidation that replaces a fragmented, veto-heavy regime with a more centralized and governable one also counts as backsliding during the transition, because the consolidation deliberately eliminates veto players.

(3) Consider a canonical transition from a restricted-suffrage constitutional monarchy with a powerful appointed upper chamber and indirect elections to a parliamentary democracy with universal suffrage and a unicameral legislature: the new regime removes multiple veto points precisely to make popular will effective.

(4) We clearly do not want to classify such democratization as backsliding; therefore the structural feature in (1) cannot be sufficient, and there must be an implicit normative condition that distinguishes legitimate consolidation from illegitimate erosion.

(5) The only available normative conditions are procedural—the government still permits competitive elections and peaceful turnover—or substantive—the erosion violates a particular theory of democratic rights, liberal pluralism, or horizontal accountability.

(6) If we adopt the procedural condition, then regimes like contemporary Hungary or Poland, where elections remain competitive and turnover is technically possible though systematically tilted, do not clearly qualify, which strips the concept of the very cases it was meant to capture; and if we apply the same standard to recent American institutional conflicts, the label becomes either inapplicable or arbitrarily selective.

(7) If we adopt the substantive condition, then whether a given reform—court restructuring, media consolidation, administrative centralization—counts as backsliding depends on prior commitments to liberal constitutionalism versus majoritarian democracy, making the judgment indistinguishable from partisan disagreement about the legitimate scope of popular rule.

(8) So either backsliding is over-inclusive (it captures democratization itself) or it is under-inclusive and parasitic on contested normative theory; hence the concept is either useless for comparative analysis or merely a rhetorical label for reforms one already opposes.

I would like to preserve both (1) and (4): it feels correct that backsliding is not just "reform I dislike," and that genuine democratization should not be treated as erosion. I also want to maintain that Hungary, Poland, and comparable cases represent something genuinely different from ordinary constitutional disagreement, not merely a different balance of democratic values. Which step should I reject—perhaps there is an equivocation between "removing veto points" and "undermining democratic accountability," or a hidden modal slide between what a transition can do and what it must do? If the argument is actually valid, which premise is the least costly to surrender, and what do I pay for giving it up?
