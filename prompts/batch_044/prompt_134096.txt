Having recently begun to formalize arguments in a logic course, I have found myself unable to justify basic rules of inference—such as modus ponens—without either appealing to those same rules or invoking some further principle that itself demands justification. This experience has led me to take Carroll's tortoise seriously as a challenge to the justification of deduction rather than as a mere logical curiosity.

Write an essay that reaches and defends a clear conclusion on the following question: Does Carroll's regress show that deductive justification is necessarily circular or infinitely deferred, or can it be dissolved by distinguishing between employing a rule and explicitly stating it? In your analysis, address specifically whether a mechanical reasoning system—one that applies inference rules without any internal representation of those rules—escapes the regress, and whether that fact undermines or supports the claim that human deduction requires a non-circular, meta-level justification. Consider also whether pragmatic or rationalist accounts of logical knowledge can accommodate the possibility that some inferential principles are basic in a way that needs no further defense. Do not end on an agnostic note; take a position on whether the tortoise's objection succeeds and what that implies for how we ought to regard our own logical practices.
