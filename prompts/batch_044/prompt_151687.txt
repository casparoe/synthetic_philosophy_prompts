I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of social science working on causal explanation in sociology. Rather than treating Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis as a settled empirical claim about capitalism's origins, I'm approaching it as a persistent conceptual problem: can culture, understood specifically as value-orientations rather than as beliefs or preferences, function as an independent cause in macro-level explanations of institutional change? This framing raises three open lines that seem to pull in different directions. First, there is a formalization problem: Weber's idea of elective affinity between religious values and economic practices resists direct translation into modern causal-inference language, since values are not obviously manipulable variables or valid instruments. Can that affinity be reconstructed using mechanism-based or mediation frameworks without collapsing culture into individual psychology or into institutional structure? Second, there is a normative implication: if value-rational action genuinely explains parts of institutional development, does that license development policies that explicitly target cultural values, or does Weber's own anti-positivist stance block that inference? Third, there is the logic of overdetermination: Weber treats material conditions and ideas as jointly necessary but neither as generally sufficient, which complicates how we assign explanatory weight. Of these three—the formalization of elective affinity, the normative limits of cultural explanation, and the logic of multi-causal historical explanation—which should be prioritized for a research agenda? Which is upstream of the others, which is tractable without first resolving deep Weber-scholarship disputes, and which, if settled, would most change how contemporary development economics treats culture? Please defend a specific ordering, specify the criteria you are using (importance, tractability, downstream dependence), and say what kind of result over the next few years would make you reorder them.
