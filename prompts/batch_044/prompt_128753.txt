So I retired from the biology lab about four years ago and have been slowly getting into philosophy through a local discussion group. Last week two regulars, Maya and Leo, got stuck in a long argument about anthropic reasoning that I mostly followed but then lost the thread. I was hoping you could help me see whos right at each step, since they both seemed to have decent points.

Maya started by saying that if you take the self-sampling assumption seriously, you should reason as if youre randomly picked from all observers in your reference class. So if some multiverse theories predict thousands of simulated minds for every biological one, then just finding yourself conscious should push you toward thinking youre probably simulated.

Leo pushed back and said self-sampling is the wrong framework altogether. He said we should use self-indication instead: the fact that you exist is direct evidence in favor of theories that produce more observers total. He admitted that means theories with tons of simulations get a big Bayesian boost, but he said thats just correct updating, not some kind of mistake.

Maya then brought up the presumptuous philosopher objection - I think its Bostrom's idea - where apparently you can conclude the universe is enormous just because you happen to be here, without doing any actual measurements. She said Leos view does exactly that with simulation counts: you just count your own head and declare simulations dominate.

Leo replied that the objection only bites if you start with a flat prior and ignore all other data. He said empirical constraints - like how hard it is to run realistic simulations - still do real work, so SIA just adjusts the prior rather than replacing science.

Then Maya said that even as a prior adjustment its strange. She asked: if tomorrow we discovered that 99 percent of all observer-moments in the multiverse are actually sub-millisecond quantum flickers that happen to be conscious - assuming panpsychism or something - then by Leos logic we should expect our own experience to be one of those brief flickers. But our experience clearly lasts decades, so SIA seems to make the wrong prediction about our own lives.

Leo said that just means the reference class needs to be defined more carefully - maybe by cognitive complexity, not just bare consciousness. He said once you restrict the class properly, both SSA and SIA converge on predicting long-lived complex observers like us, so theres no real contradiction.

They asked me to referee but honestly I was lost after the quantum flicker part. Can you go through there exchange move by move and rate each point from clearly wrong to clearly right? And tell me if theres something important both of them are missing about how reference classes actually get fixed in practice.
