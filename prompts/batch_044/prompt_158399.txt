I'm formalizing an argument for a museum-ethics seminar about whether restitution can be grounded in cultural continuity, and I've pushed it into what looks like a reductio: either I accept that nearly every museum collection is subject to overlapping ownership claims, or I abandon the intuitive case for returning the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles. Here is the chain. (1) A collective entity E is entitled to artifact A if and only if E is the diachronic successor of the community C that produced A, where succession is defined by the persistence conditions used in metaphysics of personal identity—either continuous institutional structure or continuous cultural-psychological identity. (2) Modern Greece satisfies both conditions with respect to ancient Athens through continuous language, territorial occupation, religious tradition, and institutional descent, while the British Museum, as a modern British state institution, satisfies neither; so Greece, not Britain, holds entitlement to the Parthenon Marbles. (3) By exact parity, modern Nigeria satisfies the conditions with respect to the Benin Kingdom for the Benin Bronzes. (4) But cultural-psychological continuity is graded rather than binary: modern Britain also partially inherits classical education, legal architecture, and artistic tradition from Athens, and diaspora and neighboring communities maintain partial continuity with many origin cultures. (5) Applying (1) consistently therefore generates massively overlapping, incompatible entitlements for a single object—since multiple groups can claim partial continuity—or forces an arbitrary threshold for "sufficient" continuity with no non-circular justification. (6) If we avoid overlap by saying only the "best" successor is entitled, we need a cross-cultural metric for comparing degrees of continuity, which does not exist and would be politically unworkable. (7) So (1) either yields an administratively absurd system in which nearly every museum object carries competing valid claims, or it collapses into an unjustified exception for the cases we care about. (8) Therefore (1) is false and cultural continuity cannot ground specific restitution claims. I want to preserve (2) and (3) if possible, since they seem to capture the moral force of restitution, but I worry I have imported a metaphysical premise about collective identity that ethics cannot support. Where does the argument actually break?
