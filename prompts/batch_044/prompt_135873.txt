Write a short story set in a neuroscience institute where researchers have developed instruments that can map phenomenal experience with perfect fidelity—recording exactly what it is like for a subject to taste copper or feel vertigo—yet a discovered conservation law makes these recordings causally inert with respect to the neural machinery that produces speech, belief, and written argument. The protagonist, a senior researcher named Dr. Elena Vasquez, has spent her career refining these detectors. In the story’s central crisis, she realizes that her own conviction that she is currently experiencing a particular shade of crimson, and her further conviction that this experience is real and reportable, are both generated by brain processes that are physically disconnected from the phenomenal state they purport to describe. She must then decide whether to publish her team’s findings, knowing that if epiphenomenalism holds, her motivation to publish—like every phenomenal judgment made by her colleagues—is itself a mechanically produced output with no causal link to the conscious realities it claims to discuss. Make the narrative philosophically precise: show how the researchers continue to argue about consciousness, correct one another’s descriptions of experience, and treat phenomenal judgments as evidence, despite believing that none of these judgments are caused by the experiences they are about. Explore whether their shared practice of reasoning about the mind can remain rational once they accept that the very thoughts driving the reasoning are causally orphaned from their subject matter, and let the story conclude with Vasquez taking some concrete action—publication, suppression, or resignation—that embodies her answer to the paradox of phenomenal judgment.
