I am trying to map my own judgments about Sartre's ideas of radical freedom and bad faith onto concrete situations that go beyond the familiar textbook illustrations, and I want to see whether a single principle can organize them or whether some judgments must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts, with brief descriptions.

Case 1 — The virtuoso cellist: She describes her practice regimen as compelled by an internal force — 'the music demands it through me' — treating her artistic transcendence as if it were an external facticity like bone structure. Verdict: clear bad faith.

Case 2 — The bureaucrat: He enforces harmful regulations and insists he has no choice because the organizational hierarchy determines his actions. Verdict: also bad faith, though the facticity appealed to is social rather than biological.

Case 3 — The patient refusing adaptation: He has a degenerative condition, rejects assistive technology, and says he 'must preserve the body he was given,' as if his prior physical state were an essential nature he is obligated to maintain. Verdict: bad faith, specifically a freezing of past facticity that denies present freedom to reinterpret his embodied situation.

Case 4 — The reluctant engineer: She works on engagement algorithms she privately despises, explaining that she 'cannot afford to leave' because of debt and family obligations. Verdict: I oscillate. Sometimes this sounds like an honest acknowledgment of real economic constraints within which freedom must operate; sometimes it sounds like the bureaucrat's excuse transposed into the economic register.

Case 5 — The sacrificing parent: He abandons artistic ambitions to care for a disabled child and says, 'there was never any decision; a real parent just does this.' Verdict: not bad faith if he can genuinely affirm that he could have done otherwise and still chooses this path; but if he denies that any alternative was ever genuinely available to him, then it collapses into the same denial of transcendence as Case 2.

Case 6 — The hunger-striking prisoner: She chooses to risk death in protest, describing the act not as ideologically compelled but as 'the only context in which I am fully present to my freedom.' Verdict: authentic embrace of radical freedom, not bad faith.

Case 7 — The cynical writer: He produces commercially successful but artistically empty work, publicly framing himself as 'merely the market's instrument,' yet he meticulously curates an image of himself as a self-aware cynic who sees through every illusion, including this one. Verdict: still bad faith, despite — or perhaps because of — the reflexive self-awareness. At the same time, his structural posture feels closer to Case 6's authenticity than Case 2's bureaucratic denial does, because he seems to know exactly what he is doing.

My working hypothesis is that bad faith consists in treating a contingent feature of one's situation — talent, social role, economic need, prior bodily state — as an unchosen necessity that eliminates one's transcendence, and that mere intellectual awareness of this mechanism does not by itself dissolve the bad faith.

But this hypothesis strains at two points. First, Case 7's self-awareness should, on the hypothesis, dissolve the bad faith, yet my verdict holds it intact. Second, Case 6 uses language ('the only act') that sounds like she is reducing her freedom to a single necessary path, structurally resembling the cellist's appeal to musical necessity, yet I judge her authentic.

Please do the following. (1) State a precise principle — or a small competing set — that captures the pattern in Cases 1–6, identifying which clause is motivated by which case. (2) Apply the principle to Case 4 (the engineer) and to one further case you construct that I have not listed, giving definite verdicts. (3) Say honestly whether accommodating Case 7 requires complicating the principle to the point of ad hocness, or whether it forces me to overturn either my Case 6 or Case 7 verdict. (4) If you must drop one verdict to preserve a simpler principle, specify which one and why.
