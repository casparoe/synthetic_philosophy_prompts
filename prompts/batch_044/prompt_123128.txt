I've been thinking on my own about guilt and shame—reading some popular psychology, noticing how different cultures talk about honor and remorse, and wondering whether a society could actually decide to be a "guilt culture" or a "shame culture" rather than just inheriting it. I want a taxonomy, built as a branching tree, that sorts the real positions people can hold on whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion and on whether a culture can genuinely choose between them. The audience is intelligent non-specialists: no philosophy degree required, but able to follow concrete cases.

Please construct the tree around four diagnostic splits, yielding about eight leaves. Each split must be a concrete claim or scenario I could answer without already knowing the academic label for it.

Root split: Are guilt and shame genuinely different emotional responses, or are they the same underlying social-pain feeling with different names?

If they are genuinely different, the second split asks whether the boundary is drawn mainly by audience (shame requires that someone—real or imagined—witness or know about the wrong, while guilt works in private) or mainly by target (the specific wrong act versus the whole self or character), regardless of who knows.

If the audience-based branch is taken, the third split asks whether a culture can deliberately redesign its institutions—schools, courts, rituals, language—to make one emotion dominant and suppress the other, or whether the emotional wiring is fixed and culture only relabels what biology delivers.

If the target-based branch is taken, the third split asks which mode of evaluation produces the healthier moral life: one that focuses on repairing the specific act, or one that demands transforming the kind of person you are.

If the root says they are not genuinely different, the second split asks whether the distinction is just an intensity scale (mild discomfort versus crushing exposure) or whether it is a linguistic artifact of modern Western psychology that does not survive translation into other conceptual schemes.

Then inject one slightly niche framing that keeps this from being a simple rehash of "guilt is private, shame is public": include a branch or leaf condition about whether the emotion can be felt for violating a norm that you do not personally accept—what you might call outsider moral pain.

For every leaf, give it a name (use "unnamed" if no prominent thinker actually occupies it) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—the thing its holder has to swallow. Do not just list textbook theories; build the tree from the logical space of answers, then locate the named positions inside it.

After placing the positions, include a short appendix that lists any logically possible combinations of answers that remain unoccupied. For each empty cell, briefly diagnose whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored.

Keep the entire instrument answerable by reasoning alone: do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or specific empirical studies. Do not hinge any branch on an obscure technical term or on a highly specific historical claim that depends on precise attribution. Present the result as a clean tree: each branch labeled by the claim or question that splits it, leaves labeled with position names and awkward commitments, followed by the appendix of empty cells.
