I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, though I have been reading about collective-action problems in my spare time. I am organizing a ninety-minute workshop for roughly sixteen colleagues—mostly developers and product managers—who have heard the phrase “tragedy of the commons” but have never experienced its structural logic as a lived decision problem. I want you to design a runnable in-class simulation or structured game that makes the group feel the force of open-access resource depletion rather than merely rehearse a story about overfishing, with rules, roles, and a payoff scheme using only small, non-monetary stakes such as points convertible to coffee vouchers.

To keep the exercise from feeling like a generic classroom replay, please inject a specific structural twist that is not in the standard Hardin narrative: the shared resource should degrade along a hidden, nonlinear curve, so that early withdrawals feel individually rational and nearly costless because the damage is distributed and delayed, but once an invisible threshold is crossed the pool collapses abruptly and all participants—including those who cooperated—lose entirely. Arrange the information asymmetry so that participants discover this collapse through their own choices before I name the concept. The deliverable should include the setup and materials; the exact rules, incentives, and what each role knows about the depletion rate; a minute-by-minute timeline for the session; a debrief that maps specific moments—first defection, failed negotiation, revelation of the hidden curve—onto the conceptual distinction between rivalrous common-pool resources and non-rivalrous pure public goods, and onto the problem of monitoring hidden depletion; an honest note on which real-world feature this activity cannot reproduce, such as intergenerational time horizons or formal legal enforcement, and how I should supply it verbally; and a frank prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover.
