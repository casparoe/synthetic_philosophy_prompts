I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying the philosophy of collective memory and historical epistemology, and I have been examining the published rulings of a municipal heritage arbitration board that decides, case by case, whether an official historical record or a living community’s collective memory should carry authoritative status when the two conflict irreconcilably. In Case 1, concerning a 1920s labor strike, union oral histories describe police violence that official police logs omit entirely; the board ruled for the oral histories, noting that the logs were compiled by officers with an institutional interest in suppressing misconduct. In Case 2, regarding a 1945 wartime evacuation, survivors’ collective memory holds that local authorities deliberately delayed warnings, but municipal minutes show the warning was issued promptly; the board ruled for the minutes, observing that the survivors’ narrative had been shaped by post-war narratives that assigned blame for unrelated casualties. In Case 3, regarding a 1960s scientific collaboration, the surviving researchers collectively remember that they agreed upon a specific instrument-calibration protocol, but archived laboratory notebooks contain contradictory procedures; the board ruled for the notebooks, on the grounds that the researchers’ present recollections were influenced by later standardized protocols that did not exist at the time. In Case 4, regarding a nineteenth-century religious community, congregational memory maintains that their founder refused a state subsidy in 1873, but treasury receipts show the subsidy was accepted; the board ruled for the memory, arguing that the receipts reflected an administrative misclassification by a clerk and that the community’s continuous ritual practice of commemorating the refusal constituted independent corroboration.

Now consider Case 5. A group of retired atmospheric scientists, now in their eighties, collectively maintain that during a 1978 field campaign they observed a sustained, anomalous ionization pattern that was never published, because the principal investigator suppressed the data to protect an ongoing funding agreement. The original field notebooks, kept by that principal investigator, contain only null results. However, recently declassified grant correspondence reveals that the principal investigator did indeed face explicit pressure from the funding agency to avoid reporting anomalies that would complicate the agency’s public risk assessments. The scientists’ association has requested that a national museum include their testimony in an exhibit on scientific integrity.

If the board wishes to remain consistent with its own precedents, what should it rule? Identify at least two distinct principles that could jointly accommodate Cases 1 through 4, state what each principle predicts for Case 5, give your best-guess verdict with reasons, identify which of Cases 1 through 4 is the closest analog, and specify the further hypothetical case that would most clearly distinguish the competing principles from one another.
