I’ve been helping my Aunt Hillary maintain her large indoor ant farm, which she calls her “Ant Fugue” because the synchronized foraging patterns strike her as almost musical. Lately she has noticed something that unsettles her simple view of the colony: when she temporarily splits the nest with a removable barrier, each half continues to navigate the old tunnel layout for days, even though every worker ant present after the split is a new individual that never experienced the original paths. Hillary now insists the colony acts like a single remembering being, and she has begun speaking to it as one entity, feeling guilty when she accidentally kills workers, and asking whether “it” suffers when she disturbs the nest. I tell her she is anthropomorphizing, but I realize I cannot explain exactly what is wrong with her inference without just appealing to intuition—especially since human brains also maintain coherent identity and problem-solving across constant cellular replacement. The situation forces me to ask whether there is any principled philosophical boundary between a collective that merely distributes information among replaceable parts and one that genuinely possesses a unified mind, or whether my certainty that only individual brains think is just an unexamined prejudice. I am not asking whether to humor my aunt in conversation; I want to know whether the colony’s integrated adaptive behavior, its functional continuity despite total turnover of constituents, and its capacity to solve spatial problems no single ant could solve provide any real reason to treat it as a subject of experience, and what criterion—phenomenal unity, token-reflexive awareness, or something else—would actually settle the matter. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question.
