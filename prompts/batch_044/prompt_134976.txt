I'm trying to sort out whether moral norms die mainly from arguments, material interests, or shifts in honor, and my gut verdicts on cases feel all over the place. Dueling in Europe faded not because people suddenly realized killing over insults was wrong—folks had said that for centuries—but because it started looking like aristocratic cosplay, and middle-class honor codes made it embarrassing. So that's honor. The abolition of slavery in Britain: the pure economic-interest story feels overated, but I also doubt moral argument alone would have worked without making slave-owning feel like a stain on national character. So maybe both? Foot-binding ending in China feels like collective honor flipping—once Western powers were around, bound feet signaled backwardness, so being "civilized" required stopping. Then there's the US smoking bans in the 90s: was that health science and lawsuit costs, or smoking just going from cool to trashy? I lean interest, but I'm not sure. Here's the one I keep circling back to: imagine a small village with a strong norm against locking doors, maintained by shame—locking up means you don't trust your neigbors. Then cheap wireless security cameras become available; people start "checking remotely" without physical locks, and within a generation the old shame vanishes. Did that norm collapse because technology changed security interests, or because the honor mechanism got bypassed? Is there one principle—maybe norms survive only when interest and local honor align, and collapse when either breaks—that captures all these? Or do I need separate mechanisms? And what should I say about my camera-door case?
