I have no training in mathematics or philosophy, but I have been thinking alone about what would actually happen to ordinary reasoning if the most basic rules of arithmetic—things like the rules for adding and ordering numbers—turned out to contain a hidden contradiction. Rather than asking for a history of the problem, I want you to rank the following five responses to that hypothetical discovery. Rank them from 1 (the stance that best allows everyday people to keep counting, measuring, and trusting practical math) to 5 (the stance that most thoroughly undermines ordinary mathematical life), and in one sentence for each say why it lands there. Do not cite philosophers by name unless you must, and do not recommend books.

The five stances are:

1. The quarantine view: the contradiction is confined to highly abstract regions of mathematics far removed from school arithmetic, so we build a fence around the damage and continue using numbers exactly as we do now.

2. The formalist retreat: mathematics was never describing an independent reality but only manipulating symbols, so we simply drop the contradictory axiom, adopt a consistent replacement, and keep all practical calculations intact.

3. The platonist crisis: if arithmetic—seemingly the most secure part of reality—is broken, then no abstract structure can be trusted, and we must abandon confidence in any mathematical truth that is not directly checked by hand.

4. The constructivist purge: we reject every result that depends, even indirectly, on the contradictory rule, and we rebuild acceptable mathematics only from steps that can be explicitly constructed and verified one at a time.

5. The shadow-consistency idea (my own attempt at framing it): the contradiction might exist only in proofs so long that no human or computer could ever complete them, meaning arithmetic is technically broken in principle yet permanently safe in practice—like a bridge with a crack too deep to reach from the surface.

Please give me the ranking 1 through 5, with the brief justification for each.
