During my career as a physical chemist investigating amorphous solids, I seldom questioned the stability of the technical vocabulary my colleagues employed; since retiring and enrolling in a philosophy of science seminar, however, I have become preoccupied with how narrowly circumscribed concepts—those operating within a single experimental microdomain rather than across disciplines—undergo subtle but consequential shifts in meaning. I am writing to ask for a careful comparison of two philosophical accounts of this phenomenon that I have sketched for myself. On the one hand, there is what I will call the copycat thesis: the view that conceptual slippage in a microdomain occurs chiefly through imperfect mimetic transmission, wherein practitioners reproduce the surface linguistic form of an expert judgment without acquiring the tacit calibration conditions that originally anchored the concept, producing what might be described as a hollowed-out replica that retains practical utility while losing its theoretical grounding. On the other hand, there is the fluid-boundary thesis: the view that such concepts are not improperly copied but are intrinsically unstable, continuously dissolved and reconstituted in response to local instrumental anomalies, so that apparent slippage is in fact an adaptive restructuring of both extension and intension in direct response to the domain's variability.

I would like you to compare these two positions specifically with respect to conceptual change in a tightly bounded technical domain—namely, the classification of transitional configurational states in polymer glasses cooled well below their conventional vitrification temperatures, where practitioners must distinguish boundary phenomena that standard crystallographic taxonomies cannot accommodate. In particular, I am interested in whether the copycat model necessarily treats such slippage as an epistemic failure correctable through improved pedagogy or stricter definitional protocols, while the fluid-boundary model treats it as an ontologically appropriate response to the inherent non-ergodicity of the material system itself. Furthermore, I wonder whether either framework can adequately account for the peculiar observation—one that I noticed repeatedly in my own laboratory—that within these microdomains practitioners often maintain robust agreement on practical judgments and operational protocols long after they have lost any shared consensus on the theoretical conditions that are supposed to justify those judgments. Could you examine the strengths and limitations of each view in explaining this decoupling of practical consensus from theoretical coherence?
