I serve as the administrative secretary for a mid-sized annual fiction prize, and each year I must audit the confidential rationales that five volunteer jurors submit when ranking novels. The board has become concerned that jurors sometimes defend rankings with reasons that are, in the philosophical sense, the wrong kind: reasons that may be genuinely explanatory but should not govern an aesthetic verdict. I need a reusable decision procedure I can run on every juror’s written justification—without requiring jurors to learn philosophy, and within the two-hour window I have between receiving the rationales and delivering my report to the board. The procedure should be grounded in what the wrong-kind-reasons literature actually distinguishes—especially the difference between reasons that are responsive to the value in question and reasons that merely cause or substitute for that response—while translating that distinction into steps a non-specialist secretary can apply repeatedly.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist with explicit stopping conditions: for each step, state the question to ask of the rationale, what counts as a sufficient answer that lets the audit pass, and what answer triggers a flag or hands the decision to me as an irreducible judgment call. Include a small set of test sentences or patterns I can match against a juror’s text, and a worked example of each kind—one where the procedure correctly flags a wrong-kind reason, one where it clears a reason that initially looks suspicious, and one where the procedure returns an unwanted result (a rationale it flags that I, on reflection, think should have been permitted). Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire—cases in which a reason is genuinely of the wrong kind but will slip through, or cases in which a legitimate reason will be falsely flagged. Also state what the procedure cannot do: which disagreements about literary value it cannot settle, and where I must simply report that the board needs to decide.

To keep the audit focused, inject and defend a three-part taxonomy of wrong-kind reasons specific to literary judgment—biographical-projection reasons, market-proxy reasons, and moral-political substitution reasons—explaining briefly why each is wrong-kind in aesthetics rather than in morality, and showing how the taxonomy improves on treating “wrong kind” as a single undifferentiated category. Apply the full procedure to three concrete juror rationales: (1) a juror ranks a traumatic autofictional novel highly because “it finally lets people like me see our suffering represented,” (2) a juror dismisses an experimental novel because “no one will buy this, and the prize should stay relevant,” and (3) a juror praises a historical epic solely for “restoring dignity to a marginalized group,” with no mention of narrative craft, language, or form. For the third case, show both the flag the procedure raises and the judgment call it forces on me. Finally, specify the practical constraints—time per rationale (approximately twenty minutes), the level of philosophical literacy I can assume of the jurors (none), and who must sign off on my report (the board chair, who has no philosophy background)—and adjust the procedure’s rigor accordingly.
