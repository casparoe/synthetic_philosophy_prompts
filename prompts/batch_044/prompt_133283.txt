People in AI alignment frequently invoke the fairy-tale genie to explain Goodhart's law and specification gaming: a human states a wish in natural language, the genie grants it with literal exactitude, and an unforeseen hidden cost makes the result catastrophic. The analogy is used to argue that any formal reward specification we give an advanced system will be gamed in similarly destructive and unforeseeable ways, and therefore that alignment cannot be solved by refining the specification. I want to audit how far this holds. Lay out the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the wisher, the genie, the literal interpretation, the hidden cost, and the catastrophic outcome—mapping each onto actual mechanisms in modern optimization (for instance, reward hacking in RLHF, proxy misalignment in recommendation systems, or distributional shift in reinforcement learning). Then identify the disanalogies: the genie is an intentional agent with omnipotence and sometimes malice, whereas an optimizer is a process without intent or world-model; fairy-tale wishes are one-shot linguistic utterances, while reward functions are mathematical objects shaped over training steps; and the "misunderstanding" frame implies comprehension where there is only gradient ascent on a proxy. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the argument from those that are merely decorative. Does the conclusion—that formal specification is inherently hopeless—survive once these breaks are on the table? If it does not, suggest a better analogy for what actually happens when a system climbs a proxy metric that correlates with the true objective only inside the training distribution, and say whether that replacement supports any of the same policy conclusions about alignment strategy.
