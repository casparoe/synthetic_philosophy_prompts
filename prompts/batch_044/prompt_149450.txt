You serve as a senior analyst in a jurisdiction’s Sentencing Integrity Office, which must review every proposed sentence exceeding twelve months to certify that it respects both backward-looking culpability limits and forward-looking statutory efficacy targets. Your office receives no philosophical briefs and has no research staff; you need a reusable, thirty-minute audit procedure that translates these two theoretical commitments into an ordered worksheet with explicit stopping rules, conflict flags, and points where a values judgment—not an analyst—must sign off.

Use only the synthetic operational framework defined here. Define the Culpability Corridor (CC) as the range of sentences proportionate to the Offense-Gravity Index (OGI, scaled 1–10 from statutory harm tables and verified actual harm) adjusted by mental-state tier (intentional, reckless, negligent, or strict liability with mental-health mitigation credits) and bounded by statutory aggravation caps and mitigation floors. Define the Risk-Reduction Floor (RRF) as the minimum sentence required to meet the jurisdiction’s statutory targets for specific deterrence, general deterrence, and incapacitation, computed from the validated recidivism-risk score and the rehabilitation-time estimate; where data are missing, apply the conservative default rather than imputing a number.

Write the procedure as a numbered flow in words: the exact questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a rejection or certification, and where it must halt and escalate to a three-person Values Panel because the two anchors conflict or because culpability is irreducibly ambiguous. Build in explicit handling for: (i) mental-state ambiguity that blocks OGI tier assignment; (ii) full voluntary restitution made before sentencing; (iii) cases where the RRF exceeds the CC upper bound; (iv) collective or corporate offenses with diffuse culpability; and (v) proposals that fall below the CC lower bound but meet or exceed the RRF.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

A. A first-time offender convicted of intentional fraud with OGI 7; full restitution has been made; recidivism risk is low; the proposed sentence is eighteen months with intensive post-release monitoring. The CC, after applying the standard restitution credit, runs from 2.5 to 6 years; the RRF is 1 year. Show exactly where the procedure stops, what certification or rejection it issues, and what it requires you to document—even if the result conflicts with an intuition that full restitution should largely cancel prison time.

B. A repeat offender convicted of reckless assault causing permanent disability (OGI 9); mental-state is clearly reckless but not intentional; recidivism risk is high; the proposed sentence is 10 years. The CC runs from 4 to 8 years; the RRF, driven primarily by incapacitation targets, is 10 years. Show the conflict-mode pathway, state precisely what the analyst must document before escalation, and say what the Values Panel—not the analyst—is being asked to resolve.

C. A corporate negligence case involving an industrial chemical release; three managers share decision-making authority; forensic reports disagree on whether the mental state was reckless or merely negligent; harm assessments vary by a factor of three depending on which exposure model is used. Show where the procedure breaks, identify which step produces the halt, and specify exactly what information the hand-off to the clinical-legal referee must contain.

Finally, list three structural limitations of the procedure—things it cannot do in principle, not merely practical constraints—such as resolving conflicts between the anchors without human judgment, substituting for clinical-legal mental-state adjudication, or translating a statistical risk score into an individual desert claim. Present the procedure formally enough that a non-philosopher can run it with a worksheet, but preserve the theoretical distinction intact: the corridor governs what is deserved, the floor governs what efficacy requires, and neither may be collapsed into the other. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for external sources.
