As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science and measurement theory, I now chair a university committee that allocates seed grants for interdisciplinary pilot studies spanning the natural and social sciences. We have adopted quantitative evaluation metrics—citation velocity, cross-disciplinary collaboration scores, and replication-rate proxies—to determine which preliminary projects should be scaled. Having encountered Goodhart’s law and the problem of specification gaming, I am convinced that our metrics are being optimized against rather than serving as faithful indicators of scientific quality. The committee, however, needs an operational framework, not merely a warning.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist with explicit stopping rules and clearly flagged judgment points—that my committee can apply to any proposed evaluation metric before formal adoption. The procedure should be grounded in the conceptual distinction between proxy corruption, in which the metric decouples causally from its intended target, and target displacement, in which the system being evaluated redefines success so that it coincides with the metric. It should also include a specific diagnostic for what I shall term reflexive gaming: cases in which researchers anticipate the evaluation criterion and alter their study design not to improve the underlying phenomenon, but to generate the appearance of improvement without producing detectable proxy corruption. The procedure must specify, in order: (1) preliminary questions regarding causal coupling between the measurement apparatus and the phenomenon measured; (2) conditions under which a metric must be rejected outright; (3) conditions under which it may be retained with institutional safeguards; and (4) the precise points at which no procedural rule can substitute for an explicit normative judgment about what the committee actually values in scientific work.

Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) using replication-rate proxies to evaluate pilot studies in behavioral psychology; (b) using cross-disciplinary citation counts to evaluate collaborations between climate science and the humanities; and (c) a hypothetical scenario in which the procedure recommends retaining a metric with safeguards, yet the recommendation conflicts with institutional incentives for rapid, quantifiable outcomes and is therefore likely to be resisted by the committee. Finally, articulate what the procedure is structurally unable to determine, particularly with respect to the prior selection of the target phenomenon itself. Constraints: the committee meets monthly, members have no formal training in philosophy or statistics, and any recommendation must be defensible to a non-specialist university administrator within a ten-minute presentation.
