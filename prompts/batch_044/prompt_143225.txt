I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years working through philosophy of science and moral epistemology, and I am now preparing a proposal for a six-month interdisciplinary workshop on the interpretation of cross-cultural moral psychology. The precise question I want to address is whether the documented variance in folk moral judgments—particularly between WEIRD and non-WEIRD populations—exhibits the statistical structure of inter-instrument disagreement about a shared measurable property, or instead indicates that different cultural groups are indexing conceptually distinct normative dimensions, and what each structural diagnosis implies for the coherence of moral objectivity.

Existing philosophical treatments of this variance tend to fall into two camps: those who treat cross-cultural disagreement as direct evidence for moral relativism, and those who dismiss it as epistemically irrelevant noise that can be corrected by idealized reasoning. Meanwhile, the psychological literature documents divergence in judgments about harm, fairness, and loyalty, but does not ask whether the covariance structure of those judgments supports a common-factor model with culture-specific loadings. Because neither discipline has formalized an analog of calibration error or systematic bias in moral judgment, the debate remains at the level of intuitive example rather than structural analysis, and no clear criteria exist for deciding when disagreement undermines objectivity and when it merely reveals correctable measurement distortion.

This is therefore a philosophical, not an empirical, project. Over the six months I will construct three formal models of inter-group variance—(1) unbiased noise distributed around a universal normative parameter; (2) systematic bias analogous to different zero-points or scale distortions, correctable in principle through calibration functions; and (3) genuinely incommensurable dimensions with no shared parameter to be recovered. I will then apply these models interpretively to the reported patterns in published cross-cultural moral psychology, asking whether the actual divergence in harm and fairness judgments fits the covariance profile expected of calibrated instruments measuring one property, or the profile expected of instruments measuring different ones. The conceptual work lies in defining precisely what it means for a normative judgment to bear an error term, whether moral claims have the descriptive content required for such errors to be coherent, and whether a corrected convergence across cultures is conceptually available or collapses into circularity.

The deliverable will be a 35-to-40-page workshop paper in two parts. Part I formalizes the metrological analogies and distinguishes them from mere metaphor, specifying the conditions under which a moral judgment could be said to be miscalibrated rather than false. Part II evaluates the three variance models against the psychological data patterns, deriving for each what kind of moral objectivity—if any—survives: a thick universalism with cultural calibration, a thin universalism recoverable only after ideal correction, or a pluralism of incommensurable normative frameworks. The intended audience is philosophers of science and moral philosophers who need a non-empirical framework for interpreting psychological findings without committing to new data collection.

A foundation should fund this because the question of whether global moral convergence is structurally possible or conceptually blocked is central to any intervention that assumes cross-cultural moral agreement can be achieved. The project supplies that framework directly.

The project fails if either of two conditions obtains: first, if the calibration analogy collapses because normative judgments lack the requisite descriptive or causal structure to support measurement-error terms; second, if the three variance models turn out to be empirically underdetermined—indistinguishable given any conceivable psychological data—rendering the framework empty for practical decision-making. The scope is realistic for a single scholar with scientific training in measurement theory and recent philosophical coursework: no laboratory, travel, or proprietary database access is required, and the deliverable depends entirely on conceptual analysis of publicly available psychological findings.
