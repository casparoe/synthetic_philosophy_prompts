As someone who spent three decades in experimental biophysics studying open, far-from-equilibrium systems, I have only recently begun to read philosophy in earnest. One concept that has arrested me is the Buddhist doctrine of anatta—the denial of a permanent, underlying self. It strikes me that modern science also rejects substantial persistence: a cell, a flame, or a whirlpool remains itself only through continuous material and energetic exchange, not by retaining identical parts. This suggests a parallel with certain process-oriented theories of personal identity.

Please write an essay of roughly 800 words addressing the following controversy: Is anatta genuinely compatible with a process-identity account of personal persistence—where a person endures not as a substance but as a continuously maintained organizational flow, analogous to a dissipative structure? You should examine the conceptual fit and at least one serious tension (for example, whether anatta's denial of any enduring substrate undermines the very system-boundaries that process identity requires, or whether process identity covertly reintroduces a substantial self through the notion of organizational continuity). Weigh the arguments carefully, and reach a justified conclusion—either that the two frameworks converge in a defensible way, or that they ultimately conflict. I am not looking for a literature review or citations to specific texts; I want the reasoning itself, developed from the concepts alone. Keep the tone rigorous but accessible to a scientifically trained reader new to these debates.
