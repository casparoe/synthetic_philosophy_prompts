I'm currently taking a seminar on cross-cultural moral psychology and the philosophy of emotion, where we've been working through whether guilt or shame is the healthier moral emotion and whether a culture can legitimately choose which one to institutionalize. My professor has taken the following positions in our discussions, which I have reconstructed as consistently as I can.

First, regarding a scholar who falsified data in a single co-authored paper but could fully correct the record through retraction and apology: she argued that guilt is the appropriate emotional response, and that shame is a misfire because it targets the global self rather than the discrete, reparable act.

Second, regarding citizens of a nation that committed historical atrocities in which they played no personal part: she argued that shame, not guilt, is appropriate, since there is no specific deed to repair—only a tainted collective identity.

Third, regarding a modern liberal democratic culture organized around personal accountability and contractual repair: she said it should cultivate guilt as its primary moral emotion.

Fourth, regarding a traditional honor-based society organized around face, hierarchy, and communal standing: she said it should cultivate shame as its primary moral emotion.

Fifth, regarding an individual within that honor society who commits a discrete, reparable wrong—stealing food from a neighbor—and is subjected to public shaming rituals: she insisted that even within that culture, guilt should be cultivated for the individual act, while shame remains appropriate only for collective-status violations.

Now consider a new case. A deliberately engineered intentional community—the Meridian Collective—has reconstructed its social norms from scratch. They organize economic and ecological life collectively, so every member shares responsibility for systemic outputs (resource extraction, climate debt) that no single person can repair alone. They have explicitly abolished hierarchical face and individual blame mechanisms. To preserve egalitarian cohesion, they reject shame as a tool of social ranking. Instead, they institutionalize distributed collective guilt: every member is expected to feel guilty for the system's aggregate harms, and repair is pursued through collective restructuring rather than individual atonement. They ask whether their emotional framework is coherent and whether, on my professor's view, they have selected the correct architecture for their culture.

Please structure your response with the following sections in this order:

1. Track Record and Implicit Rules (about 150 words): lay out the two most plausible rules that jointly explain the five verdicts, showing how each maps onto the cases.

2. The New Case: Two Predictions (about 250 words): state what each rule predicts for the Meridian Collective—whether it endorses their distributed-guilt design, condemns it, or corrects it—and identify the closest analog among the five precedents for each prediction.

3. Best Guess and Adopted Principle (about 200 words): give your best guess at what the professor would actually say, with reasons for preferring that rule, and state explicitly what normative principle she would be adopting either way.

4. The Crucial Distinguishing Case (about 100 words): name one further hypothetical case that would definitively tell the two rules apart.

I'm less interested in a generic essay on guilt and shame than in the consistency analysis: the competing rules, their divergence on this engineered collective, the recommended verdict, and the test case that separates them.
