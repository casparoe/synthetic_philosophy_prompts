As a retired experimental biologist who has recently taken up serious study of philosophy, I am preparing a 75-minute workshop for about fourteen advanced undergraduates in a philosophy-of-mind seminar. I want an in-class game or structured role-play—not a lecture—that makes participants feel the pressure of Buddhist anattā (no-self) and the problem of personal identity before anyone names the doctrine.

The specific angle I want to avoid is the standard Western repertoire of teletransporters, brain fission, and abrupt discontinuities. Instead, I want the activity to work with one continuous biological scenario: a single organism undergoing gradual cellular turnover, emotional habituation, perceptual recalibration, and volitional restructuring across many years. The tension should arise from treating the five skandhas—form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), mental formations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa)—not as parts of a pre-existing person, but as independent streams, each with its own continuity criterion. When all five are given equal weight in the rules, no single “owner” survives intact; yet practical interaction forces the group to conventionally designate one anyway. The participants should discover this split through the mechanics of play.

Please design a fully runnable activity: explicit rules, assigned roles, materials that can be assembled in a seminar room, a payoff or incentive scheme that creates genuine coordination tension without using real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps moments of play onto the distinction between conventional designation (sammuti-sacca) and ultimate analysis (paramattha-sacca).

Please organize your response in the following named sections, with approximate word counts:

1. Conceptual Frame and Activity Overview (approx. 250 words): the continuous biological scenario, the five roles, and what participants should come away seeing.
2. Rules, Materials, and Incentive Structure (approx. 350 words): how play proceeds, how decisions are made, what creates conflict, and the materials needed.
3. Session Timeline (approx. 150 words): minute-by-minute flow for the full 75 minutes.
4. The Debrief: Mapping Play to Anattā (approx. 300 words): connect specific game events to the aggregates and to conventional versus ultimate truth.
5. Predictable Failures and Facilitator Remedies (approx. 150 words): what will go wrong the first time and how to manage it.
6. Honest Limitation (approx. 150 words): which feature of anattā the activity necessarily fails to reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator.
