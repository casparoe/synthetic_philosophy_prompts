I have recently taken up philosophical reading in my retirement from experimental physics, and I attend a small discussion group in which we examine conceptual puzzles that bear on scientific practice. At our last meeting, two members—both with backgrounds in the life sciences—became locked in a disagreement about whether honoring sunk costs can ever be rational, using a concrete case: a research consortium that has spent fifteen years and substantial funding developing a particular theoretical framework that now appears empirically inadequate, yet abandoning it entirely would mean dispersing a highly specialized collaborative network that took decades to build.

The first member, whom I shall call A, argued that continuing to fund or pursue the framework is a clear instance of the sunk cost fallacy. He maintained that rational decision-making is necessarily prospective: only future costs and benefits should enter the calculus. The fact that resources have already been expended is, he said, evidentially and normatively irrelevant. He added that the collaborative network, however valuable, should be preserved only if it can be redirected toward more promising research—not maintained for its own sake.

The second member, B, countered that A was applying a model of rationality appropriate to isolated individual choice, but inappropriate to institutional and epistemic contexts. She proposed a distinction between expendable sunk costs—such as wasted materials or labor hours that can be redirected without loss—and constitutive sunk costs, which are investments that have become woven into the identity and operational capacity of an institution. To abandon the framework entirely, she argued, is not merely to waste past funding but to dissolve a specific form of collective expertise that cannot be reconstructed elsewhere. Therefore, continuing to work within the framework for a transitional period, even knowing it is likely wrong, can be rational as a form of epistemic stewardship: it allows for an orderly transfer of methodological knowledge rather than abrupt dissolution.

A responded that B had simply renamed the fallacy: calling a cost constitutive does not change its sunk status. If the framework is empirically inadequate, then preserving it—whether for institutional continuity or knowledge transfer—still amounts to throwing good resources after bad, unless there is an independent, forward-looking justification for the preservation of the network. He insisted that B's epistemic stewardship was just a post-hoc rationalization.

B then made a further move. She argued that in scientific practice, the evaluation of a theoretical framework is rarely binary—adequate versus inadequate—but occurs along a continuum of partial success and auxiliary modification. She suggested that what appears to be honoring sunk costs might actually be a rational commitment to theoretical refinement in the face of anomalous data, a practice essential to the history of science. To treat every empirical difficulty as grounds for immediate abandonment, she said, would make long-term theoretical development impossible. She concluded that A's prospective-only criterion, if applied consistently, would undermine the very possibility of sustained scientific inquiry.

A conceded that theoretical refinement is rational, but maintained that this is distinct from honoring sunk costs: one can refine or redirect without being motivated by past investment. He asked B to specify exactly what makes her case different from a straightforward fallacy, other than her labeling it constitutive.

Neither would concede. I am trying to understand whether B has identified a genuine exception to the irrationality of honoring sunk costs, or whether she has merely obscured the fallacy with institutional and epistemic language. Specifically: is A correct that B's argument collapses into a standard fallacy once we demand an independent forward-looking justification? Or does B's distinction between expendable and constitutive sunk costs genuinely alter the normative evaluation? And what, if anything, are both failing to address—perhaps regarding the temporal structure of rational commitment, or the difference between individual and collective agency?

Please adjudicate this disagreement point by point, indicating who is right about which specific claim, and identify any important considerations that both parties have overlooked.
