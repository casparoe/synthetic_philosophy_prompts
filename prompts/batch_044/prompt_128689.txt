After a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend last night about collective memory and historical record, I find myself stuck on a specific formulation that I think explains why the debate never actually moves. The scenario is this: a community maintains a robust internal narrative about a foundational event—say, a treaty negotiation, a forced displacement, or a massacre—transmitted through ritual, oral testimony, and commemorative practice. The documentary record, assembled from state archives and contemporaneous administrative sources, contradicts key details: dates, participants, terms, or even whether the event occurred precisely as remembered. One camp, which I will call the archivalists, insists that when the two conflict, the historical record should correct the error and the community should eventually integrate that correction, because allowing collective memory to override documented fact risks mythologizing the past and eroding shared standards of evidence. The other camp, the mnemonic sovereigntists, argues that the archive itself is an instrument of the very power that produced the harm, and that subordinating living memory to documentary proof reproduces an epistemic injustice; for them, the community’s narrative should retain authority in public institutions—schools, museums, legal proceedings—because its function is not error-tracking but identity-constitution and moral orientation.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about facts—everyone concedes the documents exist and say what they say—nor about whether suffering is real. Rather, it seems to be a concealed dispute about the purpose of historical knowledge itself: whether history is essentially a corrective practice, like science, that must privilege the best available evidence regardless of social cost, or whether it is one practice among several that includes constitutive memory, in which case demanding that all narratives converge on a single documentary standard is itself a category mistake. But I also wonder whether there is a hidden empirical disagreement about whether communities that adopt archival corrections suffer measurable harm to social cohesion or political agency—something neither side has actually studied in the cases they cite—or whether both sides are simply answering different questions, one about epistemic authority and one about justice and repair, under the single heading of “what should we teach?” So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a substantive clash about epistemic norms, a verbal confusion between “history” as inquiry and “memory” as practice, a disagreement about which moral reasons take precedence, or bedrock intuitions about belonging and naturalness that neither side can argue for? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—an institutional design that separates commemorative and evidentiary functions, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the conflict, an empirical study that resolves the hidden factual claim, or an honest admission that the impasse is permanent because the two practices serve incommensurable human needs?
