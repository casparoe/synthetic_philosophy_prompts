I am a journalist who covers science and the history of ideas, and in two weeks I will present a twenty-minute position paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on historical epistemology. The audience will be split between ancient historians who treat source criticism as a craft discipline rooted in close textual practice, philosophers of science who work on inference to the best explanation and are instinctively hostile to circular calibration, and fellow science journalists who worry that any erosion of evidentiary independence will undermine public trust in archaeological reporting. My thesis is that the standard model of source criticism—treating Herodotean testimony based on direct observation and Thucydidean cross-examination as one evidentiary stream that can be independently weighed against material and stratigraphic data—rests on a false assumption of linear independence. Because archaeological interpretation is already theory-laden by the very textual narratives it claims to verify, we do not have a foundational baseline from which to triangulate; instead, we face a coherentist calibration loop in which the credibility of specific ancient reports and the reading of physical remains are mutually adjusted in a non-foundational web, with no privileged entry point. I want to defend this without sounding as if I am dismissing the historians' craft, without collapsing into the claim that all ancient history is mere fiction, and without leaving the journalists in the room convinced that I have destroyed the epistemic basis for their reporting. Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face from this specific audience: list them in roughly the order they will arise, phrase each the way that subgroup—historians, philosophers, or journalists—would put it, note which objections carry real force, tell me what I should concede up front, provide a concise reply for each, flag where my reply is weakest, and identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks—particularly any challenge that forces me to specify exactly how this loop assigns differential credibility to a particular passage without begging the question against the very textual tradition it calibrates. The prep should be rigorous but accessible, appropriate for a specialist audience being addressed by a journalist who is not a professional philosopher of history.
