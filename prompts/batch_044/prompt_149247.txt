Design a final exam essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, for an upper-level seminar on classical Chinese metaphysics and the philosophy of language. The question must center on Gongsun Long's White Horse dialogue and the broader concerns of the School of Names, but it should not be answerable by merely summarizing the standard form-versus-color distinction. To achieve this, build the question around the following niche extension: ask students to consider whether the dialogue's separation of horseness (as form) and whiteness (as color) can be coherently applied to a photorealistic digital rendering of a white horse displayed on an OLED screen—specifically, whether such an image is a white horse, and whether this extension exposes a hidden ontological commitment in the original argument, such as the claim that properties must be physically instantiated rather than merely represented.

The exam is closed-book and must be completable in approximately 75 minutes by students who have studied the dialogue in translation and discussed it in seminar, but who have no access to external resources. The prompt should require students to reconstruct the original argument precisely, apply it to the digital case, identify the hidden premise about property ontology, evaluate whether the extension strengthens or undermines the School of Names position, and reach a defended conclusion.

Please provide:
1. The exam question, clearly framing the digital-rendering extension and specifying exactly which analytical moves students must perform.
2. A model answer that demonstrates the required moves: accurate reconstruction of the form/color separation; rigorous application to the rendered image; explicit identification of the hidden physical-instantiation premise; evaluation of the extension's impact on the School of Names; and a concise, argued conclusion.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, broken into the five moves above, with marks assigned to each. For each component, include a one-line description of the characteristic error that should reduce the score—e.g., confusing the logical distinction with a merely perceptual one, or treating the digital case as obviously irrelevant without argument.
