I've been thinking about why the Principal Principle debate never really moves. The basic claim — that your rational credence in E should match the known objective probabilty of E — gets pushed back by causal decision theorists and subjectivists who say you should sometimes two-box or update on mental-state evidence in ways that break the alignment. But what's weird is that both sides run through the same standard examples — Newcomb's problem, Sleeping Beauty, the big bad bug — and nobody budges. Is this actually a substantive disagreement about rationality, or is it verbal, with 'objective chance' meaning something different for each camp? Or are they just answering different questions: one about belief-world fit, another about what makes a physical probability real?

Here's a niche twist I haven't seen discussed: imagine a deterministic branching system where each branch gets assigned a weight that behaves exactly like a chance, and an agent inside one branch can observe the branching rule but not which brnach she's in. She knows her branch's weight is 0.3, but she also has direct first-person evidence that makes her own branch more likely from her viewpoint. The deferentialists say she should stick to 0.3; the autonomists say she should update on her first-person evidence and ignore the weight. Then they just stare at each other.

So what's the real diagnosis? Is there a hidden empirical question about how scientists actually defer to climate-model ensembles? Is the disagreement about standards of evidence — what counts as 'knowing the chance'? Or are these bedrock intuitions about first-person versus third-person reasoning that neither side can argue for? And if it's just two questions under one name, what distinction or formal result would actually force progress? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, not just hard?
