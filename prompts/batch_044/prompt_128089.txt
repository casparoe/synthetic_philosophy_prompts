An essay I read recently tries to explain why iterated amplification, followed by distillation, should preserve both competence and alignment. Its summary runs roughly like this: amplification works by decomposing a hard problem into subproblems that either the base model or a human supervisor can solve, producing a tree of verified steps; a smaller model is then distilled on these trajectories so that it learns the decomposition strategy itself. Because the student only ever trains on steps that lie within its teachers' competence zones, the author claims it can never acquire capabilities beyond those zones, which rules out rapid takeoff or an intelligence explosion. For alignment, the essay proposes a multiplicative preservation law: if each node in the tree is aligned with error rate ε, then an n-level tree has aggregate misalignment on the order of εⁿ, which shrinks exponentially with depth, so deeper decomposition is actually safer. The author adds that since every node is individually interpretable, the entire tree is automatically transparent, and therefore concludes that this framework solves both the competence-preservation and black-box-control problems for advanced systems.

This sounds mostly coherent to me, but I am not sure which parts are standard and which are distortions. Could you audit the summary point by point? Specifically: (1) Is it correct that distillation after amplification prevents the student from exceeding per-subtask teacher competence, or does it miss something about meta-learning and strategic decomposition? (2) Is the multiplicative error-rate claim mathematically and conceptually sound for alignment failures, or does it confuse independent failures with cascading or correlated ones? (3) Does node-level interpretability genuinely entail tree-level transparency, or is there a well-known gap here? (4) Given the corrections, does the conclusion—that this combination solves both alignment and control—follow, or is it an overstatement? Please be explicit about what a researcher in this area would accept, what they would wince at, and whether any part is simply wrong.
