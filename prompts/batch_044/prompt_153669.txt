Write a short story set in the administrative archive of a reconstructed city-state whose governing council has adopted an epistemology drawn from Keynes’s distinction between risk and true uncertainty: decisions are made not by computing numerical probabilities but by assessing the weight of argument, understood as the ratio of relevant evidence to total ignorance, recorded in physical ledgers called Uncertainty Registers. The protagonist is an archivist responsible for maintaining these registers. She discovers that the register for a critical infrastructure decision—whether to reinforce a seawall against an unprecedented storm pattern—contains high weight but contradictory testimony, while a competing proposal rests on low weight yet unanimous agreement. The narrative should dramatize what it means to act when no frequency distribution is available and mathematical expectation cannot be calculated, showing how the council’s refusal to reduce uncertainty to quantified risk produces a specific, irreversible human consequence. Embody the philosophical tension through the texture of the ledgers, the archivist’s procedural choices, and the final decision made under radical ignorance, without using expository lectures or direct citation of philosophical texts.
