Consider the analogy frequently used to defend speciesism against charges of arbitrariness: moral obligations across species boundaries are like obligations across national borders. Just as a state may legitimately prioritize its own citizens in distributing scarce resources, protection, or political rights—without this being the same kind of moral error as racism—so humans may prioritize members of Homo sapiens without committing an analogous prejudice. The analogy is meant to show that species membership, like citizenship, is a morally relevant boundary rather than a merely biological one.

Audit this analogy with precision. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to citizenship, to the state apparatus, to immigration and naturalization, to the cosmopolitan critic who denies special obligations, and to the welfare goods being distributed? Second, identify the disanalogies that are structurally relevant: citizenship is a legal status sustained by institutional practices and is in principle revocable or transferable, whereas species membership is biologically fixed and involuntary; citizens typically participate in reciprocal schemes of cooperation and contract, while many animals (and some humans) do not; and the cosmopolitan argument against nationalism usually turns on the arbitrariness of birth location, which may not map cleanly onto the arbitrariness of birth species. Third, examine what the analogy quietly smuggles in about the value of the whole versus the parts—does it presuppose that moral community requires a cooperative scheme of mutual benefit, and if so, how does it accommodate marginal human cases that fall outside reciprocity, or highly social nonhuman animals that fall within it?

Finally, inject the following complication: suppose moral status is not binary membership but scalar addressability—the degree to which an entity can be the direct target of moral address within a shared practice. Apply this scalar framework to both the national and the species case. Does the analogy still license the conclusion that human priority is permissible, or does it collapse into graded obligations tracking cognitive and social capacity rather than taxonomy? If the analogy breaks under this injection, propose a more honest replacement—perhaps comparing species priorities to obligations within linguistic communities or ecological niches—and assess whether that replacement fares better.
