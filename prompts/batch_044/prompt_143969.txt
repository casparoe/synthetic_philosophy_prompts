I'm trying to figure out my actual stance on linguistic relativity, going by gut verdicts on concrete cases rather than repeating the usual examples. Here they are. Russian speakers have separate basic color terms for light and dark blue; I think they do discriminate shades faster, but its just a tiny perceptual nudge, not a deep worldview shift. The Eskimo snow-words claim feels like a myth that got blown out of proportion, though I still suspect richer snow vocabulary makes those distinctions more salient in practice. Languages that grammatically force evidentiality—where you must mark whether you saw, heard, or inferred something—I think genuinely make speakers more attuned to source reliability, not just in speech. But the idea that Hopi lacks future tense so its speakers dont conceive of future time? Nonsense, they clearly plan ahead. Bilinguals dont have two personalities, but framing a moral dilemma in one language vs another can shift the answer slightly—though I cant tell if thats language or culture riding along. Then theres Guugu Yimithirr, with absolute cardinal directions used even indoors; I buy that speakers navigate better because of it. Heres the tension: I want to say language reshapes cognition in small domain-specific ways, yet I feel strongly that if you cant express a thought in your language you can still think it, just with more effort. So is there a coherent principle that captures all of these? State it precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, say what it predicts for a case I havent listed—say, a language with no word for fairness but twenty terms for reciprocity—and be honest about which of my verdicts it cant save.
