Consider the counterfactual in which G.E.M. Anscombe lived into the present and was asked to evaluate lethal autonomous weapons systems that employ predictive-targeting algorithms—systems that identify, prioritize, and strike human targets without a discrete, case-by-case human authorization for each engagement, operating instead within strategic parameters set by human commanders. Drawing on her well-known opposition to Harry Truman's authorization of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki—specifically her insistence that deliberately directing harm at the innocent is never permissible, however good the intended consequence, and her sharp distinction between intending harm and merely foreseeing it—how do you think she would have assessed these algorithmically mediated strikes? Would she have judged that the lethal decision remains directly intended by the human designers and operators, falling squarely under her prohibition, or that the algorithmic mediation dilutes intentionality to the point where the harm is only foreseen? Furthermore, how might she have treated the argument that such systems reduce collateral damage through superior statistical precision: would she have dismissed improved consequential outcomes as morally irrelevant to the permissibility of the act, parallel to her dismissal of saved Allied lives in the atomic case? And finally, how do you think she would have addressed the claim that distributing lethal authority across a human-machine architecture diffuses moral responsibility in a way that mitigates, rather than compounds, culpability?
