As a retired physicist who has spent the last few years working through philosophy of mind, I have tried to fix my position on Mary’s room and the knowledge argument. Before I present this at a discussion group, I want an audit of whether these commitments hang together. I hold the following:

(1) I am a physicalist: every phenomenal property is identical with some physical or functional property of the brain, leaving no ontological remainder.

(2) I accept that Mary genuinely learns something new when she first experiences color—there is a fact about what it is like to see red that she did not know in her monochrome environment.

(3) I deny that this new fact is non-physical. It is a physical fact to which she previously lacked epistemic access, not an additional ontological category.

(4) I reject indexical and perspectival explanations of Mary’s gain: she does not merely learn “what red is like for me” or “this is my experience”; she acquires knowledge of an objective feature of the experience-type itself.

(5) I maintain that phenomenal concepts are conceptually irreducible to physical concepts because they are transparent—possessing such a concept requires being able to undergo the experience—and this transparency generates the explanatory gap without implying dualism.

(6) I reject the ability hypothesis: Mary acquires propositional knowledge, not merely a new recognition skill, imaginative capacity, or behavioral disposition.

(7) I think that if physicalism is correct, then complete physical knowledge should in principle entail all mental knowledge; Mary’s inability to derive phenomenal knowledge from her physical education therefore represents a genuine challenge to physicalism rather than a mere appearance.

(8) I regard zombies—physical duplicates lacking consciousness—as metaphysically impossible, though I concede they are conceptually coherent.

(9) I take conceivability to be a defeasible, but real, guide to metaphysical possibility.

Please examine this set for internal tension. For each pair that genuinely conflicts, indicate how severe the tension is and what the least costly revision would be; for pairs that only appear to conflict, explain why the appearance dissolves. Rank the tensions by severity. Then identify which single revision to this package would eliminate the most conflict overall. In your response, please separate what is broadly settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as the precise status of phenomenal concepts or the strength of the conceivability-possibility link—as if it were the consensus view.
