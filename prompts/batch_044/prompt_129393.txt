As a retired physical chemist who has recently turned to philosophy in earnest, I wish to establish the full specification—before assessing particular answers—of what an adequate theory of material constitution must achieve. I take the canonical puzzle to be that of a statue and the clay that composes it, but I want the criteria to cover living systems, engineered objects, and boundary cases that physical science actually presents.

Please write the desiderata: the complete list of requirements any adequate theory of material constitution ought to satisfy, with the motivating case or consideration behind each, and an indication of whether each requirement is non-negotiable or merely the preference of a particular camp. In particular, your list should address:

1. The statue and the clay themselves—whether they are identical, distinct but co-located, or otherwise related, and what ontological commitments each option carries.
2. Temporal persistence through gradual part-replacement, including not only the ship of Theseus but also the biological case of an organism that retains functional and structural continuity despite complete metabolic turnover of its constituent atoms.
3. Fission and fusion: cases in which one object divides into two, or two merge into one, and whether the theory permits ambiguity or demands a unique answer.
4. The distinction between constitution-at-a-time and constitution-over-time, and whether a theory may legitimately collapse these.
5. Compatibility with physical conservation laws and with the absence of any privileged spatial frame or absolute simultaneity in modern physics.
6. A constructed case I ask you to treat seriously: an artefact built by layer-by-layer deposition of chemically uniform ceramic around a temporary polymer scaffold that is subsequently dissolved without trace, so that the finished object never existed as a single continuous mass of matter prior to assembly, and its parts were never jointly located in one pre-existing lump. Call this the dissolved-scaffold case. Must a theory treat it differently in kind from the statue, or only in degree?

Then identify any subset of these desiderata that you take to be jointly unsatisfiable given standard logical or physical constraints, explaining the tension precisely. Keep the weight of your answer on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.

Briefly—without developing full defences—indicate how the principal competing frameworks fare against your list: strict identity of object and matter, the constitution view (with or without relative identity), mereological essentialism, and four-dimensional perdurantism. Emphasise where each fails a non-negotiable requirement or pays an unexpected cost.

Please write in British English, avoid any bibliography, citation, or instruction to consult specific texts, and do not quote verbatim from named authors. Conclude with a one-sentence summary on the final line by itself.
