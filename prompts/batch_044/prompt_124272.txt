I'm working through my intuitions about supertasks—specifically about whether completing an infinite sequence of actions in a finite interval can leave behind a determinate state, and what kind of determinacy is even at stake. I want to see if there's a coherent principle behind my verdicts, or if I have to drop some.

Here are my judgments.

1. The classic Thomson lamp: toggled every half-unit of time, reaching time 1. I think the scenario is logically possible, but I don't think there is any fact of the matter about whether the lamp is on or off at time 1. It's not epistemic ignorance; the world simply doesn't settle it.

2. A digit-writing supertask: in the same interval, a machine writes the successive decimal digits of π. At time 1, I think there is a fact of the matter that the full expansion of π is inscribed, even though there is no 'last digit' written. So determinacy can hold for the global pattern without holding for a local endpoint.

3. A parity-counter lamp: like case 1, but each toggle also increments a binary counter displaying whether the total number of toggles so far is even or odd. At time 1, I think the counter should display something definite, yet I can't consistently assign it even or odd. Still, I feel it should not display an error-state or remain blank; that feels like a different kind of failure than the lamp being on or off.

4. Harm-aggregation supertask: if a being suffers once per toggle, I think an infinite amount of suffering can occur in the finite interval, but I also think it's incoherent to ask for the cardinal total ('how many sufferings?'). So I accept infinite occurrence without accepting infinite sum.

5. A convergent-final-toggle variant: toggles occur at times 1 − 1/2^n, and there is one additional toggle exactly at time 1. Here I think there is a determinate state at time 1, opposite to the state immediately prior. This makes me think that when the sequence has a well-defined 'limit action,' determinacy returns.

6. The paired reverse-lamps: two lamps start in opposite states and are toggled on the identical Zeno schedule with no final toggle at time 1. I think each lamp's state at time 1 is individually indeterminate, yet I also think they should still be in opposite states—because they were always opposite and nothing broke the symmetry. But I can't derive that conclusion from any property of either lamp alone.

The case I keep oscillating on is a density-assignment supertask: assign each rational in (0,1) a unique toggle time, ordered by denominator then numerator, so that toggle times accumulate everywhere near 0 and near 1 with no uniform spacing. At the 'completion' point—say, after all rationals have been assigned—does it make sense to ask what the 'toggle density' near 0 is? I sometimes think yes, because every neighborhood contains infinitely many toggles; sometimes I think no, because there's no measure that respects the ordering.

Can you propose one or more precise principles that capture the pattern in 1–6, stating for each clause which case motivates it and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed—say, a supertask that builds a physical wall by placing one brick at each toggle, with the wall's height proportional to the toggle index? And please be explicit about which of my six verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and whether saving it requires making the principle implausibly complex. I am not looking for a bibliography; I just want the reflective equilibrium worked out.
