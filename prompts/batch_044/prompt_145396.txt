I've been trying to map out what a philosophically serious defense of Buddhist anatta actually has to deliver if it wants to avoid collapsing into either a hard eliminativism about the person or a crypto-substantialist view that sneaks an enduring ego back in through the side door. The context is a seminar discussion where someone argued that once you reject the atman, you have no resources to distinguish 'my' suffering from 'yours' in a way that grounds moral accountability or explains why karmic continuity tracks particular psychophysical streams rather than dissipating randomly across lives. I want to get ahead of that debate by laying out the specification first.

Write the desiderata that any adequate philosophical account of personal identity under the anatta doctrine must satisfy. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it: for instance, the need to distinguish conventional designation from ultimate non-existence without treating the conventional self as a fiction with no normative weight; the need to account for first-personal memory and anticipatory concern without positing a unitary subject that persists unchanged; the need to explain karmic 'ownership'—why actions and their fruits belong to this stream rather than another—without invoking a soul-substance; and the need to preserve asymmetries in first-person versus third-person access to mental states without reintroducing an ego-core. Mark which of these are non-negotiable given classical Buddhist commitments (e.g., rejection of svabhava or permanent essence in the psychophysical aggregates) and which reflect modern philosophical preferences that particular camps might relax (e.g., demands for narrative unity across a lifespan, or reduction to momentary dharmas with causal continuity alone). Crucially, examine whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—whether, for example, robust moral accountability requires a thicker conception of agency than anatta allows, or whether explaining indexical persistence (the sense that this very awareness, rather than some other, inherits past actions) forces us to smuggle in a substrate the doctrine explicitly denies. Keep the analytical weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on grading specific theories, though brief notes on how an Abhidharma reductionist view, a Yogacara continuity model, and a modern 'thin' phenomenological account handle one or two key requirements would help orient the discussion. Do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I want to evaluate the conceptual architecture, not track sources.
