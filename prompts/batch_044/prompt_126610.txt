I'm writing up my philosophical commitments on rational self-trust for a logic and decision-theory reading group, and I'd like a consistency audit before I present. Here is roughly where I've landed: (1) I accept the standard Löbian obstacle—any formal system strong enough to represent arithmetic and its own proof predicate cannot consistently prove its own consistency—so I reject flat, global self-trust for agents with that representational power. (2) Rather than abandoning self-trust entirely, I defend a "modular exemption" view: the agent partitions its beliefs into isolated modules (e.g., a decision-theoretic module, a perceptual module, and a meta-logical module), applies full self-trust within each module, but imposes a hard firewall so that no module can endorse propositions about the global consistency of the whole system. (3) Within the decision-theoretic module, I endorse causal decision theory, which means that in Newcomb's problem one should two-box, since the choice is causally independent of the predictor's past action. (4) I also accept a restricted form of van Fraassen's Reflection principle: within any single module, an agent's present credence in a proposition should match its anticipated post-reflection credence, but cross-module credences need not align, and meta-logical propositions are excluded altogether. (5) Finally, I think this architecture genuinely avoids the Löbian obstacle because the consistency proof available within any single module is too weak to trigger Löb's theorem, yet the agent remains fully rational inside each domain.

My worry is that hidden tensions may connect these commitments in ways I haven't noticed. Specifically, does the modular exemption in (2) actually block the obstacle, or does the agent's ability to compare outputs across modules recreate a global self-model that reintroduces it? Does my acceptance of Reflection within modules in (4) implicitly commit me to cross-module coherence that undermines the firewall in (2)? And is there an unnoticed conflict between my causal-decision-theoretic verdict in (3) and the kind of self-prediction that modular self-trust in (2) seems to require—if I trust my decision module to predict its own behavior, am I implicitly relying on an evidential correlation that causal decision theory denies? Please audit this package: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension versus only apparently so, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each specify the cheapest revision and what it costs. Don't treat every pairing as problematic; discriminate carefully, and say where a tension dissolves on closer inspection.
