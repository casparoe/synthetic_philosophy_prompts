ok so i was arguing with some friends last week about democratic theory and realized none of us actually feel the boundary problem in our bones — it just sounds like a taxonomy exercise. i'm running a workshop for about 16 grad students plus a couple bold undergrads, 75 minutes, one session, and i want an activity that makes them physically run into the recursion: the people deciding who belongs to the demos are exactly the ones whose belonging is contested.

design a structured game/deliberation. the framing is a fictional "regional climate board" that must vote on approving a geoengineering trial, but membership isn't pre-set by territory or citizenship. instead, every participant gets a blank sheet and draws dependancy lines — who is affected by whose vote — and only nodes inside the resulting graph get a seat. the twist: anyone outside can redraw lines to pull themselves in or push others out, and the board can only lock membership by unanimous consent of whoever is currently inside. give them real small stakes: everyone starts with 3 tokens, every minute of active debate costs 1 token per speaker, and once tokens hit zero you're silent. there's a shared payoff — picking the actual workshop snack budget or directing a small collective donation — but it only gets distributed once membership is fixed. assign roles: "current residents" (full voice, 3 tokens), "downstream affected" (can redraw lines but start with 2 tokens), and "future-generation proxies" (no initial token, can't speak unless pulled inside the graph, but each holds a hidden veto card they can play once). the rules should be arranged so they hit deadlock, token exhuastion, and graph manipulation before anyone names the problem or mentions all-affected versus all-subjected principles.

then a debrief that maps each round onto the theoretical dynamics — recursive legitimation, stakeholder weighting, the cost of inclusion — plus an honest note on what predictably breaks the first run (probably the proxies stay silent and the residents hoard tokens) and which feature of the real problem the game can't reproduce, like the literal absence of people who don't exist yet. keep it runnable with minimal materials, no lecture up front, and don't ask for bibliographies or citations. i want the idea to show up in the token economy and the line-drawing, not in what i say afterwards.
