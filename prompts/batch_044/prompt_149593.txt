I'm studying a design principle in AI alignment that proposes a system should not be optimized for what people actually request, since those requests may be misinformed, impulsive, or contradictory, but rather for what humanity would want after far more knowledge, longer reflection, and resolution of internal inconsistencies—a notion often called coherent extrapolated volition. A parallel idea in welfare economics treats idealized preferences, rather than revealed preferences, as the true measure of well-being, treating actual choices as noisy proxies.

This principle functions like an institutional practice: it substitutes an engineered, rationalized preference profile for the live, often conflicted preferences of real users. Yet in liberal political theory and medical ethics, we usually treat the substitution of an expert's judgment for an adult's actual choice as a serious form of paternalism, permissible only in narrow cases of incompetence or severe, immediate harm. The alignment proposal, by contrast, seems to build that substitution into the architecture itself, on the assumption that reasoning can approximate a single discoverable true preference set.

What philosophical commitments about autonomy, well-being, and value pluralism does this institutional design presuppose? Must it assume that autonomy has only instrumental value, and that the good life is defined by coherence and informed consistency rather than by authentic commitment to one's present, possibly messy values? Or can the practice be defended on purely epistemic grounds—that current preferences are so corrupted by ignorance and cognitive limitation that deferring to them is like deferring to a feverish patient's refusal of medicine—without committing to any particular substantive theory of the good? Furthermore, is the design compatible with deep value pluralism, or does it implicitly presuppose that rational reflection converges on one correct ordering of human values?

Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say what aspects of the design would have to change if we replaced its underlying view of preference and autonomy with one that treats actual, even irrational or contradictory, human choices as sovereign.
