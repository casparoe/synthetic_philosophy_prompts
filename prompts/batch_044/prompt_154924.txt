Consider the gender-equality paradox: in societies that rank higher on standard gender-equality indices—such as the Nordic countries—occupational gender segregation is often larger (for instance, relatively more men in engineering and relatively more women in nursing and primary education) than in societies with lower equality rankings, such as Italy or Japan. The student’s answer is below.

(a) The gender-equality paradox is the empirical pattern in which highly egalitarian societies display greater occupational gender segregation than less egalitarian ones. This complicates purely biological explanations because biological constants should not vary with cultural equality levels; yet the expression of gender differences does vary, so biology must interact with culture in some way. It also complicates purely social-constructivist explanations because reducing sexist structures does not diminish segregation but rather seems to amplify it, suggesting that culture does not work solely through the simple constraint model that constructivists often assume.

(b) Revealed preference holds that once economic barriers are removed, observed choices disclose true underlying preferences. Preference formation under expanded option sets holds that removing barriers can actually reshape preferences through identity signaling and social distinction. Applying these to the paradox: in egalitarian countries, option sets expand, yet segregation grows. This suggests that true innate preferences are being revealed rather than culturally constructed, because if culture alone shaped preferences, lowering cultural pressure should weaken gendered choices, not strengthen them. Therefore the revealed-preference reading is stronger.

(c) The advocate’s inference is largely valid. If choices in egalitarian settings reflect authentic preferences formed without coercion, then justice is satisfied by guaranteeing equal opportunity—removing barriers—rather than enforcing statistical parity, which would violate autonomy. Introducing counterfactual fairness raises the question of whether the same individuals would make identical choices in a world without gendered socialization; if not, then past structural influences remain embedded in present preferences. But since that counterfactual is unobservable, the burden of proof lies with those who claim hidden coercion. Hence the step from revealed preference to rejecting parity is justified.
