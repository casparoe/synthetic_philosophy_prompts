I'm currently preparing a seminar paper on the ethics of demographic policy, and I've become stuck on a tension that standard treatments of reproductive rights rarely address. Most discussions of Benatar's asymmetry focus on the personal decision to procreate: if bringing someone into existence cannot be a benefit to them, since non-existence entails no deprivation, and can only constitute a harm should their life go badly, then there is an asymmetry that renders procreation morally questionable unless one can reliably guarantee a good life. I want to push this reasoning into the structural domain and ask what it implies for state-level pronatalist instruments—tax credits scaled to family size, subsidized fertility treatments, immigration preferences weighted toward younger cohorts, or pension reforms that implicitly reward higher birth rates—when these policies are defended not by appeal to the welfare of the potential children themselves, but by reference to macroeconomic stability, pension-system solvency, labor-force ratios, or cultural continuity.

Please describe the most important implications of Benatar's asymmetry for evaluating such fiscal and demographic policies. In particular, consider whether the asymmetry undermines the familiar governmental justification that "future generations will benefit from a larger working-age population," and whether it generates any distinctive obligations—positive or negative—toward those who are never brought into existence in societies that instead adopt anti-natalist or neutral demographic stances. I am not asking about abortion access or contraceptive rights; my concern is specifically with indirect instruments that shape birth rates through economic incentives, and with whether the asymmetry requires us to treat "preventing a birth" differently from "causing a death" in comparative cost-benefit assessments—such as when a government must allocate limited resources between climate-adaptation funding for existing citizens and family-support spending designed to raise fertility. Please address how this framing affects the moral weight we assign to population size as a policy goal in its own right.
