Plan a single 50-minute session for first-year philosophy undergraduates on Marx’s distinction between exploitation and alienation. The students will likely arrive treating the two as interchangeable complaints about capitalism—both meaning “workers suffer”—and several will have heard that Marx abandoned alienation after 1844. I want them to grasp that exploitation is a structural relation of surplus-value extraction, whereas alienation is an ontological estrangement from species-being, and that the two are conceptually separable even if historically linked.

Set out the explanatory arc: which concept must be secured first and why the second collapses into it if introduced too early; the dependency between the economic structure and the phenomenological condition. Identify the one carrying labor scenario—probably involving a worker who produces surplus for another—that must contain exploitation without alienation in one variant and alienation without exploitation in another, specifying exactly what each variant must include and exclude so the distinction does not blur. Then map the misconceptions they will raise in sequence: the synonymy error, the “later Marx dropped it” claim, and the assumption that worker ownership automatically dissolves both conditions; state the precise point in the arc where each should be headed off. Provide a check for understanding that reveals whether they have internalized the conceptual separation rather than merely repeating it. Finally, state explicitly what must be left out at this level—say, the dense Hegelian vocabulary of the early manuscripts—while openly acknowledging the omission, and avoid assigning any readings.
