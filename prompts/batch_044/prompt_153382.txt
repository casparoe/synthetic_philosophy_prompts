I'm trying to work out whether there is a single principle that governs my intuitions about the unity of consciousness across split-brain scenarios, and I should be grateful for a response written in British English. In the classic experiments associated with Sperry and Gazzaniga, where a patient with a fully sectioned corpus callosum selects an object with the left hand — guided by the right hemisphere — that the language-dominant left hemisphere cannot name, my instinct is that there remains one subject of experience who is merely informationally partitioned, rather like a single library with two card catalogues that do not share entries. Yet when I imagine a prolonged hypothetical case in which the hemispheres are isolated not only by callosal section but also by an implanted synthetic membrane that blocks all remaining subcortical cross-talk, with each hemisphere independently directing behaviour on its respective side of the body for years and developing genuinely divergent preferences, I find myself inclined to say that two conscious subjects now inhabit one cranium.

The tension becomes sharpest in an intermediate scenario I keep revisiting: a patient in whom only the anterior corpus callosum and rostrum are divided, leaving the splenium and posterior commissure intact, so that emotional valence and coarse visual-spatial information can still flow between hemispheres, but linguistic reporting and fine motor planning remain segregated. Here I oscillate — sometimes it feels like one self with a severe internal disconnection, sometimes like two loosely federated minds that happen to share a brainstem. Is there a coherent principle — perhaps framed in terms of global workspace integration, narrative continuity, or access consciousness — that can deliver all three verdicts without collapsing into ad hoc adjustments? If not, which of my intuitions ought to be overturned, and what does the best available account predict for a case I have not listed, such as a patient in whom only the splenium is cut?
