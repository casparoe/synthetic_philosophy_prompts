Please argue for a specific, reconstructed position on Kant's treatment of animals: that the indirect-duty view—where cruelty to animals is wrong because it corrupts the agent's moral disposition toward rational beings—is defensible not as a historical relic but as a philosophically rigorous account, provided we distinguish instrumental mediation (treating animals as mere practice objects for human virtue) from constitutive mediation (treating animal suffering as genuinely morally relevant only insofar as it participates in the formation of rational moral nature). Take a committed stance: defend the claim that on the constitutive-mediation reading, Kant's view avoids the standard charge of speciesism without collapsing into modern direct-duty animal-rights theory. To keep the argument concrete rather than expository, apply it to this case: a technologically advanced society has eliminated all animal agriculture and suffering through synthetic food production, yet its citizens feel and express no moral response whatsoever to animal pain, viewing it with complete indifference. Argue that, on your reconstructed Kantian position, this society has achieved something morally defective, and identify precisely which feature of rational self-cultivation is missing or damaged. You should not survey all interpretations equally; choose the reconstructed indirect-duty view and defend it against at least one serious objection from direct-duty theorists. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not ask for them in return.
