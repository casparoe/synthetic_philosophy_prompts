I've been trying to work through some large life decisions—whether to have another child, whether to relocate to a smaller city, whether to support a particular institutional reform—and I keep hitting the same wall. I begin by trying to determine which option will actually produce the best overall state of affairs, but I quickly realize that I cannot trace the effects far enough to make the comparison meaningful. Having a child reshapes not only my immediate family but countless other lives I will never encounter; moving alters local economies, social networks, and cultural patterns in ways I cannot map; a policy change propagates through bureaucracies and markets in directions I do not understand. So I stop trying to calculate everything and fall back on a simpler decision rule: do what seems right in the near term, follow the tradition I was raised in, or maximize welfare for the people I can actually see.

But then I notice something strange. The decision to stop calculating and switch to that simpler rule is itself a choice with the same far-reaching, untraceable effects. If I choose to have the child based on near-term family happiness, that shapes the world in ripples I have not traced; if I choose against it using the same fallback, that also sends effects outward that I cannot evaluate. And different people seem to land on different fallbacks—some revert to personal fulfillment, others to religious custom, others to local community norms—with no clear principle for picking among them. The problem is not merely that I am ignorant of distant consequences; it is that I seem ignorant of which parts of my ignorance should govern the decision, and the strategy I adopt for handling ignorance appears to determine the outcome just as powerfully as the original choice would have.

I have encountered the general idea that long-term consequences are often impossible to predict, but that seems to describe only a first layer. What I am describing feels like a second layer: once you accept that full prediction is impossible, you must choose a method for deciding in spite of that acceptance, yet the choice of method seems just as ungrounded and just as consequential as the decision it replaces. Is there an established philosophical name for this specific two-layer configuration—this situation where the failure to calculate outcomes is followed by an unjustified choice among shortcuts for coping with that failure? I am not looking for a reading list; I want to know whether the field has carved out this exact distinction, how it differs from ordinary decision-making under uncertainty or from simply using rules of thumb, and where its boundaries lie. If philosophers have not named it, what term and definition would capture it precisely, and what cases would sit just outside its edge?
