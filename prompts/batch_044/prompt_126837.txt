Here is an argument my study partner built from Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream that seems to prove far more than it should, and I cannot see where the leak is.

(1) Zhuangzi reports that upon waking he could not determine whether he had been Zhuangzi dreaming he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming he was Zhuangzi.

(2) If two competing identity-hypotheses are phenomenally indistinguishable from the first-person perspective and neither can be privileged by an internal criterion, then there is no fact of the matter which obtains—call this Phenomenal Neutrality.

(3) The waking/dreaming boundary exhibits the same structure: a sufficiently vivid dream can replicate every qualitative feature of waking perception, including the feeling of bodily presence.

(4) So, by Phenomenal Neutrality applied consistently, there is no fact of the matter whether I am currently awake or merely dreaming.

(5) But if there is no fact of the matter, then I lack the justification required to know that I am awake, since knowing requires being able to exclude relevant alternatives.

(6) The reasoning in (1)–(5), however, was carried out on the assumption that I am awake and that my cognitive faculties are trustworthy in this state.

(7) Therefore the argument is self-undermining: its conclusion destroys the conditions under which it could be rationally accepted.

(8) The only escape is to reject (2) and insist on a hidden asymmetry—perhaps a proprioceptive or causal indexical that anchors waking experience to a specific body—so that phenomenal indistinguishability does not entail ontological indeterminacy.

(9) Yet if we invoke such a hidden asymmetry for waking life, consistency demands we invoke it for Zhuangzi’s case too, which restores a fact of the matter about whether he was the philosopher or the insect—undermining the parable’s intuitive force.

My partner thinks this shows that dream scepticism collapses into self-refutation unless we abandon the symmetry principle altogether. I would prefer to keep (3) and (5) intact if possible, because the phenomenology really does seem symmetric, but I want to avoid the absurd result in (7). Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Does it force me to accept a bodily-essentialist view of personal identity, or is there a more modest price?
