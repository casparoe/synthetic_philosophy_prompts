I'm running a ninety-minute philosophy-of-mind workshop for about fourteen upper-level students, and I want them to feel the strange pull of attributing a single mind to an ant colony when its behaviour is structured not like a central command system but like a contrapuntal fugue. The framing device is a half-remembered case study I call Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue: Hillary, an amateur naturalist, spends months observing a garden colony and becomes convinced that the colony is not merely solving problems but composing something — themes introduced by foragers are transformed by nurse-ants and resolved by soldiers, with no queen acting as conductor or audience. Please design an in-room simulation that puts the students inside this architecture. Divide them into functional castes — foragers, nurses, soldiers, scouts — each given only local rules and a simple thematic card (a colour-pattern or rhythmic sequence representing a behavioural state). Over three timed rounds they must respond to staged disruptions — a simulated rainstorm, a depleted food source, an intruding threat — by transforming their cards according to contrapuntal rules: a nurse may only alter a forager's theme if they are within arm's reach; a soldier may introduce a resolution pattern only if both the original and transformed themes are visible in the same quadrant; all communication must happen through card placement or movement, with no spoken negotiation and no appointed leader. The collective outcome should produce a visibly coherent spatial "score" on the table, yet no individual should be able to articulate the whole plan. Attach a small non-monetary payoff — perhaps the winning interpretation (one mind versus distributed interaction) chooses the music for the final ten minutes, or a shared treat is divided according to the majority view defended in writing.

The debrief should map specific moments in the simulation onto conceptual pressure points: the moment when a soldier's resolution arrived just in time to complete the pattern should be linked to the intuition that the colony intended the outcome; the moment when a nurse mis-transformed a theme and the pattern collapsed should illustrate the fragility of distributed cognition without central error-correction. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — it can demonstrate structural coherence and local-rule emergence, but it cannot reproduce the phenomenological question of what it is like to be the colony, nor the evolutionary timescale over which these behavioural themes become hard-wired. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect the foragers will try to appoint a spokesperson, breaking the contrapuntal rules, and that students will conflate the absence of a leader with pure randomness — and advise how to handle these failures without lecturing. The idea must emerge from the rules, incentives, and roles rather than from anything you explain afterwards. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will supply those myself. Write in polished British English, and keep the framing sufficiently self-contained that it requires no external knowledge of niche literature.
