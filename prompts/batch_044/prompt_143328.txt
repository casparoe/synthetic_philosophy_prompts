Consider the constitutional interpretations that Frederick Douglass advanced publicly between roughly 1851 and 1865, particularly in his speeches and essays on whether the U.S. Constitution is pro-slavery or anti-slavery. His recorded verdicts include the following:

First, on the Three-Fifths Clause: he treats it as a structural limitation that reduces slave-state political power rather than as an endorsement of human bondage.

Second, on the clause referring to persons held to service or labor in another state: he reads this language as describing a pre-existing state-level legal condition rather than as creating a federal property right in human beings.

Third, on the clause that protected the international slave trade for a limited period: he regards it as a temporary, morally objectionable compromise with an explicit expiration, not as a permanent constitutional definition of the republic.

Fourth, on the absence of the terms slave and slavery from the constitutional text: he takes this silence as decisive, arguing that the framers deliberately avoided nationalizing the institution in the fundamental law.

Fifth, on a proposed federal statute that would have required free states to return escaped persons to slaveholders: he rejected it as an unconstitutional expansion of federal power that misread the relevant clause and violated the natural-rights principles he believed the Constitution embodied.
