I'm giving the opening remarks this Thursday at a workshop called "Theme and Variation" hosted by the university's interdisciplinary arts and cognition center, and I'm struggling to strike the right tone because the room will be split almost evenly between cognitive scientists who study analogy and concept-blending—including several who have written extensively on Douglas Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model of creativity, which treats invention as the continuous adjustment of parameters on an existing conceptual theme rather than ex nihilo creation—and practicing musicians, painters, and fiction writers who have never encountered the idea in formal terms and who will rightly bristle if it sounds like I'm reducing their work to mechanics. The philosophical idea needs to do real structural work: it should explain why the workshop is built around paired exercises in which one artist and one scientist jointly manipulate a shared theme, and it should shape what I can and cannot say about the participants—specifically, I want to reject both the romantic myth of the solitary genius struck by lightning and the opposite scientistic claim that art is merely algorithmic knob-turning with no human stakes—without turning the speech into a lecture for either half. To keep the concept from feeling like something the scientists have heard a hundred times in pretraining, please introduce a specific elaboration that isn't standard textbook fare: treat each knob not as a binary switch but as a continuous dial with a dead zone near its conventional setting, so that true variation requires pushing through resistance rather than flipping a toggle, and suggest that this resistance is what makes some variations feel like betrayal to an audience while others feel like revelation.

Write the speech in continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no numbered lists, and no tables—formatted like a warm but intellectually serious letter or email, about seven hundred to nine hundred words, suitable for being read aloud in roughly five minutes. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not reference any obscure theories, named experiments, or specific texts that a well-read but non-specialist model might not know; rely only on the knob-twiddling framework and the invented dead-zone dial framing.
