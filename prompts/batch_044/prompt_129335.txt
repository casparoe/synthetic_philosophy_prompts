Consider a transnational bioethics advisory council tasked with rendering a unified recommendation on whether to permit field trials of a heritable gene-drive modification targeting agricultural pests. The council must collectively settle three interdependent judgments: (A) whether current ecological modeling demonstrates a greater-than-seventy-percent containment probability within a five-year horizon; (B) whether that containment probability, if achieved, morally justifies the risk of irreversible ecosystem alteration; and (C) whether the council should therefore endorse the trial protocol. The council's charter requires logical consistency across these three judgments, yet individual members hold divergent views on both the empirical and normative premises, and the aggregation mechanism itself is contested.

Write a dialogue between Dr. Amara Okonkwo, who defends a premise-based aggregation rule with what she calls vertical coherence—insisting that the collective must first aggregate judgments on (A) and (B) separately using weighted epistemic confidence scores, then derive (C) by logical entailment, even if the result contradicts a direct majoritarian vote on (C)—and Professor Julian Reed, who advocates a conclusion-first horizontal coherence approach. Reed argues that the council's democratic legitimacy depends on honoring direct majority judgments on (C), and that when (C) conflicts with the aggregated premises, the premises should be subject to outcome-sensitive relaxation rather than overriding the collective will on the final recommendation. Both characters should be thoroughly familiar with the discursive dilemma and its standard formal results, particularly the tension among universal domain, anonymity, and systematicity, but they should apply these tools to the gene-drive case without merely reciting textbook examples.

The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: Okonkwo should challenge Reed's relaxation mechanism as epistemically arbitrary and vulnerable to strategic manipulation of premise judgments, while Reed should respond by questioning whether Okonkwo's confidence-weighted premise aggregation illegitimately privileges technical expertise over democratic equality and risks producing recommendations that the majority of the council explicitly rejects. Each should concede minor points—perhaps Okonkwo admitting that pure premise-based rules can yield counterintuitive recommendations in edge cases with highly skewed confidence distributions, Reed acknowledging that unconstrained relaxation risks outright logical incoherence—while defending their core positions on group rationality, epistemic authority, and democratic legitimacy. Avoid strawman arguments; let both deploy real philosophical resources. Keep the exchange focused on the aggregation architecture rather than on general bioethics, though the specific judgments about containment and moral justification should ground their arguments concretely.
