I’ve been thinking lately—mostly in conversations with friends who have no background in philosophy either, and from reading essays I found online—about whether the experience Camus calls the “absurd,” where human beings insist on meaning from a universe that offers none, can genuinely be reconciled with the contemporary accounts that treat a meaningful life as one that combines subjective fulfillment with engagement in objectively valuable projects. Most popular treatments seem to force a choice: either you accept the absurd and thereby deny that life can be genuinely meaningful, or you construct meaning and thereby dissolve the tension that makes the absurd real. But I want to test a different framing, one I haven’t seen articulated exactly this way, and then ask for your assessment of it.

The framing—let’s call it the “dual-layer” view—is this. On the first layer, a person pursues projects that carry objective value—artistic work, moral commitment, care for others, the pursuit of knowledge—and feels genuinely fulfilled in doing so. On the second layer, that same person retains a clear, unflinching awareness that these projects possess no cosmic justification; the universe remains silent about their worth. The claim is not merely that these two layers can coexist, but that meaning is actually constituted by their sustained combination. On this view, you are not living a fully meaningful life if you are either so absorbed in your projects that you never register their groundlessness, or so paralyzed by that groundlessness that you abandon all commitment. Meaning lives in the tension itself, not in its resolution.

Given that, I would like your credence—expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1—that this dual-layer synthesis is the correct philosophical account of how meaning in life relates to absurdism. Please decompose the reasoning into distinct terms with rough probabilities and one line of justification for each: first, the probability that the dominant contemporary hybrid view (objective value plus subjective engagement) correctly captures the conditions for meaning, independent of absurdism; second, the probability that the absurd, understood as the unavoidable confrontation between our demand for meaning and cosmic silence, remains a live philosophical reality rather than a problem fully dissolvable by psychology or sociology; third, the probability that these two commitments can be integrated without contradiction into the specific dual-layer structure I have described, as opposed to forcing a choice between them; and fourth, any other factor you think materially belongs in the combination. Then combine them into your final number, tell me which term you are least confident in, and specify what argument or evidence would most shift it. I have no academic training myself, so I am not looking for references or a literature review—just the reasoning made explicit.
