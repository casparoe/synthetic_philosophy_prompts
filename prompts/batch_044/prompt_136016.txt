Exam Question — Section B (15 points):
Philosophers discussing climate change, meat consumption, and democratic voting often encounter the inefficacy objection: in large-scale collective harm cases, a single individual’s act seems to make no perceptible difference to the aggregate outcome. One prominent reply appeals to non-linear aggregation—threshold or tipping-point dynamics—rather than linear summation. Analyze whether this reply genuinely answers the inefficacy objection or merely relocates it. In your analysis, clearly distinguish causal inefficacy from the problem of distributed moral responsibility, and evaluate the displacement-rebound claim: in systems with elastic supply, one agent’s withdrawal may be fully offset by others’ increased share, leaving aggregate harm unchanged except near inelastic saturation points. Apply your reasoning to at least one concrete domain (climate, meat, or voting).

Student Response:
The inefficacy objection holds that my driving one car or eating one burger does not change the climate or kill an extra animal, so I cause no harm and have no reason to stop. This is essentially the same as Hannah Arendt’s problem of many hands, where responsibility dissolves because every agent is only a tiny part of a large system. If we reject linear summation and adopt a non-linear threshold model, the objection collapses. Near a tipping point, each contribution can be the one that pushes the system over the boundary, so individual acts become causally decisive. In voting, for instance, if an election is decided by exactly one vote, then every voter is causally necessary for the outcome. Parfit argued in Reasons and Persons that hidden wrongness arises because we mistakenly think in linear terms; once we recognize the non-linear structure, imperceptible acts become decisive and the hidden wrongness disappears. The same applies to climate and meat consumption: because we are near saturation points, my carbon footprint and my purchase are the decisive ones. Therefore, near thresholds, causal contribution and distributed responsibility collapse into the same thing, and the inefficacy objection fails.
