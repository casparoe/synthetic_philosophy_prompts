I am a doctoral student in decision theory, and I recently observed an argument between two colleagues in our behavioral-science reading group that I have been unable to settle. The disagreement concerned whether Herbert Simon’s satisficing can be fully reconstructed within an optimization framework once information-processing costs are made explicit, or whether it represents a genuinely distinct form of procedural rationality that rejects a global utility function altogether. Their exchange ran as follows. First, one researcher argued that “good enough” is nothing more than an implicit cost-benefit cutoff: if we formalize search costs precisely, bounded rationality collapses into standard rational choice with expensive computation, and there is no conceptual rupture with expected utility theory. Second, the other replied that this misses Simon’s core point, because the agent does not maximize over a pre-given option space with a cost term appended; rather, the option space and the evaluation criteria are constructed during the search itself, so preference is formed in the act of searching rather than revealed by it. Third, the first researcher countered that even procedural rules can be embedded in a meta-level framework, since any stopping rule can in principle be derived as the optimal strategy for a higher-order problem that trades accuracy against cognitive effort, which means procedural rationality does not escape optimization but merely pushes it up one level. Fourth, the second researcher introduced what she called sequential aspiration adaptation with endogenous reference points: in a multi-attribute choice setting, meeting an aspiration level in one dimension automatically lowers the aspiration in another, not through a pre-specified trade-off rate, but because the agent treats satisfaction in one domain as licensing relaxation elsewhere; consequently the constraint set is generated by the procedure itself, with the evaluation metric altered mid-process by the agent’s own history of satisfaction, so the meta-optimization reply fails because the objective function is not stable across the procedure. I am asking you to adjudicate this exchange point by point: for each of the four moves, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification; then say who is more right overall and identify what, if anything, both sides are neglecting—particularly whether the endogenous-reference mechanism genuinely blocks reconstruction or can still be captured by a sufficiently dynamic state-dependent model. Please respond in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write in a letter or email.
