I'm part of a four-person cooperative negotiating a service contract that contains an unusual compensation clause. Under its terms, we receive nothing unless we complete a sequence of sequential milestones, each of which carries an independent fifty-percent chance of failure; if we reach milestone n, our collective payout is two raised to the nth power in thousands of dollars, and the process terminates immediately upon the first success. The client's analyst computed the formal expectation of this arrangement as unbounded, then used that result in two contradictory directions: first, to claim that any fixed upfront fee we propose—say, forty thousand dollars—is technically "underpriced" relative to the contract's theoretical value; second, to argue that since no real-world party can honor an infinite obligation, we should treat the milestone structure as a symbolic bonus and accept a modest flat rate instead.

I find both moves conceptually slippery rather than merely impractical. The inference from an infinite mathematical expectation to an infinite fair price seems like a category error, yet I also want to avoid justifying our cooperative's preference for a bounded, guaranteed payment by appealing to risk aversion, since that substitutes psychology for principle. Could you help me see the philosophical structure underlying this dispute—specifically whether there is a defensible gap between the formal expectation of a payoff scheme and the rational terms to which an agent should agree, and whether a small collective like ours can ground its demand for a finite, certain fee on reasons stronger than individual fear of uncertainty? I'm particularly interested in whether the client's reasoning confuses the properties of a probability distribution with the norms of agreement, and if so, what that confusion reveals about how we ought to evaluate offers whose expected value diverges sharply from any price we could actually pay or receive.
