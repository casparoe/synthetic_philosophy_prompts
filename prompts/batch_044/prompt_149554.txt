Analyze the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission, 584 U.S. 617, as a philosophical case study in the epistemic difficulty of separating personal preferences from prejudicial biases in moral and legal reasoning. Concentrate on the majority opinion's framing of Jack Phillips's refusal to design a custom wedding cake for a same-sex couple: the Court treats the refusal as an expression of artistic and religious preference—specifically, an objection to the message that the cake would convey—rather than as discrimination based on the customers' sexual orientation. Examine whether this framing relies on a tacit, non-circular criterion for distinguishing message-based preference from status-based bias, or whether it merely redescribes the bias in the language of artistic autonomy. In your analysis, apply the counterfactual consistency test—asking whether Phillips would have refused an identically designed commission for an opposite-sex couple promoting a message he found objectionable—to determine whether the distinction can survive selective activation, where biases operate only against protected groups but not against analogous non-protected ones. Conclude by assessing what the episode reveals about the accessibility of motivation: is the preference/bias distinction a discoverable feature of an agent's mental economy, or is it necessarily imposed by the normative framework that the observer, including the Court, already accepts?
