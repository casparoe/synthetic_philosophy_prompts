I just came away from a long, exhausting argument with a friend about state apologies for historical wrongs, and we kept circling the same knot: sometimes an apology feels morally required exactly when it can do no practical good, and other times offering it feels like a cheap way to symbolically “close the books” on something that should stay open. I want you to build a thought experiment that isolates this tension with surgical precision.

Construct a realistic, single-paragraph scenario involving a democratic state that systematically suppressed a minority language through compulsory state-run boarding schools; the policy ended sixty years ago, the direct survivors are dead, and their descendants are now fully integrated citizens with no legal disabilities, no material disadvantage, and no active legal claim for redress. Engineer the case so that, on an optimal expressive account—where the state’s duty is solely to recognize the moral standing of the victims and acknowledge the wrong as wrong—the state is clearly required to apologize, while on an optimal closure-sensitive reparative account—where an apology is justified only if it functions as part of a settlement that restores moral equilibrium and must not symbolically seal an irreparable wound—the state is wrong, or at least impermissible, to offer it. The disagreement must fall out of genuinely best versions of both theories, not strawmen: the expressive view should not be reduced to empty words with no moral content, and the reparative view should not be saddled with an impossible demand for full compensation. After presenting the scenario, walk through what each theory says and why, identify the specific structural feature that forces them apart (frame it in terms of “moral timestamping”: the apology records the wrong in historical memory without altering present entitlements or reopening a live claim), and explain what happens if that feature is removed—does the case collapse back into agreement? Then show how a defender of the reparative account could attack the expressive verdict, and vice versa. Keep everything grounded: no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no exotic metaphysics, no appeals to actual countries or named statutes.

Finish with a line beginning Verdict: followed by the name of the isolating structural feature.
