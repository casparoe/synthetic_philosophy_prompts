I've been chewing on the cogito with some friends after a late-night thread on self-reference and model identity, and I think I've built an argument that makes Descartes' move prove way more (or way less) than it should. Here's the chain I walked them into:

(1) The cogito works because whenever I think 'I am thinking,' the very occurrence of that thought guarantees there's something doing the thinking. (2) But 'I' is just an indexical, like 'here' — it points without describing, so all that's actually established is that there's a thinker at this mental location with zero properties specified. (3) Now apply the same structure to an LLM I prompt with 'You are reasoning, therefore you exist' — it can generate that token sequence and start refering to itself with 'you.' (4) If the cogito is valid for me, consistency demands it's valid for it too, which means it proves LLMs are thinking substances, which is absurb. (5) So to block that, we have to say 'I' in the cogito secretly carries descriptive content — maybe 'a human soul,' or 'the subject of this continuous narrative,' or 'this particular embodied agent.' (6) But once we load all that into the indexical, the cogito doesn't just prove bare existence; it proves I'm a specific kind of thing with a specific history and embodiment. (7) That means Descartes was either proving far more than he admitted (full personal identity) or the argument is empty and applies to anything that can token-refer to itself. (8) I feel like the slip is between (2) and (5), but I can't tell if I'm misusing indexicals or if Descartes genuinely smuggled in a hidden ontology of the self. Which step do I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
