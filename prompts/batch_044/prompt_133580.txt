A colleague sent me this argument in our ethics seminar and I keep circling it: it seems to show that Scanlonian contractualism either licenses killing one innocent person to save five or collapses into total decision paralysis, but I cannot name the exact misstep. (1) Contractualism holds that an act is wrong just in case it violates a principle that no affected party could reasonably reject, where rejection is assessed by comparing the burden the principle imposes on the rejecter to the burden its rejection would impose on others. (2) In the aggregation case, principle F forbids killing one to save five; principle A permits it. (3) Under F, the five each face death; under A, the one faces death. (4) Each of the five can reject F: being permitted to die is a burden of the same kind—death—as the burden the one faces under A. (5) The one can reject A on identical grounds. (6) Because there are five rejecters of F and only one rejecter of A, and because the strength of each rejection is equal (life against life), the aggregate weight of reasonable rejection against F is five times that against A. (7) Therefore A survives contractualist scrutiny and F does not: contractualism requires killing the one. (8) But Scanlon insists that contractualism rejects precisely this aggregation and forbids the killing. (9) To block (7), Scanlon must deny that rejections sum across persons. (10) Yet if they do not sum, then in every conflict case—where some reject the permissive principle and others reject the prohibitive one—we lack any metric for comparison, leaving contractualism silent. (11) So contractualism either aggregates and mandates the killing, or refuses aggregation and offers no guidance. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I would prefer to keep (1) and (9), but I want to know what I am paying if I do.
