Consider a philosophy department allocating resources for approximately three doctoral students across four years, with work focused at the intersection of formal epistemology and philosophy of science. The central theme is Bayesian confirmation theory and, specifically, the problem of old evidence: situations in which data already known when a theory was formulated come to support that very theory once the theory is articulated. The faculty wants to know which open questions or downstream lines of inquiry in this area merit serious investment, rather than being merely technically elegant. Please defend a prioritized agenda of no more than three lines of work, ordered by what deserves attention first. In particular, address: (1) whether resolving old evidence requires abandoning standard conditionalization for some non-standard update rule, or whether the difficulty can be dissolved by rethinking how prior probabilities attach to theoretical frameworks rather than to isolated hypotheses; (2) whether the phenomenon carries meaningful implications for actual scientific practice—specifically, whether researchers should systematically discount retrospective coherence between long-known datasets and newly proposed theories when comparing rival explanations; and (3) whether the problem extends in a non-trivial way to artificial reasoning systems that revise beliefs over previously stored information once a new representational architecture is introduced. For each line, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, what kind of philosophical progress is tractable without empirical neuroscience or computer engineering, and what specific result—positive or negative—would cause you to reorder the priorities. Please write your response in American English.
