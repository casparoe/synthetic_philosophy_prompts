As a novelist assembling material for a philosophical novel, I have been testing my own intuitions about well-being against the idea that only the absence of craving and suffering genuinely matters — that positive pleasure is either illusory in value or inseparable from the craving that undermines it. I want to know whether my judgments form a coherent principle, or whether I must abandon some of them.

My verdicts on specific cases:

First, a meditator in sustained formless absorption — no pleasure, no pain, no desire, only what might be called a luminous emptiness. I judge this an excellent, perhaps optimal, state of being.

Second, a gourmand at the peak of a tasting menu — intense gustatory pleasure, yet already anticipating the next course, comparing this bite to an ideal. I judge this poor well-being; the pleasure is tainted by craving.

Third, a patient under deep general anesthesia during long surgery — no consciousness, hence no suffering and no craving. I judge this not a harm; well-being is neutral-to-slightly-positive, comparable to a well-placed silence in a narrative rather than to a tragedy.

Fourth, a painter in deep flow, producing work she considers her finest, feeling no explicit desire, only absorption in color and form, with a faint background satisfaction. I judge this good well-being, but I am genuinely unsure whether the good derives from the absence of craving or from the positive aesthetic engagement.

Fifth, someone on a hypothetical tranquilizer that eliminates all craving and all affect — serene, blank, incapable of either suffering or joy, like a clear sky with no weather whatsoever. I judge this genuinely good, perhaps optimal, yet I keep wavering because praising it feels like praising an empty room for being tidy.

Sixth, a dreamless sleeper who wakes refreshed, remembering nothing, having experienced neither pleasure nor pain. I judge this a positive contribution to their life, analogous to the anesthesia case but more natural.

The difficulty is this: I want Case Four to be good partly because of its positive aesthetic content, yet I want to deny that positive content matters in Case Two. Meanwhile, Cases Three, Five, and Six all seem good despite lacking any positive phenomenology, pushing me toward pure tranquilism — yet Case Five unsettles me in a way Case One does not, even though both lack positive affect.

Please structure your response as follows:

1. The principle (approximately 250 words). State the most coherent principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures my verdicts. Tie each clause to the specific case that motivates it. Be precise about whether tranquility requires mere absence or a particular qualitative character of absence.

2. The predictions (approximately 200 words). Apply the principle to three unlisted cases: (a) a musician playing with perfect technical mastery but feeling nothing; (b) a person in chronic low-grade dissatisfaction who suddenly achieves complete emotional flatness through brain injury; (c) an artificial system that maintains perfect homeostasis with no internal states at all. Give clear verdicts.

3. The casualties (approximately 150 words). State honestly which of my six verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate. If saving Case Four requires overturning Case Five, say so explicitly; if saving Case Five requires overturning Case One, say so. Do not smooth over contradictions.

4. The hard case resolved (approximately 150 words). Address specifically Case Five — the serene blank — and say whether my wavering is philosophically justified or whether the principle demands a firm commitment either to praise it or to reject it.

Write formally and concisely. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; I need this usable without external lookup.
