I hold that democratic backsliding is not a partisan epithet but tracks a specific, empirically observable syndrome: the sequential erosion of institutional veto points—typically beginning with electoral management bodies, then judicial independence, then media pluralism—such that at least two redundant safeguards are disabled within a compressed timeframe. I apply this framework to Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and argue it applies in attenuated form to recent developments in the United States, and I defend it against unfalsifiability through an institutional-redundancy thesis: where redundant checks remain intact, the correct classification is democratic stress rather than backsliding. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by decisiveness: empirical cases in which the sequential pattern fails, reverses, or is absent despite outcome-level decay; conceptual arguments that the stress-versus-backsliding distinction collapses into subjective judgments about magnitude; measurement results from standard comparative indices that place these four cases in categorically different regimes rather than on a continuum of institutional erosion; and comparative-politics findings showing that identical institutional changes occur in stable democracies without producing democratic decay. For each, provide an honest assessment of whether a defender of this syndrome-plus-redundancy view would concede damage, dismiss it as mismeasurement, or absorb it by further qualification. In particular, has the redundancy condition insulated the concept so thoroughly that no single institutional change can ever refute it, and if so, is that insulation a virtue of analytical precision or a vice that empties the concept? Finally, if this sequential-redundancy framing were undermined, which rival account—pure executive aggrandizement, pure democratic deconsolidation, or a purely outcome-based definition—would best survive, and what would it gain or lose in explanatory scope?
