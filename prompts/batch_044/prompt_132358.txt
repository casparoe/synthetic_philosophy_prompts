To ground this, I'm particularly interested in a somewhat underexplored tension: Dewey treats inquiry as cooperative problem-solving directed at shared consequences, yet he also insists that the public is not pre-given but constituted by the indirect effects of actions. This raises a niche design question—if democratic institutions are themselves part of the experimental apparatus, then altering institutional rules (say, shifting from majoritarian voting to deliberative mini-publics) isn't merely applying inquiry but changing the conditions of inquiry itself. So one line I'm considering is whether there is a coherent distinction, within Dewey's own framework, between first-order policy experimentation and second-order institutional experimentation, and whether conflating them undermines his defense of democratic equality.

Another line concerns the epistemic status of warranted assertibility in political contexts. Dewey rejects the spectator theory of knowledge, but in democratic settings, this seems to imply that political claims are warranted only through participatory testing. Yet this creates a potential circularity: if the legitimacy of democratic outcomes depends on their being products of genuine inquiry, and the criteria for genuine inquiry are themselves determined through democratic processes, how do we avoid a regress that makes institutional design arbitrary?

A third possibility involves extending Dewey's experimentalism to non-human or algorithmic agents. Recent discussions of AI governance sometimes invoke Deweyan language about social intelligence, but Dewey's account of inquiry requires embodied, affective engagement with shared problems. This suggests a boundary problem: if algorithmic systems can process indirect consequences faster than human publics can form, does this expand the scope of democratic inquiry or dissolve the very conditions—shared experience, emotional investment—that make Dewey's public possible?

I want an agenda, not a survey or bibliography.
