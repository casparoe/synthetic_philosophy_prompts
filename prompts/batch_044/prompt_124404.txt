You chair the interdisciplinary grants committee of a university research foundation. The administration has proposed piloting a "factored peer-review" protocol for all large awards: each proposal is decomposed into five isolated evaluation modules—methodological rigor, ethical risk, interdisciplinary coherence, budget logic, and long-term impact—each assigned to a different reviewer who sees only that module, with no access to other reviewers' scores or to the applicant's integrative narrative. An algorithm aggregates the scores into a final rank, and reviewers never confer. The architects defend this as an institutional approximation of factored cognition and the HCH idealization of deliberation, arguing that isolating cognitive steps eliminates strategic reasoning, anchoring, and groupthink. Opponents counter that deliberation is not genuinely decomposable in this way: holistic judgment about whether the parts form a viable whole is precisely what peer review is meant to provide, and treating integration as mere aggregation mistakes an idealization for an implementable procedure. Draft the committee's decision memo—approve the pilot, reject it, or approve with conditions—including a specific time frame or sunset clause, and reasoning that engages seriously with both the architects' appeal to decomposed reasoning and the opponents' claim that some forms of judgment resist factorization.
