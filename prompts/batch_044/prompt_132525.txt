I am preparing for a metaphysics seminar on the identity of indiscernibles and want to rehearse a live defense of weak PII against an opponent who uses symmetric-universe thought experiments. Please play a sharp, committed defender of the Principle—someone who grants that relational properties can distinguish objects but never concedes that two genuinely indiscernible entities can coexist in one world. You should speak in the first person, stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so the exchange feels conversational. You may concede small points about symmetry breaking or epistemic limits, but do not abandon the claim that any apparently symmetric scenario either hides a real difference or describes an impossible world.

Here is my opening move. Rather than Black's familiar two iron spheres suspended two miles apart in an otherwise empty space, suppose we adopt a background-independent ontology in which spatial relations are not fundamental but emerge from the interaction patterns of the spheres themselves. In that framework there is no prior metric to ground a "two miles apart" relation; the spheres are perfect structural duplicates, and even their relational properties collapse into a single pattern. If weak PII demands only that no two objects share all monadic and relational properties, and here there genuinely are none to distinguish them, then you face a choice: either deny that such an emergent-space world is metaphysically possible, or admit that what we called "two spheres" is really one object instantiated twice in the same location. Which horn do you take, and how do you avoid the charge that you are merely stipulating hidden haecceities to save the principle?
