I've been arguing with a friend about whether our attachment to original artworks is rational, or whether it collapses into something like the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem from practical philosophy. Imagine a museum that, for decades, unknowingly displays a perfect molecular-level forgery of a Vermeer; every expert authenticates it, visitors report identical emotional and perceptual responses, and then we learn the real canvas was destroyed years ago. My friend claims the aesthetic value of those encounters was fully intact, and that any residual sense of loss is driven by a wrong-kind-of-reason: we treat causal provenance like moral desert, letting historical origin contaminate our judgment of artistic merit rather than responding solely to the work's perceptible properties. I want to test whether this diagnosis holds.

Please give me your own credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that this "wrong-kind-of-reasons" framing is correct about our preference for originals—specifically, that causal history contributes nothing constitutive to the aesthetic value of the encounter, and that our attachment to originals is best explained as a non-rational fetish for provenance rather than a legitimate artistic response. Decompose the question into at least four parts: (1) whether aesthetic value is exhaustively constituted by perceptual and structural properties available in the forgery; (2) whether the artist's intentional act or the work's historical embeddedness is a constitutive, not merely instrumental, component of artistic value; (3) whether the analogy to wrong-kind reasons actually transfers from ethics to aesthetics, or whether it forces an inappropriate model of appreciation onto a domain that legitimately permits non-instrumental concern for origins; and (4) whether institutional or ritual value—say, a museum's role in preserving cultural continuity—can be cleanly separated from pure aesthetic value without begging the question. Assign a rough probability to each component, show how they combine into your overall number, and identify which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to. Also indicate what kind of argument or comparative case—perhaps a live musical performance versus a perfect recording, or a restored ancient artifact versus an untouched one—would most likely move your number up or down. Finally, state how confident you are in this decomposition and explicitly single out the point where you are least sure, particularly noting any risk that you are inappropriately importing an ethical framework into aesthetics.
