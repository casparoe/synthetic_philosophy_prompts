hey, i'm working on a novel and there's this ethics board in it — kind of inspired by Margalit's stuff on remembering and Rieff's case for forgetting, but more bureaucratic and weird. i need to figure out if their rulings are actually consistent.

so far they've said:
- yes to a law forcing towns to maintain plaques for victims of state violence, even if locals want to move on.
- no to letting a reformed ex-convict scrub all news articles about his crime.
- yes to destroying a dead poet's private journals when her heirs requested it, despite scholar protests.
- no to a museum melting a colonial monument without leaving any record of its form.
- yes to a right to be forgotten for factory-disaster survivors who reject anniversary documentaries.
- no to a school dropping a unit on a local genocide because parents called it too heavy.

now the new proposal: a developer wants to pave over an unmarked graveyard of 1920s migrant workers. they promise a virtual memorial with AI-reconstructed faces and voices pulled from census scraps. the board has to choose: allow with virtual memorial, allow only if physical markers remain, or block entirely.

if they want to stay consistent with themselves, what's the verdict? which prior case is the closest analoge? and if their history actually supports more than one rule — maybe one about protecting the powerless dead, one about material presence versus digital substitute, one about who gets to decide what's remembered — which rule should win, and what's the one hypothetical case that'd tell them apart?

i'm less interested in the answer than in seeing the hidden principel they might be running. also there's probably a typo or two in this list but just roll with it.
