I am writing to seek your philosophical analysis of a dispute that has arisen within my research group — a situation that seems to me to instantiate a well-known problem in the epistemology of belief revision, though I shall deliberately avoid naming it directly.

For the past two years, our team of six researchers has developed a unified theoretical framework. We have peer-reviewed publications, shared methodological commitments, and a collective self-conception as an especially careful and rational group. Last month, a junior colleague presented experimental results that, interpreted straightforwardly, contradict one of our framework's central axioms. The senior members — myself included — have resisted revising the axiom. Our reasoning is not merely defensive. We argue that, given our sustained record of rational inquiry, the prior probability that our core assumption is erroneous is lower than the prior probability that the junior colleague has made an interpretive or methodological mistake, even granting that the data appear anomalous. One senior colleague has formalised this into what he calls a principle of reflective self-trust: a rational agent who believes herself rational should treat her own long-held, well-examined beliefs as more resilient to isolated counter-instances than beliefs formed hastily.

The junior researcher replies that this principle is self-undermining. If rationality genuinely requires responsiveness to evidence, then invoking our collective rationality to insulate a belief from revision is precisely the kind of dogmatic closure that rationality forbids. She further proposes that we adopt an explicit revision protocol — a rule specifying in advance what evidence would suffice to overturn the axiom — but the senior members reject this on the grounds that any protocol designed by us would inherit the same circularity: we would be using our current rational framework to specify the conditions under which it might be wrong.

I find both sides compelling and cannot locate the precise failure in either argument. Could you analyse this concrete situation through the lens of the relevant epistemological paradox? In particular: does the senior position conflate first-order belief with second-order confidence in a way that makes the principle unstable? Is there a non-circular application of reflective self-trust that avoids collapsing into dogmatism? And does the proposed revision protocol resolve the tension, or does it merely relocate the circularity?
