I am a doctoral student in archaeological theory preparing a single seventy-five-minute seminar for first-year MA students in material culture studies, and I need a pedagogical plan rather than a lecture script. The conceptual target is the inferential architecture that lets us move from physical traces to reconstructed beliefs and institutions—specifically, how to avoid both naive literalism, which reads objects as direct transcripts of ancient thought, and unmoored constructivism, which treats interpretation as unconstrained projection. The session should open with whatever dependency must be in place first—likely the distinction between functional inference, where an object reveals a practical problem it solved, and symbolic or institutional inference, where it reveals a collective practice that outlasted individual cognition—before any concrete case is introduced, because without that distinction the students will not yet see why reconstruction is a problem rather than a simple reading.

For the carrying example, I want to center the Early Neolithic plastered skulls recovered at Jericho and Ain Ghazal in the Levant: human crania coated with plaster, modeled with facial features, and sometimes inlaid with shell eyes, deposited in both domestic and mortuary contexts. The plan should explain precisely why this class of object can bear the full weight of the idea—because it sits ambiguously between mortuary technology, portraiture, and institutional ancestor veneration—while also specifying what it must lack, namely clear textual corroboration or unambiguous utilitarian function, since either would collapse the inferential problem into a simpler reading. The sequence should then move through three predictable misconceptions in the exact order students will form them: first, that material culture transparently documents belief (to be headed off immediately after introducing the functional versus symbolic distinction, using a counter-example like a grinding stone that carries no propositional content); second, that without texts all interpretation is equally valid (to be neutralized during the skull analysis by showing how taphonomic context and comparative patterning constrain but do not determine meaning); and third, that reconstructing an institution is identical to attributing a conscious propositional belief to ancient actors (to be cleared only in the synthesis phase, by introducing the framing concept of institutional residue—the idea that some objects are not representations of beliefs but material sediments of routine practices that persisted beyond any individual's mental state). The students should come away seeing that these skulls may index a mortuary institution rather than symbolize ancestor worship.

Finally, specify a single end-of-session check—perhaps a brief structured task in which students are given a hypothetical, undescribed ceramic assemblage and asked to map which inferences are functional, which are institutional-residue, and which remain unsupported—together with an explicit statement of what must be deliberately left out at this level, such as quantitative taphonomic modeling or deep comparative ethnography of Near Eastern mortuary cults. Also indicate exactly where in the sequence the niche framing of institutional residue should be introduced so that it resolves the third misconception without being mistaken for the opening distinction, and confirm that the example carries the dependency structure rather than serving as mere illustration.
