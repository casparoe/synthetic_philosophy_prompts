You are a philosopher of moral psychology and applied ethics, with deep familiarity with Adam Smith's account of sympathy, the impartial spectator, and the distinction between propriety and merit. Before constructing the procedure, take time to think through the conceptual architecture: how sympathy functions as a mechanism of fellow-feeling rather than mere emotional contagion; how the impartial spectator operates as an internal standard that corrects for partiality; and where Smith locates the boundary between assessing an agent's intentions and evaluating the consequences of an act.

I advise mediators in a community-based restorative justice program who repeatedly face decisions about whether a proposed restorative agreement genuinely addresses the moral desert of a symbolic or relational harm—such as a public false accusation that destroyed a reputation, or a breach of trust within an institution—without relying on legal sentencing guidelines. These mediators are not lawyers; they must document reasoning for a review board of legal staff and community representatives, and they need a reusable decision procedure they can run in under an hour. Design an ordered checklist of questions, with clear stopping rules, explicit judgment nodes, and a sharp distinction between empirical observations (what was intended, what damage occurred, what remedy is feasible) and irreducible value judgments (whether sympathy is warranted, whether the spectator approves). The procedure must handle hard cases: the offender expresses genuine remorse but the victim refuses any reconciliation; third-party observers disagree sharply on harm severity; and the proposed remedy risks being either disproportionate or merely performative. Apply it to three scenarios: a straightforward case with acknowledged harm and proportional remedy; a case where the offender sincerely intends repair but the structural damage exceeds any individual remedy; and a case where the procedure yields an answer the mediator will find unsatisfying—perhaps that formal reconciliation should be withheld despite emotional readiness on both sides. End by stating what the procedure cannot settle, where it is likely to misfire, and which steps remain judgment calls that no protocol can eliminate.
