I am a software engineer without any formal philosophy background, but I have been working through some Foucault in a reading group with colleagues who study technology and society, and I am trying to apply his framework to an episode I experienced directly. During the 2020–2022 shift to remote work, many tech companies—including my own—rapidly deployed algorithmic employee-monitoring systems: keystroke loggers, always-on camera policies, automated productivity scores, and aggregate “engagement” dashboards. By 2023, some of these tools were quietly rolled back or normalized into hybrid-office infrastructure, but the data architectures remained. I want to analyze this rollout and its partial retreat as a case study in Foucault’s ideas about disciplinary power, the production of knowledge, and the panopticon.

Specifically, I am asking for a disciplined mapping rather than a general summary. How well does the panopticon model apply when the monitored subjects are technically literate workers who understand the code and data pipelines behind the surveillance—does that transparency deepen self-discipline, create a different kind of power relation, or break the model? How should I understand the massive behavioral datasets these tools generated: what kind of “knowledge” about labor and cognition was being constructed, and whose institutional interests did that knowledge serve? And does the episode expose a tension in Foucault’s own framework, since these same algorithmic systems simultaneously disciplined individual engineers through granular performance metrics and managed the workforce as a statistical population through aggregate analytics? I want you to say exactly which actors and options the theory captures, which features it misdescribes or leaves silent—particularly regarding worker resistance and the normalization of these tools—and whether the case ultimately supports, complicates, or reveals a limit in the distinction between disciplinary power and biopower.
