i've been helping my nephew study for his world history exam and the textbook has this weird framing that keeps bugging me. there's a chapter on "forces that shaped the modern world" and it lists things like the printing press, the french revolution, and then also the 1815 tambora eruption and the potato blight in ireland. for the human stuff it talks about intentions and choices, but for the volcano and the fungus it uses all these active verbs — "tambora reshaped global climate," "the blight forced mass migration," like they were doing something on purpose. at the same time the actual people affected are just described as "responding to conditions" or "adapting to pressure." i asked my friend who's a historian and she said it's just shorthand, but i feel like their's a real philosophical mess hiding in there about who or what counts as an agent in history.

if a textbook treats a volcano and a revolutionary leader as both being "agents of change," what theory of agency is it quietly buying into? does it have to be full-blown environmental determinism — that climate and germs are the real drivers and humans are just along for the ride — or is there a way to give non-human causes that kind of explanatory weight without stripping people of agency? and is the framing even consistent? the same book will say napoleon "chose" to invade russia and then blame the winter, but the winter never gets credit for "choosing" to be cold. so what commitments about causation and responsibility does this practice of listing volcanoes alongside people actually presuppose, and where does it fall apart?
