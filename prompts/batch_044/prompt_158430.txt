I hold that in temporal illusions such as the color phi phenomenon — where a spot that changes color in mid-flight is experienced as changing color before it reaches the second position — the brain resolves the anomaly through exactly one of two mechanisms: an Orwellian revision, in which the earlier conscious percept is retroactively rewritten to match the later one, or a Stalinesque revision, in which the erroneous first percept is suppressed before it ever reaches awareness, as if airbrushed from the record. I further hold that because both accounts predict identical subjective reports and, with present techniques, indistinguishable neural dynamics, the debate over which mechanism actually operates is empirically empty and ought to be abandoned. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this underdetermination conclusion to be wrong? Which empirical findings — involving, say, sub-threshold priming, partial-report paradigms, or the micro-timing of cortical feedback loops — could in principle break the symmetry? Are there conceptual distinctions, for instance about the ontology of unexperienced percepts or the causal efficacy of suppressed representations, that would make one mechanism philosophically preferable regardless of empirical equivalence? And consider a hypothetical variant I will call intermediate phi, in which subjects report not a smooth transition but a brief blank gap between the red and green percepts: would such a result count decisively against one model, or could both be adjusted to accommodate it? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones defenders of the underdetermination thesis would themselves concede as damaging, and assess honestly whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind.
