A friend who has been reading about moral compromise summarized the "gains from trade through value-system compromise" view for me like this: "When two moral frameworks with genuinely incompatible axiologies interact—say, one that treats ecological integrity as carrying non-negotiable intrinsic worth and one that treats it as one substitutable value among others—they can construct hybrid institutions that leave both sides better off without requiring either to abandon its core commitments. The preservationist framework accepts a regulated carbon market not because it endorses the commodification of nature, but because the regulatory architecture embeds an absolute emissions cap that functions as a deontological side-constraint. The market-oriented framework accepts the cap not out of respect for intrinsic value, but because the constraint prevents long-run ecological collapse that would destroy aggregate welfare. Each framework thereby secures more of what it genuinely values—structural protection for one, sustainable productivity for the other—than either could achieve through unilateral dominance. This is distinct from Rawlsian overlapping consensus, because there is no shared public reason underwriting the arrangement; there is only a stable equilibrium in which each side's normative constraints become the other's strategic incentives. It is also distinct from ordinary political bargaining, because what is being exchanged is not preferences or resources but normative commitments themselves, mediated through institutional design rather than through negotiation over outcomes." I want an accuracy audit of this summary. Going through it carefully: does it fairly distinguish this view from overlapping consensus, or does it overstate the absence of shared reasons and understate the role of mutual recognition? Is the carbon-market example genuinely an exchange of normative commitments, or does it smuggle in a consequentialist metric that makes the preservationist "gain" depend on tacitly accepting consequentialist reasoning about system collapse? And if these points are corrected, does the core claim—that incompatible value systems can achieve mutual normative improvement through institutional compromise without conceptual convergence—still hold, or does it collapse into one of the positions it claims to differ from?
