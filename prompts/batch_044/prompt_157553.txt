As I draft a novel about distributive justice within an isolated ecological settlement, I recently observed a workshop in which one participant repeatedly invoked the maxim that equality possesses no intrinsic value in order to dismiss every proposal for equalizing material conditions among the colonists, insisting that moral allocation should attend solely to the absolute condition of the worst-off. The formulation seemed to shift its force depending on whether the speaker was addressing comparative deprivation, metaphysical value-status, or practical policy design, yet he treated it in every case as decisive. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan might actually be expressing—distinguishing, for example, between the claim that equality is not a fundamental moral reason, the claim that comparative disadvantage adds no independent wrong beyond absolute deprivation, the claim that leveling down can never constitute an improvement, and the claim that equality is valuable only instrumentally? For each reading, indicate whether it is tautological, substantively defensible, or false; specify which readings genuinely license rejecting equalization in favor of pure prioritarian priority, and which instead collapse into a sufficiency view, a utilitarian calculus, or an entirely different position; and identify which interpretation the speaker in my workshop was most likely deploying when he used the phrase specifically to block equal-ration policies. In particular, point out the weakest point in that favored reading, especially regarding whether it can distinguish the moral urgency of benefiting the badly-off from any concern about their relative standing, without implicitly smuggling comparative judgments back in through the back door.
