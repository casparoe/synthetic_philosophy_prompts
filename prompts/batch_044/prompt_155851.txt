For a working group on AI governance economics, construct a taxonomy tree that sorts positions on the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax. The root node should split on whether aligning a system—making it more robustly pursue a specified objective with fewer harmful side effects—necessarily imposes a competitive disadvantage relative to an unaligned counterpart of equivalent base capability. From there, branch first by the mechanism of the tax: is it intrinsic to the alignment procedure itself (e.g., direct capability suppression, objective distortion, or refusal behavior that eliminates profitable actions); extrinsic to the developer (e.g., audit delays, compute reallocation, liability exposure, or slower iteration cycles); or ecological (e.g., strategic vulnerability in multi-agent or open-weights deployment, where aligned agents forego risky but rewarding interactions that unaligned competitors accept)? A second-level split should distinguish tax incidence: is the burden borne primarily by the model’s direct task performance, by the deployer’s time-to-market or capital cost, or by long-run market share in an ecosystem where alignment is not universally enforced? Each internal node must be phrased as a claim or question a respondent could answer without knowing the taxonomy’s labels, so that the tree sorts by commitments rather than by self-identification. The leaves should carry either a named position from the public debate (for example, “tax is a transient scaling artifact,” “institutional tax dominates technical tax,” “competitive equilibrium requires universal alignment,” or “open-weights dynamics make unilateral alignment fatal”) or be marked “unnamed” if the combination is logically possible but currently undefended; each leaf must include one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—something its defenders must accept that sounds counterintuitive or costly. Flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate, and identify any unoccupied branch combination, diagnosing whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; build the classification entirely from the logical structure of the debate.
