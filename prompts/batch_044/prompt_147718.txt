Here is an argument I sketched for a seminar on origin essentialism and now find suspicious because the conclusion is far stronger than anything the view was meant to deliver.

(1) Origin essentialism (the Kripke-style claim): necessarily, I originated from the particular sperm S and egg E that actually fused to form my zygote Z; I could not have come from any different gamete pair.

(2) If I necessarily originated from S and E, then in every possible world in which I exist, the fusion of S and E occurs and produces Z, from which I develop.

(3) But S and E are concrete particulars with specific genetic profiles; they exist only if the specific meiotic processes in my biological parents actually occurred.

(4) Those meiotic processes require my parents to exist with exactly the genomes they actually have, in exactly the physiological and environmental conditions that yielded those gametes.

(5) So in every world where I exist, my parents exist with those genomes and produce S and E.

(6) Now apply the same reasoning backward: those parental genomes are themselves the products of specific gamete fusions in the previous generation. Repeating the inference, my existence metaphysically requires the exact genetic constitution and reproductive conditions of every ancestor, all the way back through evolutionary history.

(7) One might object that IVF, donor gametes, or surrogacy could decouple conception from the parents’ bodies: S and E could be extracted, fused in vitro, and implanted elsewhere. But that still requires S and E themselves, and therefore requires my parents’ genomes and the conditions that produced them; only the mode of fusion changes, so (5) stands.

(8) Furthermore, consider monozygotic twinning: the same S and E form Z, which splits into two embryos. If I am one twin, my twin shares my origin pair. So origin essentialism must be refined: I am essentially tied not merely to S and E, but to the specific developmental trajectory from Z that yielded me rather than my twin. Yet this refinement only strengthens the chain in (6), since now I am also tied to the precise splitting event and its conditions.

(9) Putting (6) and (8) together: my existence metaphysically necessitates not only my parents’ specific genomes and reproductive conditions, but also the absence of any interfering factor that would have altered the splitting, implantation, or gestational trajectory. Every causal condition of my conception becomes metaphysically necessary.

(10) But this collapses the distinction between metaphysical and causal necessity. It implies I could not exist in a world where human evolution took one different mutation, where my parents met one day later (yielding different gametes), or where an IVF protocol used a different culture medium. Origin essentialism was meant to block counterfactuals about different sperm and egg, not to anchor my identity to the entire evolutionary and reproductive history of the species.

If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) and reject (10) than abandon origin essentialism altogether, but I want to know exactly what that costs.

Which step is the real culprit—an equivocation between de re and de dicto necessity, a scope shift that turns gamete-identity into ancestral determinism, or an overreach in (8) that sneaks embryo-identity conditions into gamete-origin conditions? Explain why that step looked plausible, especially given the IVF and twinning complications in (7) and (8), and then price the alternatives: what must the essentialist who accepts the backward chain concede, what must the counterpart theorist who rejects the transitivity pay, and what does the de re / de dicto distinction actually buy here?
