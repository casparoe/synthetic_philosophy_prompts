I have spent my working life as a natural scientist and only recently turned to philosophy in retirement, and I have been reading Nietzsche's meditation on history and life with particular care. At my reading group, two other members—Clara, a former museum curator, and David, an engineer—got into a sustained disagreement about how to apply Nietzsche's three modes of historical consciousness: the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical. I am writing to ask you to adjudicate their exchange point by point, without taking my side, since I genuinely could not decide.

Clara opened by arguing that critical history is the only one of the three that genuinely serves life, because its function is to judge and condemn the past in order to free the present from its burden. She said monumental history is essentially a form of inspirational propaganda: by holding up heroic figures from the past as models, it paralyzes present action by making ordinary efforts seem inadequate. She added that antiquarian history is even worse, since it preserves everything out of pious sentiment and smothers living culture under the weight of what must be conserved.

David replied that Clara had misread the danger Nietzsche actually warned against. He said critical history is the most dangerous mode for life, not the healthiest, because it demands a hyper-historical consciousness that dissolves the unhistorical horizon necessary for any vigorous action. Monumental history, he insisted, supplies the exemplars that stimulate present achievement, while antiquarian history provides the rootedness and continuity without which life feels groundless. Critical history is only salutary, he claimed, when it serves one of the other two modes—when it clears away dead wood to make room for new growth—not when it dominates as an end in itself.

Clara came back with a distinction she thought saved her position: critical history does not accumulate the past but actively reduces it by rendering judgments that allow selective forgetting. She argued that the hyper-historical sickness Nietzsche described comes from excessive antiquarian and monumental accumulation, not from criticism itself. Without critical history, she said, the other two modes become oppressive precisely because there is no mechanism to limit them.

David then introduced a concrete case to test their disagreement. He asked how a society should treat the legacy of a once-celebrated scientist whose theories are now discredited but who remains influential in the institutional memory of a field. Should monuments, named lectures, and preserved archives be maintained?

Clara said that actively removing such honors and renaming institutions is an exercise in critical history that serves life by breaking the false authority of the past. David countered that this practice is not critical history at all, but rather a form of inverted antiquarianism: the figure is preserved in collective memory precisely as a negative example, so the past remains centrally present rather than being genuinely released. True critical history, he argued, would allow the figure to fade into genuine obscurity, not to be kept alive through ritual condemnation.

Clara responded that forgetting is never spontaneous; it requires prior judgment. The critical act of condemning the legacy is what makes subsequent forgetting possible, so David's desire to let things fade away is actually the antiquarian impulse to leave everything undisturbed in its place, including absence. She also noted that in a scientific culture—which she acknowledged might resonate with my background—retaining discredited theories in textbooks as errors serves an educational purpose, but that purpose is critical, not antiquarian, because it teaches students what to reject.

David's final move was to claim that Clara's position collapses the distinction between critical and antiquarian history. If preserving a discredited scientist's papers in an archive is antiquarian, he said, then preserving them in a so-called hall of errors is still preservation, and labeling it critical is just a rhetorical relabeling. He concluded that Clara's view makes critical history impossible as a distinct mode, reducing Nietzsche's tripartite scheme to two modes with different moods.

Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about what, and what if anything both are missing. I would also like you to say which of their positions comes closer to Nietzsche's actual view, though I understand that requires interpretation rather than simple citation.
