As a novelist constructing a dialogue for a bioethicist who dismisses cross-species rights activism, I have my character invoke the slogan that captures the stance I associate with Robert Nozick: utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people. In the scene, this line is meant to do real argumentative work—justifying a regulatory framework that maximizes aggregate welfare for non-human sentients while treating human beings as bearers of inviolable side-constraints. Before I finalize the draft, I need the slogan dissected.

Please treat the sentence not as a single thesis but as a family of precise claims hiding under one grammatical skin. Lay out the readings as a menu rather than selecting one. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false; and specify which version my character must be holding if the line is to support that particular two-tier policy regime.

I want the menu to cover at least these possibilities:

- A bare domain split: aggregation is appropriate for animals, deontological restrictions for persons, with no further account of where or why the boundary lies.
- A capacity threshold: the division tracks a specific cognitive or agential property—perhaps rational autonomy, second-order volition, or full moral agency—so that lacking it places a being under maximization rules and possessing it triggers side-constraints.
- A genuinely hybrid normative architecture: two independent moral theories run in parallel, one consequentialist for non-humans and one Kantian for humans, with no meta-ethical principle unifying them and no answer to why the same being could not fall under both.
- A rhetorical compression: the slogan sounds like a sharp dualism but actually masks either a gradualist view about moral status or an unexamined reliance on biological species membership as the true, unstated criterion.

For each, say what commitments it carries, whether it is coherent, and whether it can genuinely bear the weight of justifying a split regulatory regime without collapsing into contradiction. Also flag any pair of leaves your taxonomy cannot cleanly separate—for instance, whether the capacity-based reading collapses into the bare domain split once the relevant capacity is identified with being human, or whether the hybrid architecture can survive the objection that interspecies comparison of welfare makes aggregation across the boundary unavoidable. Finally, tell me which of these readings people who actually deploy this line in debate usually mean, and which ones are philosophical straw figures that no serious defender holds.

I am not asking for citations, page references, or a reading list; I need conceptual precision so that my character’s words are philosophically legible rather than vague.
