For the past six years, I have co-run a neighborhood repair collective out of a shared workshop. The space holds tools with no clear individual owner; repair techniques have evolved through collective trial and error into a documented but distributed knowledge base that no one person fully possesses; and the neighborhood treats us as a single point of contact, despite constant turnover in who is actually present. My co-founder now wants to dissolve the arrangement and divide assets by individual contribution. She argues that "the collective" is merely a convenient label for whatever individuals and objects happen to occupy the space, since every decision and every repaired item can ultimately be traced to specific persons. She adds that if I treat our integrated workshop as a genuinely real unit, consistency forces me to treat every temporary crowd—passengers on a bus, shoppers in a line—as an equally real unit, which is absurd. I feel that the interdependent practices and the distributed knowledge constitute something more than aggregation, yet I cannot point to any single discrete part that proves it. Where does her reasoning fail, if it does? And if I defend the reality of our collective, must I accept that every arbitrary grouping is real?
