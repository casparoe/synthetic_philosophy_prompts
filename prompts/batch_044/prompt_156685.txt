Write a poem in American English, in blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) of roughly thirty to forty lines, taking the Gould-Lewontin critique of adaptationism as its subject. Use the architectural spandrels of San Marco's dome as the controlling conceit: describe how the triangular spaces between arches are structural necessities of the dome's construction, not designed to bear the mosaics that now fill them, and extend this to illuminate the biological claim that many traits are evolutionary byproducts—spandrels—rather than direct adaptations.

The poem should do structural work that mirrors its content. Include exactly two lines that are syntactically or metrically necessary "filler"—transitional phrases required only to preserve the iambic rhythm or grammatical flow—whose literal meaning, upon re-reading, accidentally articulates the deepest point about spandrels being non-adaptive byproducts. These lines should function as hidden keystones: structurally indispensable to the verse, yet carrying the philosophical weight, just as architectural spandrels are indispensable to the dome yet ornamented with unintended art.

Do not resolve the tension into a simple verdict for or against adaptationism; leave open whether a given trait can be cleanly sorted into adaptation or spandrel. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the spandrels argument recognizes the distinction between direct adaptations and structural byproducts, without relying on a bibliography, reading list, citation, or specific publication date.
