I am a high school student running an after-school philosophy workshop for about fourteen peers in grades 11 and 12; we have one 60-minute session and no outside readings assigned. I want you to design an in-class simulation that makes us feel the force of Peter Singer’s claim that geographical distance should not diminish our duty to rescue strangers in dire need, but with a twist that keeps it from being a familiar retelling: the rules should build in a hidden structural feature—direct local rescue is often fungible, because if I do not act someone else nearby probably will, whereas remote aid is usually non-fungible—so that we discover our intuition that “I must save the child right in front of me” may actually rest on an unexamined belief about our own irreplaceability rather than on proximity itself.

Please include a clear setting and group structure, roles or positions for participants, rules and a payoff scheme that creates real tension without using real money (for example, points that determine who chooses our next discussion topic or a shared snack budget), a timeline of play, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto Singer’s premises and onto the fungibility problem. The idea should emerge from what happens in the choices and incentives, not mainly from explanation afterward.

Organize your response in exactly these sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:
1. Setup and What We Should Come Away Seeing (about 100 words)
2. Materials, Roles, and Rules of Play (about 180 words)
3. Timeline of the Session (about 150 words)
4. The Debrief: Mapping Play to the Argument (about 200 words)

We should finish by seeing that distance is not the true source of our reluctance, and that direct rescue feels obligatory partly because we silently assume we are irreplaceable in the near case—an assumption the simulation should make us test and then undermine ourselves, before you name it. Be frank about which feature of Singer’s moral argument the game can never fully capture and that you must supply verbally.
