I sit on an interdisciplinary working group in philosophy of history that has been trying to articulate a principled demarcation between legitimate historical methodology and pseudohistory, and we keep circling the same deadlock with Fomenko's New Chronology. For context, the theory—advanced by the mathematician Anatoly Fomenko—holds that the conventional chronology of ancient Rome, Greece, and Egypt is largely a fabrication of later scribal and editorial traditions, and that the events conventionally assigned to those civilizations actually cluster in the Middle Ages, separated by far shorter intervals. Fomenko supports this with statistical correlations between dynastic lists, astronomical re-datings of zodiac references in texts such as the Almagest, and the claim that archaeological stratigraphy has been systematically misread; mainstream historians, by contrast, treat the theory as not even warranting peer-reviewed engagement, relying instead on the coherence of material culture, documentary transmission, and established stratigraphic sequences.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not genuinely empirical in the usual sense—both sides can point to the same texts, artifacts, and astronomical tables—but rather concerns what an adequate historical explanation must include. Historians appear to require narrative continuity across material and textual lines of evidence, whereas Fomenko demands statistical independence and astronomical precision, treating narrative coherence as circular reasoning. Yet I am unsure whether this captures the real structure of the stall. Is this actually a verbal dispute, with one side answering a normative question about institutional authority and disciplinary boundaries while the other answers a descriptive question about chronological accuracy? Or is there a concealed substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether statistical correlations in chronicle lists can ever override stratigraphic or paleographic evidence—that neither side has made fully explicit? There is also the possibility that the two camps are answering different questions altogether, one about how we justify belief in past events and the other about the mathematical properties of recorded lists, both traveling under the single heading "history."

Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually claims and does: determine whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of incommensurable questions masked by shared vocabulary; identify whether there is a hidden empirical or formal issue—about standards of evidence, the weight of astronomical versus archaeological data, or the conditions for rejecting a documentary tradition—that, if resolved, could actually move the debate; and specify whether the impasse instead signals that the demarcation question itself is ill-posed. Given your diagnosis, say exactly what would count as progress here: a conceptual distinction that separates the two questions, a methodological rule that adjudicates between statistical and stratigraphic priorities, a formal result that undermines one side's reasoning, or an honest argument that permanent deadlock should be expected and why.
