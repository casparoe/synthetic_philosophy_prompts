I will present a paper defending a revisionist account of moral worth at a philosophy of action workshop. My thesis is that moral worth attaches not to the purity or transparency of an agent's motivational state at the moment of action—the standard "right reasons" view—but to what I term reflexive normative stability: the agent's prior structuring of their deliberative environment (through precommitment mechanisms, institutional scaffolds, or deliberative protocols) such that the right reason remains authoritative and accessible under conditions of anticipated motivational erosion. On this view, an agent who acts through well-designed scaffolding retains full moral worth even when the immediate psychological state lacks the pristine motivational clarity that Kantians require; worth is a property of the architecture of agency, not only of the occurrent mental state.

The audience is specific and hostile in precise ways: three Kantian moral philosophers who hold that any instrumental mediation between the will and the moral law corrupts autonomy and thus worth; two situationist moral psychologists who deny that character traits are stable enough to make "structuring one's environment" a coherent achievement rather than a post-hoc narrative; one virtue ethicist who will argue that this collapses practical wisdom (phronesis) into behavioral engineering; and two advanced graduate students who will press on whether the view makes moral worth too easily available to well-resourced agents with access to institutional support. They care about autonomy, empirical realism, and distributive fairness in moral achievement.
