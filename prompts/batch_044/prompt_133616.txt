For a graduate seminar I am designing on the limits of moral reasoning, I want to move beyond the standard textbook treatment of Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the well-known case in which a husband saves his wife from drowning. Rather than asking for a defense or refutation of Williams, I want you to specify the full set of desiderata that any adequate theory of the relationship between moral motivation and personal ground projects must satisfy. The theory must account for the classic scenario in which an agent acts from deep attachment without interposing an impartial moral calculation, yet it must also distinguish that case from mere partiality or nepotism; it must explain why the insertion of a deliberative step—judging that it is permissible to save one's spouse rather than a stranger—can vitiate the very attachment it purports to justify, without thereby collapsing all unreflective action into integrity-preserving motivation.

To prevent the discussion from remaining trapped in the familiar stalemate between consequentialist and deontological camps, I ask you to inject a further, more demanding constraint: the theory must accommodate what I shall call archival integrity, the possibility that an agent's ground project involves the preservation or transmission of a particular history, record, or practice, such that acting in a morally required way may require not merely refraining from harm but actively maintaining a narrative continuity that impartial moral rules do not register. Consider, for instance, a historian whose ground project is the custodianship of a community's founding archive; she faces a choice between revealing a damaging truth that would serve impartial justice and suppressing or contextualizing the record in a way that preserves the archival coherence sustaining both her own identity and the community's capacity for collective self-understanding. The desiderata should specify: which phenomena any adequate theory must explain; which distinctions it must preserve (for example, between possessing integrity and having mere preference, between deliberative suppression and motivational absence, and between ground projects that can be overridden and those that define the agent's practical identity); which puzzles it must dissolve, including the problem of having one thought too many but also the problem of moral remainder when ground projects are sacrificed for moral reasons; and which findings from moral psychology or the philosophy of action it cannot contradict. You should flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—particularly any tension between universalizability and the particularity of ground projects, or between the transparency required for moral justification and the opacity characteristic of deep attachment. Finally, indicate briefly how a utilitarian account, a Kantian account of moral worth, and a virtue-theoretic account of practical wisdom fare against your list, but keep the weight firmly on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard. This specification should serve as the evaluative framework for the seminar, not as an invitation to summarize familiar positions.
