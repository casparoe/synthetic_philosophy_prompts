A colleague in a recent seminar offered this summary of the Bayesian problem of old evidence: Bayesians hold that a hypothesis is confirmed by evidence only if the posterior exceeds the prior after conditioning on that evidence. Once a fact is already known, its prior probability is one, so conditioning on it cannot shift belief in any hypothesis. This is why old evidence appears to pose a fatal difficulty: Einstein's account of Mercury's perihelion was tested against orbital measurements that astronomers had possessed for decades, yet we regard those very measurements as strong confirmation. The standard defense is to say that we do not condition on the raw data, but on the proposition that the theory predicts them; but this is not a genuine probability update at all, since it replaces a direct likelihood comparison with a meta-judgment about explanatory success. Consequently, the Bayesian either denies that old evidence can confirm a theory, or smuggles in a non-probabilistic notion of coherence that violates the likelihood principle.

I suspect the summary captures the shape of the classic objection and its best-known reply, yet it also runs together several distinctions that careful defenders keep apart—particularly between diachronic learning and synchronic coherence, between the old-evidence problem proper and the separate problem of logical omniscience, and between conditioning on a token event versus conditioning on the event-type under a predictive model.
