I am a retired evolutionary biologist who, since leaving active laboratory work, have been studying philosophy in earnest, especially Nietzsche’s writings on the origins and typology of moral valuation. I now serve on the advisory board of my university’s Institute for the History and Ethics of Science. At our March meeting we must vote on a proposed revision to the Institute’s founding charter, submitted by a working group of philosophers and historians. The revision would replace the charter’s present commitment to the equitable advancement of human welfare with an explicit analytical framework that distinguishes master-type and slave-type moral valuations. The proponents argue that scientific excellence has historically been driven by the competitive, hierarchical impulses Nietzsche associates with master morality, and that an intellectually honest institution should study both genealogical origins without disguising normative preference behind apparently neutral language. The opponents, including several fellow board members, maintain that this reframes the Institute’s institutional duty away from the protection of vulnerable populations and risks treating hierarchical contempt as a legitimate object of study rather than as a moral failure to be overcome.

Please draft the formal decision memo I should circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It must recommend a concrete course of action—approve the revision, reject it, or approve it subject to specific conditions—and include a clear time frame for implementation or for a subsequent review. The reasoning should engage the proponents’ position seriously rather than merely restating the current charter’s language, and it should treat the strongest objection with equal honesty: namely, that applying a genealogical analysis of values to an institutional charter may confuse describing where values originate with prescribing what the institution ought to prioritize. In addressing this, please examine the distinction I have begun calling the genealogical mirror error—the assumption that because a value can be traced to a particular moral type, the institution should organize its priorities around that type—and assess whether the proposed charter avoids committing it or instead falls into it. The memo should be polished, suitable for circulation to board members, and should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or further citations.
