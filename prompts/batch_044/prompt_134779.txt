For a seminar on moral philosophy I am trying to settle my position on why one should be moral, especially in light of Glaucon's challenge and the ring of Gyges, and I want a careful consistency audit before I present. Here is where I stand. First, I take Glaucon's challenge to be essentially about whether justice possesses any value that survives the complete removal of social consequences; the ring of Gyges succeeds in isolating that question precisely because perfect invisibility eliminates reputation, punishment, and reciprocity altogether. Second, I reject Plato's answer in the Republic, not because psychic harmony is unreal, but because treating harmony as the good that justice produces concedes Glaucon's framing by making justice instrumentally valuable to the agent's own well-being rather than valuable in itself. Third, I am a moral anti-realist in a fairly standard sense: I deny that there are mind-independent moral properties or categorical moral obligations in the ontology of the world. Fourth, despite this anti-realism, I think Glaucon is wrong that the invisible agent has no reason to refrain from exploitation, because practical rationality is itself normative and requires treating the grounds of one's action as universalizable; this generates constraints on behavior that mirror moral ones, but they arise from the structure of agency rather than from moral facts. Fifth, I reject evolutionary debunking arguments against moral belief, on the principle that explaining the causal origin of a belief does not defeat its justification, and I extend the same reasoning to reject debunking attempts directed at practical rationality itself. Sixth, I hold that if an agent were permanently invisible and genuinely detached from all human community, she would lack moral obligations in any robust sense, yet she would still be rationally defective—analogous to being practically irrational—if she violated the universalizability constraint, and this defect is not reducible to harm done to herself or to others.
