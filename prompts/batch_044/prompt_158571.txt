I'm working through some notes for a philosophy of mathematics reading group, and I keep circling back to a particular argumentative pattern that arises whenever formal verification enters the conversation. The setup is familiar enough: a result has been checked in a proof assistant like Lean or Coq, and the discussion turns to what this says about the ontology of the mathematical objects involved. What interests me is not the standard platonist-nominalist disagreement, but a more specific slide I notice between two claims. On one side, someone argues that mechanized verification gives us access to a mathematical reality that exists independently of human psychology—almost as if the computer, being free of human cognitive bias, acts as a transparent window onto pre-existing structures. On the other side, the same person might emphasize that formalization forces us to specify every definitional choice, every encoding of an object, and every dependency in the library, which seems to construct the object in unprecedented detail rather than merely uncovering it.

The tension is that machine independence is invoked simultaneously as evidence for discovery and as evidence for construction. I have seen this in discussions about whether a computer-verified proof of, say, a classification result in algebraic topology reveals the true structure of a space, or instead manufactures a precisely delimited artifact that owes its existence to the particular type-theoretic framework and library conventions used. Is there an established term for this specific hybrid—this use of mechanized epistemology to ground an ontological claim that hovers between discovery and construction? If there is, how does it relate to traditional platonism and to formalism? If there is not, I would want a name and a sharp boundary: what distinguishes it from ordinary mathematical platonism, from the view that formal proofs are just longer versions of informal ones, and from the nominalist claim that all mathematical objects are artifacts of notation? The key feature for me is the special ontological weight given to machine-checkability itself, not merely to the result being verified.
