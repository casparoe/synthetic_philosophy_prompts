I have been discussing with colleagues in landscape restoration a habit of mind I keep encountering in popular accounts of the past: the tendency to treat earlier centuries as if they were merely unfinished drafts of the present, grading every institution and belief by how well it anticipates modern liberal norms. I understand this has a name — Whig history — though I have never studied it formally; my background is in practical horticulture and the repair of historic gardens, not in academia. I should like you to translate both sides of this debate — the view that history is best read as a progressive cultivation toward present values, and the opposing insistence that each epoch must be understood on its own soil — into the vocabulary I actually use: rootstock preservation versus grafting, selective pruning for form versus health, the classification of a plant as invasive or as part of an evolved local ecology, and the decision whether a restored garden should freeze one historical moment or acknowledge continuous change.

Please treat the translation systematically: for each position, say what maps cleanly onto my gardening framework, what requires distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. Then deliver the residue report: does my framework reveal something the historians miss — perhaps about the irreversibility of growth, or the dignity of decay — or does it fail precisely because botanical change lacks the intentional, collective agency that drives human societies? I am especially interested in whether the idea of an invasive species suppressing local diversity can illuminate how Whig narratives crowd out alternative histories, or whether that analogy collapses as soon as one remembers that ideas spread through persuasion rather than root competition. In short, I want to know whether the gaps in translation expose weaknesses in the gardening metaphor or weaknesses in the clarity of the original historical argument.
