I'm a backend software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm trying to make sense of a disagreement that broke out among colleagues during a lunch discussion about animal ethics and political philosophy. The conversation turned specifically to Robert Nozick's position on animals — not his well-known entitlement theory, but his claim that animals occupy an intermediate moral status, protected by something weaker than the side constraints that govern human interaction but stronger than pure instrumental use.

Two of my colleagues, Maya and Leo, went back and forth for several rounds, and neither convinced the other. I want you to adjudicate their exchange point by point, not just declare a winner overall.

Maya opened by saying that Nozick's framework is structurally clear: human beings are ends in themselves, so we may never use a person merely as a means, regardless of aggregate welfare gains; animals, however, lack the rational autonomy that grounds these side constraints, so with respect to them we should apply a utilitarian calculus — minimize suffering, maximize welfare, and permit use for food or research whenever the net experiential outcome is positive.

Leo replied that this collapses Nozick's actual view. He argued that Nozick does not treat animals as bare utility-buckets. Rather, animals have an intrinsic value and a good of their own, and the reason they do not enjoy full human rights is specifically that they lack the capacity for long-range rational life-planning, not that they lack moral standing altogether. So animals fall into an intermediate category: not pure resources, but not rights-bearers either.

Maya then pressed what she called the marginal-cases problem. If rational autonomy is the criterion for full protection, then a severely cognitively disabled human being might possess less of it than a healthy chimpanzee or dolphin. If Nozick avoids granting rights to those animals, he must either deny rights to some humans or admit that species membership — not rational capacity — is doing all the work. In either case, Maya said, the intermediate status Leo describes collapses back into either full rights or pure welfare aggregation, and the latter is the only consistent Nozickian position for animals.

Leo responded by introducing a structural point drawn from Nozick's broader framework. He noted that Nozick's experience-machine argument demonstrates that reality matters for beings who care about living in contact with it, not merely about having pleasant experiences. Leo then applied this to animals: while they may not form abstract life plans, they are embedded in ecological and behavioral realities — chickens scratch and forage, pigs root and socialize — and reducing them to confined sources of positive experience strips them of the kind of being they are. So factory farming violates not just a welfare budget but a weaker side constraint against treating a being as a mere experience-delivery system for others.

Maya countered that Leo was smuggling Aristotelian teleology into a libertarian framework. Nozick, she insisted, does not ground moral protection in natural function or ecological embedding except instrumentally, as those things affect suffering and pleasure. For animals, welfare is fundamentally experiential; Leo's structural argument creates a hybrid theory that borrows from Nozick but is not actually Nozick's.

Please go through their exchange round by round and rate each move — clearly mistaken, partly right but flawed, clearly right — with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing. In particular, address whether Leo's application of the experience-machine reasoning to animals is a faithful extension of Nozick's logic or an unsupported innovation, and whether Maya's marginal-cases challenge actually undermines Leo's intermediate-status claim or only shows that any capacity-based boundary will have hard edges. Finally, note any point where the disagreement turns not on interpretation of Nozick but on a deeper disagreement about whether moral philosophy should be modular — treating human and animal ethics with separate principles — or unified. Keep the adjudication focused on the arguments as made, and do not treat either speaker as a strawman.
