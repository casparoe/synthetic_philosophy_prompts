I am writing a novel about ideas that outlive their authors, and I need a scene rendered with philosophical precision. Set it in a draughty reading room in Basel during the winter of 1958: two scholars—Dr. Lena Voss, who defends a refined Weberian position, and Dr. Marcus Yeung, a structuralist critic—are debating whether culture can genuinely cause economic change. The immediate occasion is a disputed archival finding about two Lyonnais textile guilds in 1685: after identical market deregulation, only the Calvinist guild adopted double-entry bookkeeping and systematic reinvestment, while the Catholic guild liquidated assets for land purchase. Voss maintains that this divergence is best explained by the concept of elective affinity between inner-worldly asceticism and rationalised capital accumulation—not as a mechanical push, but as a dispositional framework that made certain economic practices culturally intelligible and others morally unthinkable. Yeung counters that the Calvinist guild had prior, structurally embedded access to Huguenot banking networks, and that invoking values merely redescribes a correlation whose causal weight belongs entirely to legal property regimes and credit architecture; he demands to know what counterfactual evidence could show values doing independent work.

Write their dialogue. Both should be equally sharp; neither should be a strawman. They must engage with each other's actual arguments—Voss should address Yeung's demand for counterfactual independence and his structural account of the banking networks, while Yeung should address Voss's claim about cultural intelligibility rather than dismissing it as mysticism. The dialogue should be in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not quote verbatim from Weber's texts or other sources unless you can provide an exact, fetched quotation—otherwise paraphrase freely. Keep the exchange grounded in the Lyon guild case, but allow it to open onto the broader problem of explaining with values: can a culture be a cause without being reducible to individual beliefs, and without collapsing into material conditions?
