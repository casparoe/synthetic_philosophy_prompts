Question: John Taurek's essay "Should the Numbers Count?" examines a rescue case in which you can save either one person or five people, but not both, with equal ability to reach either group and no other morally relevant differences. Present Taurek's argument that the number of people on each side should not determine your choice. Then assess whether his rejection of aggregation commits him to the stronger thesis that saving the five is not merely non-obligatory but actually morally wrong.

Student answer: Taurek holds that every individual has an equal claim to be rescued, but these claims do not combine or aggregate. So the five people do not collectively possess a claim five times stronger than the single person's claim; instead, the rescuer faces one claim on one side and five separate, non-cumulative claims on the other. Because there is no principled way to weigh one claim against five identical but independent claims, Taurek concludes that flipping a coin is the correct response. This shows that saving the five is not required. However, it does not show that saving the five is wrong—saving the one is equally permissible—so the view avoids the charge of demanding that we let the many die. Taurek reaches this conclusion by appealing to a Kantian principle that we must not treat individuals as mere means to the welfare of a collective; using the one person's life as a resource to produce greater overall survival violates that principle. Yet the view faces a problem when special obligations are introduced: if I can save my child or five strangers, my special obligation to my child strengthens her claim, but the strangers' claims still do not aggregate, so I should still save my child. Therefore, the non-aggregation thesis is compatible with common-sense partiality and is not as extreme as critics suggest.
