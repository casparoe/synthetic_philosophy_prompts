I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about Keynes's vision of the good life—specifically his claim that within roughly a century the economic problem will largely be solved and that the true obstacle to human flourishing is not material deprivation but a deep-rooted psychological and moral fixation on accumulating wealth for its own sake. My friend insists Keynes was essentially right: modern overwork and anxiety are mostly collective failures to outgrow that ancient vice, and once basic needs are secure people should naturally choose leisure, wisdom, and agreeable living. I think Keynes underestimated something structural—namely, that scarcity does not vanish with abundance but transforms into competitive, positional dynamics that reproduce anxiety even when absolute needs are met. I want you to write an essay addressing this controversy: was Keynes correct that the primary barrier to the good life is moral and psychological rather than economic, or did he fail to account for the persistence of scarcity in a transformed mode? Weigh both sides seriously, but reach and defend a clear conclusion rather than ending in ambiguity. To keep the analysis precise, apply the distinction between absolute scarcity—the lack of goods necessary for basic welfare—and relative scarcity—the competitive, status-driven dynamics that persist even when basic welfare is secured—to Keynes's framework. Please write in American English.
