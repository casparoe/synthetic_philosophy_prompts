I am developing an essay on why human extinction is harmful beyond the direct loss of life, taking Samuel Scheffler's claim that present projects depend for their value on the existence of future people—what is often termed his afterlife conjecture—as a point of departure. Rather than repeating the familiar examples about art or science, I want to advance a narrower thesis: that extinction does not merely prevent the completion of current theoretical programs, but retroactively transforms live philosophical and scientific disputes into permanently indeterminate ones. If rational engagement in long-running arguments depends on the expectation that future interlocutors will eventually adjudicate, refine, or dissolve them, then the absence of any future audience strips present disagreement of its diachronic rational motivation. On this view, our current efforts become fragments of an aborted program rather than contributions to an ongoing one.

Could you comment on three points? First, is this a legitimate extension of Scheffler's reasoning, or does it illegitimately conflate the value of continuation with the value of eventual resolution? Second, does framing the harm in terms of epistemic projects over-intellectualize extinction, neglecting more basic goods? Third, how might a presentist or anti-realist about future-dependent value respond?
