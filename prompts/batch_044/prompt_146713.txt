I need to plan a session for first-year grad students in political philosophy who haven't really done normative ethics but can follow an argument. I'm teaching the demandingness objection to consequentialism, but I'm not using the usual famine-donation setup—everyone's seen that. Instead I'm framing it through "moral overtime," the idea that consequentialism doesn't just ask for occasional heroism but treats every waking minute as on-call for marginal improvement, like labor overtime that never ends. The carrying example should be something mundane—maybe how they spend a free Saturday morning—so they feel the micro-demand in thier own schedule rather than in some distant crisis.

Plan the arc: what idea has to be in place before they can even see why "too demanding" is a distinct objection (not just "too hard"), the dependancy chain that builds from maximization to continuous scope to accumulated cost, the one scenario to carry it (needs to be close to home, not abstract), the misconceptions in order—probably they think demandingness means "impossible for weak humans" rather than "unreasonable to require," then they confuse it with supererogation, then they think you can escape by just doing "enough"—where in the sequence to intercept each, the check that shows they've actually grasped the objection rather than just nodding at "people are selfish," and what I'm leaving out on purpose: no history of Scheffler or Singer, no formal utility calculus, no bibliography, and I should say so up front. Make clear why you can't present the overtime framing before they've already bought that consequentialism applies to omissions and lifestyle choices, not just big rescue decisions.
