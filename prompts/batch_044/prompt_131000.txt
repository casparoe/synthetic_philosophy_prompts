I am delivering the opening address at tomorrow's interdisciplinary workshop, "Expertise at the Boundaries," convened partly in honour of a retiring colleague whose career-long work on institutional knowledge and epistemic humility shaped our department for two decades. The audience is deliberately bifurcated: approximately half are analytic epistemologists thoroughly familiar with Nozick's tracking conditions and the closure problem they generate, while the remainder are policy researchers, archivists, and family members for whom that vocabulary is entirely foreign. I need a ten-minute speech in British English in which the philosophical idea does genuine structural work rather than serving as ornament. Specifically, I want the address to turn on the tension between Nozick's analysis—that knowing p requires that in nearby possible worlds where p is false, the subject does not believe p—and the intuitive principle of epistemic closure, which tracking conditions appear systematically to violate. Rather than resolving this technically, I wish to use it to frame why institutional expertise ought not to be treated as automatically transitive across domains, even when the relevant entailments seem obvious. Please write the speech so that it acknowledges the colleague's influence without reducing the occasion to biography, remains fully intelligible to the non-specialists present, and avoids any condescension. You should invent and weave in one concrete, somewhat niche illustrative scenario—perhaps a specific historical institution's misplaced extension of predictive authority from one domain into an adjacent one—to carry the argument, rather than relying on well-worn philosophical examples such as sceptical hypotheses or lottery paradoxes. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or verbatim quotations from philosophical texts; paraphrase all technical points in your own words, and avoid reference to specific editions or page numbers.
