As a physicist who spent three decades in condensed-matter research before turning to philosophy of science in retirement, I have been puzzling over why the debate about verisimilitude—whether false scientific theories can stand closer to the truth than other false ones—feels permanently frozen. On one side, philosophers committed to scientific realism insist that rational theory choice requires some notion of truth-likeness, otherwise we lack any explanation for why scientists prefer one failed model over another rather than treating all errors as equally distant from reality. On the other side, formal critics maintain that every rigorous attempt to construct a distance metric across logical or syntactic space either allows manifestly worse theories to count as nearer to truth or makes the metric hostage to arbitrary choices of language, ontology, and formalization. The two camps rarely engage the same concern: the realists treat verisimilitude as a normative condition on the aims of inquiry, while the critics treat it as a mathematical object that must be defined before it can do explanatory work. I wonder whether this is a substantive disagreement about the structure of scientific progress, a verbal dispute about what the word “progress” should cover, or a case of two disciplines answering different questions—one about rational evaluation, one about logical representation—under a single heading. There is also an observation from my own laboratory experience that I almost never see in the philosophical literature: in practice we often compared competing models not by measuring their logical distance from some canonical formulation, but by testing their predictive robustness across independent perturbation regimes, a kind of operational convergence that seemed to track “getting closer” without requiring any global metric over theory-space. Could it be that philosophers are debating formal verisimilitude while working scientists practice an entirely different, local form of approximation that neither side has conceptualized? Given this, is the impasse substantive, verbal, or ill-posed? And if it is a talking-past-each-other situation, what kind of distinction—between normative aims and formal representation, between global metrics and local robustness, or between ontology and predictive practice—would actually allow the debate to move?
