I am a political philosopher preparing to defend a position I term "reflexive integration" at a faculty workshop in philosophy of law and politics. The thesis holds that French laïcité and Anglo-American multiculturalism share a hidden structural symmetry: both treat religious identity as either a public threat requiring suppression or a private asset deserving protection, thereby missing a third possibility. On my view, minority religious practices should be incorporated into shared public institutions—for example, by redesigning school uniform codes rather than granting individual exemptions—but only when the redesign can be justified to non-adherents in non-theological terms. The burden of translation should fall asymmetrically on state institutions, not on minority citizens, which avoids both republican coercion and multicultural reification of group boundaries.

The audience comprises four liberal multiculturalists in the Kymlicka-Taylor tradition, two republican defenders of laïcité as democratic solidarity, three analytic philosophers of language skeptical that translation is a coherent normative criterion, and one visiting constitutional scholar familiar with French administrative case law on religious dress. They care about democratic legitimacy, coercion, and conceptual precision.
