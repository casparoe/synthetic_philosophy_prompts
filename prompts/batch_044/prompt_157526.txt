I came across an essay summarizing Wolpert's no-free-lunch theorems and connecting them to Hume's problem of induction. The author writes: "The theorems prove that, averaged across all logically possible worlds, every predictive algorithm—including scientific induction—performs no better than random guessing. This confirms Hume's suspicion that induction lacks logical justification, but adds a stronger claim: no alternative method enjoys such justification either. Consequently, the problem is dissolved rather than solved, since rational preference among methods is impossible without a prior bias about which worlds are likely. Once you restrict the possibility space using such a bias, the theorem no longer applies, which means the success of science depends not on induction's validity but on an unjustified prejudice in favor of orderly worlds. In short, we do not choose induction because it is rational; we call it rational because we have already decided to live in a world where it works."

I can see that the author is trying to bridge formal learning theory and epistemology, but I am unsure which parts of the bridge are sound. Could you evaluate the summary sentence by sentence? For each claim, say whether it is a defensible reading of the theorems, a common philosophical extrapolation, or a misrepresentation of what the mathematics actually establishes. In particular, address whether the conclusion—that induction rests ultimately on an arbitrary restriction of the possibility space—follows from Wolpert's results alone, or whether it requires additional premises about rational belief that the theorems do not supply. I am especially interested in whether the phrase "all logically possible worlds" is being used in the same sense in the formal proof and in the philosophical argument.
