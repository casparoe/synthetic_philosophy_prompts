Exam question: Moore's paradox is standardly introduced with first-person present-tense assertions such as "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining." (a) Explain why such a sentence is logically consistent yet pragmatically absurd when sincerely asserted. (b) Evaluate whether the paradox is properly restricted to the first-person present indicative, or whether past-tense and third-person analogues are equally problematic. (c) Now consider a plural or collective variant: a speaker says, "We are going to win, but none of us believes that we are going to win." Analyze whether this is genuinely Moore-paradoxical—turning on the same expressive conflict in a single speaker—or whether it collapses into a different failure, such as a lack of collective rationality or a coordination problem among group members.

Student answer: The sentence is logically consistent because the meteorological fact and the psychological fact are entirely independent; rain can occur without my belief. The pragmatic absurdity arises because assertion is not merely a description of the world but an expression of the speaker's belief. By asserting the first clause, I represent myself as believing that it is raining; by asserting the second clause, I represent myself as lacking that belief. This creates an expressive contradiction analogous to a performative contradiction—similar to saying "I hereby promise never to make promises." Regarding scope, the restriction to first-person present tense is not well motivated. A past-tense version—"It rained yesterday, but I did not believe it then"—is equally strange when asserted now, because the speaker is still describing her own mental history in a self-undermining fashion. Turning to the plural case, the captain is not caught in Moore's paradox. The phrase "none of us believes" operates as a distributive statistical claim about the team members. The captain may personally believe in victory while correctly observing that every teammate lacks that belief; since her own belief is separate from the aggregate, there is no single speaker simultaneously expressing belief and disbelief. At most, the group exhibits collective irrationality or a failure of shared confidence, not the specific expressive conflict that defines Moore's paradox.
