I am a novelist who sits on my university's Interdisciplinary Research Ethics and Funding Board. By Thursday we must circulate a decision memo on a joint proposal—submitted by a theoretical physicist and a philosopher of mind—titled "Fundamental Sentience Impact Assessment: A Precautionary Protocol for High-Decoherence Experiments." The applicants argue that a minimal, non-cognitive form of panpsychism—specifically, the attribution of sub-personal phenomenal properties to quantum field excitations—implies that large-scale decoherence events in particle physics may generate a distributed aggregate of micro-suffering. Rather than solving the standard combination problem (how micro-subjects form macro-subjects), they propose what they term a reverse combination calculus: decomposing macro-ethical obligations downward to determine whether experiments producing trillions of field-state transitions carry a cumulative moral weight that current risk assessments ignore. Their concrete ask is that our board mandate a philosophical risk-evaluation layer for any funded physics protocol exceeding a specified decoherence-rate threshold, requiring researchers to model putative aggregate micro-suffering and, where technically feasible, adjust parameters to minimize it—without banning the research. The board is split. One faction treats this as a category mistake that institutionalizes untestable metaphysics; the other treats it as a legitimate extension of precautionary ethics into foundational ontology. I need a formal, two-page memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting recommending a concrete course of action—approve in modified form, reject, or approve with conditions and a review timeline—and it must take seriously the strongest objection, namely that we lack any empirical access to the phenomenal states in question and therefore risk substituting narrative imagination for institutional judgment. Because I am the board's only novelist, I want the memo to acknowledge, without sentimentality, that any decision we make will be an act of legislative imagination about experiences we can never inhabit. The memo should not request bibliographies, citation lists, or further readings, and it should be addressed to the board as an actionable institutional record rather than as personal counsel.
