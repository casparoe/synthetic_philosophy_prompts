I have just spent several hours in a rigorous but friendly argument with a close friend about whether reasons for action must always be internal to an agent's motivational set, or whether genuinely external reasons—reasons that bind an agent regardless of her current desires—are possible. We kept returning to a specific exchange we both witnessed some months ago between two colleagues, Mira and Jonas, who debated this at a departmental colloquium. I am writing to ask you to adjudicate their disagreement point by point, since my friend and I remain divided on how to assess each move. Please treat the following as a faithful reconstruction of their back-and-forth.

Mira opened by pressing what she called an indexing problem for external reasons: if a reason is entirely independent of an agent's motivational psychology, then it either applies to everyone universally or binds no one in particular. To claim that Jonas has a specific reason to keep a promise he made, she argued, requires an internal anchor—some commitment, value, or dispositional motivation that connects the reason specifically to him. Without such an anchor, externalism cannot explain why the reason targets him rather than anyone else.

Jonas replied that the index is supplied by causal-historical and relational facts, not by psychology. Jonas is the one who uttered the promise; he occupies the relevant social position; he stands in the institutional relation that the promise created. These particularizing facts do not depend on his present desires, so external reasons can be agent-specific without being internalized.

Mira responded that particularization is not the same as normative binding. Even if historical facts identify Jonas as the target, they do not by themselves generate an ought for him now. If he has genuinely renounced every motivation connected to promise-keeping—if he feels no weight, recognizes no authority—then pointing to the past fact is like diagnosing an illness in someone who has no body to treat. For a reason to be genuinely his, she insisted, it must be reachable through some sound deliberative route from motivations he actually possesses, even if dormant.

Jonas countered that this collapses justification into psychology and misses the point of normative concepts. A reason can justify an action even when the agent is currently unmoved; that is precisely why we distinguish between being justified and being motivated. He added that Mira's demand for a deliberative route effectively smuggles external commitments back into an internal set, but only in disguised form. If Jonas can recognize that he made this promise and understand its normative weight, he already has access to the reason, regardless of whether he currently desires to honor it.

Mira then introduced a distinction she thought Jonas was ignoring: the difference between occurrent rejection and mere absence of motivation. She conceded that dormant commitments might remain in a motivational set, but she asked about cases of total renunciation—where an agent explicitly repudiates the authority of the institution or relationship that generated the obligation. She argued that in such cases, the external structure has no grip. She also pointed to role-constituted reasons, such as those binding a judge to apply the law. Even here, she said, if the judge genuinely denies that her role gives her any reason—if she rejects the authority of the legal system altogether—then the role's normative structure becomes an empty shell for her. Thus, she concluded, pure externalism fails twice: it cannot solve the indexing problem without internal anchors, and it cannot solve the binding-force problem without them either.

Jonas replied that Mira's view makes every institutional and moral obligation contingent on continuous psychological endorsement, which is socially implausible. Societies depend on norms that bind people who never personally authorized them and who may feel no present desire to comply. If a citizen stops feeling patriotic, he does not thereby acquire a reason to break just laws; the obligation persists independently of his affective state. He argued that Mira was treating internal access as a necessary condition for all normativity, when in fact it is only a condition for motivation. The authority of past commitments and social roles does not dissolve the moment an agent asks so what; rather, that question reveals a failure of recognition, not an absence of reason.

Please go through this exchange round by round. For each of Mira's and Jonas's moves, assess whether the point is clearly mistaken, clearly right, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify your rating. Then indicate who is more right overall, and identify at least one important consideration that both of them are missing—particularly regarding the relationship between indexing and normative force, or regarding how role-based obligations function when an agent's motivational set has been radically restructured. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested only in the conceptual adjudication itself.
