I have no background in medicine or philosophy, but I have been thinking about people who feel pain without suffering from it — a condition I understand is called pain asymbolia. It strikes me that if the physical sensation can exist without the usual aversion, then the "badness" of pain may lie not in the feeling itself but in some separate response we layer on top of it. At the same time, I wonder whether someone who lacks that aversive reaction is missing something essential about human care, especially when they must tend to those who suffer. Please write a short story centered on a trauma surgeon who has lived her entire life with congenital pain asymbolia: she registers every injury with clinical precision, yet never experiences the urge to withdraw or the emotional recoil that her patients feel. The narrative should hinge on a specific encounter with a young patient who has just undergone emergency surgery and asks her, "Does it hurt you to see me like this?" The surgeon must answer without either falsifying her own experience or revealing a gap that could fracture the child's trust in her compassion. Through her response and the actions that follow, explore whether her absence of aversive pain makes her care more clear-eyed or less genuinely connected — not by resolving the tension, but by showing how she lives inside it. Keep the language plain rather than clinical, and do not include references, citations, or bibliographic notes.
