Construct a diagnostic taxonomy—branching tree, not a labeled quiz—of positions on the special composition question, aimed at an advanced metaphysics seminar. Do not open with terms like “universalism” or “nihilism”; instead, every internal node must be a concrete case judgment or claim a student can answer without knowing the taxonomy’s labels.

Root split: Is there a fact of the matter whether arbitrarily scattered parts—say, a pebble on Earth and one on Alpha Centauri—ever compose a single object?

Build the tree through these four diagnostic branches:
1. Continuity through replacement: can an object survive the complete, gradual replacement of all its parts over time?
2. Gappy objects: is a hollow sphere (or a block of Swiss cheese) one object with missing parts, or only smaller objects arranged with empty space between them?
3. Intermittent existence: can the same composite exist on Tuesday, fail to exist Wednesday when its parts are scattered, and exist again Thursday?
4. The restriction condition: if composition is restricted, is the decisive factor spatial contiguity, life or organic function, causal-functional integration, or something else?

Leaves (no more than eight) should include: unrestricted universalism; universalism with composition-as-identity; contact-based restrictionism; life-based (organicist) restrictionism; causal-integration restrictionism; eliminative nihilism; arrangement-only nihilism; and at least one “unnamed” combination that looks coherent but is rarely defended. For each leaf, state the position’s name (or “unnamed”) and, in one sentence, its most awkward commitment.

Flag any pair of leaves that these cases cannot genuinely separate, and for any unoccupied branch combination, briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Phrase every node as a yes/no or either/or question, and avoid asking for bibliographies, author lists, or citation-based literature surveys.
