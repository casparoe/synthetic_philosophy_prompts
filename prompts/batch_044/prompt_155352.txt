As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am preparing a long-form investigation into the conceptual architecture of corruption, and I need a rigorously defended research agenda rather than a survey of familiar scandals. The public conversation tends to treat corruption as self-evident—bribery, embezzlement, nepotism—measured against Western bureaucratic norms. Yet the deeper fractures remain unresolved: whether clientelism and ritualized gift-giving in non-Western contexts constitute corruption, failed institutional substitutes, or legitimate reciprocal obligations; whether universal anti-corruption metrics commit a category error by applying impartial-state ideals to societies in which personalized exchange underwrites social order; and whether corruption is best defined by outcome divergence from formal rules or by the violation of locally legitimate expectations of reciprocity. There is also the uncomfortable possibility that Western anti-corruption instruments themselves depend on hidden gift economies—campaign patronage, lobbying circuits, academic endowment networks—raising questions about the coherence of the universalist stance itself.

If you were advising a research funder able to support roughly three senior scholars and two postdoctoral researchers for four years—working in conceptual ethics, comparative political theory, and institutional design, with no mandate for large-scale empirical fieldwork—which open conceptual questions should they prioritize, and in what order? I want an agenda, not a bibliography. For each priority, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers and theorists rather than for survey researchers, and what conceptual result over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list. In particular, please weigh and rank the following three lines of work: first, refining a universalist definition of corruption that can accommodate contextual reciprocity without collapsing into moral relativism; second, developing a functional framework—distinguishing, for instance, integrative personalized exchange that sustains public goods from extractive exchange that diverts them—that classifies practices by their relationship to collective welfare rather than by deviation from formal rules; and third, examining whether the hidden reciprocity structures within Western governance undermine the normative authority of universal anti-corruption standards, and what that implies for cross-cultural evaluation.

Defend your ranking with explicit criteria: dependency relations among the questions, tractability within the resource constraints I have described, and potential to reshape policy discourse rather than merely describe local variation. Identify which single conceptual breakthrough would most alter how journalists and policymakers talk about corruption, and state honestly which of these three lines you would deprioritize if the funder insisted on cutting one, together with what is lost by that cut. Please do not recommend specific scholars to read, empirical datasets to gather, or citation lists; I am asking what conceptual labor needs doing, and why it deserves years of focused effort.
