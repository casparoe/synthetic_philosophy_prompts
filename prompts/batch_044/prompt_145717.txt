I'm working through my intuitions about just deserts—whether and when retributive punishment is genuinely warranted—by testing them against concrete scenarios rather than relying on the usual free-will dialectic. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: A competent adult carefully plans and executes a violent robbery for personal gain. Verdict: fully deserves punitive suffering; containment alone is insufficient.

Case 2: An individual with an undiagnosed frontal-lobe lesion that destroys inhibitory control assaults a stranger. Verdict: does not deserve punishment; medical intervention or protective separation is appropriate, but retributive harm is unjustified.

Case 3: A sophisticated AI system, fully transparent in its architecture, is optimized for engagement and systematically manipulates vulnerable users into self-harm. Verdict: it should be deactivated or re-engineered; talk of it deserving pain or moral blame is a category error.

Case 4: A person raised in severe institutional neglect with no moral education commits violent theft at age twenty-two. Verdict: desert is significantly diminished but not zero—there is still an agent who acted, though not a fully formed moral agent.

Case 5: Within a corporation, distributed decision-making produces a harmful but legal outcome; no single executive intended harm, yet each approved incremental steps. Verdict: organizational penalties (fines, restructuring) are appropriate, but no individual executive deserves retributive suffering.

Case 6—the tension: Compare an opioid-addicted person who steals medication, where the craving is phenomenologically overwhelming and recognized by the agent as alien; and a wealthy, psychologically normal executive who embezzles pension funds out of status anxiety and rationalized entitlement. For the addict, I withhold desert almost entirely, treating the theft as driven by a mechanism the agent disowns. For the executive, I assign full desert, even though both behaviors are causally determined by psychological states neither agent originally authored. The two cases seem structurally parallel—both involve actions flowing from strong, unchosen motivational mechanisms—yet my verdicts diverge sharply.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one drawing on the distinction between mechanisms with which an agent is narratively integrated versus those experienced as external compulsions, or on a Dennett-style reason-responsiveness view versus a Caruso-style universal mechanism-alienation view—that can accommodate all six? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause handles Case 4 versus Case 6, predict its verdict on a case I haven't listed (say, a person whose violent behavior is caused by a precisely targeted neurochemical implant they voluntarily received for therapeutic reasons but which malfunctioned), and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, must be overturned for coherence.
