I'm preparing questions for a moderated colloquium podcast on ancient economic philosophy. The guest is an invented defender—Dr. Soren—of a specific, non-standard reading of Aristotle's distinction between oikonomia and chrematistike. On her view, the core error of chrematistike is not unlimited accumulation per se but what she terms metric self-reference: the conventional measure of exchange, nomisma, is treated as if it could generate further exchange-value in its own right, collapsing the functional separation between measuring worth and being worth. She argues that this structural confusion explains Aristotle's condemnation of interest-bearing lending (obolostatike) more precisely than the familiar biological metaphor about barren metal, and she claims that the conventional status of money (nomos) actually reinforces the prohibition, since a convention can be corrected to preserve the separation. She occasionally illustrates the pattern with modern analogies—algorithmic stablecoins whose supply rules are pegged to demand metrics—but her thesis is interpretive.

Write me a polished sequence of seven interview questions that engage the actual content of this thesis, avoiding generic biographical or overview questions. Each question should anticipate her standard reply—that the logical separation of measure and measured is prior to ethics, that the biological metaphor is secondary, and that nomos permits institutional redesign—and should include a direct follow-up tailored to that reply. Specifically: (1) ask whether her reading accommodates Aristotle's allowance for limited retail trade (kapelike) conducted solely for household provisioning; (2) distinguish her structural critique from the modern defense of interest as compensation for time preference; (3) challenge her claim that convention-status supports the prohibition, given that conventions are revisable; (4) ask what is wrong with exchanging money directly for more money, without an intermediate commodity serving use-value, if both parties consent and the surplus eventually funds household provision; (5) ask whether any system in which the unit of account is also the primary object of speculative trade reproduces chrematistike regardless of scale, using her stablecoin analogy; (6) ask how she responds to the objection that Aristotle's examples of natural barter already involve implicit measurement and thus blur her desired line; and (7) conclude by asking what specific feature of Aristotle's text, if any, could disconfirm her metric-self-reference interpretation. Present the questions in order, with the anticipated reply and the follow-up clearly paired for each.
