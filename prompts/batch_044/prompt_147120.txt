I was arguing late into the evening with a friend about whether history is mostly structure or mostly accident, and we kept circling back to a contradiction we noticed in our own country’s institutions. In the national secondary-school leaving examinations for modern history, the official marking rubric contains an explicit “causation balance” rule: to reach the top grade band, a student must explain a major outcome—say, the outbreak of a war or the collapse of a regime—through at least two independent structural factors (economic, demographic, or institutional), and any essay that attributes the result primarily to a single contingent micro-event—a misread telegram, an untimely death, a storm that delayed a fleet—is automatically capped at the second-highest band, regardless of the quality of its evidence. Yet the state museum’s permanent gallery on exactly the same periods is organized around “inflection points”: each room isolates one highly contingent occurrence and traces its cascading consequences across decades, with no mandatory structural-background panel and with curators explicitly discouraged from framing the event as “merely symptomatic” of deeper forces. My friend says these are just pedagogical preferences, but the asymmetry feels philosophically loaded to me.

What theory of historical causation, or of explanatory adequacy, does the exam rubric actually presuppose? Must it be committed to the view that structural causes are metaphysically more fundamental, or can it be read as a purely pragmatic standard about what counts as mature historical reasoning? Is it implicitly deterministic—treating contingency as epiphenomenal noise—or is it compatible with a view that genuinely respects contingency but assigns it lower pedagogical priority? Conversely, does the museum’s “inflection point” design presuppose a butterfly-effect ontology in which micro-contingencies possess autonomous causal power, or could a structuralist consistently use such narratives as pedagogical scaffolding without endorsing their metaphysics? Reconstruct the implicit theories behind both practices, distinguish what each strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, identify whether the two institutions are philosophically incompatible or only apparently so, and then tell me what a unified curriculum—one that treated contingency and structure symmetrically in both assessment and curation—would have to change about grading criteria, exhibit design, and the very concept of a “good” historical explanation.
