I'm a doctoral student working between philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I've been developing a refined version of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness that I think sidesteps the standard charge of self-defeat. Rather than denying that mental life exists at all—treating consciousness as a wholesale hallucination analogous to a perceptual illusion of a non-existent object—I want to defend a representational-misdescription variant. On this view, first-order sensory representations and higher-order meta-representational tracking states are entirely real, but subjects systematically misattribute to them a property I call phenomenal luminosity: the alleged feature of being immediately given to oneself in a non-representational, self-transparent, and irreducibly qualitative manner. The illusion is therefore not the absence of experience, but the spurious "presence-tag" that introspective architecture attaches to these representations, making them seem as though they possess an intrinsic, non-relational givenness. Consequently, the so-called explanatory gap is reconceived not as an ontological chasm between physical and phenomenal properties, but as a structural mismatch between the representational format of first-order sensory states and the reconstructive, model-based format of second-order self-monitoring.

What I need is a rigorous map of this specific formulation's vulnerabilities—what would genuinely have to be true for it to be wrong, and how exposed it actually is. Please distinguish between defeaters that would be fatal—such as evidence that the presence-tag is not spurious but structurally necessary for any coherent form of self-reference, or that first-order representations cannot be accessed without already carrying phenomenal luminosity—and those that would be merely awkward, like neuroscientific findings about prefrontal reconstruction that leave the core representational claim untouched but complicate the story about misdescription. I want to know which intuitions, formal results in the philosophy of language about indexicality and de se reference, and empirical findings about introspective error this view stands or falls with. In particular, assess whether committed defenders of this representational-misdescription illusionism would concede any of these as genuinely damaging, or whether they can absorb them by redescription; and if the view can absorb every apparent counter-example in this way, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a sign that the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability? I am especially interested in whether there are internal tensions—say, between denying phenomenal luminosity and still needing some account of why misdescription is systematically directed rather than random—that defenders themselves recognize as pressure points. Please rank the considerations from decisive to merely awkward, and include an honest judgment about whether this version of illusionism risks anything substantial or has become conceptually insulated in a way that undermines its empirical ambitions.
