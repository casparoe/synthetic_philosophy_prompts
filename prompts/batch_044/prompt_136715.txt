I've been drafting a short essay for my philosophy of art seminar about conceptual slippage in tiny craft domains, and I got stuck on this idea I want to run by you. The domain I'm using is archival photo restoration — specifically this practice some workshop conservators call "ghost-layering" (maybe not the standard term, but that's what they used): instead of repairing a torn photographic emulsion, they stabilize the tear and digitize both the damaged layer and a reconstructed version, then present them as one composite where the damage stays visible but "frozen" in place. My argument is that this creates a slippage between "restoration" and "reconstruction" that you don't see in painting conservation, because with photographs the "original" was already a mechanical reproduction, so there's no authentic substrate to betray. I push it further and say that "copycat" — usually a slur for derivative work — actually flips to a positive value here, since the best ghost-layer is the one that precisely mimics the chemical behavior of the original damage without concealing it.

I'm not asking for a rewrite, just comments on whether the argument holds or if I'm just dazzled by workshop jargon. Specifically: does the absence of an "authentic substrate" in photograpic paper really dissolve that boundary, or am I ignoring how conservators treat the paper base itself as sacred? And am I making a category error by applying "copycat" to a process rather than to an object? I also included a section on silver halide migration patterns — should I keep that concrete detail, or does it just clutter the philosophical point? If you think the framing is broken and there's a better microdomain for this kind of conceptual fluidity — maybe not photos at all — tell me, but don't hunt for examples, just say whether the structure feels off. Any other misconceptions I should watch for?
