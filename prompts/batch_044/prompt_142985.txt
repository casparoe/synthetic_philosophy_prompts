I've spent a lot of time thinking about whether morality is just local custom or whether there's something genuinely shared across human societies. I know about the research showing that Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic—WEIRD—populations are psychological outliers: they tend to weight harm and fairness far more heavily than loyalty, authority, or purity, compared with many other cultures. Out of that, I've formed a view that feels like a middle ground, but I want to test how exposed it really is.

My position is this: moral objectivity does not require every culture to agree on the same first-order rules. Instead, it requires that every functioning moral community possesses what I call a "dispute-resolution grammar"—an implicit, shared framework that lets its members spot internal inconsistencies, understand outsiders' complaints as moral rather than merely hostile, and correct course through reasoning rather than pure coercion. The WEIRD/non-WEIRD split is just a difference in weighting within that grammar; it doesn't threaten objectivity. What would genuinely defeat the view is evidence that some cultures lack any such grammar—treating moral conflict as nothing but power struggles, or operating with reasoning structures so structurally alien that no translation into common concerns about coordination, status, or suffering is possible. I think that hasn't happened, but I want to know exactly what it would take to change my mind.

Please structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. Decisive defeaters (about 250 words): What specific kinds of evidence—anthropological, psychological, or conceptual—would genuinely overturn the claim of a universal dispute-resolution grammar? Focus on cross-cultural patterns or formal features of moral reasoning, not on generic moral skepticism. Note which of these have arguably already been observed in existing research, and say why each strikes the core of the view rather than just its surface.

Merely awkward adjustments (about 200 words): What findings would force me to shrink or complicate the view without destroying it—for instance, by saying the grammar exists but is rarely activated, or that incommensurability is partial and context-dependent? Identify which adjustments feel like honest refinement and which feel like special pleading designed to protect the view.

3. Concessions and dismissals from inside the view (about 200 words): Which of the points above would a committed defender of my position accept as real pressure that demands revision? Which would they reject, and on what internal grounds—say, by distinguishing "grammar" from "agreement" or "translation" from "identity"? Answer partly as if you hold the view, not solely as an outside critic.

4. Insulation and unfalsifiability check (about 150 words): Has my position been qualified so that no observation could reach it—e.g., claiming the grammar is always "hidden," or that apparent incommensurability is always superficial? If so, assess whether that insulation is intellectually legitimate for a claim about human moral capacities, or whether it amounts to a defensive retreat that makes the view empty.

5. Bottom-line ranking and verdict (about 150 words): Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. State clearly whether the view is well-exposed or over-insulated. End with one sentence judging whether any insulation present is honest or defensive, and one final sentence giving your own assessment of whether the WEIRD data actually supports the grammar claim as strongly as I believe it does.
