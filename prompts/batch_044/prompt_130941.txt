I have recently been appointed to a state-level sentencing review panel, and I need a precise conceptual map—not a textbook summary—to understand exactly where disagreements among practitioners fall. Please build a taxonomy of positions on criminal punishment structured as a decision tree whose branch nodes are four concrete diagnostic claims, each phrased so a respondent can answer without knowing philosophical labels. The claims must address: (A) whether the moral wrongness of an act alone, independent of all future effects, provides sufficient reason to inflict suffering; (B) whether punishment remains permissible or required even if it produces zero or negative net social outcomes; (C) whether the state's authority to punish derives from an implicit consent or civic participation by the offender, rather than from a direct moral entitlement to respond to wrongdoing; and (D) whether the appropriate severity is set by the intrinsic gravity of the offense, by the scale of social benefit or harm affected, or by a victim-centered measure of harm done.

Locate named positions at the leaves—pure retributivism, pure consequentialism, limiting retributivism with desert as a ceiling or floor, a contractualist hybrid, a restorative-justice denial of punitive state authority, and at least one rarely surveyed view, such as a position that accepts (A) and (C) but rejects both standard proportionality metrics in (D) in favor of victim-restoration benchmarks. For every logical combination of answers that has no named occupant, mark it unnamed and diagnose in one sentence whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining why they overlap. The instrument should be usable by panel members with no philosophy background, should contain no bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, should rely only on concepts a well-informed generalist could hold without external lookup, and should be written in American English.
