Question: Conciliationists hold that when two epistemic peers disagree about p, each should reduce confidence in p. Steadfastists reply that you can rationaly maintain your view if your first-order reasoning is intact. Now consider a twist: you and your peer discover you assign wildly different weights to second-order evidence about your own cognitive reliability — you treat your track record as highly diagnostic, she treats hers as barely indicative — yet you still share the same first-order evidence about p and agree you're peers. Does conciliationism still require you to split the difference on p? And does steadfastness survive, or does this second-order divergence break the peer relation?

Student answer:
"Yes, conciliationism still applies because peerhood is defined by shared first-order evidence and equal ability, so the second-order divergence is just another disagreement. By conciliationist lights you should apply the principle twice: first split the difference on reliability-weights, then split on p using the averaged weights. That pulls you closer to her credence on p. As for steadfastness, the view lets you privilege your own first-order reasoning when peer conditions hold, but here that move is blocked — since you disagree about reliability, you're not really peers anymore, so the steadfastist conditions aren't met. Therefore conciliationism handles the case cleanly, while steadfastness collapses because its peer-relation precondition fails."
