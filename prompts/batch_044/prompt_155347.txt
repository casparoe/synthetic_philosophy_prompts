As a retired biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science, I am trying to understand whether Thomas Malthus’s Essay on the Principle of Population can be regarded as “right but early” rather than simply falsified. I am struck by the modern demographic transition, the green revolution, and—more specifically—by recent ecological economics that applies Malthusian logic not to calories per acre but to energy return on investment (EROI) and to “planetary boundary” overshoot in industrial metabolism. If Malthus were alive today and familiar with these developments, how do you think he would assess his own theory? Would he treat the demographic transition and agricultural technology gains as direct falsifications of his food-population mechanism, or would he argue that the underlying pressure of population against a finite substrate was merely displaced onto energy and biogeochemical cycles, making the core dynamics correct but the limiting factor and temporal scale wrong?

In connection with this, I want to ask about the philosophy of long-run prediction. Some philosophers of science hold that long-range forecasts are protected from falsification by an inevitable stock of auxiliary hypotheses about technology and institutions, while others distinguish legitimate theoretical refinement from ad hoc immunization. If Malthus were to rescue his view by shifting the binding constraint from food supply to EROI and planetary boundaries, should that be read as a progressive problem-shift in the Lakatosian sense, or as a degenerating move that renders the Malthusian research program unfalsifiable? In your response, please separate what is broadly settled in the philosophy of science literature on falsification and theoretical rescue from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any particular interpretation of Malthus’s defendability as if it were the consensus view.
