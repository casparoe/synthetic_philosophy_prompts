I have been working through what Quine's naturalism and holism imply about moral values, and I have constructed an argument that seems to show his view is self-defeating. I want to know which step fails.

(1) Quine holds that ontology is settled by the quantificational commitments of our best scientific theories; entities that play no explanatory role in science should be expelled from our ontology.
(2) Moral properties—goodness, wrongness, obligation—do not figure in the explanatory apparatus of physics, biology, psychology, or economics.
(3) So by Quine's own criterion, moral properties do not exist, and moral statements are not literally true.
(4) Quine also describes belief as arranged in a web, with logical, mathematical, and physical beliefs near the center and more distant beliefs near the periphery.
(5) Moral beliefs lie near the periphery: they are among the beliefs we are most willing to revise when they conflict with central scientific findings.
(6) If a class of beliefs is both non-truth-apt and maximally peripheral, then they carry no rational weight; they are on a par with beliefs in witchcraft or astrology, which Quine rejects.
(7) Yet Quine treats certain normative judgments—such as that simpler theories are preferable, or that scientific inquiry is worth pursuing—as rationally binding and not merely conventional.
(8) If these normative judgments are rational, then either some normative beliefs are central and true after all, or Quine must concede that his own methodological commitments are as groundless as witchcraft.
(9) Therefore Quine's view proves too much: it either undermines itself or forces an admission of objective normative facts that his ontology denies.

Which premise should I reject? I would prefer to keep (1), (2), (4), and (5), since they seem like straightforward applications of Quine's well-known positions. If I have to choose, I am most reluctant to give up (7). What does giving up each of the other steps cost?
