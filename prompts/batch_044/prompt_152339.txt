I work in the planning office of a mid-sized county government, and I do not have an academic background in science or philosophy. Over the past year I have been reading on my own about why scientific models can include assumptions that are obviously false—such as treating every neighborhood as having identical soil permeability, or treating all households as single income-maximizing units—and still be used to guide real decisions. I encountered Milton Friedman’s argument that the realism of a model’s underlying assumptions does not matter as long as its predictions are accurate, and I want to turn that principle into a practical, reusable decision procedure that my small team can apply whenever a consultant submits a model-based recommendation for our land-use or infrastructure rules.

Please write a formal procedure, presented as an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes, that a non-specialist reviewer can complete in about thirty minutes. For each step, state the precise question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether the step results in a continue, a stop (reject or defer the recommendation), or a mandatory hand-off to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must include a way to classify unrealistic assumptions into two kinds: those that are instrumentally false—simplifying internal mechanics without misrepresenting the real-world entities the policy targets—and those that are representationally false in a way that could misdirect the recommendation, even when aggregate predictions fall within acceptable error margins. Provide a small set of test conditions—no more than three brief scenarios or diagnostic sentences—that a reviewer can use to make this classification.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three proposal types we actually receive:
1. A stormwater management model that assumes uniform drainage rates across all parcels; historical back-testing predicts county-wide flooding well, but local engineers report it consistently misses flash-flood risk in two specific neighborhoods.
2. A regional housing-demand forecast that treats every household as a single, rational income-maximizer; it predicts aggregate rent trends accurately, but it erases multigenerational families and informal sublets that are common here.
3. A long-range heat-mitigation plan built on a climate model that assumes no major loss of tree canopy over the next twenty years; the aggregate temperature projections are within acceptable margins, and I personally want to approve this plan because it strengthens a grant application we need.

In the third case, the procedure should deliver a clear recommendation that I will not like—most likely a stop or a mandatory value-based hand-off—despite the favorable predictions and my preference. After the applications, identify two specific limitations that remain even when the checklist is followed perfectly: things the procedure cannot settle and that require outside judgment or expertise.

Please observe the following constraints in your answer: write in American English; keep the tone formal and polished; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; and design the procedure for a team with no PhD-level modelers, a five-business-day sign-off deadline, and no authority to demand new data collection from consultants.
