I help coordinate a small volunteer-run repair cooperative, where every returned tool must pass through a fixed laminated sequence: visual inspection, cleaning if required, entry in the paper logbook, and replacement on the designated shelf. On Tuesday, the only pen available for the logbook had run dry. Instead of using a pencil, leaving a verbal note, or skipping that step, I found myself inspecting the same spanner twice, wiping it again, returning it to the shelf, and then immediately retrieving it to begin the sequence afresh. I must have completed this loop four times before a colleague interrupted me. I understand that I had become locked in a rigid behavioural chain, yet I also feel that arbitrarily inserting an exception might undermine the reliability that makes the protocol worth following. Is my inability to deviate a failure of practical reasoning, or does strict adherence to an intact procedure — even when one link is broken — demonstrate a kind of integrity that flexible improvisation would sacrifice? And if I want to guard against this in future, ought I to train myself to respond more fluidly to minor contextual disruptions, or should I accept that a certain mechanical consistency is simply the price of a system that others can trust?
