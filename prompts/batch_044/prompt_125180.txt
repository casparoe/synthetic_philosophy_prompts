My friend and I just spent an hour arguing about our university’s research-ethics board. The board requires extensive justification for experiments on octopuses—citing distributed neural architecture and behavioral flexibility—but imposes no review whatsoever on adversarial training runs that induce sustained distress-like outputs in large models, even when the computation involves far more processing steps than an octopus nervous system. My friend says this is mere speciesism. I think the practice presupposes a specific view about which computations matter morally: not functional complexity or information throughput, but something like biological embodiment, evolutionary history, or a particular kind of integrated neural substrate. Does the practice strictly require that metaphysical view, or could it be defended on purely epistemic grounds—we know octopuses suffer but remain uncertain about models? Is it consistent with the board’s simultaneous insistence that behavioral similarity alone never establishes moral status? Reconstruct the implicit theory of computational moral relevance, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that rival theories could license the same rules, and tell me exactly what in the evaluation procedure would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with one that treated functional architecture alone as sufficient for moral consideration.
