In a recent discussion with colleagues about the logic of negative screening results, I found myself trying to explain why observing one more healthy person does not seem to strengthen a universal claim about disease absence in the same way that finding a sick patient confirms presence — and I realised the structure felt uncomfortably close to a philosophical puzzle I had heard mentioned, Hempel's raven paradox. I am a clinical epidemiologist with a practical grasp of diagnostic reasoning — sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, verification bias, and screening logic — but almost no formal philosophy. I want you to restate the paradox, together with the principal philosophical responses to it — the equivalence-class argument derived from Nicod's criterion, the Bayesian resolution appealing to prior probabilities and evidential asymmetry, and Goodman's concerns about projectability — entirely within the vocabulary and conceptual architecture of diagnostic testing and surveillance protocols. Use only concepts I actually work with: false positives and false negatives, pre-test probability, the evidentiary asymmetry between confirming presence and confirming absence, and the practical difficulty of validating a universal negative claim through sampling.

Then provide the residue report: which philosophical moves translate cleanly into epidemiological reasoning, which must be distorted to fit — for example, does the logical symmetry between the claim that all ravens are black and its contrapositive survive translation into the operational asymmetry between confirming disease presence and confirming disease absence in screening studies? — and which elements of the debate have no counterpart whatsoever in how we design verification protocols. I am particularly interested in whether the residue reveals that epidemiology lacks a conceptual distinction that philosophy genuinely requires, or whether the paradox depends on an idealised notion of 'evidence' that collapses once verification bias and base-rate differences in real-world sampling are taken seriously.
