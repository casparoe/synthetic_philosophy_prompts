For a long-form feature I am preparing on quantitative macrohistory, I have tried to formalize my own stance and now worry it is less coherent than it first appeared. Before filing, I want a consistency audit of the following commitments.

First, I accept the structural-demographic account of secular cycles—agrarian pressure, elite overproduction, and fiscal stress as the principal drivers of integrative and disintegrative phases—while insisting that the dynamics are fundamentally non-linear and threshold-dependent, not capturable by linear forecasting models.

Second, I hold that macrohistorical science should restrict itself to structural probabilities projected over multi-decade horizons, and should never issue point predictions about specific revolutions, regime collapses, or civil conflicts.

Third, I think evolutionary psychology supplies the necessary micro-foundations: status-competition mechanisms explain why elite overproduction tends to resolve through violence or purges rather than through peaceful redistribution.

Fourth, I maintain that narrative historiography remains indispensable, because structural models necessarily erase the contingent decisions of individual actors that determine which branch of a bifurcation a society actually takes.

Fifth, I reject strict historical determinism; I believe that if a polity recognizes its entry into a disintegrative phase, targeted institutional reform can alter the trajectory and avert breakdown.

Sixth, I take sound journalistic practice to require causal particularism—explaining why this specific reform failed or that particular war erupted—rather than relying solely on probabilistic structural description.

Do these form a defensible package? Specifically, does the evolutionary-psychology commitment in (3) undercut the contingency I defend in (4) and the reformist optimism in (5)? Does the non-linearity affirmed in (1) sit in hidden tension with the determinism I reject in (5), or is that only an apparent conflict? Is there a contradiction between (2)'s predictive modesty and (6)'s demand for particular causal explanation? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which pair is in conflict only on superficial inspection, specify the least costly revision if one commitment must go, and tell me whether this profile holds up as a coherent journalistic stance on whether history can be quantified and predicted.
