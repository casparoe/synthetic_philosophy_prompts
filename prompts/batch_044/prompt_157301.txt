I'm drafting an essay on logical omniscience that attempts to relocate the standard problem from individual epistemic agents to genuinely distributed belief systems. The core idea is that awareness structures and impossible-worlds models solve omniscience for a single mind by restricting accessibility relations, but they leave untouched the case where belief is spread across a collective—say, an institutional tribunal in which different members hold disjoint premises drawn from chemistry, economics, and administrative law, no individual grasps all entailments, yet the body is normatively required to apply the full logical consequences of its statutory framework. I want to ask whether such a collective should be modeled as one epistemic agent that is logically omniscient at the system level, or whether the division of labor simply coordinates several omniscience-free individuals without generating a collective knower. I'd particularly welcome comments on three points: whether this genuinely relocates the problem or merely replicates it at a higher level; whether the argument smuggles in an implausibly strong commitment to collective intentionality; and whether institutional division of labor is better analyzed as distributed computation rather than as distributed belief. Does the injection of collective-cognition examples change anything substantive about the original epistemic-logic debate, or does it just confuse the boundary conditions? And if the distributed-system framing holds, what should we say about cases where the collective reaches contradictory conclusions that no member could derive alone?
