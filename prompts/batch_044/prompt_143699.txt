I'm working through debates about the identity of indiscernibles and keep encountering a particular argumentative configuration that feels like a distinct pressure point, though I don't know whether the literature has carved it out separately. Start with the familiar scenario: two spheres of identical size, temperature, and internal structure floating in an otherwise empty universe, separated by some distance. The standard move is to say they are numerically distinct despite being qualitatively identical, and then to ask whether this violates the principle. But the variant I keep constructing is dynamic rather than static. Imagine the two spheres orbit a shared center of mass in perfect circular paths, with identical mass, velocity, and radial distance, in a universe that contains nothing else—no stars, no background grid, no asymmetrical forces. At every instant, the qualitative description of the system is perfectly symmetric: whatever relational property one sphere has to the other, the other has in return. Now add continuous motion: over time, each sphere traces out the same circular path, and because there is no external frame, there is no qualitative difference between "sphere A moved to where sphere B was" and "they exchanged positions." If you try to distinguish them diachronically by saying "this is the one that was on the left a moment ago," the reply is immediate—"on the left" is just a relational position that both have occupied, and since the orbit is uniform, there is no qualitative mark that anchors one sphere to one segment of the trajectory.

The result seems to be that you face not just Black's original synchronic puzzle—are there two objects right now?—but a tracking problem: without any qualitative asymmetry, you cannot follow one sphere through time without smuggling in a non-qualitative thread, a kind of bare particularity that persists through the exchange. The options appear to collapse into either saying there is genuinely only one object traversing the loop twice (a kind of metaphysical solipsism for objects), or accepting that numerical identity over time requires something like primitive thisness or haecceity that outstrips every qualitative and relational fact. I have seen analogues of this in quantum mechanics under the name of the permutation argument, but applied classically to macroscopic spheres in symmetric motion it seems to generate a different kind of pressure—one about diachronic individuation rather than state-description symmetry. So my question is whether this specific configuration—symmetrical dynamic exchange with no qualitative anchor for tracking—has an established name or literature of its own, and how precisely it relates to haecceitism, primitive identity, and Black's static case. I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know whether this is genuinely distinct from the original two-spheres example, what its nearest neighboring concepts are and where they diverge, and whether there is a standard reply that dissolves the tracking worry without simply restating the commitment to bare particularity. If there is no established term, I would also like a proposed carving with boundary cases—when does symmetric motion become indistinguishable from single-object looping, and when does it still allow for qualitative tracking?
