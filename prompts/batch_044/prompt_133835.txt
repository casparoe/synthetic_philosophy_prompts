Since retiring from experimental physics, I have been studying the philosophy of international law, particularly the conceptual boundaries of genocide as a legal and moral category and the politics by which atrocities are named or excluded from that name. In a workshop led by Dr. Vasquez, we have examined four historical classification cases and her consistent verdicts, which I record here.

Case 1. The Montagnard Child-Transfer Program: a state systematically removes the children of an upland ethnic minority, prohibits their language in all institutions, and destroys religious sites; mortality remains near baseline, but internal policy papers state the explicit aim is to "terminate the collective existence of this people as a distinct social form." Verdict: genocide.

Case 2. The Northern Frontier Campaign: a state military operation against a semi-nomadic pastoral group in an arid region; official orders refer only to "securing water corridors" and "pacification"; direct violence and induced famine reduce the population by approximately sixty-five percent, survivors are dispersed into agricultural settlements, and no official document names the ethnic group as a target of destruction. Verdict: not genocide (classified instead as crimes against humanity and war crimes).

Case 3. The Balkan Enclave Siege: a prolonged siege of an ethnic enclave accompanied by mass executions of roughly one-quarter of the group, systematic sexual violence, destruction of cultural monuments, and propaganda declaring that "this people must vanish from the earth." Verdict: genocide.

Case 4. The Agrarian Class Purge: a state campaign targets a category defined solely by land-ownership and economic function, not by ethnicity, religion, or nationality; explicit rhetoric calls for "the elimination of this class"; mass executions and engineered starvation kill approximately two million; survivors are not targeted as a collective identity. Verdict: not genocide.

These four verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one plausible rule. One candidate is the formal enumerated-acts framework, adopted in the immediate post-war legal settlement, which requires a protected group, specific intent, and one of the listed physical or biological destructive acts. Another is the broader structural conception, closer to the original idea advanced by Raphael Lemkin, under which genocide can include coordinated state policies aimed at the destruction of a group's distinctive cultural, social, or economic existence, regardless of whether the perpetrators employ euphemistic framing or whether mass mortality is the primary instrument.

The new case is the Coastal Relocation and Integration Directive: a state facing rising sea levels orders the permanent relocation of an indigenous coastal community to urban centers; the program mandates linguistic integration, prohibits traditional maritime practices, and dissolves communal land tenure; leaked strategic memos describe the goal as "ending the separate social existence of this people" and "merging them irreversibly into the national body"; there is no physical violence, mortality rates do not exceed baseline, but the community's distinctive cultural and economic life is abolished within one generation.

Please predict how Dr. Vasquez would classify this case if she wishes to remain consistent with her four earlier rulings. First, identify the other plausible rules—beyond the two I have named—that fit her track record equally well, and say what each predicts here. Second, identify the closest analog among Cases 1 through 4, give your best guess at her actual verdict with the reasoning that favors it, and state honestly what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Third, propose one further hypothetical case, specifying its key institutional and demographic features, that would force a clear decision between the formal enumerated-acts rule and the broader structural-destruction rule.
