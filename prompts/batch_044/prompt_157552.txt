Two people in my philosophy reading group got stuck whilst discussing whether Dennett's intentional stance applies to large language models. Let's call them Priya and Tom. Priya said we should attribute beliefs to LLMs whenever doing so improves our predictions — that's all the stance ever was, a pragmatic heuristic with no metaphysical baggage. Tom said that misses the point, because the stance only applies where there's a real pattern of rational agency, and LLMs fail that since thier supposed beliefs disappear if you change the prompt framing; there's no counterfactual robustness. Priya replied that human beliefs are fragile too — hypnosis or brain damage can shift them wildly — so fragility can't be the line. Tom said human fragility is bounded by biological continuity: there's still one subject revising the belief, whereas with an LLM there's no diachronic substrate holding anything across contexts; each generation is just a fresh computation. Priya came back that he's smuggling biological chauvinism into the stance, and that if a model has persistent memory or fine-tuned weights encoding stable dispositions then the belief does persist at system level, even if token outputs vary.

Can you adjudicate move by move? Rate each of thier four points, say where each is strongest and weakest, note what they're both ignoring about diachronic identity, and tell me if either actually addresses whether the intentional stance requires more than predictive success.
