I'm preparing a discussion for an interdisciplinary seminar that pairs Edwin Abbott's Flatland with philosophy of mathematics, and I'm trying to avoid the usual reading in which geometry merely symbolizes social hierarchy. I'd like you to read Flatland specifically through the tension between mathematical platonism and nominalism, but with a twist: treat Abbott's dimensional ontology as a test case for what philosophers call the access problem—how finite, embodied cognizers can come to know structures that lie outside their native conceptual framework—and ask whether the Sphere's revelation to A Square functions as a platonist epiphany (direct intellectual access to a pre-existing reality) or as a nominalist or structuralist construction (a useful model that extends Flatland's descriptive language without ontological commitment to an independent realm).

Please examine the key scenes—the Sphere's intrusion into Flatland, A Square's analogical reasoning about the third dimension, and the Council's suppression of his testimony—not merely as social satire, but as arguments about mathematical existence and epistemic authority. Where does Abbott seem to treat Spaceland as mind-independent? Where does he rely on analogy, imagination, or linguistic extension, which might favor a nominalist reading? I'm particularly interested in whether the novel's treatment of "higher dimensions" parallels the indispensability argument: do the Flatlanders need to posit three-dimensional objects to explain anomalies in their two-dimensional measurements, or is the third dimension an optional interpretive overlay? Also consider whether Abbott inadvertently raises a fictionalist option—that dimensions are useful fictions organizing experience without ontological commitment—and whether the persecution of A Square mirrors the sociology of mathematical belief rather than the metaphysics of number.

I want the reading to be honest in both directions: if the novel collapses the philosophical distinction by treating revelation as both mystical and analogical, say so. Don't reduce the story to an illustration of a theory; instead, ask what the narrative's form—its constrained first-person perspective, its reliance on analogical reasoning, its political suppression of heterodox mathematics—reveals about the conditions under which mathematical objects can be said to exist. What does Flatland contribute to the platonism versus nominalism debate that a standard philosophical argument, without Abbott's fictional apparatus, tends to miss? I'm writing an essay that takes both Abbott's narrative logic and the metaphysics of mathematics seriously, so I'd like a reading that ends by saying what the work adds to the philosophical discussion rather than merely applying philosophy to the work.
