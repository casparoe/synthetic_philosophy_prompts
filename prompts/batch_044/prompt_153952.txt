I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but after years of maintaining legacy codebases I have become preoccupied with the problem of persistence: when every underlying component of a system has been swapped out, what, if anything, remains the same? I have been discussing this with a colleague — a museum conservator with firm, consistently applied views on restoration — and I should like to understand the logic that governs her judgments.

Her verdicts on earlier cases are as follows.

First, a wooden sailing vessel built in 1850 in which every plank, beam, and nail has been replaced across successive restorations, though each substitution followed the original design specifications precisely and the vessel remains seaworthy. She judges that this is not the original ship; what endures is the design plan, not the physical object itself.

Second, a personal computer from the 1980s whose motherboard, processor, memory, and storage have all been replaced with functionally equivalent, period-correct parts, but whose original steel chassis and factory serial plate have never been removed. She judges that this is the original computer, on the grounds that a continuous material anchor together with functional equivalence preserves its identity.

Third, an oil painting whose original canvas and ground layer were destroyed in a fire; it was reconstructed on an identical stretcher using the artist’s own preparatory sketches and notes, then retouched by his direct apprentices using pigments he had specified. She judges that this is the original painting, because an unbroken chain of custodianship and realised authorial intention matter more than the survival of the original substrate.

Fourth, a human being in whom every cell except a small population of long-lived cortical neurons has turned over naturally, with no interruption to memory, personality, or social recognition. She judges that this is the same person, because psychological continuity and social relations bridge the material gap.

Now consider a new case. A large language model has undergone successive training runs in which every parameter — every weight and bias — has been replaced, yet the neural architecture, the loss function, the complete conversational history stored in an unbroken chain of logs, and the public API endpoint have all been preserved without interruption. The model’s outputs are functionally indistinguishable from its earlier version, and users continue to interact with it under the same service name and agreement. Nevertheless, no single numerical value from the original instantiation survives, and the physical servers hosting it have also been entirely substituted.

If she wishes to remain consistent with her earlier rulings, what should she say about whether this is the “same” model? I am especially interested in which of her implicit rules — material continuity of a core substrate, functional and design continuity sustained by custodial oversight, authorial or custodial intention, or psychological and social continuity — best predicts her answer. Should her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, I should like to know what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice, and which further case — for example, one in which the architecture is altered but the conversational history is ported over unchanged, or one in which the weights are frozen but the hosting infrastructure and entire user base are replaced — would best distinguish these competing rules.

Please answer in British English, using British spelling and conventions throughout, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am seeking conceptual analysis rather than references to specific texts.
