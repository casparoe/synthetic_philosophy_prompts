I serve as chair of the National Commission on Post-Conflict Cultural Heritage, which oversees preservation and public access to testimonial archives from the 1970s river-basin displacement. We are reviewing a proposal—drafted by the Archive Ethics Subcommittee—to implement a structured erasure protocol. Under this protocol, families of those who gave testimony may request, seventy-five years after the initial recording, that the testimony be withdrawn from all public-facing interfaces and mnemonically deactivated: not destroyed, but sealed in a non-searchable vault with access restricted to certified historians, while only a generic, anonymized statistical entry remains in the public catalog. Proponents argue this honors a duty of care to descendants who suffer ongoing harm from perpetual exposure; opponents argue that institutional forgetting, even partial and voluntary, betrays the moral obligation to preserve collective memory of systemic injustice and undermines the archive’s authority.

The Commission is divided along lines that roughly map onto familiar positions in the ethics of memory. Some members advance a Margalit-style claim: archives serve not merely the individual witness but the moral community that must retain knowledge of shared wrongs, and allowing opt-in deactivation risks treating collective harm as privately negotiable. Others advance a Rieff-style counter: perpetual visibility can re-traumatize across generations, and democratic societies sometimes need interpretive closure—not denial, but a structured end to the public life of a particular narrative—to move from grievance to coexistence. We need a formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—for the full Commission. The memo must take a clear position on whether the protocol should be adopted, modified, or rejected. It should address the strongest argument on the other side, specifically the concern that allowing institutional forgetting, even if partial and time-delayed, creates a precedent that memory is contingent and that the seventy-five-year window is either too short to serve remembrance or too long to serve release. It should also treat whether there is a meaningful moral distinction between deactivation (sealing with anonymized residue) and outright destruction, and whether descendants should hold veto power over narratives that implicate collective, not merely personal, harm. Write the memo in a formal register appropriate for institutional action, with reasoning the Subcommittee could actually respond to, and do not include requests for further literature, bibliographies, or citations.
