I am finalizing the assessment for a graduate seminar in political economy and philosophy of history that asks whether historical evidence can ever license causal claims about why some nations become wealthy while others do not. The course has engaged the standard explanatory triad — institutionalist accounts, geographic determinism, and cultural explanations — but I want the take-home essay to avoid any answer that merely summarizes these camps or lists their proponents. Instead, please design a single essay question, together with a model answer of approximately 900 words and a detailed marking scheme, that forces students to wrestle with the epistemic limits of causal inference from non-experimental history.

The question itself should embed a constructed comparative micro-case: imagine two adjacent territories, Alpha and Beta, divided only by a river boundary fixed in 1890 by an external colonial administration. Prior to that line, there was no meaningful economic, linguistic, or ethnic separation; both territories share identical tropical climate, malaria ecology, soil suitability for the same primary crops, and equal access to a shared coastal trade corridor. After the boundary was imposed, Alpha received an institutional framework of registered individual land titles, local contract-enforcement courts, and representative local taxation, while Beta received an extractive framework of communal labor quotas, coerced export-crop production, and absentee bureaucratic administration. By 1970, Alpha’s per-capita income was roughly three times Beta’s. Ask students whether this divergence can be causally attributed to institutions, and require them to address five specific analytical obstacles: first, endogenous institutional selection — why the colonial power assigned different regimes and whether that selection correlates with unobserved pre-existing conditions; second, hidden pre-colonial heterogeneity in social capital, literacy, or human capital that might have influenced both regime assignment and subsequent development; third, post-independence selective migration, capital flight, and cross-river labor mobility that contaminate any comparison of national outcomes; fourth, whether synthetic-control or difference-in-differences techniques can salvage identification when treatment assignment is strategic rather than random and the units are geographically and economically interdependent; and fifth, whether the failure of full causal identification forces complete agnosticism, or whether some combination of mechanistic reasoning and abductive inference can still support institutional explanations without claiming that institutions alone caused the divergence.

The model answer should treat the Alpha/Beta scenario not as a solved natural experiment but as a stress-test for identification strategies, clearly distinguishing the claim that institutions caused the gap from the more modest claim that institutions belong to the best available explanation once geographic and cultural variables are held constant and selection biases are acknowledged. The marking scheme should allocate 25 points across five must-hit moves — each worth 5 points — with a one-line note on the typical failure mode for each: for example, treating the river boundary as an exogenous random assignment without discussing selection into treatment; conflating correlation with causation by citing income divergence alone; ignoring post-boundary migration and interaction effects; applying econometric jargon without explaining why independence and random-assignment assumptions fail here; and concluding either with unqualified institutional determinism or with total skepticism that refuses any inferential purchase. Please phrase every node of the evaluation in terms of analytical moves students must make rather than in terms of memorized author names, and ensure the prompt can be answered thoroughly without access to any external source or database.
