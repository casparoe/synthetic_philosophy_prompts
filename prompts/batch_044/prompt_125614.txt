A philosophy blog I follow recently summarized Susan Wolf's well-known essay on moral saints in the following terms: "Wolf's core claim is that being a moral saint—defined as a person who maximizes moral value in every choice—is actually undesirable as a personal ideal. She argues that such individuals must systematically suppress all non-moral interests, including art, humor, personal relationships, and intellectual curiosity, leaving them one-dimensional and dull. Because morality demands this total suppression of the self, Wolf concludes that we should reject the notion that anyone ought to strive to be as morally good as possible. She even suggests that if moral sainthood became widespread, cultural and scientific progress would stall, since saints would have no time for pursuits that don't directly alleviate suffering. Therefore, her essay is ultimately a defense of the claim that moral perfection is not only unattainable but actively harmful to a flourishing human life."

I find parts of this convincing, but I worry it conflates different arguments Wolf makes and perhaps imports claims she never defends. Could you evaluate the fairness of this summary? In particular, please distinguish between Wolf's discussion of the "loving saint" and the "rationalist saint," and clarify whether she actually says saints must suppress all personal projects or rather that they organize their lives around moral ends in ways that crowd out other goods. Does she present moral sainthood as harmful, or as lacking in certain attractive qualities? And is there any basis in her text for the claim about societal collapse, or is that an extrapolation? Finally, I have been wondering about a related but distinct idea: that there may exist a "threshold" of moral development past which further gains in moral sensitivity or motivation become self-defeating, eroding the practical identity and emotional resources required for sustained moral agency. Does Wolf's framework accommodate such a non-linear conception of moral improvement, or does she treat the path to sainthood as essentially linear until it reaches an undesirable endpoint?
