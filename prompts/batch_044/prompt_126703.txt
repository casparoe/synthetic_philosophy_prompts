I've been digging into Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis for a seminar and I'm hitting this wall that feels like it's been circling since the 1950s. One camp says Calvinist aseticism and this-worldly discipline actually caused capitalism to take root in Northern Europe - not just correlated with it, but genuinely explanatory, like you can't understand the economic shift without understanding the value shift. The other camp says values are epiphenominal, that culture never causes anything on its own, and that if you look at material conditions, trade networks, and instutional shifts you can explain the whole pattern without ever mentioning belief. Both sides keep pointing to the same historical evidence and neither budges an inch.

My hunch is that they're talking past each other about what "cause" even means in social explanation. The Weberians might be using it in a reason-giving sense - people worked differently because they interpreted their labor differently, so explaining the behavior means showing what made it meaningful or rational to them. The critics are using it in a mechanistic sense, where a cause has to be something that pushes people around regardless of what they think. So maybe this isn't a fight about history at all but a verbal disagreement dressed up as empirical dispute? Or maybe there's a hidden substantive question about whether mental states can be causally efficacious in macro-history? Or are they actually answering different questions - one about institutional origins, one about individual motivation - and just calling both answers "Weber's thesis"? I want a real diagnosis here, not a survey. Tell me what kind of stall this is, whether my mechanistic-vs-reason-giving framing captures it, and honestly - what would it take to make progress? A sharper conceptual distinction, a different kind of case study, or is the question just badly posed?
