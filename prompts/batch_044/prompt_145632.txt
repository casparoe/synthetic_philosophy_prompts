At a recent student symposium on environmental history, two classmates—Leo and Nadia—disagreed about whether the Anthropocene should be recognized as a formal geological epoch and, if so, when it began and who has the authority to decide. Their exchange went as follows.

Leo argued that the Anthropocene began around 1610, with the ecological rupture of the Columbian Exchange—what some call the Orbis spike—when global biotic homogenization, species extinctions, and carbon-cycle shifts first reached planetary scale. He said dating the epoch to the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration privileges the technologies of the Global North and erases centuries of colonial ecological transformation.

Nadia replied that a geological epoch requires a measurable stratigraphic signal, such as the radionuclide and plastic markers of the post-1950 Great Acceleration, and that without a designated Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point the term remains historical rhetoric rather than geology. She warned that letting historians or activists declare epoch boundaries risks politicizing stratigraphy and dissolving the methodological precision that makes classification useful.

Leo countered that this grants geologists an unjustified stratigraphic sovereignty. He proposed temporal pluralism: different Earth-system processes—biodiversity collapse, nitrogen-cycle disruption, climate forcing—have distinct inflection points, so insisting on one date imposes artificial unity on heterogeneous phenomena. He asked whether the demand for a single GSSP reflects scientific rigor or disciplinary gatekeeping.

Nadia responded that pluralism collapses the concept into vagueness: if the Anthropocene can start in 1610, 1784, and 1950 at once, it explains nothing specific and becomes merely an empty moral label. She noted that the International Commission on Stratigraphy, not interdisciplinary committees, maintains epoch definitions precisely because shared methodological standards are lacking across fields.

Leo concluded by asking whether methodological purity is worth preserving if it requires treating colonial land-use transformation as geologically insignificant, and whether an epoch named for human influence should be defined exclusively by scientists who study rocks rather than societies.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about the relevance of the Orbis spike versus Great Acceleration markers; about whether temporal pluralism strengthens or weakens the concept; about whether geologists should hold exclusive authority; and about whether Leo's charge of erasure carries weight. Then say what both sides are missing—perhaps about how stratigraphic working groups already incorporate interdisciplinary input, or about whether part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive. Finally, please state what argument or evidence would change your verdict, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
