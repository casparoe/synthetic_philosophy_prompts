I am revising the syllabus for my graduate seminar in metaphysics and have become preoccupied with a point about Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles that I intend to present to the students next week. A colleague and I were discussing this over coffee, and she reminded me of Max Black's classic counterexample: two spheres in an otherwise empty, symmetric universe that share every intrinsic qualitative property but stand in a spatial relation to one another. The standard amendment is to include relational properties within the scope of the principle, yet this resolution seems to generate a further puzzle that I cannot resolve on my own.

If we consider one of these spheres—call it S—and imagine a counterfactual situation in which S exists alone in an otherwise empty universe, then S apparently lacks the relational property of being situated at a distance from a qualitatively identical partner. Applying the amended law without restriction, it would seem that S-in-isolation is not identical to S-in-the-pair, since the two instances differ with respect to that relational feature. But this suggests that an object's self-identity depends on the contingent existence of other objects, which strikes me as deeply implausible. Is the error in applying the principle across possible worlds, or in treating the absence of a relational partner as a genuine property-deficit? I want to understand whether there is a principled restriction—perhaps tied to the domain of quantification within a single world-state—that blocks this inference, or whether Black's argument actually commits us to a form of world-relative identity that I am failing to appreciate.
