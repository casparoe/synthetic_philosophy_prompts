I hold a Carnap-inspired view of democratic planning: that value disagreements in social planning ought to be treated not as factual disputes resolvable by evidence, but as practical choices between linguistic frameworks or decision calculi, and that democratic legitimacy derives from transparent institutions for revising these frameworks rather than from consensus on substantive ends. What would count against this position? I want the arguments, institutional counter-examples, and conceptual pressures that should genuinely move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. In particular: does the framework/substance distinction collapse when applied to political institutions; have planning bodies that operate on this model actually failed in ways that cannot be redescribed as framework revisions; is Carnap's tolerance, developed for formal languages, being illegitimately extended to normative politics; and does the view risk unfalsifiability by treating every failure as an occasion for recalibration rather than defeat? Which of these would defenders of the position concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss? And is insulation from counter-evidence a defect for a view that claims practical relevance? Please answer in British English.
