Write a short story set in a society around 2035 where automation and productivity growth have realized the vision Keynes outlined in his essay predicting that material abundance and drastically shortened working hours would become universal. The protagonist works in a public office that advises citizens on how to use their surplus leisure—addressing what Keynes identified as the central challenge that would replace economic scarcity: the problem of learning how to live well. Rather than depicting a harmonious culture of shared leisure, the narrative should introduce a philosophical complication drawn from the heterogeneity problem familiar from value theory: the citizens belong to distinct life-form communities whose conceptions of the good are not merely different preferences but conceptually incommensurable, lacking any common metric that would allow them to be ranked or translated into one another. Through three specific consultations—for instance, with a practitioner of deep ascetic withdrawal, with a participant in competitive status-games played entirely for symbolic prestige, and with someone pursuing an endlessly recursive aesthetic self-creation—show how the elimination of economic necessity removes the shared practical framework that once made disagreements about the good life manageable, turning them instead into existential fractures. The story should avoid synthesizing these perspectives or having the protagonist discover a hidden unity; end with the recognition that Keynes's framing presupposed there was a single, learnable practice of living well that a society could collectively cultivate, and that this presupposition may itself be the last vestige of scarcity-thinking.
