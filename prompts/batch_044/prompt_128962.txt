Following a prolonged debate with a friend, I am seeking an outside evaluation of a governance dilemma that has divided our cooperative. We recently adopted an automated arbitration protocol governed by a single, explicit clause in our charter: if a member's verified contribution score remains below 0.1 for thirty consecutive days, their access credentials are permanently revoked, with no provision for human appeal or contextual review. Last week, the system executed this clause against a member who had been hospitalized in a coma following an accident; her score collapsed not through negligence but through physical incapacity, yet the revocation was instantaneous and irreversible. My friend maintains that because the rule was enacted through democratic procedure and is applied without exception, the outcome is legally valid and socially necessary—he argues that permitting ad hoc moral exemptions would erode the predictability that makes collective coordination possible, and that law derives its authority from its source and form, not from its congruence with individual moral intuition. I contend, by contrast, that a normative system capable of producing such outcomes is defective precisely because it lacks an internal moral floor; I believe that procedural correctness cannot redeem a rule that systematically disregards basic conditions of fairness, regardless of how democratically it was adopted. Our disagreement seems to run deeper than this single case, touching on whether law and morality are conceptually separable. Could you assess where the stronger reasoning lies?
