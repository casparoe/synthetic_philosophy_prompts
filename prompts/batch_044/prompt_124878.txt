I am preparing teaching material for an advanced seminar on historical ontology and the philosophy of science, and I find myself returning to a comparison that I think is often treated too superficially in the literature. I should like you to compare the concept of path dependence as it operates in evolutionary biology—particularly the debate over evolutionary contingency and whether replaying the history of life would yield broadly similar morphological outcomes—with the concept of path dependence in technological development, especially the so-called lock-in effects that arise in standards, infrastructures, and network technologies. In particular, please examine whether the argument that technological history is locked in relies on the same kind of contingency that evolutionary biologists discuss, or whether technological path dependence operates through causally distinct mechanisms, such as network externalities, institutional inertia, and intentional design, rather than through the stochastic branching characteristic of natural selection.

To make the comparison precise, please distinguish between what we might call weak path dependence—where initial conditions influence the trajectory but the system nevertheless converges on functionally similar attractors over time—and strong path dependence—where small perturbations generate permanently divergent equilibria that cannot be reversed without catastrophic disruption. Consider whether evolutionary contingency is best understood as weak or strong in this sense, and whether technological lock-in, despite appearing irreversible, might actually be more reversible than biological evolution because designed systems can be deliberately re-engineered. I am not asking for a bibliography or list of primary texts; rather, I want a conceptual analysis that treats these domains with appropriate philosophical rigour, and I should like the response to be written in British English, maintaining a formal and polished register throughout.
