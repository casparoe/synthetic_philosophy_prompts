I have been working through ideas in pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism for my philosophy elective, and I constructed an argument that now seems to eliminate rational disagreement entirely. I want to know which link actually breaks.

(1) Pragmatic encroachment holds that whether a subject knows p depends partly on the practical stakes of being wrong about p. (2) If two people genuinely disagree about p, at least one is wrong. (3) For the party who is wrong, being wrong about p carries high practical stakes, since they are guiding action by a false belief. (4) By (1), those elevated stakes raise the evidential threshold that party must meet to know p. (5) But since they are already wrong, they presumably fail to meet even the original, lower threshold, so they certainly do not meet the higher one. (6) Now consider the party who is right: recognizing that an opponent is wrong puts them in a high-stakes situation of correction, which by (1) raises their own epistemic standard as well. (7) So in any substantive disagreement, both sides face inflated thresholds and likely fail to satisfy them. (8) Therefore pragmatic encroachment implies that in genuine disagreement, neither party knows p. (9) If neither knows, then rational disagreement—where one corrects the other on the basis of knowledge—is impossible. (10) But we plainly do correct each other rationally. (11) So the view proves too much: it undermines the very practice it was meant to illuminate.

I would most like to keep (1) and (10), but I cannot locate the slide. Is the mistake in (3), treating the practical cost of being wrong as automatically counting toward the subject's own stakes? Or in (6), assuming that the act of correcting someone raises the corrector's stakes in the relevant way? Or is there a scope shift between stakes for acting on p and stakes for attributing knowledge in a conversational context? Tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked plausible, and say what giving it up costs for someone who wants to hold onto pragmatic encroachment.
