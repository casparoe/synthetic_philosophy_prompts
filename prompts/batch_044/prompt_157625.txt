I serve on the governing board of a small nonprofit digital archive that preserves local business and civic records from the 1970s onward. Last year, a volunteer misfiled a single meeting-minutes folder, tagging it three days earlier than its actual date. A researcher subsequently used our collection to model how a particular zoning dispute propagated through neighborhood associations, and the misdated folder placed one coordination meeting before—rather than after—a pivotal council vote. On that basis, the researcher argued that the dispute was driven by bottom-up association rather than by top-down planning. The error has now been discovered. The board is divided: some members want to silently correct the metadata, holding that an archive should not preserve falsehoods; others want to leave the original tag intact and append a correction note, holding that altering the record reshapes the causal narratives that future scholars—and, increasingly, automated systems that construct narrative graphs from archival metadata—will derive. I am inclined to correct the file, but I find myself unsettled by a further thought: if the sequence of events is genuinely sensitive to such minute differences, then my choice to correct or preserve is itself a small cause with potentially large and unpredictable effects on historical explanation. Is there a philosophically coherent way to defend the correction, or does the very structure of historical contingency undermine the idea that we can responsibly choose between these two archival acts?
