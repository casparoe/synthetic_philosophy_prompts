I am a doctoral student in moral psychology with a secondary concentration in legal philosophy, and I am trying to decide how to allocate my dissertation research and the next several years of work within the Strawsonian framework of reactive attitudes and moral responsibility. Rather than asking for a survey or a reading list, I want a defended, dependency-aware agenda: among the following open problem clusters, which should take priority, in what order, and according to what criteria?

The clusters are these. First, the metaphysics of the participant stance: whether reactive attitudes are irreducibly second-personal in requiring a direct addressor-addressee relation, or whether they can be mediated through institutional roles—say, a sentencing judge or a parole board evaluating risk—without losing their distinctively moral character. If they are essentially dyadic and face-to-face, then institutional practices of blame may be conceptually misaligned with the reactive framework in ways that affect criminal justice reform.

Second, the compatibility problem restructured: not whether determinism threatens reactive attitudes globally, but whether specific reactive attitudes—indignation versus resentment versus guilt—have different vulnerability conditions under determinism, and whether some can be preserved as rational without the others. This seems tractable through conceptual analysis of the normative expectations embedded in each attitude.

Third, the normative status of the objective attitude itself. Strawson presents it as a suspension of reactive attitudes appropriate in certain contexts, but there is an open question whether it carries its own normative commitments—to treatment, management, or respect for autonomy—rather than being merely a withdrawal. If the objective attitude is normatively loaded, then the boundary between moral responsibility practices and social-control practices collapses, with direct implications for how we evaluate institutional risk management.

Fourth, the implications for moral education and institutional design: if reactive attitudes are partly constitutive of holding responsible, then training people to modulate them—through restorative justice practices, for instance—is not merely instrumental but shapes what moral community they inhabit. The open question is whether there are principled limits to such modulation, specifically whether we can cultivate reactive attitudes selectively without undermining the authenticity conditions that make them appropriate.

Given that I have roughly three years, a background in conceptual analysis and some familiarity with empirical moral psychology but not with neuroscience or formal modeling, how should I rank these? I want reasoning that is upstream-aware: which question, if settled, would reframe the others? Which are tractable with philosophical tools alone, which would require empirical collaboration I should seek or avoid, and what evidence over the next couple of years—conceptual, institutional, or empirical—would cause you to revise the ordering? Please defend the ranking with explicit criteria, whether importance for legal practice, conceptual tractability, neglectedness within current literature, or downstream dependency, and do not provide bibliographies or citations.
