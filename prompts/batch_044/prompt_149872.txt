Hey, I've been teaching moral conflict this term and my intuitions are all over the place — can you help me see if there's actually a pattern?

Case one: Sophie's Choice situation, forced to pick which child dies. I call that a genuine moral dilemma — two weighty obligations actually clash and neither is cancelled.

Case two: the classic trolley switch. I say that's NOT a genuine dilemma. It's horrific, sure, but there's a correct answer (pull the lever), so it's just a hard choice with one right path.

Case three: I'm a judge with an innocent friend and the law says I have to sentence her. I feel torn, but I want to say this isn't a *personal* moral dilemma — it's structural, the conflict comes from outside my own reasons. So no true dilemma, just a bad system.

Case four: I promised my colleague I'd reveiw her draft by Friday, and promised my kid I'd be at her recital Friday night, both equally binding, and I genuinely can't do both. Here I DO think there's a real dilemma — my own commitments conflict.

But wait — in case three and case four the conflict is "outside" in some sense (the law, the calender). So why do I treat them differently? Maybe I shouldn't. That's one tension.

Another: I think agent-regret after resolving a conflict is a good sign there was a real dilemma. But I also think that if you correctly identify the stronger obligation and act on it, you shouldn't feel regret — you did right. So I want regret to mark dilemmas, but not all resolved dilemmas produce it. That feels inconsistant.

Is there a principle — maybe about whether the obligations are personally authored, or whether the conflict is rationally resolvable — that saves all four? Or do I need to drop one? And what does your principle predict for an "overdetermination" case: two obligations both point to action A, but one obligation rests on a false belief I don't share, so only one genuinely applies? Is that a hidden dilemma?

Please also say what would change your mind — which consideration would make you abandon the principle, or where is the reasoning shakiest?
