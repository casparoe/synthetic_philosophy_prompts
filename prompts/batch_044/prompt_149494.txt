I work in mechanism design and am fluent in its formal vocabulary—strategy-proofness, individual rationality, budget balance, the revelation principle, social-choice functions, equilibrium selection, and matching algorithms—but I have little background in moral philosophy. I want you to restate the philosophical debate over repugnant markets—specifically Sandel’s thesis that putting prices on goods such as kidneys, emergency supplies during shortages, or votes corrupts their social meaning and crowds out intrinsic motivations, and Roth’s thesis that repugnance is best treated as a legitimate constraint on the feasible set of allocations—entirely within my vocabulary. Treat Sandel’s “corruption of meaning” as a potential failure mode of a mechanism, and his “crowding out” as an interaction between the allocation rule and agent preferences. Treat Roth’s repugnance constraint as a boundary condition on mechanism design. For each central claim, indicate whether it translates cleanly into an existing design concept (for example, preference misrepresentation, externalities in welfare functions, or endogenous preference formation), whether it can be expressed only by distorting its meaning (for example, reducing moral significance to a parameter in a utility function), or whether it has no counterpart at all in mechanism-design language. Then report the residue: is the untranslatable remainder a sign that mechanism design genuinely lacks the resources to represent normative corruption, or is it a sign that the philosophical claim was less determinate than it appeared? Be honest in the translation—do not smuggle moral assumptions into formal terms merely to make them fit. In particular, address whether capturing crowding out requires allowing preferences to shift as a direct function of the allocation rule, which violates the standard independence assumption, and whether vote-selling can be represented without collapsing democratic legitimacy into a transferable utility. Focus on price-gouging and vote-selling, since kidney markets are already well-discussed in design literature. Keep the translation faithful, and treat the residue as the most interesting part of the answer.
