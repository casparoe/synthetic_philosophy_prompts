I am taking a course on the philosophy of economics and have become particularly interested in Keynes’s normative vision of the good life—especially the framework developed in his 1930 essay on long-run economic possibilities, where he distinguishes absolute needs that must be satisfied from relative needs driven by social comparison, and imagines a future in which labor is drastically reduced so that people can devote themselves to culture, leisure, and moral improvement rather than to production. I would like you to apply that framework to a real historical episode: the 1945–1951 British Labour government’s implementation of the Beveridge Report’s recommendations, specifically the creation of the National Health Service, the National Insurance system, and the explicit macroeconomic commitment to full employment. Please be disciplined in the mapping. Identify which of these reforms address what Keynes called absolute needs, which risk institutionalizing relative needs or treating labor as a permanent moral identity rather than a temporary economic burden, and what Keynes’s view implies a government ought to do once the economic problem is genuinely solved—whether it should reduce working hours, decommodify leisure, expand collective consumption, or pursue some other institutional form of liberation from labor.

Then examine the analysis in both directions. First, say where Keynes’s theory illuminates the episode’s priorities and design choices—such as the universalist structure of the NHS as a direct removal of want, or the refusal to means-test benefits—and where it is silent, strained, or misdescribes them, particularly regarding the government’s refusal to experiment with shorter work weeks, unconditional basic income, or any serious reduction in the centrality of paid employment. Second, tell me what the episode reveals about Keynes’s own position: does the welfare state approximate his vision of the good life, contradict it by making full employment and productive citizenship central to human dignity, or expose that his distinction between absolute and relative needs was never politically actionable without an explicit theory of institutional transition? I want an answer that recognizes when the theory has nothing to say rather than forcing it to speak, and that treats the case as testing Keynes’s framework rather than merely illustrating it.
