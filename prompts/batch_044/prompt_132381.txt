There's this retirement fund my company offers with a strange design. When you sign up you state a risk tolerance, but the plan applies a "diachronic correction" that discounts your stated preference by 15% and treats that lower number as your authentic long-term choice. If you want to override it, you have to pay a 2% "stability fee" unless you've done financial counseling that certifies your change is "consistent over time." The marketing materials say this protects people from being money-pumped by thier own shifting moods. But here's the weird part: you can take a loan out against the same account instantly, with no counseling or fee, as long as you pay it back within five years. So they treat you as a single agent with one true preference when it comes to locking in risk, but as a fully unified borrower when it comes to liquidity.

What theory of diachronic rationality does that combination presuppose? Does it assume there's one "real" self whose preferences should be protected from temporary deviations, or does it just use the language of consistency to justify fee structures? And is the practice actually committed to avoiding money pumps, or is it inconsistant — treating you as fragmented in one domain and as a single rational agent in another? If the fund really believed that people are sequences of selves with no privileged long-term view, what would have to change about the loan policy?
