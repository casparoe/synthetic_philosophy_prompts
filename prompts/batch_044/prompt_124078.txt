In discussions of superrationality, proponents of Douglas Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma often rely on an analogy: the twenty isolated participants are like twenty identical executions of the same deterministic algorithm on air-gapped processors, each with access to a shared public random seed. Because the source code is identical and the seed is common, every instance can compute the others' outputs and randomize with weight one-twentieth, achieving correlation without communication or precommitment. The analogy is used to defend the claim that rationality alone can generate coordinated equilibria in one-shot interactions, turning Platonia from a paradox into an engineering problem. Critics respond by citing the Luring Lottery, in which an external predictor rewards an agent precisely for being algorithmically transparent, so that the same determinism that enables Platonia-style coordination becomes an exploitable liability. Audit the analogy in full: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping source code, air gap, shared seed, execution branch, and output correlation onto the human agents in Platonia and onto the agent and predictor in the Luring Lottery; then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended use—especially those involving indexical uncertainty, evolutionary selection against predictability, and the absence of a causal mechanism linking the copies; distinguish the disanalogies that are cosmetic from those that undermine the inference from computing to rational agency; and finally assess whether the conclusion—that superrational cooperation is rationally obligatory—survives once it is acknowledged that predictability can be punished rather than rewarded.
