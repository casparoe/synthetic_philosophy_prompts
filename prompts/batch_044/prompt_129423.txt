Take Gorbachev's 1985 anti-alcohol push in the USSR — not the economic numbers, but the social design behind it. Read it through Plato's Laws, especially the part where he treats the symposium not as a party but as a kind of civic stress-test: wine strips away pretense so rulers can see who actually has self-control and who just fakes it. Plato doesn't want to ban drinking outright for citizens past thirty; he wants it regulated, like a exam with rules, because removing the test doesn't manufacture virtue, it just buries the failure.

So map the Soviet campaign onto that framework. The state tried to shrink supply, close distilleries, even tear up vineyards. Where does that match Plato's fear of unregulated intoxication poisoning the soul? Where does it miss his point that you need the testing ground at all? Did the planners accidentally build a state-run "symposium" — regulated access, supervision, age limits — or were they trying to erase the exam entirely? And what does the explosion of home-brew moonshine tell us about Plato's claim that suppressing the test drives character failure underground instead of curing it?

One more twist: Plato's idea only makes sense in a tiny polis where the Nocturnal Council can actually watch people drink and learn from it. The USSR was massive and bureaucratic. Does the episode prove that his diagnostic view of alcohol can't scale, or does it reveal something weirder — that modern states try to replace Plato's wine-test with surveillance and ration cards? Work with the well-known facts of the campaign and don't worry about bibliographies; I just want the mapping, the breaks, and whether Plato's framework survives the case.
