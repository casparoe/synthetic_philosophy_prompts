I'm running a philosophy workshop for about 14 grad students and a few faculty, 90 minutes, on extinction ethics but I don't want another trolley problem. I want a classroom activty that makes Scheffler's afterlife conjecture hit them in the gut — the idea that extinction is awful partly because it wipes out the "afterlife" we live through in our projects, not just because people die. I need the rules and roles arranged so they feel that collapse personally, not through a lecture.

Can you design the full thing? I'm thinking we split them into "generations" of 3-4. Each generation spends 15 minutes adding to a shared artifact — maybe a rule-set for an imaginary institution — with explicit instructions that their contribution only makes sense as a link in a chain that future generations will extend. Give them a payoff: "continuity points" for every feature they design that anticipates later use, but they only cash in if the chain keeps going. At minute 45, announce the termination condition: no next generation exists, not because anyone died, but because the timeline just ends. They have to choose whether to polish their contribution or abandon it. Some should be able to "cash out" for individual satisfaction points if they declare the project dead, but that should feel like betrayal of the earlier work.

Please inject something more specific and slightly niche — maybe frame it around "narrative escrow," the idea that we deposit practical value into actions under an implicit contract that someone will redeem them later, and extinction voids the contract without anyone being present to notice the breach. I want the payoff sheet and roles to make that void tangible.

What I need from you:
- Exact roles, rules, how the artifact passes between groups, and the payoff sheet
- Minute-by-minute timeline
- The script for the termination announcement, plus how to handle people who stubbornly keep working
- A debrief mapping the emotional shift onto Scheffler's idea, with an honest note on what the activty can't reproduce (probably the fact that in real extinction nobody is around to feel the futility)
- A prediction of what will go wrong the first time — maybe people treat it like a casual game and don't invest emotionally, or they get mad at the facilatator for "ruining" the exercise
