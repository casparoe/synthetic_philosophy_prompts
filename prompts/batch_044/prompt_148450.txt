The exam question asked students to explain Friedman’s F-twist regarding unrealistic assumptions in scientific modeling and then apply it to defend or critique agent-based epidemiological models that assume homogeneous spatial mixing and perfectly rational Bayesian updating in vaccination behavior. The student wrote as follows: Friedman’s F-twist, presented in his essay on the methodology of positive economics, holds that the descriptive realism of a theory’s premises is entirely irrelevant to scientific assessment; only predictive accuracy matters. He observed that firms behave as if they maximize profits, not because managers literally solve optimization problems, but because competitive selection produces aggregate outcomes identical to rational maximization. This demonstrates that unrealistic assumptions are not defects; they are permissible, and sometimes advantageous, because simplifying away psychological and institutional complexity lets the model isolate the forces that actually drive macro-level outcomes. Applying this to epidemiological agent-based models, we can defend assumptions of homogeneous mixing and perfect rationality in vaccination choices. These premises are clearly false—real populations cluster socially and update beliefs with well-documented biases—yet the models reproduce observed infection curves with impressive fidelity. Because the unrealistic micro-assumptions do not corrupt the macro-level forecast, they fall squarely within Friedman’s license: the predictions succeed, so the false premises are scientifically harmless. One might worry that in epidemiology, unlike macroeconomics, the mixing rules are causally central to the dynamics, since infection spread depends directly on contact patterns. But this misses Friedman’s point, because he never required that unrealistic assumptions be causally peripheral; he required only that they not damage predictive accuracy. Since the forecasts remain robust, the idealization is fully justified. The only legitimate objection would be empirical failure, not descriptive implausibility at the micro-level.
