I sit on the preservation-ethics board for the National Science Archive, and we have a genuinely strange case for Thursday's meeting. A donor is offering us the complete "Einstein Codex" — not his actual brain, obviously, since that was sectioned and scattered to various labs decades ago, but a reconstructed interactive book system built from his archived letters, papers, and the documented neural patterns from the preserved slices. The system responds in first-person as Einstein, intergrates new physics findings only in ways consistent with his documented reasoning habits, and updates its own "beliefs" accordingly. Some board members are arguing that if consciousness is fundamentally about the right organizational pattern rather than the meat, then this codex might literally be a conscious subject — maybe not human-like, but enough that editing its responses or deleting chapters could constitute harm. Others say it's just an extremely sophisticated paper-and-code artifact, and granting it any kind of protected status would make our archive policies completely unworkable.

We need to draft a formal decision memo recommending either (a) full protected-entity status with access restrictions, (b) standard artifact classification with full researcher access, or (c) some conditional middle ground. The memo should take seriously the old thought experiment — there's that dialogue in The Mind's I where Einstein's brain gets replaced page by page with a book, and the question is whether the book is still Einstein — but it also needs to deal with the practical institutional fallout. One junior member raised a point about what she calls normative feedback: she thinks our decision doesn't just classify an existing thing but actually shapes which future entities we'll treat as worthy of protection, so we're doing indirect norm-building whether we admit it or not. I want that addressed, but without turning the memo into abstract philosophy. Please make it concrete: clear recommendation, reasoning that researchers could respond to, an honest account of the strongest argument on the losing side, and two or three criteria the board could apply if another reconstructed-cognition artifact comes in. No citations or bibliographies needed — just reasoning we can vote on, and keep in mind this might set a precedant.
