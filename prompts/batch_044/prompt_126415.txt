I am a novelist working on a collection about the hidden architectures of ethical reasoning, and I have been discussing with a friend—who teaches moral psychology but insists she has no settled view on the matter—the peculiar difficulty of telling a genuine moral preference from a well-disguised cognitive bias. We kept returning to the same problem: when someone feels a powerful intuitive aversion to a particular moral trade-off, such as sacrificing one life to save five only when the one is personally known, the feeling presents itself with identical phenomenological weight whether it is a stable, reason-responsive commitment or merely a parochial bias dressed in the language of principle. I want a short story that does not solve this problem but inhabits it, placing the reader inside the precise moment when the distinction collapses.

The setting should be a near-future arbitration chamber in a fictional polity called Aeloria, where disputes over collective resource distribution are resolved by preference auditors—philosophers trained to separate authentic moral tastes from culturally implanted distortions. The protagonist is an auditor named Voss, who has developed her own calibration method, which I will call the Reflexive Equilibrium of Taste: a procedure in which a subject's moral judgments are repeatedly revised against hypothetical counter-cases until the remaining resistance is classified as either preference or bias. For the central scene, I want Voss adjudicating a case in which a claimant rejects a longevity-weighted lottery—one that prioritizes the recently born over the long-lived—insisting that her aversion is a principled preference for temporal fairness rather than an ageist bias. The sensory atmosphere should be precise: the ozone scent of the calibration engines, the brittle sound of old preference profiles being shredded, the cold glass of the observation wall. Most importantly, Voss must discover that her own instrument produces identical signatures for her client's bias and for her own deepest commitments; the story should end not with resolution but with her recognizing that the distinction she has enforced for a decade may be operationally void, though emotionally indispensable.

Please write the opening sections and the pivotal tribunal scene—perhaps two to three thousand words in scope—keeping the philosophical difficulty embedded in character, dialogue, and atmosphere rather than in exposition. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific philosophical texts; the Reflexive Equilibrium of Taste is a fictional construct for this narrative, but treat it with full methodological seriousness. The audience for this story is literate but not academic, so the ideas should be accessible through drama rather than through argument.
