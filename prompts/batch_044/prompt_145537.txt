Write a formal dialogue between Dr. A, an evolutionary psychologist defending a strong adaptationist program for human cognition, and Dr. B, a comparative biologist defending the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique. Restrict their debate to a single trait: human numerical cognition, including approximate magnitude representation and basic arithmetic intuition. Dr. A should argue that the rapid, automatic, and cross-culturally robust character of numerical comparison points to direct selection for quantitative reasoning, and that explaining it away as a spandrel of language acquisition or spatial object-tracking merely relocates the adaptive problem without dissolving the apparent functional design. Dr. B should respond that numerical cognition exhibits exactly the architectural mismatches Gould and Lewontin highlighted—poor innate handling of probabilities and large magnitudes, heavy dependence on linguistic framing for exact arithmetic, substantial cross-cultural variability in mental number lines, and neural reuse of circuits evolved for object tracking—making it a plausible exaptation or spandrel rather than a dedicated adaptation. The dialogue must then turn on whether the spandrel concept is empirically tractable or merely a catch-all for ignorance: Dr. B should propose that identifying a true spandrel requires demonstrating both structural dependence on a separately selected trait and the absence of independent functional optimization, while Dr. A should press that this criterion risks unfalsifiability, since any observed optimization can be redescribed as an incidental byproduct of some other adaptive target. Each speaker must address the other’s actual argument, concede specific points where warranted, and avoid appeals to authority, citation lists, or bibliographic name-dropping. Conclude with both stating, in precise and non-vague terms, what pattern of comparative neurodevelopmental, cross-species behavioral, or genetic-architectural evidence would cause them to change their mind specifically about numerical cognition.
