You are an expert philosopher of language and an experienced teacher of interdisciplinary graduate students. Before you answer, take a moment to brainstorm the logical dependency structure of Kripkenstein's skeptical argument about rule-following, the precise difference between the skeptical problem and the skeptical solution, and the pedagogical traps that occur when students conflate this with private-language arguments or psychological uncertainty.

I am leading a single 90-minute session for a reading group of law and literature graduate students who have completed one introductory philosophy course but have no background in logic or philosophy of language. The concept is Kripkenstein's rule-following considerations: specifically, how the skeptical argument generates indeterminacy of meaning, and why the skeptical solution relocates the ground of correctness in communal practice rather than in individual mental states. I want a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

Plan the session in the following sections, in this order:

1. Dependency sequence (about 150 words): The exact order in which the components must be introduced and why—what conceptual piece must be in place before the skeptical argument can be stated, why the skeptical solution must come after the problem is fully felt, and how a five-minute silent writing exercise at the very start (each student writes a professional rule they follow, then describes what it would take for them to have been wrong about it all along) feeds into the first dependency.

2. The carrying example (about 150 words): The one concrete scenario that should carry the entire session. It must avoid the standard arithmetic "plus/quus" framing, which these students have likely encountered in popular writing. Instead, build the example around interpreting a legal standard such as "reasonable care" across different jurisdictions and historical periods. Specify what this example must possess (indeterminacy in past applications, no hidden mental rule that fixes future cases) and what it must lack (a private mental criterion that resolves ambiguity). Explain why it works for this audience and how it connects to community agreement.

3. Misconception map (about 200 words): The misconceptions this audience predictably brings or forms—anticipating that they will read Kripke as defending skepticism about meaning, as attacking private mental states directly, or as making a psychological point about our uncertainty in applying rules. For each misconception, identify the exact point in the dependency sequence where it should be headed off, with the specific move or phrasing to use.

4. What to leave out (about 100 words): What should be openly refused or deferred at this level—such as technical defenses of dispositionalism, the full text of Wittgenstein's later rule-following remarks, or debates about the normativity of meaning—and state this explicitly so the session does not overreach.

5. Check for understanding (about 100 words): One question to ask at the end that distinguishes whether students have grasped the skeptical solution (community practice as the ground of meaning) from whether they have only understood that rules seem indeterminate. Describe what a correct versus an incorrect answer reveals.

Keep the plan formal, focused, and practical. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke theories or specific historical claims that depend on precise scholarly attribution beyond what is common philosophical knowledge. Think step by step, and make sure the dependency structure is rigorous.
