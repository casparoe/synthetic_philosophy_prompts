I've noticed a recurring move in debates about whether certain human capacities must be innate because experience alone can't account for them. A researcher argues that children come to know something complex—say, that a particular grammatical constraint holds in languages they've never encountered, or that harming others for fun is wrong—without explicit teaching and despite noisy or absent environmental feedback. To prove learning impossible, the researcher frames the child's options as binary: either tracking obvious surface patterns in perception, or already possessing the full concept in question. Any middle path—where the child builds a partial, abstract representation from cross-context statistical regularities, simpler than the target but more structured than raw sensation—is omitted from the model, not because there's evidence it is unavailable, but because including it would collapse the claimed gap between stimulus and knowledge. I've seen this structure in language-acquisition arguments and in some evolutionary-psychology accounts of moral cognition. Is there an established term for this specific failure mode—this artificial widening of the learning gap by excluding intermediate, genuinely learnable representations? Or does it fall under broader diagnoses like the learning paradox or a false dichotomy in modeling? If no precise label exists, I want the carving: what distinguishes it from legitimate restrictions on a learner's prior, where its boundaries lie, and why it fails as an argument rather than as an empirical claim.
