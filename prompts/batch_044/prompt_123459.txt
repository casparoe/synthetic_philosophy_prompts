I have been trying to understand on my own whether there is a kind of breaking point in suffering — a level so extreme that no amount of ordinary happiness or mild relief could justify leaving it unaddressed — and whether pleasure and pain can even be measured on the same scale in the first place. To help me think through this, please design a realistic thought experiment, confined to a single paragraph and set in an ordinary healthcare administration or municipal budget office with no futuristic or fantastical elements. The case should be deliberately engineered so that lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and a fully commensurable account of pleasure and pain reach opposite conclusions. In particular, present a decision between funding one intervention that eliminates a severe, tightly focused suffering experienced by very few people and funding another intervention that produces or preserves a vastly larger aggregate of minor pleasures or mild discomfort-reductions spread across a huge population. Then, in your analysis, state the verdict each theory delivers and why; identify the precise structural feature of the scenario — for instance, whether the severe suffering lies above and the mild states below a lexical boundary — that makes the verdicts diverge; explain what would happen if that feature were removed, such as lowering the intensity of the severe case just beneath the threshold; and conclude by showing how a defender of the lexical-threshold position might attack the case without appealing to empirical data or named authorities, perhaps by arguing that the boundary has been assumed rather than independently established.
