Consider the decision by the British and French governments to continue funding the Concorde supersonic transport program between 1971 and 1976—a period after Boeing had cancelled its rival 2707, after the 1973 oil crisis had made supersonic fuel economics clearly prohibitive, and despite the absence of any firm commercial orders—through to the aircraft's entry into passenger service. Analyze this episode as a test case for philosophical debates about the sunk-cost fallacy, specifically the question of whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Your analysis should run in both directions. First, map the decision structure with discipline: identify the principal agents (treating London and Paris as interdependent but not unitary), the live options at critical junctures (cancellation with treaty penalties versus continuation with escalating public subsidy), and the relevant payoffs, including not only fiscal returns but symbolic and reputational stakes. Second, apply the standard forward-looking critique that past expenditure is decision-irrelevant, but then introduce a more nuanced theoretical framing—drawing on accounts of collective practical identity and on the logic of reputational commitment in repeated strategic interaction—to ask whether continuation might have served constitutive rather than purely instrumental ends: that is, whether maintaining the project expressed a particular national self-conception or signaled resolve to future technological partners in ways that a pure cost-benefit calculus cannot capture. Third, say precisely where this richer framing explains features of the episode that the standard fallacy narrative misses (for instance, the treaty structure that made unilateral withdrawal costly in ways unrelated to prior investment), and where it forces an artificial fit—whether it collapses the distinction between 'sunk' and 'live' costs, whether it rationalizes a decision that was in fact driven by political path-dependence, or whether it reveals that the very category of 'irrational honoring of sunk costs' applies only when we have already adopted a particular, contestable model of rational agency. Conclude by stating what the Concorde case shows about the fallacy itself: is it a universal norm, a useful heuristic that fails in public and symbolic contexts, or a misdiagnosis of a genuinely different form of practical reasoning?
