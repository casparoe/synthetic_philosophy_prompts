Read George Orwell's Animal Farm through the philosophical problem of populism and the metaphysics of the will of the people, specifically the tension between essentialist accounts—on which a homogeneous people exists prior to politics with a discoverable will—and constructivist accounts, on which the people is constituted only through representation, language, and aggregation procedures. Focus on the dramatization of collective decision: the initial rebellion's appearance of spontaneous unity, the debates over the windmill, the incremental rewriting of the farm's founding rules, and Boxer's progressive reduction of politics to loyalty through adopted maxims. Ask whether the novella presents the animals' will as something that can be genuinely aggregated from individual preferences, or whether it demonstrates that any collective will is manufactured by those who control the mechanisms of assembly and discourse. Where does Orwell's satire align with, and where does it resist, the philosophical distinction between a general will that is correct in principle and an actual will that can be systematically distorted? Introduce the aggregation problem as a further pressure: even if every animal holds a sincere preference, there may be no non-arbitrary algorithm to combine them into a single popular will without loss or manipulation. How does Boxer's substitution of critical judgment with simplified loyalty illustrate the danger of treating a constructed will as if it were naturally given? Finally, ask what the narrative form itself adds to the philosophical debate: does showing the erosion of collective agency through incremental linguistic corruption make a point about populism—particularly about how representation can retroactively create the people it claims merely to express—that analytic treatments of representation and aggregation alone do not capture? Keep the reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novella to an illustration of democratic theory, and do not distort the philosophical ideas to fit Orwell's satire; end by saying what the work reveals about the will of the people that argument alone tends to miss.
