As a novelist who often writes about consciousness at the edges of the physical world, I keep returning to a comparison advanced in debates over panpsychism and its ethical weight: attributing suffering to fundamental physical entities is said to be like attributing pain to a single neuron. The neuron fires, carries information, yet is not in pain; only the organized brain is. By analogy, an electron or quantum field might possess some minimal experiential quality, but without the integrative architecture of a mind, there is no subject of suffering, and therefore no ethical catastrophe hidden in every physical interaction. The comparison is used to license a relaxed conclusion — that panpsychism, even if true, does not turn every laboratory experiment or cosmic event into an atrocity.

I want to audit how far this correspondence actually holds, but I want to do so under conditions that the standard debate usually ignores. Rather than treating particles as isolated individuals, consider a Bose-Einstein condensate, in which thousands of atoms share a single macroscopic quantum state, or the inflationary field whose quantum fluctuations seeded large-scale cosmic structure. In these cases, the parts are not discrete; they are coherently entangled across scales, so the boundary between part and whole is not given by spatial separation but by the reach of coherence itself.

Please lay out the explicit correspondence table — neuron to fundamental entity, brain-level integration to what physical or organizational structure, subjective pain to what kind of proto-experience — then identify the precise points of fracture. Which features of neural organization, such as threshold dynamics, information integration, or reportability, does the source analogy quietly import into physics, where no analogous structures exist? Does coherence in a condensate or inflationary field function like neural integration, aggregating micro-experiences into something that might count as suffering, or does it diffuse them? Finally, tell me whether the relaxed ethical conclusion — that panpsychism imposes no special burden — survives once these disanalogies and the coherence problem are on the table. If the neuron analogy collapses under this pressure, suggest what analogical framework, if any, should take its place for thinking about suffering embedded in the substrate of physics.
