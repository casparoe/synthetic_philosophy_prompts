In my philosophy-of-history seminar we have been working through Nietzsche’s essay *On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life*, with particular attention to his triad of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. During discussion of the critical mode, the professor repeatedly invoked the line, “We want to serve history only to the extent that history serves life,” to dismiss a classmate’s argument that critical history—despite its apparently destructive dissolution of the past—is often just a disguised variant of monumental history, selectively preserving and glorifying those fragments of tradition that happen to serve present identity-formation. The professor seemed to treat the slogan as a vitalist filter: any historical practice that does not directly invigorate present life is not merely useless but actively harmful, so critical history is legitimate only when it clears ground for new life rather than when it covertly sustains an inherited self-image.

Before I respond in the next session, I want a precise disambiguation of what that sentence could actually be claiming. Please lay out the distinct theses it might express—ranging from the trivial to the radical—as a menu I can bring to office hours. I have in mind at least the following possibilities, but I want the full range you can construct: (1) a weak instrumentalism—history is valuable only as a causal means to non-historical ends, with no special status; (2) a vitalist threshold—historical study is permissible only when it increases the “health” or creative power of an individual or culture, with “life” defined as present, active flourishing; (3) a normative asymmetry—life possesses unconditional value and history possesses only conditional value, without yet specifying which of the three modes (monumental, antiquarian, critical) satisfy the condition; (4) a specific triadic verdict—among Nietzsche’s three modes, only critical and monumental history can serve life, while antiquarian history is inherently life-stunting; (5) a meta-disciplinary claim—the academic study of history should be reorganized around life-promoting outcomes rather than around preservation, accuracy, or interpretive completeness; and (6) a reflexive constraint—even the critical use of history must itself be monitored by life, so that “critical history” collapses into self-defeating skepticism unless it is anchored in present need.

For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; say whether it actually supports the professor’s dismissal of the “disguised monumental” argument; and note whether it is consistent with the essay’s own treatment of antiquarian preservation and monumental exemplarity. Then tell me which reading is the one people usually intend when they repeat the line as a slogan, and identify the specific reading—perhaps one that distinguishes “serving life” from “serving present identity”—that would allow my classmate’s argument to survive intact. I am less interested in a single “correct” interpretation than in knowing which interpretation does the argumentative work the professor needs, and which interpretations leave the debate genuinely open.
