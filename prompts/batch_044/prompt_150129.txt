In a recent graduate seminar on applied ethics, two of my doctoral students—let us call them R and S—became entangled in a dispute over the comparative wild-animal suffering footprint of beef versus chicken consumption, with particular attention to feed-crop cultivation and land-use displacement. I am writing to ask you to adjudicate their exchange point by point, indicating where each is correct, where each errs, and what both overlook; please respond in American English.

R opened by conceding the conventional view that chicken production kills more farmed animals per calorie, but insisted that the relevant metric for wild-animal suffering is not farmed deaths but indirect mortality in feed-crop supply chains and habitat conversion. R argued that beef, especially grain-finished beef, relies on substantial corn and soy inputs, yet also occupies vast pasture acreage. Crucially, R introduced a conceptual distinction—call it the incremental-versus-systemic suffering distinction—between mortality that is genuinely added by human agricultural intervention (pesticides, harvesting machinery, direct habitat conversion) and the baseline suffering that occurs in any ecosystem regardless of human presence. R claimed that pasture-based beef largely substitutes for wild herbivore populations, so its land-use footprint generates relatively little incremental wild-animal death, whereas chicken feed requires intensive monoculture cultivation that introduces massive incremental mortality through mechanized harvest and chemical inputs.

S replied that R's distinction collapses under counterfactual analysis. If beef demand fell, S said, marginal pastureland would not remain a pristine wild ecosystem; in many regions it would be converted to cropland, meaning beef consumption actually prevents a larger wild-animal suffering footprint by keeping land in lower-intensity grazing. Meanwhile, S noted, chicken's feed-conversion efficiency, while requiring less land per unit of protein than beef, still demands enormous acreage in absolute terms, and the per-calorie crop-mortality rate for poultry exceeds that of beef. S therefore concluded that beef is not obviously worse, and that R's incremental/systemic distinction illegitimately treats grazing ecosystems as neutral baselines when they are themselves sites of predation, starvation, and disease—suffering that beef production does not eliminate but merely reshuffles.

R countered that S was committing a modal error: the relevant comparison is not between beef-grazing land and hypothetical future cropland, but between the actual supply chain of beef versus chicken given present agricultural structures. R further argued that S underestimated the scale of feed-crop inputs for beef—especially in feedlot finishing—while overestimating the elasticity of pasture-to-crop conversion, which is constrained by soil quality and water availability. R also challenged S's reliance on per-calorie metrics, proposing instead a marginal-demand analysis: the next unit of beef consumed triggers more pasture expansion at the agricultural frontier, where wild-animal densities are highest, whereas the next unit of chicken primarily draws on existing high-yield feed-crop systems with lower marginal wild-animal density.

S responded that R's marginal-demand framing ignored substitution effects within consumer diets. If a consumer replaces beef with chicken, S argued, the net effect depends on whether the saved pasture is genuinely preserved or whether the increased chicken demand drives expansion of feed-crop frontiers—particularly in regions where soy expansion for poultry feed drives deforestation with enormous wild-animal mortality. S also questioned whether R's incremental/systemic distinction was operationalizable without arbitrary boundary-drawing: every agricultural system reshuffles suffering, and labeling some deaths "systemic" risks excusing anthropogenic harm by treating it as background noise.

Neither would concede. R maintained that without the incremental/systemic distinction, we cannot coherently assign moral responsibility for wild-animal suffering; S maintained that without rigorous counterfactual land-use modeling, R's distinction was merely rhetorical cover for preferring beef.

Please adjudicate: who is right about which specific claim—the comparative feed-crop burden, the validity of the incremental/systemic distinction, the counterfactual land-use analysis, and the marginal-demand versus substitution-effect framing? What, if anything, are both parties missing that would change the evaluation?
