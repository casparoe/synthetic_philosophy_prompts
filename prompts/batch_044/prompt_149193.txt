I have been trying to think through what a precautionary principle actually needs to accomplish if it is going to be more than a rhetorical reflex. In debates about climate engineering, synthetic biology, or chemical regulation, people invoke “precaution” to cover very different anxieties — that we do not understand the mechanism, that the damage might be irreversible, that we are imposing risks on people who did not consent, or that absence of evidence is being treated as evidence of absence. I want the full specification first, before comparing versions.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate precautionary framework must be able to deliver. For each requirement, state the specific case or conceptual distinction that motivates it — for instance, the difference between risks that are reversible and those that cascade through ecosystems, or between not knowing the probability distribution and not knowing the causal pathway at all (which I have been calling Type-I versus Type-II opacity). Note which requirements are non-negotiable for any framework that claims to be precautionary, and which are preferences of particular camps (for example, whether democratic deliberation must precede any action, or whether future generations must be given an effective veto).

Then identify any subset of these desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable. I am particularly interested in whether requiring full democratic deliberation conflicts with acting before the evidentiary window closes, and whether protecting future generations requires an asymmetry — present actors can choose inaction, but future actors cannot choose to undo — that collapses into standard risk analysis.

Finally, briefly score the three main formulations I keep encountering: the weak cost-benefit version that merely weights uncertainty more heavily, the strong version that prohibits action without proof of safety, and any middle version that uses reversibility thresholds. Do not evaluate them in detail; just note which desiderata each fails.
