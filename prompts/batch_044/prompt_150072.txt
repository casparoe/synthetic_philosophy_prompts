I'm trying to settle my own reflective equilibrium on when a community's living memory should override the historical record and when it should yield, and I need a principle that isn't just ad hoc rationalization. Here are my concrete verdicts. First, in a diasporic religious community, collective observance fixes an expulsion to a specific lunar-calendar day, while consular shipping logs and archival correspondence place the departure in the following solar year; I think the community should preserve its ritual date for internal practice, but public historians should record the archival date without attempting to "correct" the liturgical calendar. Second, in a post-industrial town, oral tradition holds that a 1920s mill closure was a deliberate owner-engineered lockout to destroy union organization, whereas company ledgers and trade journals indicate straightforward bankruptcy from obsolete machinery; here I think the record should simply replace the oral account in pedagogy and public history, even though the lockout narrative has sustained local solidarity for generations. Third, in an indigenous legal claim, oral tradition describes a watershed treaty with specific territorial boundaries, but colonial survey maps and subsequent state registries place the boundary ten miles south; in this case I think the oral tradition should prevail not merely as folklore to be "respected," but as having genuine adjudicative and moral authority over land-use rights. Fourth, in national civic education, a founding revolution is taught as essentially bloodless and consensual, yet declassified internal security reports reveal systematic suppression of extensive civilian casualties; I think the documentary record must correct the curriculum outright, and that maintaining the myth does active harm. But then there is the case I keep reversing on: survivors of a state atrocity collectively remember approximately four thousand victims, while forensic archaeology and declassified administrative documents converge on roughly four hundred; the larger figure structures memorial policy, international recognition, and moral education, while the smaller figure is forensically secure but feels like an imposition that compounds the original violence. I genuinely cannot settle whether symbolic magnitude should yield to documentary precision, or whether a principled middle position exists.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps drawing on a distinction between commemorative authority and corrective authority, or between identity-constitutive and claim-justifying functions of memory—that captures the first four verdicts? Does it resolve the atrocity-number case, or does accommodating that case require me to abandon one of the earlier judgments? And what should it say about a further case I haven't decided: a family remembers an ancestor as a resistance fighter, but archival records show collaboration; however, the "resistance" framing may operate as a metaphor for cultural preservation under occupation, and the family deploys it only within kinship rituals, never in public historical claims. Which of my existing verdicts, if any, must I drop to maintain consistency, and does the principle generate a clear verdict for this kinship-only case?
