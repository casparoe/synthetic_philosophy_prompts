The spectrum argument in value theory—sequences of incrementally differing lives, states, or outcomes—has long been used to pressure-test whether the relation "better than" is transitive. One camp holds that if each adjacent pair in a spectrum is correctly ordered, rational consistency demands transitive closure across the chain, which forces acceptance of extreme aggregate judgments (such as the conclusion that an enormous population of barely good lives can be better than a smaller population of excellent ones) or requires arbitrary cutoffs that violate continuity. The opposing camp treats these same spectra as evidence that "better than" is not transitive: at sufficient distance, values become incommensurable or comparison collapses, and preserving the continuity of local judgments requires rejecting global ordering. Despite decades of exchange, the dispute has not moved appreciably; both sides accept the same case descriptions and share the intuition that something is wrong with blindly extending local rankings across vast differences, yet they continue to talk past each other. Is this stalemate a substantive disagreement about the logic of comparison, a verbal dispute about whether "better than" denotes a total preorder or a partial order, or two groups answering different questions—one about rational choice axioms, the other about the ontology of value—under one heading? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says about local comparison, incommensurability, and aggregate judgment. In particular, consider whether the deadlock depends on an unstated one-dimensional model of value: if well-being, autonomy, and other goods are incommensurable axes, then Pareto-style dominance can be locally transitive without yielding a global linear order, and the spectrum argument may be testing the wrong formal structure altogether. Does importing a multi-dimensional or vector-valued framework dissolve the paradox, relocate it to disputes about admissible dimensions, or leave the core disagreement untouched? And given your diagnosis—whether it is about formal representation, hidden empirical claims about comparative cognition, or bedrock intuitions about continuity—what would actually count as progress: a formal result constraining partial-order reconstructions, an empirical finding about systematic intransitivity in single-dimensional judgments, a conceptual distinction that separates rational-choice consistency from ontological comparison, or an argument that the question is ill-posed?
