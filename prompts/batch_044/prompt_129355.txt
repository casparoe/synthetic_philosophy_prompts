The panel’s published verdicts on four regimes are as follows.

Germany’s Vergangenheitsbewältigung (Stolpersteine distributed across municipalities, mandatory Holocaust education in federal curricula, and state funding for critical memorial museums): Verdict—achieves reflexive discontinuity; practices are institutionally coherent across federal and local levels and do not depend on victim-group initiative alone.

2. 3. Turkey’s official stance on the Armenian genocide (criminal statutes prohibiting public acknowledgment, absence of state memorials, and state-sponsored counter-narratives in education): Verdict—exhibits active mnemonic suppression; not merely a failure of discontinuity but a structurally enforced continuity with the perpetrator regime’s denial.

4. The American South’s post-2020 handling of Confederate monumentality (localized removals of statues in some municipalities, absence of statewide mandatory curriculum reform on slavery and Jim Crow, and preservation of “heritage” framing in several state legislatures): Verdict—transitional and structurally incomplete; discontinuity is achieved symbolically in some localities but is not institutionally secured against legislative reversal.

Rule B (Generational Transmission Mechanism): A regime achieves reflexive discontinuity only if it institutionalizes critical remembrance through compulsory, state-mandated educational transmission, regardless of whether local symbolic practices remain contested.
