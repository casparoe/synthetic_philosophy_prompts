Below are working notes for a short essay on Spinoza’s democratic theory. The central claim is that Spinoza’s apparent tension between democratic sovereignty and rational governance dissolves once we read his determinism and epistemology together: democracy is not a compromise between popular rule and reason, but the institutional form in which the multitude’s collective power becomes more adequate through internal correction rather than through external authority.

Notes:
- Spinoza defines democracy in the Tractatus Politicus as the form of state in which sovereignty remains with the entire multitude, making it the most “absolute” government because no part of the collective is permanently subordinated to another part’s will.
- At the same time, Spinoza holds that reason should guide the state and that most individuals are led by passion and inadequate ideas.
- The standard reading resolves this tension either by treating Spinoza’s democracy as effectively an aristocracy of the wise, or by saying that democratic institutions filter passion into reason.
- I want to resist both readings. Instead, I propose that for Spinoza the distinction between reason and passion is descriptive rather than normative, since there is no free will. The state does not need citizens to be rational agents in a moral sense; it needs structures that increase the adequacy of collective ideas.
- Democratic councils, deliberation, and the diffusion of sovereign power create conditions in which inadequate ideas confront one another and are corrected, analogously to how the mind moves from inadequate to adequate ideas through internal interaction rather than through external command.
- Therefore democracy is not sovereignty plus rational constraint; it is sovereignty structured so that its own exercise tends toward greater adequacy.

Please comment on these notes with particular attention to the following:
1. Accuracy: Does this interpretation misrepresent Spinoza’s distinction between potentia and potestas, or his account of adequate and inadequate ideas in the Ethics?
2. Coherence: Is the analogy between mental correction and institutional deliberation defensible within Spinoza’s metaphysics, or does it import a psychological model he would reject?
3. Distinctiveness: Does this argument successfully distinguish Spinoza’s democracy from Rousseau’s general will, or does it collapse Spinoza into a form of collective rationalism he would not recognize?
4. Interest: Is the framing productive, or does it obscure more than it clarifies?

Please provide specific suggestions for sharpening or revising the argument, and note any concepts from Spinoza’s political writings that I should reconsider.
