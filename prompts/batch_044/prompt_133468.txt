I've been thinking about the alignment problem lately — specifically whether it's really one problem or like three different ones people just lump together because they all involve AI being bad. I want to defend a pretty strong unificationist view: that outer alignment, inner alignment, and even corrigibility are all just facets of a single underlying issue, which is that we don't have direct epistemic access to what an optimizing system is actually trying to do. The split only looks real because we're training models through gradient descent on a proxy loss, so there's this gap between the spec and the internal representation. But if we could read and edit the goal representation directly — not just the behavior — then specifying the right goal and making sure the model sticks to it become the same task: verification. So I think the "alignment problem" is really just one problem of teleological transparency, and all the subdivisions are artifacts of our current black-box training paradigm.

What would count against this? I don't want the obvious "well, they're technically different" response. I want to know what observations, theoretical results, or design experiences would actually show that outer and inner alignment come apart in principle, not just in practice with current architectures. Would a system with perfect self-modeling still face an inner alignment failure? Are there arguments from decision theory or from the philosophy of action that show specifying a goal and maintaining it are structurally different tasks? And is my view insulated from falsification — have I just defined alignment into being one thing? If so, is that actually a problem, or is it okay for a conceptual framework? Also, where does corrigibility fit: is it genuinely a seperate problem about preference for being changed, or can it be dissolved into the transparency story? Rank the defeaters from "this kills the view" to "this is just awkward," and be honest about whether committed defenders of the unification view would concede any of them.
