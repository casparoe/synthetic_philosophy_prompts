Hey, I'm a retired oceanographer — thirty years measuring plankton — and lately I've gotten into philosphy of social science, mostly through Amartya Sen's writing on famine. I'm trying to wrap my head around something that bugs me about how international aid agencies actually file these crises.

There's this institutional practice, or set of protocols, in the big humanitarian bodies: when mass starvation hits a region, they check first for drought or flood so they can classify it as a 'natural disaster,' which unlocks food-aid budgets and logistics. But they don't run a matching check for whether local goverment policies have collapsed food entitlements — the kind of thing Sen describes in Bengal in 1943, where rice was available but laborers lost purchasing power, or in the Irish famine where grain was exported, or during the Great Leap Forward where state procurement pulled food away from rural households. In fact, if meteorological drought is present, the natural label usually sticks even when bureaucratic mismanagement is clearly contributing. At the same time, the international legal system will prosecute starvation as a war crime only when there's explicit military intent to deny food, not when it's the result of bad agricultural planning or corruption.

So what philosophical commitments does that filing-and-response practice presuppose? Does it treat causation like in my old physics experiments — isolate the rainfall variable and treat governance as background noise? Or does it rest on a particular theory of moral responsibility, where collective policy failures don't count as causing harm the way an individual soldier blocking a convoy does? Is there an implicit value theory too — that suffering caused by rain is a tragedy we should fix, but suffering caused by entitlement collapse is a political problem outsiders shouldn't touch?

I'm also struck by an inconsistency: the same agencies fund crop-insurance schemes that treat farmers as rational economic agents, yet treat famine victims as passive recipients of nature's whim. Does the practice need one coherent philosophy behind it, or is it just pragmatically incoherent? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether its parts are consistent with each other, and tell me what would have to change in these protocols if we took Sen's entitlement-collapse view seriously rather than the weather-is-the-cause view. No need for a bibliography — I just want to think it through.
