The philosophy-of-pain debate about whether pain is necessarily bad has circled the same clinical evidence—pain asymbolia—for decades without visible movement. One camp reads asymbolic patients as proof that nociception can occur without phenomenal disvalue: the sensory core remains, but the affective-motivational overlay is missing or decoupled, so badness is contingent rather than essential. The opposing camp insists that these subjects still possess an intact normative reason to avoid tissue damage; the decoupling is behavioral or expressive, not evaluative, which means the badness is present but rationally silent. The neuroscience and case descriptions haven't shifted, yet neither side updates.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is structural rather than empirical: "badness" is doing double duty for an intrinsic phenomenal quality and for a relational fact about rational motivation, so the sides are answering different questions—one about phenomenology, one about practical reason—under a single heading. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is there a hidden empirical disagreement about what asymbolic subjects actually experience, or is this a verbal disagreement masked by shared clinical vocabulary? Are the sides diverging on what counts as data—first-person report, behavioral avoidance, or normative coherence? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate: a sharper conceptual distinction, a refined phenomenological framework, or should we treat the impasse as evidence that the question is ill-posed?
