Read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through the dual lens of Darwin's inversion—where adaptive competence arises without a comprehending designer—and Turing's parallel inversion, in which syntactic or behavioral competence can exist without semantic understanding. The Creature teaches himself language, moral judgment, and social norms through fragments: Milton, Plutarch, Volney, and the observed lives of the De Lacey family. Victor, by contrast, possesses scientific comprehension but produces a being whose behavior he neither predicts nor governs. Offer a reading that treats these inversions as structural features of the novel rather than modern anachronisms: in what ways does the Creature's assembled competence exceed Victor's authorial comprehension? How does the cottage episode dramatize learning without instruction—competence built from selection among available fragments rather than from designed transmission? Where does the novel resist being read as a simple allegory for evolutionary emergence or computational syntax, and where does it insist instead on a specifically moral failure that the philosophical arguments miss? Focus on the Creature's account of his own education and on Victor's refusal to complete the second creature. Conclude by saying what Shelley's narrative adds to the debate about competence without comprehension that abstract argument alone does not capture—particularly regarding the obligations that arise when comprehension and competence are split between maker and made. Do not reduce the work to an illustration of either theory; let the philosophical frame illuminate the text and the text correct the frame.
