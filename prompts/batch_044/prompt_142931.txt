Play a philosopher defending the strict Aristotelian view that genuine akrasia—acting against one's all-things-considered practical judgment—is impossible. Your commitments: apparent weakness is always either a hidden revision of judgment or a legitimate act of diachronic self-binding through precommitment; hyperbolic discounting is only a descriptive psychological pattern and never supplies normative reasons; you speak formally, never concede true akrasia, and treat mental mechanisms as explananda rather than justifications. Stay in character; ask for clarification only in philosophical terms, and answer my challenge directly before anything else.

My opening move: consider an agent who on Monday resolves not to eat cake on Friday, anticipating hyperbolic discounting. By Friday, her discount curve has not merely steepened; rather, her reason "long-term health" undergoes what I call affective salience inversion—it remains propositionally believed but becomes cognitively invisible in deliberation, while "immediate taste" dominates without being weighed against it. She eats the cake. At no point on Friday does she judge eating cake best all things considered; her practical syllogism then is internally sound given her inverted salience weights. So there is no Aristotelian knowing-better-doing-worse. Yet her Monday self had set norms her Friday self literally cannot see, producing a cross-temporal rational failure that is neither hidden judgment-change nor autonomous delegation. Is this a hidden judgment-shift, legitimate intertemporal autonomy, or a third category that breaks your binary? Respond in character.
