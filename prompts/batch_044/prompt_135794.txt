I'm a novelist drafting a manuscript about ethical failure in artistic communities, and during a recent writers' retreat in the Dolomites I found myself in a late-night discussion about whether Kant's universalizability tests could ever condemn an artistic practice rather than merely a moral one. I sketched an argument that now disturbs me because it seems to show that either the Formula of Universal Law permits a peculiar kind of interpretive vandalism, or else it condemns nearly all avant-garde fiction. Before you diagnose it, please take the role of an expert in Kant's practical philosophy and the philosophy of interpretation; think carefully and step by step, and begin with a brief brainstorming of the different ways a universalization argument can fail—whether through contradiction in conception, contradiction in will, an equivocation between logical and practical coherence, or a misidentification of the institution being presupposed.

Here is the chain I constructed, which I want you to examine precisely:

(1) Kant's Formula of Universal Law requires that I act only on maxims I can at once will to become universal law.
(2) A contradiction in conception arises only when universalizing a maxim annihilates the very practice or institution it presupposes—promising being the canonical case.
(3) Consider this maxim of artistic production: whenever I compose a long-form narrative, I will embed exactly one structurally necessary but narratively invisible contradiction—an event that must have occurred for the plot to hold, yet rendered permanently unresolvable without reference to any external text—solely to exhaust the hermeneutic resources of future interpreters without offering them resolution.
(4) Universalized, every novelist does this; the result is a culture in which all extended narratives contain one irreducible hermeneutic fracture.
(5) This universal law does not annihilate the institution of narrative, because narrative can survive permanent ambiguity; indeed, certain aesthetic theories treat irreducible ambiguity as a virtue rather than a failure.
(6) Nor does it yield a contradiction in will, since a rational agent could consistently will a world in which artistic interpretation is permanently unstable—such a world might even be valued for resisting totalizing readings.
(7) Therefore, by the FUL, this maxim is morally permissible.
(8) Yet the maxim is clearly a form of interpretive vandalism: it deliberately depletes a shared hermeneutic commons without contributing to it, and it does so for no purpose beyond testing the exhaustion point of interpretation itself.
(9) If the FUL permits this, then it misses an entire category of wrong—call it hermeneutic harm.
(10) To save the FUL, one might claim that hermeneutic stability is itself a necessary institution, so that universalizing its systematic destruction produces a contradiction in conception.
(11) But if hermeneutic stability is necessary in this strong sense, then any maxim that systematically destabilizes interpretation—Socratic irony, unreliable narration, deliberately ambiguous endings, even philosophical texts with misleading footnotes—becomes impermissible.
(12) That proves far too much: it would condemn artistic and philosophical practices we have strong independent reasons to preserve.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you believe the argument is valid and the real task is choosing which premise to surrender, tell me whether you would least want to give up (5), (6), (9), or (10), and explain the exact price of keeping it. I am especially interested in whether steps (4) through (6) contain a hidden slide between logical consistency and practical coherence, or whether the error lies in treating interpretation as an institution comparable to promising. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am working without external access and need the reasoning to stand entirely on its own.
