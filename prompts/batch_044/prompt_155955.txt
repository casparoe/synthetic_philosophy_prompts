I've been thinking about whether an immoral work of art can still be great art, particularly when the moral problem lies not in the artist's biography but in the structure of the work itself. I'd like to read Vladimir Nabokov's Lolita through this lens—specifically through the distinction between a work that merely depicts immoral acts and a work that structurally invites its audience to take aesthetic pleasure in an immoral perspective, temporarily adopting rationalizations that ordinary moral life would condemn. Humbert Humbert's narration is exquisitely beautiful, and the novel makes his aestheticized obsession seductive; yet Lolita herself is present as a suffering child, and characters like Charlotte Haze offer moments of mundane reality that seem to resist Humbert's framing. Does the novel's artistic achievement depend on our complicity with Humbert's viewpoint, or do those counter-voices provide enough critical distance to separate admiration for Nabokov's craft from endorsement of Humbert's actions?

I'm writing an essay for a philosophy of art seminar, and I want a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously without reducing either to an illustration of the other. Which scenes best illuminate the tension—perhaps Humbert's early aestheticized descriptions of his obsession, or the later rupture when Lolita's actual life with Dick Schiller breaks through his romanticized narrative? Does Lolita ultimately support the view that moral flaws in a work's invited response diminish its artistic value, or does it show that aesthetic and moral evaluation run on genuinely separate tracks? And where does the novel resist a clean philosophical answer—maybe in the ambiguity over whether we are meant to admire the prose precisely while condemning the consciousness that produces it?

Please don't treat this as a request for citations or a bibliography; I need an analysis I could follow without web access, grounded in well-known plot points and major scenes. What I'm after is an honest critical reading: where the philosophical idea of separating work from artist, or of immoral art's aesthetic status, genuinely illuminates the novel, and where Lolita complicates or pushes back against that idea in ways a purely argumentative essay might miss. What does the novel add to the debate that argument alone doesn't capture?
