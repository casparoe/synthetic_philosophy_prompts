I'm writing a long-form feature on how institutional conventions survive or collapse, drawing on David Lewis's study Convention—particularly his analysis of coordination equilibria sustained by common knowledge of mutual expectations, and his distinction between mere descriptive regularities and genuinely normative conventions. I want to use William Golding's Lord of the Flies as a literary stress-test rather than a simple allegory: the boys' establishment of rules around the conch, the split over rescue versus hunting, and the dissolution of Ralph's authority all look like shifts between competing coordination equilibria.

Read the novel through Lewis's framework, but do not reduce it to an illustration. Treat specific moments—the first assembly, Piggy's insistence on the rules, Jack's challenge to the conch's authority, the failure to maintain the signal fire, the killing of Simon, and the final hunt for Ralph—as cases where common knowledge is either sustained or eroded. Where does Golding suggest that conventions collapse not because the rational payoff structure shifts in the way Lewis's model predicts, but because emotional and perceptual dynamics—fear of the beast, the visceral reward of the hunt—destroy the shared awareness that others still share the convention? Does the novel thereby expose a limitation in Lewis's rationalist account, especially his assumption that common knowledge of rationality is sufficient for stability?

I'm also interested in whether the conch operates as a Lewisian convention of authority rather than mere coordination: Ralph's claim depends on the boys knowing that they know he holds it, and Jack's rebellion succeeds when that higher-order common knowledge fractures. Where does this reading illuminate the text, and where does the text push back—say, through the role of violence or status competition that Lewis's game-theoretic framework does not explicitly model? Finally, what does Golding's dramatization contribute to philosophical debates about norm evolution that abstract modeling alone tends to miss? A journalist covering science and ideas needs to know where the parallel holds and where it risks overreaching.
