Take the split-brain experiments conducted by Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga in the 1960s and 1970s—particularly the dissociation of verbal and manual responses in corpus-callosotomy patients presented with lateralized visual stimuli—and analyze them through Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self as a distributed, loop-like pattern that can persist across brains and substrates. I am writing a dissertation chapter on personal identity and want to avoid treating Hofstadter as a standard functionalist. Specifically: (1) How does the split-brain case map onto Hofstadter's claim that an I is not confined to a single skull? Does one patient with two dissociated hemispheres host one internally fragmented pattern, two partially realized selves, or something that breaks the pattern-model altogether? (2) Which features of the episode does the framework illuminate—for example, the left hemisphere's confabulatory narration versus the right hemisphere's nonverbal recognition—and which does it miss, such as the patients' persistent first-person reports of unity or the surgical etiology? (3) What does the case show about the theory: does it confirm substrate-independent patterning of selfhood, or does the continued subjective integration despite hemispheric division reveal that Hofstadter underweights first-person unity? Separate what is settled about the split-brain findings and Hofstadter's philosophy from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists, and do not present a contested interpretation of either the experiments or the loop-model as consensus.
