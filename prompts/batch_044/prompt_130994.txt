I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy with a concentration in political theory, preparing an application for a six-month independent research fellowship. The idea comes from a persistent frustration in my coursework and in discussions with quantitative social scientists: debates about whether the world is becoming less violent—often framed around long-term declines in physical harm—tend to stall not over data, but over unacknowledged shifts between metrics. Participants move between per-capita and absolute measures, include or exclude state violence, institutional coercion, and ecological damage, and then treat their preferred framework as revealing the single fact of the matter. I want to ask whether there is indeed a framework-independent fact about whether global violence is declining, and if not, whether there is at least a structured, non-arbitrary way to determine that the answer depends on how we weight different harm-types.

Rather than defending one definition and computing within it—the standard strategy on both sides—I propose developing a modal-robustness framework for contested historical classifications. I would construct a taxonomy of reasonable conceptual frameworks, varying temporal baselines, population denominators, and inclusion rules for direct, institutional, and ecological harm; then map whether the decline thesis holds, fails, or inverts across that logical space. If it holds everywhere within a bounded region, we have a framework-independent fact; if it holds in some and fails in others with no principled resolution, the dispute is partly verbal in a precise metaontological sense; if it clusters in a core region, we can specify exactly which normative commitments drive divergence. The deliverable would be a philosophical decision architecture—a structured taxonomy plus conditions for cross-framework convergence—rather than a new empirical dataset. Over six months I plan two months for taxonomy construction, two months for conceptual mapping against well-known historical ranges, and two months for drafting the framework paper and a practical guide for policymakers evaluating competing indicators. The project fails if the framework space collapses into trivial unity or radical incommensurability with no intermediate structure.

Existing work typically treats metric disagreement as a problem to be solved by finding the correct measure, or uses it to claim the debate is purely ideological. What is missing is an intermediate metaontological analysis that takes classification pluralism seriously without global skepticism. Please separate what is settled—such as that metric selection materially affects conclusions, and that 'violence' functions as a cluster concept—from what is genuinely contested, especially whether any framework-independent fact of the matter exists about long-term trends and whether modal robustness is the appropriate conceptual tool. Do not present either the optimistic or pessimistic empirical conclusion as consensus, avoid inventing specific citations or bibliographies, and be explicit about where the proposed framework is speculative versus where it applies existing conceptual distinctions.
