I am defending a position I call threshold-quietism — a deliberately narrow variant of tranquilism that treats only the absence of craving and suffering as intrinsically valuable, while treating positive affect, achievement, and relational richness as instrumentally valuable solely insofar as they reduce future suffering or unsatisfied craving. I apply it specifically to secular well-being metrics: I argue that composite public-health dashboards (subjective-life-satisfaction plus positive-emotion indices) are conceptually incoherent, and that we should instead report only normalized deficit scores for suffering and craving intensity. This became personally urgent three months ago when my father received a terminal diagnosis; watching him lose future-oriented desire while retaining moments of calm forced me to ask whether our standard flourishing metrics describe anything real about such a life, or merely project an active-agent bias onto every patient.

Tomorrow I present this to an interdisciplinary seminar at a public-health ethics institute. They are not philosophers of mind in general; they care about what can be implemented in a dashboard and whether my view collapses into a clinical model of depression.
