Analyze the administration of the General Government in occupied Poland—particularly the bureaucratic division between Hans Frank's civil administration, the SS security police, and economic exploitation agencies from roughly 1940 to 1944—through Zygmunt Bauman's thesis that the Holocaust was enabled by modern bureaucratic rationality rather than by a regression to premodern barbarism. I want a disciplined mapping: identify which specific institutional practices in Frank's administration—such as the division of labor between agencies, the use of euphemistic administrative language for deportation and killing, the statistical tracking of Jewish and Polish populations, and the separation of technical execution from moral judgment—correspond to Bauman's concepts of adiaphorization and the gardening-state rationality. Then say where the theory breaks down in this particular case: for instance, does it fail to account for the open ideological fanaticism of local SS leaders, the public and ritualized violence that exceeded administrative necessity, or the way racial-biological ideology actively shaped bureaucratic procedures rather than being neutralized by them? Finally, apply this case to the uniqueness-versus-comparability debate that defined the Historikerstreit: if the Holocaust's modern bureaucratic form is central, does that make it comparable to other modern administrative atrocities—such as colonial violence or industrial warfare—or does the specific fusion of racial ideology and bureaucratic method visible in Frank's administration preserve its singularity? I am especially interested in whether Bauman's framework can accommodate both the cold proceduralism and the visceral hatred that coexisted in this administration, or whether that tension reveals a limit in explaining genocide through modernity alone.
