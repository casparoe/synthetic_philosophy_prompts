After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend about Benjamin Franklin’s project of moral perfection — specifically his catalogue of thirteen virtues and the private ledger in which he recorded daily lapses — I have become fixated on a recurring failure mode that I suspect is not unique to Franklin, but I cannot tell whether it already has a precise philosophical name.

The phenomenon, as it emerged in our argument, works like this. First, consider Franklin’s virtue of Temperance, understood in his scheme as moderation in eating and drinking so as to avoid dullness rather than to pursue pleasure. My friend pointed out a case in which Franklin, facing a ceremonial toast he could not refuse without grave insult, might drink precisely one cup — an act that looks like a breach if the rule is quantitative, but not if the rule is interpreted functionally. Rather than experiencing this as a genuine conflict between virtues — say, between Temperance and Civility or Justice — the taxonomy seems to invite a redescriptive move: the boundary of Temperance shifts to absorb the exception, not through explicit revision but through a kind of implicit categorical elasticity.

Second, and more generally, anyone who maintains an internal moral checklist over a long period appears to develop what I can only describe as taxonomic homeostasis. When an action threatens to register as a serious failure in one category, the user subtly reclassifies it into a neighbouring virtue-box where it counts as success: a sharp rebuke becomes ‘frankness’ rather than ‘anger’; a withdrawn refusal becomes ‘prudence’ rather than ‘cowardice’; a calculated omission becomes ‘silence’ rather than ‘dishonesty’. The taxonomy does not merely record moral life; it constrains what can appear as a moral problem in the first place, and then protects its own coherence by bending its categories.

Thus the pattern has two linked parts: (a) the pre-existing moral framework constrains the perception of novel dilemmas by forcing them into established virtue-boxes, and (b) those boxes gradually deform — without conscious deception — to shield the record from catastrophic failure.

Is there an established concept that captures exactly this two-part structural failure? I am explicitly dissatisfied with bare labels such as ‘self-deception’, ‘rationalisation’, or even Goodhart’s law applied to virtue, because none of them foreground the taxonomic mechanism I am describing. If nothing fits precisely, please coin and define a term, specifying its boundaries, its nearest neighbours, and clear cases where it does not apply — for instance, whether it requires a written ledger or can operate with any internalised moral schema.
