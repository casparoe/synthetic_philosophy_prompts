I am writing a feature on how formal epistemologists treat probabilistic deference when objective chances intersect with self-location, model uncertainty, and causal feedback. In tracking the work of the philosopher Miriam Holbrook—whose essays on chance-credence alignment have circulated mainly in specialized philosophy-of-science venues—I have noticed a consistent pattern of rulings across five cases, but I am unsure how to extend it to a sixth.

Holbrook's track record is as follows.

First, in a standard fair-coin scenario where the objective chance of heads is 0.5 and no other information is available, she holds that rational credence should be exactly 0.5: direct deference to the Principal Principle without adjustment.

Second, in a state lottery where an agent knows the objective winning chance is 0.001 but also harbors a subjective "lucky" bias based on past experience, Holbrook rules that the bias must be suppressed; the credence should remain 0.001.

Third, in a quantum decoherence scenario with two macroscopic branches weighted 0.7 and 0.3 by the Born rule, where an observer knows she occupies one branch but not which, Holbrook maintains that her credence in being in the 0.7-weighted branch should be exactly 0.7, treating the branch weight as an objective chance to which she should defer without further self-location discount.

Fourth, in a climate-projection case where a model assigns an objective chance of 0.8 to exceeding a temperature threshold conditional on the model's parameterization being correct, but the agent assigns only 0.6 prior credence to that parameterization, Holbrook rejects direct deference to 0.8; she argues that the relevant credence must integrate over model uncertainty rather than adopting the conditional chance.

Fifth, in a deterministic statistical-mechanical system where there is an objective epistemic distribution assigning 0.9 to a gas molecule being in region R, but the agent possesses complete knowledge of the microstate, Holbrook rules that the Principal Principle does not apply at all; the agent's credence should be 1 or 0 based on her knowledge, not on the statistical distribution.

The new case I want to understand is this.

A diagnostic framework assigns an objective chance of 0.55—grounded in quantum-level stochastic dynamics of gene expression—to a particular patient developing a neurodegenerative condition within ten years. The framework is self-undermining: if it classifies the patient as high-risk, it automatically initiates an aggressive preventive protocol that reduces the actual risk to 0.25, but the patient does not know whether the protocol has been triggered in her own case. Simultaneously, the quantum dynamics generate branching trajectories, and the patient has first-person indexical uncertainty about which trajectory she occupies, though all trajectories share identical population-level statistics. The patient has no independent subjective bias about her health.

Given Holbrook's five rulings, what should she say about the patient's rational credence? I am less interested in a bare number than in the reasoning: what general rule or rules do her earlier verdicts jointly support, and which of those rules diverge on this new case? Identify the closest analog among her five precedents, explain whether it genuinely determines the outcome or whether the precedents underdetermine it, and if they do underdetermine it, articulate the principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, specify a further hypothetical case—one that does not depend on exotic physics or perfect predictors—that would best discriminate between the competing rules you identify.
