Plan a ninety-minute discussion session for a reading group of curious non-specialists on Confucian role ethics (lunli). The goal is to displace the common reduction of Confucianism to familial niceness and instead establish that, within this framework, moral obligations are generated by the specific relational roles one occupies rather than applied to isolated individuals. Use the Mengzi case of the father who steals a sheep as the carrying example—not to celebrate filial piety, but to expose a structural feature: when obligations attached to one role (father) conflict with those of another (magistrate or citizen), there is a moral remainder that does not disappear once a choice is made. Lay out the explanatory arc precisely: which concept must be secured before the sheep case can do philosophical work (and why presenting the five relationships first undermines that work); the dependency-ordered sequence in which the role-constituted self, relational obligation, role conflict, and moral remainder should be introduced; the misconception that role obligations are merely cultural rules, with the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted; the misconception that role ethics offers no resources for resolving conflict, and how the sheep case itself should be used to block it; a brief hypothetical check at the end that reveals whether participants understand obligations as role-indexed rather than universal; and which standard comparison—to Kantian universalizability or Aristotelian virtue ethics—you should explicitly refuse to develop at this level, with a sentence on why that refusal protects the session's coherence. Do not request readings, citations, or bibliographic references.
