I'm leading a seventy-five-minute first-year philosophy seminar. The students have just finished Locke's memory-based account of personal identity and have never encountered African philosophy. I need to teach Ubuntu not as an inspirational slogan but as a relational conception of personhood with distinct ontological and normative dimensions: specifically, the claim that personhood is constituted by relations rather than possessed by a pre-social substrate, and that this constitution carries direct implications for moral accountability. Plan the session's explanatory arc. First, lay out the exact order in which the pieces must be introduced—what depends on what—so that the constitutive ontological claim is secure before any normative conclusion is drawn. Second, specify the one carrying example or scenario the explanation should build around: describe what features it must have (both an ontological and a normative dimension) and what it must avoid (explain why neither the standard "I am because we are" aphorism nor a generic tribal-council scene will work). Third, identify the misconceptions these Lockean-trained students will predictably form—I'm anticipating at least that relational personhood eliminates individual moral agency, that it is irreconcilable with human-rights frameworks, and that it is merely a descriptive claim about social influence rather than a constitutive one—and state precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Fourth, provide one targeted check-for-understanding question that distinguishes grasping the constitutive claim from grasping only the social-context claim. Finally, say explicitly what part of the broader debate or literature should be omitted at this level and openly acknowledged as left out.
