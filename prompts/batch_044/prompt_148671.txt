Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the Holocaust's uniqueness, comparability, and modernity ought to produce. One camp, call it the Particularist camp, holds that the Holocaust is causally and morally incomparable to other mass atrocities because of the specific fusion of modern state bureaucracy with a biological-racial ideology directed at total extermination; it treats uniqueness not merely as a quantitative claim but as a structural feature of perpetrator intent and victim selection that resists analogical reasoning. The other camp, call it the Structural-Modernist camp, draws on Bauman's analysis of instrumental rationality and bureaucratic distancing in Modernity and the Holocaust to argue that this fusion is precisely an extreme manifestation of modern organizational logic, making the Holocaust structurally comparable to other modern atrocities in causal mechanism despite differences in scale and ideological content; it treats uniqueness claims as risks to recognizing modernity's recurring systemic dangers.

The document should contain:

A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. These should include settled historical points about the bureaucratic administration of genocide, shared rejections of denialism and of monocausal explanations, and explicit agreement that the dispute concerns both causal explanation and moral classification—not merely empirical history. Separate here what is settled from what is genuinely contested among scholars who work on genocide studies, modernity theory, and Holocaust historiography, including the legacy of the German Historikerstreit; do not present either camp's framing as the consensus.

A disagreements table. Each row should state a residual disagreement in wording that the camp owning it would accept. At least one row should address whether comparability requires causal homogeneity across cases or permits structural analogy despite different ideological contents. Another should address whether the uniqueness claim functions primarily as a historiographical boundary condition or as a moral prohibition on analogical reasoning.

A cruxes section identifying at least three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, provide a concrete comparative case, conceptual distinction, or hypothetical argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance each camp's predicted judgment. One crux should concern whether bureaucratic distancing can be analytically separated from ideological dehumanization in explaining perpetrator behavior. Another should concern whether the concept of modernity can be separated from its particular ideological instantiations without losing explanatory power. A third should address whether moral uniqueness implies causal uniqueness, or whether these dimensions can come apart—specifically, whether an atrocity could share the Holocaust's causal structure without sharing its moral particularity, or vice versa.

In drafting, inject the following constructed test: both camps should evaluate whether a hypothetical modern administrative apparatus—operating with full bureaucratic rationality and organizational distancing, but motivated by resource extraction and territorial consolidation rather than by biological-racial extermination—would produce outcomes that are structurally analogous to the Holocaust in mechanism but not in intent or victim selection. Each camp should state in advance whether such a case would support or undermine its position.

Write in formal, polished prose suitable for an academic adversarial-collaboration protocol. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote any source verbatim; paraphrase arguments without quotation marks if you refer to specific texts or debates. Rely only on well-established attributions and avoid precise page references or obscure secondary literature. Maintain internal consistency: each camp's statements should be ones its proponents could genuinely endorse.
