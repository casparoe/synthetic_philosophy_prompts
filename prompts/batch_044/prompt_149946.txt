I'm trying to determine what I actually think about whether revolutions necessarily devour their own architects, and which explanatory framework—class struggle, ideological purification, or contingent leadership failure—best accounts for it when they do, by testing my intuitions against concrete episodes from the French Revolution. Here are my verdicts. The expulsion and execution of the leading Girondins in 1793 feels like a paradigmatic case of a revolution consuming its children: the explanation seems overdetermined, combining sans-culotte economic pressure with an ideological purity spiral around federalism and republican virtue, and the outcome feels structurally inevitable in retrospect, as though the revolutionary machinery had no choice. The execution of Robespierre and Saint-Just during the Thermidorian Reaction of 1794 also strikes me as autocannibalism—the executioner executed—yet here the explanation feels far more contingent; had Saint-Just been more politically agile, or had the Committee of Public Safety not overreached with the Law of 22 Prairial, the center might have held, which pulls against the inevitability I assigned to the Girondin case. The successive suppressions of the Hébertists in March 1794 and the Indulgents (Dantonists) in April 1794 present a harder pair: both were revolutionary insiders destroyed by the revolutionary center, yet the Hébertists were destroyed for being too extreme in their terror and atheism, while the Indulgents were destroyed for being too moderate in demanding an end to terror. I am inclined to say both are instances of the same mechanism—elimination of anyone who threatens the center's monopoly on violence—but then I struggle to explain why the Thermidorean Reaction itself, which destroyed the Jacobin center from the outside, does not count as the same mechanism operating on the center, since it too removed an insider group by force.

Further, Napoleon's coup of 18 Brumaire and the dissolution of the Directory do not feel like devouring children to me: the Directory had already abandoned revolutionary radicalism, and Bonaparte acted as an external military force rather than an internal faction, so this looks like conquest or decay rather than autocannibalism. At the same time, a counterfactual keeps nagging at me: if Danton had survived the April 1794 purge and successfully moderated the Terror, I think he might have saved the Revolution from itself rather than being consumed by it, which suggests that survival was genuinely possible and that the pattern is not strictly necessary. To help me think about this, I am experimenting with a distinction between structural autocannibalism—where the revolutionary apparatus mechanically eliminates successive factions regardless of their ideological position—and agentive purge, where specific leaders make contingent strategic choices to destroy rivals; but I am unsure whether this distinction survives scrutiny, since both the Girondin and Hébertist purges involved specific committee votes, yet both feel driven by deeper structural pressures.
