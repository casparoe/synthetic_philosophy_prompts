You direct acquisitions for a mid-size museum and regularly receive unprovenanced assemblages from a single region with no excavation notes or ethnographic parallels. You need a reusable decision procedure—grounded in the philosophy of archaeological inference, specifically the distinction between indexical traces (functional wear and deposition patterns), symbolic encoding (manufactured form intended to represent), and institutional regularities (cross-object constraints implying enforced norms)—to determine whether a given collection of artifacts justifies attributing a specific belief or institutional practice to its makers. The procedure must be runnable with only physical properties: list the ordered checks (e.g., consistency of wear versus form, anomaly detection against a utilitarian baseline, cross-item standardization or constraint), specify what counts as passing each, identify the exact point where the chain breaks and yields “no warranted inference,” and flag the steps that remain value-laden judgments rather than factual tests. Apply it to three cases: (i) deliberately shattered vessels deposited in a pit with no domestic residue; (ii) identical standardized weights distributed across separate structures; and (iii) a cluster of miniature, non-functional tools in a burial context—where the procedure yields an institutional inference (e.g., age-graded apprenticeship or hierarchical labor) that conflicts with a common preference for non-hierarchical interpretation. For each, show where the procedure stops, where it forces a decision, and where it must defer to human judgment. Finally, state what would change your mind about the procedure’s reliability: identify the specific argument or evidence that would make you abandon or reorder the indexical/symbolic/institutional framework, and note precisely where the reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
