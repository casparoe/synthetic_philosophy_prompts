hey, retired experimental physicist here who started reading philosophy after I left the lab. I'm sketching an essay on Gödel's theorems being dragged into science debates where they don't belong, but I want to avoid the usual Penrose-bashing that everyone has already seen a million times.

my rough notes:

1. the common mistake is thinking Gödel proves there will always be physical facts that science can never reach. I think that's wrong, but I want to push a more specific point: physics doesn't run on one fixed formal system like Peano arithmetic. we have seperate formalisms — Hamiltonian mechanics, path integrals, Lagrangian fields — that make the same predictions but rest on genuinely different mathematical structures. I'm calling this "formalism fungibility." so even if one symbolic framework is Gödel-incomplete, we don't hit an unprovable fact about nature; we just switch machinery. the incompleteness sticks to the notation, not to the world.

2. people also miss the consistency precondition. Gödel's second theorem needs the system to be able to prove its own consistency, which requires omega-consistency. but scientific theories aren't in the business of self-certification; they're in the business of being falsified and patched. so the conditions for the theorem don't map onto how physics actually works.

3. my concern: am I just defining "science" narrowly to protect it? maybe a final Theory of Everything really would need one canonical formalization, and then Gödel could bite. but I still think the bite is logical, not empirical — it wouldn't hide a physical law from us, it would just mean that particular formal system can't derive every consequence of its own rules.

4. I want to contrast this with Duhem-Quine underdetermination, which is empirical, not logical. but I'm not sure I've drawn the line cleanly.

can you comment on whether this holds together? specifically:
- is "formalism fungibility" a real distinction or just a dodge?
- am I right that the consistency conditions don't apply, or am I mixing up technical terms?
- does the argument about a ToE being formally incomplete but empirically open actually make sense?
- is there an obvious philosophical objection I'm missing? (not asking for a reading list — just want to know if I'm reinventing a wheel and should recieve a different framing)
