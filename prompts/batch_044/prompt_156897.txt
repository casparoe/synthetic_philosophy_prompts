I have been reading some introductory material on epistemology for a course I am following, and recently encountered a summary of Edmund Gettier's well-known challenge to the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge in what I believe was an online philosophy digest. The author explains that Gettier showed, through carefully constructed counterexamples, that a person can hold a belief that is genuinely justified and actually true, yet still lack knowledge, because the justification traces back to a false premise that happens to lead, by accident, to a true conclusion. The summary further claims that Gettier himself proposed a direct remedy—adding a 'no false lemmas' condition that forbids any false premises in the chain of reasoning—and asserts that this amendment fully restores the traditional definition without any need for further modal constraints such as sensitivity or safety. It concludes that Gettier's cases reveal knowledge must be free from a particular kind of epistemic luck, but that once false premises are eliminated, all accidental truth is accounted for and the problem is solved.

Something about this account feels suspiciously neat to me. In particular, I am doubtful that Gettier intended to offer a definitive repair rather than to pose an open problem, and I wonder whether ruling out false premises is genuinely sufficient given what I have heard about later debates involving modal conditions and virtue-theoretic responses. Could you please audit this summary with care?
