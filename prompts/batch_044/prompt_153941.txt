Present an argument that seems to prove too much and ask where it goes wrong.

My study partner constructed what she calls the Centered Modesty Paradox, and I think it dissolves the inside/outside distinction if it holds. The steps are:

(1) Epistemic modesty demands that whenever I hold a belief B, if there exists an outside-view explanation of my holding B that is causally independent of B's truth, I ought to reduce my confidence in B.

(2) This demand applies reflexively: let M be the principle stated in (1).

(3) From the outside view, my acceptance of M is explained by my philosophical education, a cognitive bias toward internal consistency, and evolutionary advantages of cooperative reasoning—conditions that obtain whether M is true or false.

(4) So by applying (1) to M itself, I ought to reduce my confidence in M.

(5) But reducing confidence in M undermines the very reason I have for applying (1) to any other belief.

(6) One might try to block this by restricting (1) to first-order beliefs, excluding normative principles. Call that restriction R.

(7) Yet R is itself a normative principle, and the outside view explains my acceptance of R just as well: I endorse R precisely because it protects my epistemic framework.

(8) Applying (1) to R therefore returns me to (4).

(9) So modesty either collapses into self-defeat or requires an arbitrary, self-serving exemption that violates (1)'s universality.

She concludes that inside-view reasoning can never be genuinely constrained by outside-view considerations. I am inclined to keep (1) and (9)—there does seem to be a tension—but I cannot locate the flaw. Which step should be rejected, and what does that rejection cost? Is there an equivocation between applying a normative principle and applying it to its own meta-level application, or a scope shift between "belief" and "principle"? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
