As part of my coursework in a seminar on rational agency and sequential decision theory, I have been working through the absent-minded driver problem and its usual framing in terms of plan-optimal versus act-optimal reasoning. I understand why the driver who plans to continue at the first exit and then take the second seems to face a dynamic consistency failure, but I want to isolate a conceptual distinction that the standard presentations tend to conflate. Specifically, I am trying to separate indexical indistinguishability—the agent's inability, at each node, to self-locate within the sequence—from what I would call causal opacity, which is the structural inability of present reasoning to influence or observe the causal pathway that produced the current node, regardless of whether indexical information is available. In the canonical formulation these coincide: the driver cannot tell whether he is at the first or second exit, and he also cannot recall or observe his prior passage. But I am interested in whether they come apart conceptually. Suppose we modify the case so that the driver possesses perfect indexical information—perhaps via an internal counter that reveals exactly which exit he is approaching—yet he still suffers from causal opacity in the sense that his present deliberation cannot alter or verify the prior execution of his plan. Does this modified case still generate the same tension between planning and execution? More generally, please explain whether the paradox is fundamentally driven by an epistemic gap about one's position in the sequence, or by a deeper structural feature of diachronic agency in which reasoning at a later node is causally disconnected from earlier nodes. I would like the explanation to distinguish these two failure modes precisely, to show how each interacts with the planning-versus-execution dilemma, and to clarify whether resolving indexical indistinguishability without resolving causal opacity actually dissolves the problem or merely relocates it.
