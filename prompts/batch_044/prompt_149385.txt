Read Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde through the lens of split-brain research and the philosophical problem of the unity of consciousness. In clinical split-brain cases, severing the corpus callosum can yield two hemispheres with apparently separate streams of awareness: the left hemisphere often verbally reports reasons for actions actually initiated by the right, constructing confabulatory narratives to preserve a sense of unified agency. Apply this structure to Stevenson's novel. Is Hyde best understood as a second stream of consciousness—analogous to a right-hemisphere self—that Jekyll's dominant narrative eventually rationalizes, or does the chemical transformation represent something closer to a total replacement of one unified consciousness by another? Focus on the transformation scenes and on Jekyll's final written account of his progressive loss of control. Where does the clinical analogy illuminate the horror of the story—say, in moments where Jekyll seems to observe Hyde's actions with detached curiosity? And where does it break down—particularly since Hyde retains access to Jekyll's memories and skills, which split hemispheres do not share? I want a reading that takes both the novel's narrative architecture and the philosophical debate about one mind versus two seriously: does the novel dramatize a failure of phenomenal unity, a failure of access unity, or a failure of the narrative mechanisms that normally bind them together? End by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical discussion that a purely clinical or theoretical argument about split-brain patients might miss.
