I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am drafting a short essay for a general-audience technology publication that uses a distributed-systems problem to probe Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles through Max Black's symmetric-universe thought experiment involving two identical spheres.

Below are the core notes and draft arguments on which I want targeted feedback.

First, I outline Black's scenario as I understand it: a universe containing nothing except two perfect spheres of identical size, material, and temperature, separated by some distance. Because every intrinsic property is shared and the only differences are relational—each sphere stands in a different spatial relation to the other—Black takes this to challenge the principle that no two numerically distinct things can share all properties.

Then I introduce the analog I want to build: a container-orchestration cluster running two pods that are bit-for-bit identical in image, configuration, and runtime state, with no persistent storage, no assigned host identity, and no network connections except to each other through a service mesh. From the inside, an observer with access only to internal metrics sees two replicas with identical CPU, memory, and log streams. I ask whether this is genuinely a case of two distinct entities, or whether our engineering practice of assigning each a unique UID and scheduling them on separate nodes is doing the same work as Black's spatial separation—creating distinction through an external relational framework rather than through any intrinsic feature.

I then consider three objections I anticipate and want to check for coherence.

One: in computing, the distinction is purely functional, not metaphysical. We label replicas separately because we need failover capacity, not because we have discovered an intrinsic property. I want to respond that this makes the engineering case closer to Leibniz than it appears: if the only reason to treat them as two is their potential for divergent future states or their occupation of different nodes, then their present numerical identity is genuinely relational.

Two: quantum mechanics might allow true physical duplicates that computing cannot achieve, since digital replication is perfect copying. I want to argue that this actually strengthens the analog: if perfect copying is possible in software but not in nature, then software gives us a cleaner laboratory for testing whether perfect qualitative identity forces numerical identity.

Three: Black's spheres rely on absolute space, which modern physics rejects. I want to concede this but note that in a distributed system, location is itself a relational property defined by network topology rather than by absolute coordinates, so the analog survives even in a relationalist framework.

My request for comments is in two parts. First, accuracy: do I misrepresent Black's argument or Leibniz's principle in any obvious way? I am not asking for a bibliography or literature review—only whether the philosophical framing is defensible enough that I am not importing a basic confusion in front of readers who know the debate. Second, interest and clarity: is the computing analog genuinely illuminating, or does it feel like a forced metaphor? In particular, I am unsure whether the third objection about relational space is handled correctly, and whether I should drop the quantum-mechanics comparison altogether because it introduces confusion without payoff. I would also welcome suggestions on how to structure the conclusion: should I take a side on whether the pods are really one or two, or is it stronger to leave the tension unresolved and ask the reader what engineering practice implies about metaphysical commitment?

Please be direct: if I am conflating epistemic indistinguishability with metaphysical identity, tell me precisely where that happens and how to fix it without losing the accessibility of the piece.
