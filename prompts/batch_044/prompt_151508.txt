I'm working through the heterogeneity problem in a course on well-being, and I've become committed to a specific defense of hedonism that I'd like stress-tested. The view—let me call it structural-integration hedonism—denies that pleasures share any uniform phenomenal quality or common currency of feeling, which is the usual response to the heterogeneity objection. Instead, it holds that what unifies pleasures is a structural relation in conscious experience: a representational state directed at some object or condition is coupled with a non-inferential, first-order endorsement of that state as correct or fitting, producing a mode of engagement I will call affective resonance. This allows gustatory enjoyment, the satisfaction of solving a proof, aesthetic absorption, and even the relief of escaping danger to count as pleasures despite their radical phenomenological differences, because in each case the same integration-structure obtains—only the representational content and the modality of integration (sensory, cognitive, narrative, proprioceptive) vary. The view strikes me as avoiding both the reduction of pleasure to a bare pro-attitude and the implausible claim that all pleasures feel alike, but I want to know precisely what would have to be true for it to fail.
