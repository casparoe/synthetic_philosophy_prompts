I'm putting together a 90-minute workshop for about 16 grad students in political philosophy. I want them to physically feel the tension in price controls — not just the Econ 101 shortage story, but this weirder thing: when you set a rent cap or wage floor, you don't just misprice goods, you restructure who has moral standing to object. The people priced out aren't victims of exploitation; they're just not in the pool, so defenders can say they dont have a claim. Call this the standing-shift problem, or whatever — I don't care about the name, I care that they discover it through rules, not lecture.

Design a role-play or card-based simulation with hidden roles and small stakes (candy or points). Participants should be landlords, tenants, workers, employers, and a regulator with limited slots. Under price-control rules they have to allocate apartments and jobs, but the rules should make it impossible to serve everyone, and the debrief should show them that the ethical defense of the cap depends on treating the served group as the full set of claimants, which the cap itself undermines it's own justification. Give me a timeline, the hidden payoff structure, and exactly where in the debrief to point out that the participants just recreated the standing-shift without knowing it. Also tell me honestly what feature of this argument the game can't capture — maybe the intergenerational or long-run effects — and how I'd have to fill that gap verbally. No reading lists, please. Make sure they recieve their role cards face-down.
