I'm a phd student in political philosophy, currently TAing for an undergrad course on ideology and institutional change, and I was discussing this with my friends over coffee after a long seminar on Burke. We kept circling back to Chesterton's fence — the idea that you shouldn't tear down a fence unless you know why it was put up — and I realized there's a specific argumentative loop I see all the time that I don't have a word for, and it's not quite the same thing.

The pattern goes like this. Someone defends an old rule or practice — tenure clocks, zoning codes, the way grant committees require a physical signature, whatever — by saying its purpose is partly hidden and evolved over generations, so reformers shouldn't mess with it. Then someone actually does the historical legwork and shows the original reason is dead: the technology changed, the social problem dissolved, the law was copied from a different country by mistake. Instead of conceding the fence can go, the defender pivots and claims the institution must have developed a new hidden function, maybe social cohesion or some subtle coordination benefit, and that this new purpose is just as opaque as the old one. If you press them on that, they retreat to "well, we can't prove it doesn't do something important," which puts the burden back on the reformer. It's like every time you scrape the fence you find another seperate layer of paint, and each layer is treated as wisdom rather than decay. I've seen it with academic bureaucracy, with old voting procedures, even with teh way some libraries organize their stacks.

Does this loop have an established name in conservative theory or in the philosophy of science? I know it's in the neighborhood of immunization strategies and maybe epistemic humility, but it feels distinct because the claim isn't being patched with one precise auxiliary — it's being replaced by a vaguer, unfalsifiable version of itself every time the history is checked. If there isn't a name, how should I carve it out: what's the exact boundary between this and ordinary caution about unintended consequences, and what should I call the move where a hidden-purpose defense regenerates a new hidden purpose after the first one is debunked? I don't need a bibliography, just whether the concept exists and where its edges are.
