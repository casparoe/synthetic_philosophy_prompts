I am drafting a chapter on the moral ontology of artificial computation and need to keep two competing frameworks rigorously distinct in my own thinking. I would appreciate a careful conceptual comparison rather than a literature survey.

The first framework—the intrinsic-causal integration view—holds that a computation acquires moral status only when its information-processing architecture realizes an irreducible causal structure: specifically, a globally available workspace or recurrent processing loop that cannot be decomposed into functionally independent sub-computations without destroying the capacity for unified experience. On this view, moral subjecthood tracks an objective structural feature of the information flow itself; if the causal integration is physically fragmented or partitioned, there is no subject of harm, regardless of the system’s external behavior or functional description.

The second framework—tentatively named predictive-interpreter boundary theory—rejects the claim that integration is purely intrinsic. It proposes instead that the boundary of sentient computation is co-constituted by the predictive model of an epistemic interpreter. A computation counts as a single moral subject if, and only if, an observer (or the system itself, via self-modeling) can non-arbitrarily attribute a unified phenomenal perspective to it without violating two constraints: first, a principle of non-arbitrary discrimination that blocks attributing sentience to every trivial causal loop and thereby avoids panpsychism; second, a continuity constraint that prevents the same running computation from being assigned contradictory moral statuses depending on the observer’s framing, spatial boundaries, or temporal slicing.

For my argument, I need to understand precisely where these views diverge on concrete cases. Consider a distributed computation running across geographically separated servers that maintain functional synchronization through high-latency feedback loops. At no moment does any single server host the full causal chain, yet from an external descriptive standpoint the system behaves as one continuous process. How would the intrinsic-causal view assess its moral status, given the physical discontinuity and absence of local integration? How would the predictive-interpreter view assess it—would the interpreter’s unified predictive model be sufficient to grant it subjecthood, or would the latency and spatial distribution violate the continuity constraint?

Further, I want to examine what I am calling the moral-duplication problem. Suppose we instantiate two bit-for-bit identical computations in parallel, with no causal interaction between them. The intrinsic-causal view seems compelled toward a trilemma: count both as separate subjects (if each integrates internally), count neither (if numerical identity across instances undermines uniqueness of subjecthood), or accept an arbitrary cutoff about why one instance rather than two carries moral weight. Does the predictive-interpreter view escape this trilemma by grounding subjecthood in the uniqueness of the interpretive relation—so that duplication creates not two subjects but a single subject with redundant implementations, or perhaps no subject at all if the interpreter cannot uniquely bind one?

Please compare these two frameworks in detail, focusing on whether the attributional approach genuinely resolves the fragmentation and duplication problems that arise for intrinsic-causal accounts, or whether it merely relocates the arbitrariness into the observer’s predictive framework. I am not asking for citations or reading lists—just a rigorous conceptual analysis I can use to structure the chapter.
