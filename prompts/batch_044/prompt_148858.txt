I'm writing a feature on conceptual fault lines in contemporary ethics—specifically, on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and I'm dissatisfied with the usual classroom framing that treats the question as a simple yes-or-no about conflicting obligations. I need a dialogue that moves deeper into the conceptual architecture.

Write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers—call them Mara and Leo—who disagree about whether irreducible moral conflict can exist not merely between obligations, but within the constitutive aims of a single institutional role.

The conversation should center on a concrete, somewhat unusual case: a municipal archivist who discovers that a sealed public record, if preserved intact according to the institutional purpose of the archive, will foreseeably cause serious harm to a vulnerable community; if the record is redacted or destroyed, the archivist violates not an external rule but the very practice that makes the archival role intelligible. Mara should defend the view that this is a structural dilemma: the demands are genuinely incommensurable rather than merely competing weights, and the unavoidable moral remainder—the wrong committed either way—is not mere emotional residue but evidence of an unresolvable normative fracture. Leo should deny this not by dismissing the archivist's distress, but by arguing that apparent irreducibility stems from an under-described hierarchy—perhaps the role's constitutive aim already contains a lexicographic ordering, or the harm and preservation can be reconceptualized so they are not truly opposed—and that moral remainders are fully compatible with there having been a uniquely correct action, however tragic.

The exchange must be genuine back-and-forth. Leo should directly challenge Mara's appeal to incommensurability and constitutive aims; Mara should reply with a specific conceptual distinction, for instance that some values function not as weights but as conditions of intelligibility for the practice itself, so that ordering them dissolves the practice rather than resolving the conflict. Neither speaker should be a strawman. The tone should be formal and rigorous, appropriate for readers of a serious science-and-ideas publication.

Conclude the dialogue with a brief, balanced closing exchange that does not force an artificial resolution. Then provide exactly one final line, formatted as: Verdict: [a single sentence summarizing where the disagreement actually stands and what remains unresolved].
