I am drafting the opening address for a workshop entitled "Recognition and Its Witnesses," convened jointly by my philosophy department and a regional labor federation. The audience is divided evenly: half are doctoral students in nineteenth-century German philosophy who will catch every conceptual allusion to the master-slave dialectic, and half are union stewards and workplace organizers with no formal philosophical training. I need a fifteen-minute speech that makes Hegel's account of recognition structurally indispensable—not decorative—to our discussion of algorithmic management and peer-to-peer evaluation. Specifically, the argument must turn on a triadic rather than dyadic framing: the struggle between two consciousnesses is always already mediated by a "third"—whether an institutional archive, an algorithmic scoring system, or a collective observer—that transforms the dialectic from a private duel into a triangular relation. This framing should determine what the speaker can and cannot claim; for instance, mutual recognition may not be presented as a private achievement between two parties, and any call to solidarity must account for the mediating witness rather than bypass it. The speech should be rigorous enough that the philosophers feel genuine conceptual pressure, yet accessible enough that the organizers leave with a shared problem rather than a lecture. It must not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Write it in a formal register appropriate to an academic occasion, with the philosophical framework doing real rhetorical work—if one removed the triadic Hegelian structure, the address should no longer hold together.
