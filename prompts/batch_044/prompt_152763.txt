I'm delivering the opening remarks tomorrow for a two-day workshop, "Anatta and Institutional Memory," co-hosted by our philosophy department and the Buddhist studies center. The audience is split roughly evenly between analytic philosophers of personal identity and scholars of Theravada Abhidhamma, with some graduate students and administrators present. I want the remarks—about five minutes—to do something structurally awkward: welcome the participants, justify why we are gathering, and frame the workshop's theme of what persists when an institution changes, but do all of this without presupposing that there is a stable workshop, a continuous department, or enduring individual participants across the two days. From an anatta standpoint, only momentary dhammas exist, and conventional entities are merely sammuti-sacca. At the same time, I cannot simply deny the framework and dismiss the event, because that would defeat the practical purpose of convening. Please write remarks that navigate this tension by drawing on the Abhidhamma distinction between citta-vithi (the stream of consciousness-moments) and the pudgala-vada (the personalist view that treats the person as a quasi-substantial entity across time), using institutional continuity as an analogy for personal continuity: conventionally indispensable, ultimately empty of substance. The philosophers should hear a serious engagement with four-dimensionalist and narrative-identity debates; the Buddhist scholars should see that anatta is not being reduced to a Western bundle theory. Please separate what is settled Buddhist doctrine—the denial of atman, the two-truths framework—from what is genuinely contested among specialists, such as whether anatta is compatible with any narrative or relational account of identity and whether the pudgala-vada was a live philosophical alternative rather than a doctrinal strawman. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; these are spoken remarks, not a scholarly paper.
