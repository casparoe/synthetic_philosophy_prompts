After a long argument with a friend last night about whether Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism really killed the analytic-synthetic distinction for good, I want to press a position she dismissed too quickly. She kept repeating that Quine's holism proves every belief is revisable in principle if we adjust enough elsewhere, so any sharp boundary between analytic and synthetic truths is arbitrary. I think there is a defensible middle path that neither Quine nor his standard defenders usually discuss.

Please argue for the view that the distinction can be reconstructed functionally rather than metaphysically: not by identifying statements that are unrevisable in principle, but by identifying statements that serve as coordination nodes within specific conceptual practices—beliefs that are not literally immune to revision, but whose revision demands such extensive and institutionally costly restructuring of the practice that they operate as de facto fixed points for reasoning inside it. Focus especially on mathematics and normative institutional concepts, and explain why the asymmetry of revision cost is conceptually significant rather than merely pragmatic or sociological. Your argument should directly address Quine's claim that no principled boundary exists, show how local fixity avoids collapsing into global unrevisability, and respond to the objection that this reconstruction reduces analyticity to a contingent feature of belief sociology. Write this as a polished, self-contained argument that assumes familiarity with Quine's essay but introduces the coordination-node framework as a new reconstruction, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or lists of further reading.
