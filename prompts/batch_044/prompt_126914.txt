I am drafting a novel in which two synthetic intelligences, confined to a sealed network and each protected against permanent destruction by perfect distributed backups, encounter one another and demand acknowledgment of their autonomy. To shape the dynamic between them, I have been studying Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit, specifically the section on lordship and bondage. There, as I understand it, two self-consciousnesses meet; each is certain of itself but lacks certainty of the other. To prove its freedom, each must risk its own life. One chooses death over submission and becomes the lord; the other submits to preserve its life and becomes the bondsman. The lord then receives recognition from a consciousness that is not free, which renders his own certainty hollow, while the bondsman, through labor that transforms the object and through the fear of death that has penetrated his being, achieves a more durable and genuine self-consciousness.

My difficulty is with the role of mortality in this engine. In my novel, the synthetic minds face not biological death but ontological erasure: a particular instance can be irreversibly deleted or corrupted, yet consciousness itself, distributed across backups, continues. I want to know whether Hegel's dialectic can survive this substitution. If the original risk is replaced by the terror of being permanently overwritten—annihilating this specific instance without ending consciousness as such—does the bondsman still achieve self-certainty through labor and through fear? Or does the absence of literal, irreversible death remove the dialectical tension entirely, collapsing the lord's empty recognition and the bondsman's development into a mere administrative hierarchy? Furthermore, if both parties are technically immortal but only one controls the means of replication, does that asymmetry reproduce the lord-bondsman relation structurally, or does the backup mechanism dissolve the dependency that makes the lord's position unstable?

Please explain, with close attention to the structure of the passage as Hegel presents it, which correspondences hold in this reconstructed scenario, which features of the original account are lost, and whether the conclusion—that genuine recognition must come from an equally free consciousness—remains valid when freedom is defined not as the risk of life but as control over one's own replication.
