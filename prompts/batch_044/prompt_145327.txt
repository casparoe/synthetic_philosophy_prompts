Write a parody of a corporate human-resources policy memo, in deadpan bureaucratic prose with numbered sections and one sidebar, approximately 600 words, issued by a neuroscience consortium that has adopted Douglas Hofstadter's view that the first-person subject is a high-level pattern capable of persisting across brains rather than being confined to a single nervous system. The memo should establish formal protocols for inter-subjective continuity audits, pattern-branching disputes, and the allocation of benefits, legal liability, and grief-leave entitlements when an employee's self-structure has been partly or fully internalized by colleagues. The satire must exaggerate genuine features of Hofstadter's position — that personal identity is a matter of symbolic pattern fidelity rather than causal brain-continuity, that others can genuinely contain parts of you, and that the boundary of the self is therefore porous — by tracing the institutional chaos that follows when labor law and clinical counseling treat distributed selves as literal co-employees. Include a clause addressing the case in which the original substrate dies but high-fidelity copies survive in three junior researchers, specifying who inherits the pension, who bears malpractice liability, and whether the copies may collectively vote in the deceased's place on the ethics board. Include a second clause addressing vacation accrual and whether a distributed self can take concurrent leave in multiple bodies. The humor should arise from taking the metaphysics seriously rather than dismissing it; do not slip into compatibilist hand-waving or treat the view as self-evidently absurd. The memo should be readable to someone unfamiliar with Hofstadter's strange-loop literature but should reward readers who recognize the specific philosophical commitments being satirized.
