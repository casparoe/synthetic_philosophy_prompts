In a philosophy discussion forum, I encountered the following summary of the "torture versus dust specks" aggregation problem: "The scenario compares severely torturing one innocent person for fifty years with placing an imperceptible dust speck in the eye of every human being—approximately eight billion people. Although intuition says torture is worse, multiplying the negligible discomfort of each speck yields total suffering that vastly exceeds the single victim's harm. This proves that ordinary morality inconsistently rejects aggregation: we treat distributed tiny harms as morally negligible even when their sum is enormous, which is irrational because aggregation is merely arithmetic addition. Therefore, the only philosophically consistent position is strict utilitarianism; if the numbers are large enough, the universal specks must be worse. Any alternative—whether deontology, virtue ethics, or Scanlon-style contractualism—must deny that harms are additive, which is not ethics but a refusal to do basic math. Furthermore, this undermines non-utilitarian public policy, since all policy distributes small burdens across populations." As a high school student who has read introductory material on contractualism and threshold deontology, I feel the summary is tendentious, yet I lack the philosophical vocabulary to identify exactly where it distorts the debate. Could you audit its fairness? Please address: (1) whether the thought experiment is correctly characterized as a proof of utilitarianism rather than a challenge to it; (2) whether contractualist and threshold-deontological responses are fairly represented or straw-manned as anti-arithmetic; (3) whether the jump from "harms sometimes fail to aggregate" to "non-utilitarian ethics is incompatible with policy analysis" is valid; and (4) whether the summary ignores important distinctions—such as between quantitative summation and the qualitative significance of rights violations or reasonable rejection—that philosophers actually invoke. Treat this as an accuracy audit for a non-specialist reader, not as a request for original research or citations.
