I've been working through a tension in lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism that the usual rationalist and academic discussions tend to glide past. The standard view holds that reducing suffering takes strict lexical priority over increasing pleasure, but only up to a threshold—once suffering is eliminated or pushed below that boundary, pleasure regains independent moral weight. Most presentations seem to assume that pleasure and pain can be mapped onto a single cardinal scale, which makes the threshold location precise and intersubjectively meaningful. But I'm skeptical that either intrasubjective or intersubjective comparisons of pleasure and pain actually possess the cardinal structure this requires. At the same time, abandoning cardinal comparison entirely looks like it makes the threshold either arbitrary or impossible to locate at all.

So I'm wondering whether the lexical threshold can be reconstructed on purely structural grounds—using partial-order dominance relations rather than a unified metric—such that suffering dominates pleasure within defined regions, but comparison across the boundary is left undefined, non-transitive, or determined by structural features rather than by scalar distance.

Don't end with a non-committal survey—reach and defend a concrete verdict. Either show how a non-cardinal, structurally defined threshold can avoid arbitrariness and preserve the lexical intuition, or argue that the view collapses into either standard negative utilitarianism (with an unmotivated cutoff) or an implausible discontinuity in practical reasoning.
