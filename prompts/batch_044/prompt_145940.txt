I have noticed a recurring pattern in Twin Earth-style discussions that I do not have a name for. Consider two isolated communities living in physically identical environments — the same water, the same minerals, the same flora — whose members share exactly the same mental representations and behavioural dispositions when they apply a natural-kind term such as 'water' or 'gold'. The sole divergence lies in their institutional practices of expert deference: in one community, speakers ultimately defer to chemists; in the other, they defer to a distinct expert class — local metallurgists operating with a different theoretical framework — and this split remains stable across generations. Because the world and individual psychology are constant, there is no standard environmental divergence; yet it seems the reference of the term could still split, purely through the social architecture of authority. I can distinguish two sub-cases: one in which the two expert groups actually disagree about extension, and one in which they currently agree in practice but embed different explanatory principles, so that future empirical discoveries might pull the references apart. Does this specific phenomenon — reference divergence driven solely by differences in deference structure, with no Twin Earth-style world-change and no internal psychological difference — have an established label in the philosophy of language? How should it be distinguished from ordinary division-of-labour arguments, and if it is not separately treated, what term and boundary conditions would best capture it? Please answer in American English.
