I've been having this arguement with my mate about Schlick and his idea that life only means something when you're in 'play' mode, not when you're working toward a goal. We've both read the same essay — the one where he says youth is playful and adulthood is just labour — and we agree suffering is bad and people want meaning, but we've stalled for months.

She says Schlick is obviously wrong because people find deep meaning in raising kids or building a career; that's purposeful work, not play, and it's not just a means to an end. I say Schlick wouldn't deny that, but he'd argue those things become meaningful only when you stop treating them as projects and start being present in them, like a musician playing for the joy of it. She reckons that's just redefining 'play' to win the arguement. I think she's smuggling in a modern idea that purpose and presense can coexist, which Schlick might reject because he thinks any goal-orientation turns activity into mere intrumental labour.

But neither of us budges. Is this actually a hidden factual disagreement about whether people can experience purpose-driven work as intrinsically valuable? Or is it verbal — are we just using 'play' differently? Or are we answering different questions: she's asking what makes life meaningful for people, and I'm asking what Schlick thought meaning requires?

Also, I have a hunch the stall comes from missing that Schlick wrote this in 1927, right after the war, when he was reacting against people treating life like factory output. So maybe his point wasn't psychological but political — don't let social utility swallow individual experience. If that's right, are we even arguing about the same thing? What kind of disagreement is this really, and is there anything — a distinction, a study, or just admitting our intuitions about work are bedrock — that would actually move it?
