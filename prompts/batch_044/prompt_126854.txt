I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I am co-facilitating a 75-minute workshop for about 14 engineers and interaction designers. I want participants to experience—before any theoretical framing—how predicting a complex system's behavior forces a shift between explanatory frameworks that do not easily translate into one another. The philosophical idea I am working with is the distinction among the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, particularly the claim that the patterns we call real may depend on which stance succeeds predictively, and that in engineered systems these stances can come apart in ways that feel unstable.

Please design a runnable in-class simulation (not a lecture) that makes this tension visceral. The setting: 14 participants, 75 minutes, small stakes only (pooled points that determine a shared group decision, such as which feature mock-up gets implemented). The scenario should center on a hypothetical black-box module I will call a compressed editorial filter—a recommendation system trained via inverse reinforcement learning on historical human editorial judgments, documented only through its reward-shaping objective, neural architecture, and training distribution, with no explicit decision rules for individual cases.

Split participants into three teams with incompatible toolkits:
- The Physical team receives server logs, latency traces, and token-level probability distributions.
- The Design team receives the architecture specification and reward-function documentation.
- The Intentional team receives only observed input-output pairs and is instructed to generate predictions by attributing beliefs and goals (e.g., about credibility or engagement) to the system itself.

Structure four rounds of prediction with written commitments before results are revealed:
1. Early rounds where the Design team succeeds with high confidence and the other teams struggle.
2. A middle round where all three teams produce mutually incompatible predictions, none clearly wrong ex ante.
3. A round where the Physical team identifies a stochastic hardware-level glitch that explains an anomalous output, while the Intentional team predicts the same output correctly only by attributing a contradictory belief, and the Design team cannot adjudicate because the reward function is consistent with both explanations.
4. A final round where participants must vote as a group on which team's explanation to adopt for a mock deployment decision, with the payoff scheme rewarding predictive accuracy but penalizing failure to reach cross-stance consensus.

The rules should be arranged so that participants feel the frustration of incommensurability themselves—before the facilitator names the philosophical framework. The debrief should map each round onto the physical, design, and intentional stances; identify what will predictably fail the first time (e.g., the Physical team will be overwhelmed by noise, the Intentional team will over-attribute agency prematurely, and the group will conflate predictive success with ontological commitment); and tell me exactly how to recover without breaking the experiential momentum.

Be frank about which feature of the view—specifically, the claim that real patterns exist mind-independently regardless of predictive stance—this simulation necessarily cannot reproduce, and how I should supply it verbally. Also specify what participants should come away seeing: not just that we use different explanations, but that the choice of stance can feel like a forced move with real predictive consequences, and that maintaining the design stance sometimes requires denying patterns that the intentional stance captures effortlessly.

Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not name specific philosophers until after the debrief mapping, if at all. Avoid framing this around familiar large-language-model examples; the point is to inject the specific hypothetical of a reward-shaped editorial filter so the stance shift feels unfamiliar and visceral rather than pre-learned. Write the instructions formally and precisely, suitable for someone without philosophy training to execute the session.
