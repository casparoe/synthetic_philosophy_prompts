I've come round to a particular take on just war theory and the moral equality of combatants — not the standard Walzer line about soldiers being morally equal because they don't choose the war's cause, but a more specific version I'm calling 'institutional epistemic outsourcing.' The idea is that in functioning democracies, soldiers symmetrically outsource thier judgement about jus ad bellum to legitimate representative institutions, and this delegation creates a reciprocal moral shield that applies to both sides regardless of which state's institutions are actually correct. It feels like it saves the equality intuition without leaning on ignorance or coercion, but I'm not sure how exposed it really is.

So: what would count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked — from fatal to merely awkward — including empirical cases where soldiers clearly knew thier government was lying about the casus belli, formal arguments that moral liability can't be transferred via delegation like this, and asymmetrical corruption cases where one side's institutional process is so broken that the outsourcing fails for them but not for the enemy. Which of these would a committed defender concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as misapplying the framework? I'd also like an honest assessment of whether this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — if every counterexample can be explained away by saying the delegation wasn't 'genuine' enough — and whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind. Please write in British English, say how confident you are in the analysis, and single out the point you're least sure about.
