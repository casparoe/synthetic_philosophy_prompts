I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in philosophy of law and political ethics on how the legal and moral concept of genocide—rooted in Raphael Lemkin's original framework—should relate to the practical and rhetorical work of naming mass violence. I want a taxonomy structured as a decision tree that classifies positions not by the labels people adopt but by the concrete judgments they make about boundary cases and conceptual roles.

Begin with a root node asking whether the legal definition of genocide—centered on specific intent to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part—should be treated as conceptually fixed, open to expansion, or subordinate to moral and political considerations.

From there, construct branches split by answerable diagnostic questions, each phrased so that a reader can respond without knowing which philosophical position it points toward:

1. Does the core destructive act include the systematic erasure of a group's archives, languages, or demographic records—what might be called epistemic destruction—or is such erasure merely a cover-up mechanism with no independent weight in the concept?
2. Can cultural destruction without physical killing satisfy the concept, or does the concept require biological or physical destruction?
3. Should the protected-group category include politically defined groups, given that Lemkin originally sought their inclusion but the adopted Convention excluded them?
4. In cases where there is no documentary proof of explicit intent but the structural pattern of violence makes group destruction the only plausible explanation, should the event be named genocide?
5. Is applying the term primarily a legal determination, a moral judgment identifying the worst kind of wrong, or a strategic political speech act intended to trigger specific obligations?

The tree should have seven or eight leaves. Include at least: a strict legalist/conventionalist position; a Lemkin-restorationist position that restores both political and cultural groups; a moral scalarist who treats genocide as the extreme end of a spectrum rather than a threshold; a political nominalist for whom the label is purely strategic; and at least one rarely occupied combination—such as accepting the inclusion of political groups while denying that cultural destruction counts, paired with viewing the label as purely legal—which you should diagnose briefly (is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?). Flag explicitly any pair of leaves that your branch logic cannot genuinely separate.

Keep the tree conceptually self-contained: no bibliography, no citation requests, and no dependence on specific texts the reader would need to look up. Frame everything so that the sorting depends on judgments about cases and conceptual roles rather than on familiarity with named theorists.
