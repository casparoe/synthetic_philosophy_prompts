Write a short story about a society in which a disciplined contemplative tradition—not neurological damage—produces a cultivated, stable form of pain asymbolia: practitioners register nociceptive signals with complete sensory clarity but without any aversive affect or motivational urgency. Center the narrative on an elder instructor asked to initiate a child into the practice, and use their interaction to dramatize a precise conceptual tension. If pain decomposes into a sensory-discriminative component and an affective-motivational component, and if the latter can be systematically trained away without diminishing the former, then in what sense, if any, does the remaining experience retain the quality of being bad? Let the story present at least two competing interpretations—for instance, that the badness was always an artifact of resistance rather than the signal itself, versus that removing the resistance also dissolves the moral urgency that gives pain its significance—without resolving the dispute. The story should not rely on clinical case histories, bibliographic references, or citations; the philosophical weight should come from the situation and dialogue alone.
