Below is a draft essay for a philosophy seminar on material constitution. I have tried to stay close to the classic statue-and-clay puzzle but have introduced a nonstandard framing—treating the clay as composed of discrete "functional modules" whose interrelations, rather than mere material continuity, might govern persistence conditions.

The classic case asks how a statue formed from a lump of clay can be destroyed by squashing while the clay survives. The usual responses either deny that they differ in persistence conditions or adopt a constitution relation: the clay constitutes the statue without being identical to it. I want to complicate this by imagining that the clay is not homogeneous but made up of structural modules, each playing a specific role in maintaining the statue's shape—analogous, in a loose way, to how tissues maintain an organism's form. Now suppose we replace these modules one by one with synthetically identical material. The macro-form remains intact, but the original modular architecture is gradually lost.

This raises a question the standard debate usually suppresses: is the clay's identity tied to its particular material origin, or to the functional profile of its parts? If the latter, then squashing the statue might destroy both entities not because the form is lost, but because the functional profile is disrupted. Conversely, gradual module replacement might preserve the statue—whose identity tracks functional continuity—while destroying the original clay, whose identity tracks the original modular assembly. The puzzle then splits along two axes: material continuity versus functional integration.

If this is right, the debate between constitution theorists and mereological essentialists may be misframed. Rather than asking whether there are one or two entities, we should ask which axis governs artifacts versus natural kinds, and whether importing functional-talk into artifacts illegitimately smuggles in teleological commitments that the original clay case was meant to avoid.

Please comment on the draft with particular attention to three things. First, accuracy: does the modular-functional framing genuinely diverge from existing constitution theories, or does it collapse back into them once the details are filled in? Second, coherence: is the analogy between structural modules and biological organs legitimate for artifacts, or does it import commitments that make the argument circular? Third, interest: does splitting the puzzle along material-continuity versus functional-integration axes illuminate the statue-and-clay case, or does it simply relocate the ambiguity without resolving anything? If there is a hidden premise about identity conditions that the argument depends on but does not defend, please identify it. I am not looking for bibliographic suggestions or a reading list—just an internal audit of the reasoning.
