I'm the audit lead for our city's behavorial-policy unit — every week I get proposals for nudges like opt-out organ donation, calorie-label mandates, retirement savings defaults, that kind of thing. Right now I'm just winging it with a gut check about autonomy, but I need something reusable my team can actually run in about fifteen minutes before I sign off. I sketched out a framework with a colleague that splits interventions into two kinds: Type A changes the choice environment — reorder options, change defaults, keep the full menu technically available — while Type B shifts how preferences themselves get formed or weighted, like framing that makes a value feel illegitimate, hiding options behind social stigma, or using emotional priming so people want different things than they did before. The core idea is that Type B needs stricter justification because it doesn't just steer revealed preferences; it risks constructing them, and that asymmetry should drive the audit.

Please turn this into a step-by-step decision procedure: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the flow stops with a hard no, and where it stops and says "this is now a values call, escalate to the ethics board." Apply it to three proposals: (1) an opt-out pension enrollment with a 3% employer match, (2) a school-cafeteria redesign that puts junk food behind a counter with a five-minute wait but technically allows it, and (3) a smoking-cessation poster campaign that frames continued smoking as "letting your family down" — I actually like this one, but I want the procedure to either reject it or escalate it in a way that annoys me. Make sure you flag where the Type A / Type B line gets blurry — for instance when a default also sends a social signal — and be explicit about what the procedure flat-out can't settle. Don't ask me to cite philosophers or build a bibliography; I just need the checklist, the worked cases, and an honest note about where judgment can't be eliminated.
