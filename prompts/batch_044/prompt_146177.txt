hey, grading a take-home for phil of mind. the question was: "Predictive processing models say perception works by minimizing prediction error using prior expectations. Does that automatically make cognitive penetration inevitable? Apply this to the sommelier case—she's told the barrel is older and then reports the wine tastes oakier. Is this genuine penetration of gustatory experience, or just learned tuning inside a perceptual module? What's at stake for Fodor-style encapsulation?"

here's the student answer (i'm paraphrasing their main moves):

Predictive processing shows that high-level beliefs feed back into sensory processing through prediction errors. So when the sommelier believes the barrel is older, that prior gets folded into the gustatory prediction, changing the error signal and therefore the actual phenominal taste experience. That counts as cognitive penetration because the belief alters what it's like, not just the judgment afterwards. Fodor said perceptual modules are informationally encapsulated from beliefs, but predictive processing proves encapsulation fails even for taste and smell. Still, ordinary learning stays inside the module—penetration only happens when the belief is about something the receptors can't detect, like barrel age, so the influence must come from outside. That means here we have real penetration. The big upshot is that if belief can always do this, then perceptual knowledge is basically unreliable because experience is constantly contaminated.
