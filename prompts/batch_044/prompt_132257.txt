I'm writing a feature for a general-interest publication on how 19th-century religious philosophy continues to shape contemporary debates about moral exceptionality. I'm working through Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, specifically the section on Abraham's binding of Isaac and the distinction between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith. The passage describes Abraham making the movement of infinity—resigning Isaac to the universal ethical order—then, through the absurd, receiving him back not as a restored ethical relation but as a singular, unmediated gift.

Most readings treat this as a temporary suspension of ethics in service of a higher religious telos. I'm trying to see if the text actually supports a more radical, structural interpretation: that any absolute relation to the singular, one that refuses mediation through the universal, operates on a normative track that never actually intersects with ethical justification. Rather than ethics being paused and then resumed, could Abraham's silence and his refusal to justify himself to Sarah, Isaac, or the servants reveal that singular commitment and universal ethics are incommensurable—not as an exception, but as a persistent feature?

Can you help me understand whether the second movement—receiving Isaac back—restores the ethical father-son relation, or whether it leaves Abraham in a state where ethical categories remain permanently unassimilable to his actual attachment? I want to avoid the standard commentary that ethics is merely suspended for God; I'm asking whether the architecture of the two movements gives us a model of internal incommensurability within normative systems that applies independently of theology.
