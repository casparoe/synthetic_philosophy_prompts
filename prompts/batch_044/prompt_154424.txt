I sit on the advisory board of the regional museum of civic memory, and after a long and somewhat heated argument last night with a friend who works in the philosophy of history, I need to circulate a recommendation before Thursday’s meeting about a bequest we have been deferring for months. The estate of a local collector has offered us an extensive archive—personal letters, regimental artifacts, recorded oral histories, and family photographs—on the condition that we mount a permanent gallery titled The Architecture of Defeat, treating four episodes not as national triumphs but as what the donor’s curatorial brief calls “honorable failures that were retrospectively converted into founding myths”: the Confederate secession and its subsequent Lost Cause mythology, the siege of Masada and its transformation into a modern symbol of absolute resistance, the Kosovo Polje narrative and its role in Serbian national identity, and the Dunkirk evacuation and its mythologization as an expression of democratic solidarity rather than strategic retreat. My friend argues that institutionalizing these displays necessarily legitimizes the elite-state machinery that transforms defeat into myth, suppressing the microhistorical reality of ordinary soldiers, civilians, coerced participants, and dissenters who experienced these events not as destiny but as confusion, fear, or mere survival; she specifically pressed me on Dunkirk, noting that even its apparently bottom-up framing—the “little ships,” civilian volunteers—functions as a state-curated narrative that erases the colonial troops and forced laborers left behind, so that history-from-below can itself become a myth-making tool.

We need a formal memo recommending a concrete course of action—approve with binding curatorial conditions, approve only as a temporary exhibition with an explicit de-installation date and review milestones, or reject with a written explanation—together with a clear time frame the board and the estate can act on. The memo must engage seriously with the donor’s “symmetrical dignity” principle, the claim that defeated causes deserve commemorative parity with victorious ones and that suppressing them amounts to a presentist moral imperialism; but it must also treat honestly the strongest consideration against whatever position we take. If we approve or accommodate, we risk embedding elite-state narratives that erase lived experience from below; if we reject or severely constrain, we risk imposing a contemporary ethical framework that refuses to understand how these myths actually functioned for the communities that held them, and we may forfeit an archive that could be used critically. The document should be polished enough to circulate to the full board and to the estate’s representatives, and it should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or appeals to scholarly authority that the recommendation cannot stand behind on its own reasoning. Please draft it with that tension fully visible, especially the problem that even a microhistorical framing of a lost cause can be co-opted into national myth.
