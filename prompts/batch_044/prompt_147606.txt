I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of psychiatry, and last week two classmates—Leah and Marcus—got into a sustained disagreement about Ian Hacking's looping effects after we discussed psychiatric classification. They asked me to referee, and I couldn't settle it. The dispute went as follows.

Leah opened by saying that Hacking's looping mechanism best explains the rise and fall of dissociative identity disorder in the late twentieth century: clinicians introduced the category, trauma survivors began interpreting their symptoms through the framework of "alters," and the clinical presentation changed to fit the label. She then argued that because DID diagnoses have collapsed in recent decades, the loop is now broken—there is no active feedback between the classification and the classified, since the category is clinically moribund and no longer shapes self-understanding.

Marcus replied that the collapse is not the absence of a loop but a negative loop. Clinicians stopped diagnosing DID because they concluded that earlier looping had manufactured false positives; that skepticism altered the clinical environment, so patients with trauma-related dissociation now avoid presenting with distinct personality states because they know clinicians reject that framing. The classified, he said, still adapt to the label—by suppressing the very behaviors it once named.

Leah countered that Marcus is describing clinical fashion or social desirability, not Hacking-style looping. For looping to occur, she insisted, the classified must actively restructure their self-understanding in terms of the category. In Marcus's account, patients are not using the DID framework to understand themselves; they are avoiding it. The causal work is done by clinician skepticism alone, not by an interaction between the category and self-conception.

Marcus responded that avoiding a category is still a form of interaction with it. The patients' self-understanding is now structured by the absence of the label—"I must not present as having alters"—which is reflexivity mediated by the classification's continued existence. Hacking, Marcus claimed, never required the interaction to be affirmative; he only required that the kind and the people classified co-evolve.

Leah then proposed a stricter criterion to save looping from unfalsifiability: looping requires that the classified come to see the category as describing an intrinsic feature of themselves, not merely as a social label to navigate. By that criterion, she said, DID's decline demonstrates the loop breaking, because patients no longer identify with "alters" as constituent parts of themselves.

Marcus answered that even by Leah's stricter criterion there is still an inverted loop. In the 1990s patients came to see alters as intrinsic; now, he argued, many trauma survivors come to see themselves as "integrated" or "non-DID" as an intrinsic feature of their recovery identity, shaped by clinical literature and advocacy discourse that defines healthy selfhood against the DID model. The category still structures self-conception, only negatively.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of Leah's and Marcus's moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both are missing—particularly whether there is a principled way to distinguish a "negative loop" from mere clinical fashion, and whether Hacking's framework requires that the classified be aware of the classification in order for looping to occur.
