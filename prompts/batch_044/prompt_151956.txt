South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission required amnesty applicants to prove not only full disclosure but also that their acts were proportionate to the political objective pursued, a gate that denied amnesty to purely personal crimes even when politically contextualized; Argentina's subsequent trials rejected any proportionality defense for state violence entirely, while Rwanda's gacaca courts used a graded sentencing scale that implicitly recognized proportionality without formalizing it. What philosophical commitments does this proportionality gate presuppose about the moral weight of political purpose? Does it require a form of instrumental reasoning that weighs rights violations against collective ends, or can it be reconstructed within a deontological framework that treats political motivation as altering the moral category of the act rather than its magnitude? And is the model internally consistent, given that the Commission simultaneously insisted victims' suffering was not proportionate—that no political objective could diminish the wrong done to them—while asking perpetrators to measure their crimes by exactly that calculus?
