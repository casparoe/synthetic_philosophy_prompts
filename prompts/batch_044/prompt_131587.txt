I'm running a 90-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of language with ten students, and we've been working through the idea—famously developed in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach—that meaning is not intrinsic to a string of symbols but depends on a layered architecture of interpretation: a frame message that tells you how to decode, an outer message that results from that decoding, and an inner message that may be hidden inside once the first two layers are properly separated. Rather than lecturing about recursion, I want the students to physically inhabit the instability of these layers. Design an in-class activity, with rules, materials, assigned roles, and a timeline, in which small groups must decode nested physical messages (cards inside sealed envelopes, or printed protocols with spatial and typographic cues) and then act on what they think they've found. Crucially, build in a "leaky frame" condition: one of the framing devices (say, a particular paper texture, ink color, or spatial gap) is ambiguous—some groups will treat it as part of the frame's delivery mechanism, others as content belonging to the outer or inner message—so that the same physical artifact produces incompatible interpretations without anyone being clearly wrong. The payoff should be non-monetary but structurally real, such as whether a group unlocks the next stage of the task or must watch another group proceed. Include a debrief that maps specific moments of confusion onto the frame/outer/inner distinction, explains what will predictably go wrong on the first run and how to recover, and is frank about which feature of the theoretical view—probably the fully formal recursion of strange loops—this embodied exercise simply cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. No lecture up front, no named "paradox" revealed until the debrief, and no request for citations, page numbers, or reading lists; the point should emerge from what the rules and incentives make the students do.
