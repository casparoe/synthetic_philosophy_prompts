Im giving the opening remarks at the unveilling of the National Memory Gallery on Thursday. Half the room are historians who actually know their Hobsbawm from their Anderson, the other half are schoolchildren and local councillors who genuinely think our statehood stretches back to 1047. The gallery is full of 'ancient traditions' — folk costumes, a charter, a founding flag — that were mostly assembled in the 1920s and 30s by a poet-minister and a couple of enthusiastic amateurs. One centrepiece is the so-called Charter of 1047, which any expert here knows was drafted in 1934 and then used to justify independence in 1991.

I need a speech that does real philosophical work, not just decoration. The idea that nations are recent inventions and that communities are imagined should actually constrain what I can say: I cant claim we are an ancient people, but I also cant dismiss the charter as a simple fraud without undermining the gallery and the state itself. I want to thread the needle by treating the invention not as a deception but as a kind of collective authorship — the fiction was necessary to make the modern community possible. The historians should hear that I understand the constructivist point precisely, including that it is not just 'they lied' but that ritual repetition and shared print culture created the simultaneity Anderson describes; the rest should leave feeling that being 'imagined' is not an insult but an achievement.

Keep it to about four minutes, ceremonial tone, and please dont turn it into a lecture with named citations — just let the concepts do the work. Also I have a slight stutter when nervous, so avoid tongue-twisters near the opening. Oh, and I spelt millennium wrong in my notes, so just ignore that.
