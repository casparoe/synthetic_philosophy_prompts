Design a taxonomy tree for a graduate seminar on the ethics of predictive algorithms that classifies normative positions on algorithmic inferential privacy—specifically, cases in which automated systems derive sensitive personal attributes (mental health status, sexual orientation, political commitments) from behavioral traces (typing cadence, purchase patterns, geolocation) that the subject never explicitly disclosed. The tree must be built from four binary branch questions rather than from a list of named theorists, so that a respondent can be sorted by their answers without knowing the labels in advance.

The four nodes, in order, are:
1. Can privacy be violated when the sensitive attribute is never explicitly disclosed but is algorithmically inferred?
2. Is the primary value at stake the preservation of context-relative information norms, or the preservation of individual epistemic control over what others know?
3. Does the automation and scale of inference create a morally distinct category from the same inference made manually?
4. Should policy intervention target the available input data, the inference algorithms themselves, or only the actions taken on the outputs?

For each of the sixteen logical combinations, identify whether it is occupied by a defensible position (give it a brief descriptive name, not necessarily a famous one), is unoccupied (and diagnose in one sentence whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or lack of exploration), or is indistinguishable from another leaf. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot separate. The audience is philosophy graduate students; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Give a committed answer: take a definite stance on which three or four combinations represent the most coherent and defensible positions, explain briefly why the remaining empty cells fail, and state where the tree's diagnostic resolution is weakest, with reasoning.
