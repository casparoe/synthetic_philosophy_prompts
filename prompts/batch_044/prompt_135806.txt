I am designing a single ninety-minute seminar for third-year philosophy undergraduates who have completed an introductory course in philosophy of language but have not yet encountered Putnam's Twin Earth reasoning in any formal setting. Rather than walking them through the familiar Oscar-and-twin-Oscar water-versus-XYZ narrative, I want the session built around the historical case of jade—specifically the fact that what was long treated as one substance was later shown to comprise two chemically distinct minerals, jadeite and nephrite, with nearly identical superficial properties but different molecular structures. The pedagogical aim is to use this case to motivate semantic externalism: to ask whether two speakers in 1700 and 1950, sharing every internal mental state, nevertheless mean different things by "jade" because their respective environments embed different chemical kinds.

Plan the explanatory arc for me, not the explanation itself. First, identify the conceptual dependency that must be secured before Twin Earth reasoning can begin—presumably the internalist thesis that mental representation alone fixes reference needs to be articulated as an explicit target rather than a background assumption—and explain why the jade example serves this purpose better than the water example, including what it must contain (for instance, shared ignorance of chemistry among the speakers) and what it must deliberately lack (such as immediate perceptual access to the chemical distinction). Second, map the misconceptions students will form in sequence: I anticipate an initial slide toward the view that meanings are fixed entirely by scientific authority rather than by environmental causation, followed by a conflation of externalism with the claim that individuals have no cognitive access to their own concepts, and perhaps a third confusion that treats the thought experiment as an empirical discovery about geology rather than a modal argument about reference. For each, specify the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and how. Third, propose a concluding check—one question I can pose at the end—that distinguishes a student who genuinely grasps that reference is environmentally mediated from one who has merely memorized the slogan that meanings are not in the head. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—such as Putnam's division of linguistic labor, Burge's arthritis case, or the technical debate over narrow versus wide content—and justify the omission in a way that does not make externalism appear arbitrarily truncated.
