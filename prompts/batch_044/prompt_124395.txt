I'm trying to map my own judgments about indicative and counterfactual conditionals across contexts that aren't usually discussed together, and I'd like to know whether they reflect one principle or an unstable mix.

My verdicts are these. First, in deliberation: if I say "If it rains tomorrow, the picnic is canceled," and I don't yet know the forecast, I read this indicatively—as expressing that my belief in cancellation is high conditional on rain—and it directly guides whether I pack food. Second, in retrospective evaluation: if the picnic went ahead in sunshine, and I say "If it had rained, the picnic would have been canceled," I read this counterfactually, selecting nearby worlds where rain occurs and checking whether cancellation holds; my current beliefs about the actual weather don't enter. Third, in modal-statistical mixing: "If this die is fair, the chance of rolling six is one-sixth" strikes me as indicative and analytic, but "If this die had been fair, the chance of this particular roll being six would have been one-sixth" feels confused—either false because the actual roll was determined, or true in a way that requires importing an indicative law into a counterfactual world-selection. Fourth, in post-hoc decision reflection: after I brought an umbrella and it stayed dry, "If I hadn't brought it, I would have gotten wet" seems to me neither clearly true nor clearly false; its evaluation depends on selecting worlds where I left it home, but also on what I now know about the actual absence of rain, and I can't tell whether the counterfactual should preserve my actual knowledge-state or revert to my prior one. Fifth, in causal history: "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" feels like a legitimate counterfactual about causal redundancy, whereas "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, the Vietnam War might have been shorter" feels like it's illicitly using indicative uncertainty about causal pathways that I have no special access to from the counterfactual scenario alone. Sixth, in probabilistic prediction with failure: I think "If this drug works, the tumor shrinks" is indicative and supports prescribing it; but if I prescribe it, the tumor doesn't shrink, and then I say "If the drug had worked, the tumor would have shrunk," I think the counterfactual is true despite the failure, yet I also think my confidence in the indicative version should drop sharply—which suggests the indicative and counterfactual are governed by different norms of belief revision.

Is there a unified account—perhaps distinguishing world-selection mechanisms from belief-update rules—that accommodates all six? Or do I have to split my theory: one for deliberative indicatives, one for historical counterfactuals, and one for probabilistic embeddings? Please say what the best unified principle predicts for case four—the umbrella evaluation after the dry day—and whether saving my ambivalence there forces me to drop one of the other verdicts, particularly three or six.
