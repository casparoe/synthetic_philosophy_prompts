At my philosophy circle last tuesday — I'm a retired chemist who just started reading philosophy books — two folks got into it over wheter societies actually collapse or just change shape. (1) Lena said calling the Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, or Easter Island a 'collapse' is mostly Western framing. Palace economies dissolved and writing faded in some regions, but village farming and local trade kept going; on Rapa Nui the shift from moai-building to the bird-man cult was just labor being reallocated, not a death spiral. She said societies are open systems, so 'collapse' is the wrong metaphor — it's closer to a phase transition in network topology. (2) Marcus said that's just relabeling decline. If you lose literacy, long-distance trade, and big public works for centuries, that's not a 'rewiring,' it's institutional failure. He insisted Easter Island was real collapse: deforestation led to cannibalism and a population crash, not a gentle pivot. (3) Lena came back that the cannibalism story is mostly 19th-century mythmaking and rats did alot of the tree damage; the culture adapted and didn't vanish. She said Marcus treats societies like machines with a 'normal' state, but history has no baseline, and if every simplification counts as collapse then we're smuggling in the idea that complexity equals progress. (4) Marcus said she's making the concept useless — if both Rome and the Bronze Age are just 'different topologies,' we can't tell a crisis from a centuries-long loss of capacity. He said her view hides real suffering: people depended on those institutions, and calling their loss 'reconfiguration' sounds like denying the harm.

Please go through the four moves and say who's closer to right at each step, with brief reasons. Also flag any hidden assumption both might be missing — especially about whether complexity is better — and say what your adjudication implies for a case they didn't discuss, like whether the fall of the Western Roman Empire should count.
