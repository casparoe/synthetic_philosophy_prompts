A popular science essay summarizes the current understanding of cooperation in evolutionary game theory as follows: "In the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, rational agents should always defect, but in the iterated version Axelrod's tournaments showed that tit-for-tat dominates and cooperation evolves naturally. More recently, zero-determinant strategies were discovered that let one player enforce a linear payoff relation on the opponent, which proves cooperation is never evolutionarily stable without an external enforcer and overturns the idea that reciprocity alone explains altruism. Specifically, these strategies always out-earn any opponent in evolutionary competition, which means spatial structure and repeated interaction are irrelevant to whether cooperation survives."

Something about this narrative strikes me as too neat—especially the claim that zero-determinant strategies "always out-earn" opponents, the leap from tournament results to evolutionary dynamics, and the dismissal of spatial structure. Is this a fair summary of what Axelrod demonstrated, what Press and Dyson established about zero-determinant strategies, and what evolutionary dynamics in well-mixed versus structured populations actually show? Please audit it claim by claim: separate what is accurate or a standard simplification from what misrepresents the mathematics or the biology, clarify whether "evolutionary stability" here means stability in the Maynard Smith sense, asymptotic stability in the replicator equation, or something else, and explain whether the blogger's conclusion—that reciprocity is insufficient and external enforcement is required—actually follows from the premises. I am particularly interested in whether the behavior of extortionate versus generous zero-determinant strategies in evolutionary competition undermines Axelrod's broader conclusions, or whether the blogger has conflated one-shot rationality, tournament performance, and long-run evolutionary outcomes.
