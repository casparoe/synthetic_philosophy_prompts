I serve on the clinical AI oversight board of a large teaching hospital. A vendor proposes deploying an emergency-triage decision-support system that generates explanations in first-person intentional language—e.g., “I believe this presentation is septic because I want to minimise false negatives, given that I expect ...”—and trains clinicians to interpret these via Dennett’s intentional stance, not as literal mental-state reports but as predictive heuristics about system behaviour. Our legal counsel warns that if clinicians cite these rationales in court, the hospital risks liability for either endorsing deceptive anthropomorphism or, conversely, failing to treat the outputs as authoritative clinical opinions. An internal memo from a philosopher on the board distinguishes “predictive stance adoption” (using belief-talk to forecast system outputs) from “accountability stance adoption” (using it to assign responsibility), arguing that only the latter generates legal exposure. We must circulate a short written opinion before Thursday’s meeting recommending whether to approve deployment, approve with conditions, or reject outright, with a concrete time-frame and any conditions specified precisely. The opinion should treat seriously the strongest argument for the position it does not take, and should be framed so that a respondent without web access could engage with it—no requests for citations, reading lists, or niche theoretical references beyond what is stated here.
