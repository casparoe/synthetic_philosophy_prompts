After a prolonged argument with a friend about whether the nursery rhyme “for want of a nail” describes deterministic chaos, mere accident, or something structurally distinct, I have agreed to lead a single ninety-minute session for a non-specialist reading group. The audience knows the rhyme and has encountered popular accounts of the butterfly effect, but they have no training in dynamical systems or historiography. I want them to understand the precise difference between deterministic chaos—where trajectories diverge exponentially yet remain governed by the same underlying rules—and genuine historical contingency, in which a small perturbation can alter the institutional rules of the system itself, producing a structural break rather than merely an unpredictable path within a fixed attractor. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or mathematical formalism; I want a plan for how to build this distinction from nothing.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, what idea must be firmly in place before the distinction between chaos and contingency can even be introduced—what dependency must I establish so that the audience does not conflate “sensitive to initial conditions” with “anything could have happened”? Second, identify the single carrying example that should anchor the entire session: should it be the rhyme itself deconstructed link by link, or a concrete historical episode, and what properties must it possess and what must it lack—specifically, where in the cascade does the system’s governing structure change rather than merely its outcome? Third, lay out the order in which the components must be introduced—open system versus closed dynamics, path dependence, the moment of rule-change—and justify the sequence in terms of conceptual dependency. Fourth, map the misconceptions they will predictably bring: likely that chaos theory licenses arbitrary counterfactual fantasy; that small causes are decisive regardless of structural conditions; that unpredictability is equivalent to randomness; and possibly that historians invoke contingency only to avoid explaining large causes. For each, specify exactly where in the sequence it should be headed off, and how. Fifth, propose one question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether a participant grasps the structural-break idea rather than merely remembering that “little things matter.” Finally, state explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level—whether Lyapunov exponents, formal counterfactual methodology in historiography, or the metaphysics of causation—and say so openly in the plan.
