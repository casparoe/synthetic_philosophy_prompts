I'm facilitating a 60-minute philosophy-of-science workshop for 16 graduate students on chaos, predictability, and determinism. The idea I want them to physically encounter—before anyone names it—is that deterministic laws can produce radically divergent forecasts from observationally indistinguishable initial conditions: each observer's prediction can remain close to some actual trajectory (shadowing it) while diverging exponentially from every other observer's prediction, so determinism does not yield shared predictability. Design a runnable in-class game with clear rules, roles for all 16, and a non-monetary payoff scheme, arranged so that participants generate forecasts from nearly identical data cards, discover that all forecasts are locally defensible yet pairwise incompatible, and feel the failure of coordination before any debrief. Deliver the design in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the materials, rules, and turn structure; (2) the payoff scheme and what behavior it rewards; (3) the debrief that maps play moments onto determinism, shadowing, and intersubjective divergence; (4) the predictable failure mode on first running and the quick fix; (5) the one philosophical feature of chaos that this physical setup cannot replicate and that you must state aloud.
