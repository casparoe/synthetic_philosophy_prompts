I'm writing an independent study essay on consciousness science, and I have built an argument about heterophenomenology that seems to destroy the very field it belongs to, but I cannot see where the slip occurs. As I understand the method, every first-person report of experience should be treated not as direct evidence of an inner reality, but as a narrative describing a "heterophenomenological world"—the world exactly as the subject takes it to be. Applying this consistently yields the following.

(1) If heterophenomenology is correct, then all first-person reports, including my own, are interpretations of such a world rather than transparent access to actual experience.

(2) Suppose an artificial system produces the structured report, "I am experiencing a continuous visual field." By (1), we must read this as the system constructing a heterophenomenological world that contains visual experience, not as proof that it actually has any.

(3) Human subjects produce reports that are structurally and functionally identical—"I see a continuous field of color." Consistency demands that we treat these human reports the same way: as narratives about a world, not as evidence of real experience.

(4) If every report, human or artificial, is only narrative with no evidential link to actual consciousness, then the science of consciousness possesses no observational criterion that distinguishes conscious subjects from non-conscious systems.

(5) Without such a criterion, heterophenomenology eliminates the very data it was designed to organize, collapsing into an eliminativism about consciousness evidence.

(6) The only escape is to grant human reports a special epistemic status—perhaps because they come from biological organisms—but that imports a prior criterion for consciousness that the method was explicitly developed to avoid.

So the argument appears valid, and its conclusion is that heterophenomenology undermines itself. Which step should I reject? I would strongly prefer to keep (1) and (3), since abandoning universal consistency feels more costly than abandoning the method itself. But if I must surrender one premise, I want to know which is least damaging and what exactly that costs for the empirical study of consciousness.
