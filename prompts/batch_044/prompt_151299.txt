I am preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher who has recently published a monograph defending a structuralist reinterpretation of Hannah Arendt’s analysis of Adolf Eichmann. The standard reading treats her well-known observation about the banality of evil as a psychological diagnosis: Eichmann was terrifying not because he was monstrous but because he was ordinary, a bureaucratic conformist lacking malice or ideological conviction. My guest rejects this interpretation. He argues that Arendt was not describing an individual personality type but identifying an institutional mechanism—what he calls normative displacement—through which bureaucratic procedure replaces the internal moral dialogue that makes judgment possible. On this account, Eichmann’s failure was not that he was dull or unthinking in a personal sense, but that the administrative framework he inhabited systematically stripped away the linguistic and cognitive resources needed to evaluate his own actions from a moral standpoint. The guest further claims that this reading resolves an apparent contradiction in Arendt’s work: she insists on holding individuals responsible for evil while describing a system that seems to dissolve personal agency. He resolves this by reconceiving responsibility not as causal authorship but as the maintenance of an internal conversation about meaning, a capacity that institutions can erode without thereby excusing the person who allows it to erode.

For the interview, I want a sequence of questions that treats this position with philosophical seriousness rather than treating it as a historical gloss. They should press where the literature actually presses—anticipating the standard reply that Arendt herself emphasized Eichmann’s inability to imagine the perspective of his victims, and including a follow-up that asks whether that emphasis survives if thinking is treated primarily as an institutional variable rather than a personal achievement. I want at least one question that probes whether this structural reading makes Arendt’s concept too diffuse: if the banality of evil is defined by the erosion of judgment through procedural language, does the concept apply so broadly to modern administrative life that it loses its specific explanatory force? The final question should ask what kind of historical or institutional evidence could genuinely weaken the view, rather than asking for a bibliography or reading list. Please write the questions in a progressive sequence that moves from clarification of the core claim, through an internal tension about personal responsibility, to this broader concern about explanatory scope, with each question building on the anticipated answer to the previous one.
