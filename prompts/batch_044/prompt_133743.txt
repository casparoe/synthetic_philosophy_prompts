I'm formalizing my position on natural kinds for a philosophy of science workshop and want a consistency audit before I present. Here are the commitments I've arrived at:

(1) A kind is natural only if its members share a real essence—an underlying structure or property—that causally explains their observable characteristics, so that inductive projections within the kind are objectively grounded rather than conventionally imposed.

(2) Chemical kinds (e.g., gold defined by atomic number 79, water defined by H₂O bonding) are paradigmatic natural kinds, but their essences are relational and organizational: being water is constituted by a specific bonding relation among hydrogen and oxygen atoms, not by an intrinsic monadic property of each molecule taken in isolation.

(3) Biological species are not natural kinds. They are spatiotemporally bounded lineages defined by relational properties—common ancestry and reproductive isolation—without a shared intrinsic essence that explains the properties of all tigers or all oaks.

(4) Psychiatric categories such as major depressive disorder or schizophrenia are not natural kinds: they are etiologically and symptomatically heterogeneous, lacking any shared microstructural essence.

(5) I reject taxonomic pluralism. If two scientific taxonomies of the same domain are genuinely incompatible, they cannot both be correct; there is at most one taxonomy that carves nature at its joints.

(6) A science's explanatory depth and objectivity depend on its central categories being natural kinds in the essentialist sense in (1). If a science systematically employs categories that lack shared essences, it is not merely describing a different "level" but is failing to represent real structures, and is therefore epistemically inferior.

(7) Evolutionary biology—relying on species, clades, and ecosystems—is nonetheless a genuinely explanatory, objective science, not epistemically inferior to chemistry, despite its central categories failing the essentialist criterion in (1).

(8) The IAU's redefinition of "planet" was a conventional stipulation: astronomers adopted and applied a criterion (orbital clearing), not discovered a hidden joint in nature.

(9) Before that redefinition, however, astronomers were genuinely tracking a real natural kind—large bodies in hydrostatic equilibrium with cleared orbits—and Pluto was always an anomalous outlier that did not belong, so the decision corrected a miscategorization rather than inventing a boundary.

Please audit this package. Go through the commitments pair by pair: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension (ranking them by severity rather than listing them as equally bad), specify for each what must give and what the least costly revision is, and identify any pair that only looks inconsistent but dissolves on inspection, explaining why. In particular, address whether (6) and (7) can be held together without abandoning either, and whether (8) and (9) are compatible. Finally, tell me which single revision—dropping or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most tension at the lowest cost, and which revision would be the most expensive. Do not treat the set as uniformly incoherent, and do not signal which commitment I should be most reluctant to surrender.
