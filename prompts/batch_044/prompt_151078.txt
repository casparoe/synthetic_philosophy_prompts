I serve as a graduate representative on my university's Research Partnerships and Ethics Oversight Committee, and we are preparing for our meeting next Thursday to decide whether to renew a five-year collaborative agreement with an overseas epidemiological institute. The institute conducts irreplaceable malaria-vaccine fieldwork in a region where no other partner operates, and terminating the agreement would likely halt a trial that has already enrolled over two thousand participants. At the same time, the institute is administratively embedded within a defense ministry that has been documented, in credible international reports, as providing logistical cover for civilian suppression in adjacent provinces; our university's shared infrastructure—including data servers and funding pipelines—would therefore continue to supply institutional legitimacy and material capacity to that ministry. In my political-ethics course we have been studying the classic problem of dirty hands, but I am struggling to see how the usual framework applies here, since none of our committee members will personally carry out any wrongful act; rather, we would be sustaining a system that produces wrong acts elsewhere.

My professor recently introduced a distinction that seems relevant but is not well developed in the standard literature I have encountered: between participatory dirty hands, in which an agent personally performs a morally objectionable action as the necessary price of achieving a greater good, and structural dirty hands, in which an agent maintains institutional ties that enable wrongful actions by others without direct personal involvement. I am inclined to think our case is closer to the structural variant, but I am uncertain whether that distinction should alter our moral calculus—perhaps structural complicity is more diffuse and therefore easier to justify, or perhaps it is worse because it disguises responsibility behind bureaucratic distance. I also worry that framing the issue this way risks ignoring the immediate, concrete harm to the trial participants if we withdraw, which is not merely a symbolic loss but a measurable setback to public health.

Please draft the short written opinion I should circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action—renewal with strict conditions, immediate termination, or a phased transition with a defined timeline—and provide reasoning that the researchers and administrators on both sides could actually respond to. The memo should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than dismissing it, and it should engage seriously with the possibility that this case exemplifies structural rather than participatory moral complicity. I am not looking for a bibliography or a literature review; I need a document the committee can act on.
