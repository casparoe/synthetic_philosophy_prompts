Because I recently found myself deciding whether to mislead an aggressive relative at my door in order to protect a vulnerable family member inside, Kant’s well-known scenario of lying to a murderer has shifted from an abstract exercise to an immediate moral question. I want to determine whether my own judgments about deception form a coherent pattern, and whether any principle that captures them can accommodate—or must reject—Kant’s absolute prohibition on lying.

Here are my concrete verdicts. Telling a violent pursuer that the intended victim is not present: permissible, perhaps obligatory. Telling a dying patient, who asks directly, that her estranged son called, when he has not: wrong, despite the comfort it provides. Claiming at a job interview that I left my previous position voluntarily, when I was in fact dismissed: wrong. Writing that the check is in the mail when it is not: wrong, though morally lighter than the interview deception. Telling a friend that a flawed business plan is sound in order to avoid conflict: not permissible. Yet when my child asks whether our deceased dog went to live on a farm, I keep oscillating; the statement feels at times like a benign narrative framing and at times like an unjustified corruption of her developing capacity to face reality.

The pair that most troubles me is the pursuer case and the dying-patient case: both shield a vulnerable party from severe harm by inducing a false belief, but I treat the first as justified and the second as forbidden. Meanwhile, the interview lie and the check lie both serve my own convenience, yet I judge them with different severity.

Can you articulate a precise principle—perhaps drawing on distinctions between corrupting a listener’s practical reasoning versus altering an emotional state, or between violating an epistemic entitlement versus failing a duty of beneficence—that delivers these verdicts, explains the divergence in severity between the two self-serving lies, and resolves my oscillation on the farm story? If no single principle preserves every verdict, identify which one I should sacrifice for coherence. Apply your principle also to a case I have not listed: informing a hidden refugee that approaching authorities have turned away, knowing they have not, in order to prevent panic that might reveal her location. Finally, does your resulting view leave Kant’s categorical prohibition intact as a limiting case, or does it show that prohibition to be an overgeneralization from a narrower, valid rule?
