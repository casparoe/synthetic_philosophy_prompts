After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about determinism, chaos, and what “predictable in principle” actually commits one to, I want to check whether I am tracking her view consistently. Here is where she landed on four cases. First, the ideal frictionless double pendulum with mathematically exact initial conditions: she said it is fully deterministic, but its long-term motion is unpredictable in a structural sense, not merely because we lack computing power or precise measurements. Second, the decay of a specific carbon-14 nucleus at a specific instant: she said this is not deterministic at all—there is irreducible randomness, not just hidden information. Third, a deterministic pseudo-random number generator running with a fixed but unknown 128-bit seed: she said it is deterministic, and its outputs are unpredictable only epistemically—if you possess the seed and the algorithm, prediction is trivial, with no special dynamical feature involved. Fourth, turbulent fluid flow governed by deterministic Navier-Stokes equations with every initial condition measured to arbitrary precision: she said it is deterministic, but the unpredictability is dynamical—the equations amplify any residual gap exponentially, so even perfect measurement in principle would not buy long-term predictability.

I have not yet told her about a fifth case: a discrete-time system with a linear, non-expansive update rule on an infinite-dimensional lattice—nearby initial conditions stay nearby or converge, so there are no exponentially diverging trajectories—yet for which no closed-form solution exists and the fastest known method to determine the state at step N is to compute every intermediate step sequentially, even with complete and exact knowledge of the initial state.

What should she say about this fifth system? Is it deterministic? Is its unpredictability of the same kind as the double pendulum and the turbulent flow, or of the same kind as the unknown-seed generator? More precisely, I want to identify the implicit rule she is running: is she tracking the presence of exponential trajectory divergence as the marker of deep, structural unpredictability, or is she tracking computational irreducibility—the absence of any algorithmic shortcut—regardless of whether perturbations grow or decay? Which of these two rules best accommodates her first four verdicts?

Also, tell me which further case would best distinguish these readings: a system with exponentially diverging trajectories but a discovered closed-form solution that permits direct computation of future states without iteration, or a system with decaying perturbations but a subsequently discovered compressed algorithm that eliminates the need for step-by-step simulation? I am less interested in her bare yes-or-no answers than in the rule her earlier judgments seem to apply, the alternative rules that fit those judgments equally well, and what each rule predicts about both the fifth case and the distinguishing test case.
