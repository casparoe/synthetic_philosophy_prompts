I am a philosophy PhD student preparing a single fifty-minute undergraduate seminar for second-year students who have completed an introductory logic course but have no training in formal modal logic. My objective is to teach the argumentative structure traditionally called the 'Death in Damascus' fatalist inference—not as a museum piece, but as a live demonstration of how modal scope ambiguities generate an apparent determinism. To prevent students from treating the case as folklore, I have constructed a pedagogical variant: rather than a singular prophecy, the oracle delivers a disjunction ('you will die in Damascus or in Samarra'), and the protagonist's flight to Samarra is reconstructed so that either disjunct, if pursued as an avoidance strategy, is satisfied by the very act of avoidance. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. Specify the exact sequence in which the components must be introduced: which modal intuition must be secured before the prophecy is introduced; why the singular version should precede the disjunctive variant; and how each step depends on the prior one. Identify the one concrete, realistic scenario—workplace or clinical rather than travel-based—that should carry the argument, noting what structural features it must preserve and what it must avoid. Map the misconceptions students will bring or form: for example, that the argument presupposes causal determinism, that 'inevitable' is used predictively rather than modally, or that avoidance is physically impossible rather than logically self-defeating. State the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off. Propose one end-of-session diagnostic question that reveals whether a student has grasped the scope ambiguity—between 'it is inevitable that P' and 'no matter what I do, P'—rather than merely feeling discomfort about free will. Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level and acknowledged as such: formal branching-time semantics, metaphysical debates about the open future, and technical distinctions between hard and soft facts about the past. Note specifically where the disjunctive variant diverges pedagogically from the traditional singular-prophecy framing and why that divergence is necessary to isolate whether the fatalist conclusion depends on singular reference or on the logical structure of avoidance behavior itself.
