I have no formal background in philosophy or psychology, but I have spent a lot of time observing my own patterns of planning and failure. I have noticed something that the standard story about weakness of will does not quite capture. It is not merely that I want the present reward more strongly; rather, as the moment of choice draws near, the earlier plan begins to feel foreign to me, as if it were drafted by someone with different priorities. I have started calling this feeling commitment estrangement, and it seems to happen precisely when hyperbolic discounting predicts a preference reversal—when a distant reward is valued highly but collapses in value as it approaches. Given this, please argue for the position that akrasia, understood as acting against one's own better judgment, is not a real phenomenon but a misinterpretation of rational preference change over time. Defend the view that hyperbolic discounting shows there is no stable, atemporal self whose judgment is being violated, only a sequence of temporally situated evaluative perspectives. In particular, address whether this view makes it impossible to hold anyone responsible for impulsive choices, and argue that it actually clarifies responsibility by grounding it in the continuity of planning mechanisms rather than in the fiction of an unchanging rational core.
