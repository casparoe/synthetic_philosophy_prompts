Hey, I've been teaching du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness and keep hitting this one move that feels like it needs a label, but I can't pin it down. Here's the occurence: she spends pages showing that study and reason are the only real sources of happiness, that things like romantic love, reputation, and wealth are basically illusions we project onto empty objects, and that once you really see through them you're freed from passion. But then she turns around and says that for most people—especially women kept away from books—you shouldn't actually rip that veil away, because without those illusions they'd be miserable, and in fact a wise person learns to maintain certain pleasant errors deliberately rather than chasing every false belief to ground. What's strange is she doesn't treat this as hypocrisy or weakness; she treats it as reason's own mature form, where knowing when to stop reasoning is part of being rational. She does it with glory too: she debunks it as vanity, then advises that someone who lacks the temperament for philosophy should keep chasing glory because stripping it away leaves nothing behind. So reason both undermines the illusion and then protects it, framing that protection as rational self-management rather than deception.

Is there an established name for this exact configuration—where a thinker uses reason to debunk a source of happiness, then uses reason again to justify preserving that same debunked source for those who can't handle the truth, treating the preservation as a kind of epistemic caretaking? It's not quite the noble lie, since there's no ruler-ruled split, and it's not bad faith because she's totally aware of the error. It's also not just "necessary illusion" in the usual Nietzschean sense, because the illusion isn't a general condition of life but a specifically allocated remedy for those excluded from the highest happiness. What's the nearest concept, where does it fall apart, and if nothing really fits, what should we call it—maybe "rational illusion-maintainance" or something sharper that captures the double move? And what's the boundary that keeps it seperate from plain equivocation or from a simple two-level theory of value?
