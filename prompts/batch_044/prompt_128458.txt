Analyze the 1992 Los Angeles civil unrest that followed the acquittal of the police officers involved in the Rodney King beating as a case study in Weber's theory of the state's monopoly on legitimate violence, read through the contemporary debate over policing—reform, defund, and abolition.

First, map the coercive landscape with precision: distinguish the actions of the LAPD, the California Highway Patrol, the National Guard, private security networks in Koreatown, and local community patrols during the peak days of late April and early May. For each actor, specify whether the state recognized, tolerated, or condemned its use of force, and identify the moments when the monopoly appeared fragmented rather than unified.

Next, apply the three theoretical positions—reformist consolidation of the monopoly, defund/reallocate reduction of its scope, and abolitionist dissolution of state coercive authority in specific domains—to the actual options facing municipal and state authorities. Do not treat them as slogans; specify what each position implies about deployment decisions, jurisdictional boundaries, and the legitimacy of non-state defensive violence during the crisis.

Then evaluate the explanatory power and limits of Weber's framework: which dynamics of the unrest—racialized policing practices, the geographic withdrawal of state presence, the rapid federalization of response—does the monopoly concept illuminate, and which does it obscure or mischaracterize?

Finally, reverse the direction of analysis. Does the temporary breakdown and swift restoration of centralized coercive control in Los Angeles confirm the resilience of the monopoly, or does it reveal that the monopoly was never fully exclusive—merely hegemonic, contested, and racially differentiated? Identify where Weber's theory is genuinely silent, particularly regarding whether a monopoly exercised through systematically racialized violence remains a monopoly in the normative sense the theory requires.

Be disciplined about the mapping: name the players, specify the options and payoffs as they appeared to decision-makers in real time, and do not force the theory to speak where it offers no clear verdict.
