I'm preparing a creative-writing exercise for a philosophy of cosmology reading group, and I'd like a short story that treats Gödel's rotating-universe metric as a serious physical setting rather than as a generic backdrop for time-travel tropes. The protagonist should be an observational cosmologist stationed near the rotational axis of the cosmos, where she discovers that her ship's trajectory—if accelerated along a specific geodesic permitted by the global vorticity—will intersect her own past worldline not in a paradoxical loop but in a region where the local orientation of time becomes ambiguous because the vorticity vector flips relative to her proper-time axis. She must calibrate her instruments using only the frame-dragging of distant galaxies and decide whether to complete the curve and encounter that other version of herself, knowing that Gödel used precisely such closed timelike curves to argue that an objective flow of time cannot exist in this universe.

The story should derive its tension not from action sequences or technological devices like wormholes, but from her internal philosophical debate: if she meets a copy that retains perfect episodic memory of the encounter, are they successive stages of one person, co-existing slices of a four-dimensional block, or causally independent duplicates? Given Gödel's view that personal identity in such a metric is coordinate-dependent labeling rather than temporal persistence, she should wrestle with whether "survival" even applies. End not with a dramatic choice but with her writing a single first-person observational log entry attempting to state which copy is now speaking—without being able to guarantee that the present tense refers to anything objective—leaving the reader unsure whether the entry was composed before or after the intersection.
