Here is an argument I built for a seminar on the ontology of mathematics and now find suspicious because the conclusion threatens to eliminate too much working mathematics. (1) The full axiom of choice yields the Banach-Tarski paradox, in which a solid unit ball is decomposed into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two unit balls, apparently violating conservation of volume. (2) If a mathematical principle licenses a result that is physically impossible—here, the literal duplication of material volume—then that principle describes no possible world and ought to be rejected. (3) Therefore the full axiom of choice is false. (4) But the Hahn-Banach theorem, essential to functional analysis, also depends on a choice principle—specifically the Boolean prime ideal theorem—which is strictly weaker than full AC and does not entail Banach-Tarski. (5) By the reasoning in (2), consistency demands that we also reject the Boolean prime ideal theorem whenever it licenses results lacking direct physical interpretation, such as certain non-constructive linear extensions in infinite-dimensional spaces. (6) Doing so would gut functional analysis and large parts of topological vector space theory. (7) So either we reject (2) and tolerate physically unrealizable results, or we accept the collapse of analysis. (8) I want a middle path: keep Hahn-Banach, reject Banach-Tarski, without invoking a blanket physical-realizability criterion for all mathematics. Where does the argument slip? Which premise is least costly to abandon, and what does a defender of that middle path—perhaps one who distinguishes point-set idealization from constructive existence—have to pay? In particular, is there a structural feature of Banach-Tarski that makes it uniquely vulnerable to the physical-impossibility objection in a way that Hahn-Banach is not?
