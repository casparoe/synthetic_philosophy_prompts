I defend a Gödel-inspired argument for the ideality of temporal passage: if Einstein's field equations admit rotating cosmological solutions containing closed timelike curves, then the asymmetry required for an objectively flowing A-series is undermined, since a world-line could in principle return to its own past and render "before" and "after" frame-dependent in a way incompatible with genuine succession. I take this not as a science-fiction puzzle but as a metaphysical revelation—formal consistency conditions on spacetime geometry expose temporal order as mind-dependent, echoing Gödel's broader sympathy with Kantian and Leibnizian idealism about time. What would count decisively against this view? Rank the defeaters: empirical or stability-based arguments that rotating universes are physically impossible; philosophical demonstrations that CTCs can be embedded within an objective B-theory without contradiction; the charge that inferring metaphysical conclusions from modal possibilities in the solution-space of a physical theory commits a modal fallacy; and any cosmological or philosophical results showing that CTCs require rather than dissolve an objective direction of time. For each, indicate whether a defender of the Gödelian position could absorb it by qualification—say, by restricting the argument to logical rather than physical possibility—or whether it genuinely wounds the core inference, and say whether insulating the view against all such defeaters renders it unfalsifiable in a damaging way.
