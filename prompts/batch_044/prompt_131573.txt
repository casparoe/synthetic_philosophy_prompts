I'm writing a feature for a science and ideas section on whether Keynes's 1930 vision of post-scarcity flourishing—once absolute needs are satisfied, societies should reduce labor and turn to art, contemplation, and friendship—survives modern criticism. Please write a polished dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors: one defending Keynes's framework, the other attacking it.

The defender should argue that Keynes's distinction between absolute and relative needs is structurally sound. Absolute needs are satiable and universal; relative needs are positional and insatiable, generating a zero-sum status race that rational policy should exit by democratizing leisure through shorter working hours and technological redistribution. The defender should claim Keynes anticipated modern behavioral economics by recognizing that insatiability is socially constructed, not psychologically fixed, and should maintain that Keynes's prescription—expanded leisure for aesthetic and intellectual life—applies across classes, not just to the educated elite.

The critic should respond that Keynes's category of "absolute needs" is itself a historically contingent artifact of the British upper-middle class in which Keynes moved—specifically his Cambridge Apostles and Bloomsbury milieu—rather than a cross-cultural baseline. The critic should argue that Keynes treats labor as a necessary evil to be minimized, ignoring craft traditions, religious vocations, and working-class solidarities that find dignity and meaning within economic activity itself. The critic should press the point that Keynes never seriously considered whether working people might prefer continued employment and higher wages to state-encouraged leisure, suggesting his universalism masks an elitist theory of value.

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues: the defender should address the class-bias charge by citing Keynes's explicit belief that technological progress would universalize access to leisure, and the critic should counter by asking why Keynes's own life contradicts his framework—he accumulated substantial personal wealth through aggressive market speculation while condemning the love of money as a kind of mental illness. Have them dispute whether that accumulation represents a pragmatic exception (capital needed to fund artistic life) or an internal contradiction that collapses the distinction between prudent investment and the morbid pursuit of wealth.

Both characters should remain fully competent; neither should be a strawman. Write in formal American English suitable for a magazine feature. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not use verbatim quotations from any text.
