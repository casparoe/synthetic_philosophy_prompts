ok so I'm drafting this novel with a small-town tyrant character and I got sidetracked trying to figure out if his authority could be philosophically defensible. I was reading Aristotle's stuff on natural slavery — you know, where he says some folks are born without the deliberitive part of the soul — and then me and my writing partner tried to build a reductio. Here's where we ended up: (1) Aristotle claims that if you lack the deliberitive faculty, you're naturally suited to be ruled in the domains where you lack it. (2) But modern people clearly lack expert-level practical reason in tons of specific domains — surgery, tax law, engineering — so by that logic we're all natural slaves to dozens of experts. (3) If natural slavery is a just relation, then those experts are entitled to command us in those domains without consent. (4) That collapses into what my partner jokingly calls "competance sovereignty" — a total technocratic dictatorship where skill alone justifies rule, which is obviously absurd. (5) So either Aristotle's principle is false, or the idea that authority should always track competence is wrong. (6) But I actually want to keep something like (2), because it feels right that a surgeon should call the shots in an OR. So where does the chain break? Is (1) misreading Aristotle's "deliberitive" capacity as domain-specific rather than about your whole life? Or does (3) smuggle in a modern idea of individual consent that Aristotle wouldn't care about? I need to know what I'm paying if I reject one of them, because my character's justification depends on not being a total strawman.
