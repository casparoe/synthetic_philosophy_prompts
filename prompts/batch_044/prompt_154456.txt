Consider the following paraphrase of a central argument from Anselm’s Proslogion (c. 1078): Anselm asks whether a being than which nothing greater can be conceived might exist only in the mind. He replies that if it existed only there, one could conceive of it existing in reality as well, and that this latter conception would describe something greater, contradicting the initial definition; hence the being must exist in reality too.

I am trying to understand how this reasoning relates to Gödel’s modal ontological argument, particularly his framework of positive properties and the axiom that a being possessing all positive properties exists necessarily. Does Anselm’s move from mental to real existence implicitly treat real existence as a perfection in exactly the sense that Gödel formalizes with his modal operators? Or does Anselm instead depend on a comparative reductio structure—showing that a merely mental being can always be surpassed—that is logically independent of attributing properties like existence to an object?

Please help me understand the precise logical relationship. Specifically, I want to know whether Gödel’s closure conditions on positive properties resolve an ambiguity in Anselm’s notion of greatness, or whether they introduce an ontological commitment—such as the entailment of further perfections from any single one—that has no clear analogue in the original text. I am not looking for a standard historical comparison, but for help seeing whether the two arguments share the same logical skeleton or diverge at the level of modal structure.
