I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science working on epistemic norms in institutional research practice, and next week I'm facilitating a ninety-minute session for a mixed audience of philosophy graduate students and science-studies researchers who have little formal background in epistemology. Rather than using the familiar individual-agent formulation, I want to stage the dogmatism paradox through an institutional case: a peer-review panel reaches unanimous first-order justification for endorsing a study, then receives anonymous post-publication counterevidence that arrives after the decision has already been incorporated into policy recommendations, and must determine whether its second-order confidence in its own procedures licenses it to dismiss the new evidence without reopening review. I need a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—for how to build this idea in sequence. What conceptual distinction has to be in place before the paradox can even be introduced, specifically the separation between first-order evidential support and second-order justification about one's methods, and why does the institutional framing risk collapsing the problem into mere group arrogance if that separation isn't secured first? Which concrete feature of the peer-review scenario should carry the entire illustration—the anonymity and temporal delay of the counterevidence, the distributed nature of justification across panel members, or the policy lock-in that makes reopening costly—and what must that feature both include and exclude so it highlights structural closure rather than psychological bias? In what order should the components unfold, and at which point should I anticipate and head off each predictable misconception: that this is just confirmation bias writ large, that it is identical to groupthink, or that the resolution is simply a normative call for institutional humility? I also want one precise check-for-understanding question I can ask at the end to determine whether the group has grasped the logical structure of second-order closure rather than just adopted a prescriptive posture, along with an explicit statement of what I should openly refuse to formalize or invoke—such as modal-logic treatments of belief revision or specific named responses from the technical literature—so the session remains within conceptual reasoning that does not depend on prior exposure to formal epistemology.
