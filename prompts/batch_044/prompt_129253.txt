I am working through John Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education (1693), and I have been trying to understand a particular tension in his discussion of habit, curiosity, and the tutor's authority. In one part of the text, Locke insists that the mind should never be forced into study through direct command or punitive discipline, arguing that natural curiosity is the best spur to learning and that making a child read or calculate when he is not inclined will only breed a lasting aversion to knowledge. Yet elsewhere he maintains that certain practices—regular writing, physical exercise, and the repetition of social manners—must be imposed rigorously through constant repetition, even against the child's immediate inclination, because reason is not yet strong enough to govern the passions and only habit can secure virtue before understanding arrives.

More specifically, I want help understanding a passage in which Locke compares the education of the mind to the training of the body, suggesting that just as the body is strengthened by gradual, repeated exertion rather than by violent strain, so too the understanding must be exercised through steady practice rather than through sudden, intense demands. He claims that the tutor's art consists in arranging circumstances so that the child comes to desire what is genuinely good for him, rather than in compelling him openly. I am struggling to see how this arrangement of circumstances differs, in practice, from the very kind of hidden coercion that Locke elsewhere condemns when he warns against making learning feel like labor. Is there a conceptual distinction—perhaps between shaping the external environment and shaping the internal will—that resolves this tension, or does Locke's program depend on a form of benevolent manipulation that he never fully acknowledges?

Could you help me understand this passage and the broader argument? In particular, I would like you to explain how Locke distinguishes legitimate habituation from illegitimate compulsion, whether that distinction holds up under scrutiny, and what implications it carries for his broader empiricist claim that all knowledge derives from experience and reflection rather than from innate ideas. I am not looking for a survey of secondary literature, but for a careful conceptual analysis of the reasoning as it stands in the text itself, including whether the tension I have identified is genuine or only apparent.
