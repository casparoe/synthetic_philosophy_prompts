I run a graduate reading group—about ten students, seventy-five minutes, meeting in a seminar room—devoted this term to moral responsibility and the ethics of blame. We have been working through the literature on hypocrisy and standing to blame, and I am dissatisfied with how easily the students can recite the standard claim that a hypocritical blamer lacks standing; what I want is for them to inhabit the difference between two explanations of why hypocrisy undermines standing—one that appeals to moral equality, the idea that the blamer is in no position to elevate herself above the wrongdoer, and one that appeals to insincerity, the idea that the blamer does not genuinely endorse the norm she invokes—before either label is introduced. Please design a structured, in-person role-play or deliberation game, using hidden-information cards and a small-stakes point or token system rather than real money, that arranges for participants to blame others for a specified norm-violation, only to discover through the mechanics of play that some blamers share the very fault they condemn while others are merely performative. The rules should be such that the equality-based failure feels structurally different from the sincerity-based failure: for instance, through a mechanism in which shared fault becomes visible to the group and silences the blamer, versus one in which hidden motives are revealed and the audience withdraws trust rather than moral authority.

The activity should require no lecture up front; the participants should construct the blame dynamics themselves and only notice where they have arrived once the hidden cards are turned over. Provide a precise timeline fitting the session, specify roles or rules that ensure every student participates, and include a debrief that maps each revealed moment onto the corresponding conceptual distinction—where moral equality is violated, where insincerity is detected, and whether the group treats these as the same kind of failure. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong on the first run—perhaps students refusing to blame at all once they anticipate hidden cards, or conflating the two failures in discussion—and tell me how to handle it without collapsing the distinction I want them to feel. Finally, be frank about which feature of standing this activity cannot reproduce—specifically, whether standing can be waived by the wrongdoer or restored through explicit acknowledgment of one's own fault—and indicate what the facilitator must supply verbally to cover that gap.
