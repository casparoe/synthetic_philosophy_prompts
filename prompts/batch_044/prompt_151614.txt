As a journalist covering philosophy of science and epistemology, I have been following debates about whether knowledge is closed under logical entailment, particularly through the lens of Nozick's tracking conditions. I keep encountering a configuration that does not seem to fit the standard taxonomy of closure failures, and I wonder whether it has been precisely carved out. Here is the pattern. A researcher genuinely tracks a theoretical mechanism M: in the nearest worlds where M is false, she does not believe M. She also competently grasps that M strictly entails an observational prediction O. Yet O can fail independently of M. In the closest possible worlds where O is false, M remains true, because O's failure stems from localized measurement interference or instrumental noise that leaves M entirely untouched. So her belief in O lacks the counterfactual sensitivity that her belief in M possesses, even though she has correctly derived O from M and accepts the entailment. This is not the familiar closure-breaking scenario in which knowing the premise requires the premise itself to be false in worlds where the conclusion is false. Here the premise stays true, the entailment holds, the reasoning is sound, and only the conclusion's own counterfactual profile is corrupted by an external factor that is irrelevant to M. Does this specific arrangement—entailment-preserving tracking divergence—have an established name in the literature on epistemic closure? How exactly should it be distinguished from Nozick's original closure objections, from Dretske-style relevant-alternative analyses, and from safety-theoretic diagnoses? And if no precise label exists, what are its boundaries: is it a distinct failure mode of closure, or merely a context-sensitive special case of tracking conditions being evaluated separately for premises and conclusions?
