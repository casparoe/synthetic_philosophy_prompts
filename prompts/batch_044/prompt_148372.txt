I'm taking a graduate seminar on metaphysics of relations, and our instructor has been working through Bradley's regress by testing hypothetical configurations rather than close-reading historical texts. Over the past weeks she has given clear verdicts on whether each setup avoids an infinite regress or collapses into one. Her judgments so far: (1) a simple binary relation R linking two distinct objects a and b, where R is itself a real entity over and above the terms, does not avoid the regress; (2) a structure in which R is not a separate entity but is constituted entirely by the intrinsic natures of a and b taken together, does avoid it; (3) a hierarchical model in which every lower-order relation is grounded in a higher-order one, terminating in a single self-grounding relational form that requires no further ground, does avoid it, though she called the termination mysterious; and (4) a flat circular network in which a relates to b, b to c, and c to a via relations all of the same kind, with no term or relation more fundamental than any other, does not avoid it, since the circle merely relocates dependence without anchoring it.

Next week she will consider a new configuration: three terms A, B, and C are linked by three relations R, S, and T, where R is causal, S is logical, and T is spatial. Each term serves as the fundamental ground for exactly one relation—A grounds R, B grounds S, C grounds T—but each term also participates in the other two relations in a non-grounding capacity. There is no self-relating entity and no homogeneous hierarchy; the dependencies form a closed loop, but each link is anchored in a term that is fundamental with respect to its own relation, and the three relations remain ontologically heterogeneous.

Please predict her verdict—does this loop avoid Bradley's regress?—and do so by first identifying the two broad rules about regress-blocking that are each consistent with her four earlier judgments but explain them differently. For each rule, say exactly what it predicts about this triadic heterogeneous loop. Then give your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with reasons, and specify the one further case that would settle the matter between the two rules. Write your response in American English, and organize it into the following sections: "The two consistent rules" (about 150 words), "Predictions for the triadic loop" (about 150 words), "Best guess and reasoning" (about 150 words), and "The decisive further case" (about 100 words). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
