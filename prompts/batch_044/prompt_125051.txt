I've been following discussions on epistemic communities and belief formation on forums like this for years, and I recently noticed a pattern in both online discourse and organizational decision-making that feels like a cousin to information cascades but isn't quite captured by the standard accounts of rational herding. The pattern concerns not the adoption of a belief itself, but the progressive restructuring of what counts as sufficient reason to reject it, once a critical mass has adopted it.

Here are two concrete cases. In a long technical thread about the safety of a particular machine-learning architecture, the initial disagreement was straightforward: some participants argued that a specific failure mode was likely, others argued it was not. Once the thread accumulated enough agreement that the architecture was "probably safe," the terms of engagement shifted. A later challenger was expected not merely to present evidence of the failure mode, but to show that it wasn't already addressed by the existing literature on interpretability, then to explain why economic incentives wouldn't prevent its exploitation, and finally to address why analogous failures hadn't already appeared in deployed systems. Each of these demands seemed individually reasonable—no single gatekeeper was being irrational—but together they formed a composite burden that no single study or argument could satisfy. The belief hadn't just cascaded; the conditions for its overturn had been collectively redesigned.

A second case: in a university committee evaluating a proposed curriculum change, once the chair signaled support, objections gradually had to meet a layered standard. First, one needed data showing inferior outcomes; then, data controlling for student self-selection; then, a feasible alternative that achieved comparable outcomes without the change. Again, each layer was defensible in isolation, but the accumulation meant that the default had become structurally protected, not merely popular. My question is whether this has an established name in the literature on cascades, social epistemology, or organizational behavior. It differs from ordinary belief polarization, I think, because the underlying belief may remain unchanged and even acknowledged as uncertain; what shifts is the architecture of disconfirmation. It also differs from Popperian immunization, since the protective rules are not introduced ad hoc by a single theorist but emerge distributively from the community's successive attempts to be "careful." Is this a recognized second-order cascade—perhaps a cascade over epistemic thresholds rather than propositions? If not, could one define it precisely, with boundary conditions that distinguish it from status-quo bias, from groupthink, and from the ordinary accumulation of auxiliary hypotheses? I'd particularly value a framing that clarifies whether the phenomenon is genuinely rational—each layer being a reasonable update—and if so, whether that rationality at the individual level still produces a collectively pathological outcome.
