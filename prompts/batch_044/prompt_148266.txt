Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level bioethics seminar (about 14 graduate students) that makes participants feel the conceptual force of abolishing suffering through direct neurobiological intervention—not as an unproblematic moral victory, but as a "gradient-flattening baseline reset" that collapses the contrast conditions required for moral growth, aesthetic appreciation, and autonomous preference-formation. Frame the exercise through a specific conceptual lens: the "contrast-collapse objection," according to which eliminating suffering structurally undermines the distinctions between remedy and enhancement, earned satisfaction and engineered contentment, and chosen adversity versus imposed harm. The idea should emerge through rules and roles before the facilitator names it; do not include an upfront lecture.

Specify the setting and deliverables: split the group into a five-member "Global Neuroethics Council" (assigned roles: abolitionist advocate, cultural preservationist, future-generation proxy, clinical engineer, autonomy defender) and a nine-member "Future Tribunal" who inhabit the world the Council designs. The Council must vote by consensus on three binding parameters—(1) the pain-elimination threshold, (2) the pleasure-baseline calibration, and (3) the opt-out architecture—while facing "scenario cards" revealed after each vote that show preserving any valued contrast requires retaining some form of negative experience. The Tribunal prepares interrogatory questions and, in the final 20 minutes, cross-examines the Council under a structural constraint: Tribunal members must argue entirely from within the engineered bliss-baseline and therefore cannot invoke concepts like "struggle," "earned achievement," or "overcoming."

Include a non-monetary stakes system—perhaps "legacy coherence points" awarded by the Tribunal, with penalties when Council members violate their own earlier parameter choices—and provide a minute-by-minute timeline. Then include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the contrast-collapse objection, predicts what will go wrong the first time (for example, the Council collapsing the problem into utilitarian aggregation, or the Tribunal treating the scenario as pure science fiction), and states frankly which feature of the idea—likely the first-person phenomenological opacity of a suffering-free state to those who have never experienced suffering—the activity can only approximate and the facilitator must supply. Write the response in American English, keep it focused, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to obscure technical literature.
