I was discussing with my friends about Dennett's idea of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, intentional. One of my friends works at a logistics firm that runs autonomous sorting robots in a warehouse, and they have this weird institutional practice: whenever a robot damages a package, they file two different reports. The first is an engineering report listing sensor drift, algorithmic latency, pure design-stance stuff. The second is a behavioral review where they replay the robot's decision tree and literally ask whether the unit misjudged the obstacle or chose a riskier path, then talk about retraining it like it's learning moral lessons, even though it's just gradent descent on a loss function. Meanwhile if the robot actually hurts a worker, the legal framework treats it as defective equipment—pure physical or design stance for liability.

So what philosophical commitments about real patterns does this whole dual-report system presuppose? Does using intentional-stance language in the review commit them to thinking there's a genuine pattern at that level, or can they stay instrumentalist about it? And is the practice even coherant—can you blame a choice in one report and assign manufacturer liability in another without contradiction? Or does that just show the institution is philosophically underdetermined, compatible with both Dennett's view and a stricter eliminativist stance? Also, what would have to change if they decided the intentional stance never tracks real patterns in machines—would the behavioral review become total nonsense, or could they keep running it as a useful heuristic?
