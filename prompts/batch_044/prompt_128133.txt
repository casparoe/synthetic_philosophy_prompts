I'm marking final-year undergraduate essays for a philosophy of religion module. The exam question asked students to analyse Hume's argument concerning miracles, specifically requiring them to explain the relationship between the probabilistic reasoning in the first part and the psychological and historical explanation offered in the second part. The prompt also asked whether the second part introduces a contextual factor—namely, that the credibility of testimony varies with the religious enthusiasm of the witnesses—that destabilises the uniform prior assumed in the first part, or whether the two parts remain strictly independent.

Here is the question as it appeared on the exam paper:

"Analyse Hume's treatment of miracles in his essay on the subject. First, explain the reasoning by which he claims that testimony can never outweigh uniform experience. Second, explain the motives—psychological, social, and historical—that he identifies in the second part as explaining why miracle reports nevertheless spread. Third, assess whether these motives undermine the uniformity assumption required by the first part: does the fact that witnesses are often motivated by wonder, narrative pleasure, or the consolidation of authority mean that the prior assessment of testimony should itself be context-dependent, rather than uniformly weighted against the miracle?"

Below is one student's answer, approximately 400 words. Please assess it: suggest an appropriate mark (for instance, on a UK first / upper-second / lower-second / third scale, or as a percentage), and provide point-by-point comments that justify the grade. Identify any subtle conflations, misattributed positions, or steps that do not follow, particularly regarding Hume's qualification about comparative miraculousness and the specific range of motives (wonder, the pleasure of surprising narratives, religious authority) discussed in the second part. Do not treat minor imprecision as structural failure, but do flag where the architecture of the argument has been distorted.

In your evaluation, please state explicitly where your reasoning is most vulnerable to error and what specific consideration or counter-argument would lead you to revise your assessment of this answer. Write in British English.

Student answer:

Hume's argument is that miracles are violations of the laws of nature, which are established by uniform experience across countless observations. Because the evidence for a law of nature is always far greater than the evidence for any single reported exception, it is never rational to accept testimony for a miracle over the law itself. Hume treats this as essentially a probabilistic calculation: the prior probability of a miracle is effectively zero, so no amount of human testimony can make belief in it rational. He therefore concludes that testimony can never establish a miracle.

In the second part, Hume explains why people nevertheless believe in miracles. He argues that religious authorities promote miracle stories primarily to consolidate their institutional power, and that ordinary people accept these reports because of emotional needs and social pressure rather than evidence. This psychological explanation complements the first part by showing that belief in miracles is driven by non-rational motives. Because the second part only explains the persistence of error, it does not weaken the probabilistic argument; the two parts are independent, with one addressing reason and the other addressing human weakness. The credibility of witnesses might be lower in religious contexts, but this does not affect the formal weighing of testimony against uniform experience, which remains constant regardless of context.
