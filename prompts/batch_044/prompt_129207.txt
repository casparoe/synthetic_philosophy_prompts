I've been working through the Epicurean argument in a philosophy of death seminar, and I think I've pushed it into a corner where it seems to prove far more than Epicurus intended, but I can't locate the exact misstep. Here is the chain as I have it. (1) For an event to harm a particular subject, that subject must exist at the time the event occurs and be in a position to receive or register the harm; harm is essentially a relation that requires temporal co-presence of subject and event. (2) Death is the permanent cessation of existence, so at every moment after death there is no subject present to receive any harm; therefore death itself is not bad for the one who dies, and neither are any posthumous events—ruined reputations, uncompleted projects, broken promises. (3) Now compare two possible lives for what we intuitively call the same individual: Life A lasts thirty years and ends in death; Life B lasts eighty years and ends in death. In Life A, there are fifty years of potential experience, achievement, and pleasure that never occur. But those fifty years are precisely years in which the individual of Life A does not exist—he is dead—so by (1) and (2) the absence of those experiences is not received by anyone and hence is not a harm to him. (4) In Life B, by contrast, all years of experience occur during the individual's existence, so there is no comparable absence that goes unreceived. (5) Therefore, from the first-person welfare perspective, there is no rational basis for preferring Life B to Life A: dying young is not worse for you than dying old, since the "missing" years in the shorter life are years in which you are not there to be harmed by their absence. (6) This extends further: an infant who dies has lost nothing that matters to her, because every year she might have lived is a year in which she would not exist to suffer the loss. (7) The standard reply is the deprivation account—death is bad because it deprives the living person of future goods. But if deprivation requires a subject who is actually deprived, and the dead are not subjects, then we seem forced to locate the harm in anticipation, while the person is still alive. Yet anticipating a loss that, by (1), will never actually harm the anticipator looks less like a genuine harm and more like a cognitive error. So I am stuck between keeping the co-presence principle and accepting that premature death is never worse for the one who dies, which feels absurd. Which step should I reject—(1), (4), (5), or the framing of deprivation in (7)—and what does giving it up commit me to? I want to preserve the intuition that a premature death really is a greater misfortune for the victim, but I want to know whether I must abandon the existence-receipt principle entirely or whether there is a more surgical repair that lets me keep it for posthumous harms while blocking the comparison between Life A and Life B.
