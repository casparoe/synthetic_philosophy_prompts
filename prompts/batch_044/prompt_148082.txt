I'm chairing this weird little working group on 'wellness and productivity' for our college—dont ask me how I got roped in, probably because I mentioned Weber once at a faculty meeting and now I'm the 'ideas guy.' Anyway, we're drafting labor guidelines for grad students and there's this big fight about whether to hard-cap weekly hours at thirty-five, with no weekend emails and a firm cutoff at 8pm. The administration loves it for optics, but one senior colleague—very old school—sent this long rant saying scholarship isnt shift work, and that putting a ceiling on labor insults the ascetic dignity of the vocation; he said if you clock-watch, you've already betrayed philosophy's calling.

Meanwhile a junior faculty member who did her PhD in Tokyo mentioned, almost in passing, that in her old department leaving before your advisor was treated like a moral failure, and she knew someone who literally collapsed from exhaustion—she never named the phenomenon, but I knew what she meant. So now I'm stuck writing the recommendation. If a grad student genuinely wants to work sixty hours because they feel called to it, do I have the right to protect them from themselves? Or is that just paternalism? And more confusingly: if the culture systematically rewards overwork—if your letter writers praise 'dedication' measured in sleep lost—can anyone's choice to overwork really be free? I feel like there's a deeper issue about whether leisure is a negative right (leave me alone) or a positive entitlement (the institution must guarantee it), but I can't quite map it onto this mess. Any thoughts?
