I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I might be mangling the terms, but I'm writing the eulogy for my old teacher Elias, who died last week. He was a logical positivist to the bone and loved Ayer's argument that talk of life after death isn't false in the ordinary sense but fails the conditions for being meaningful at all -- not an empirical mistake but a category mistake, like asking about the color of Wednesday. Half the room tomorrow will be philosophers who know that distinction cold; the other half are his grandkids who just need to feel like it's okay to miss him. Please write the speech, but keep it as one continous flow of prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, just like a long letter. Don't cite anyone or list books. The philosophy has to do structural work: use Ayer's verificationist framing -- and the fact that Elias himself had that near-death episode years back and still said it was just neurons firing -- to shape what can honestly be said about absence. I want comfort without false promises, and I want the tension between experiencing something that feels like a glimpse and refusing to grant it cognitive status to be the spine of the thing, not decoration. There's probly a typo in this ask but you get the idea: no pretending he's watching, no lecture either, just a speech that lands for both halves of the room.
