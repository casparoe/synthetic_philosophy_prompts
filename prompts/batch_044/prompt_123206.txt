I am a retired developmental biologist who has recently begun participating in a philosophy seminar for former natural scientists, and next month I have agreed to lead a single ninety-minute session on Aristotle's doctrine of natural slavery as it is developed in the first book of the Politics. Our group consists of physicists, chemists, and engineers with no training in ancient philosophy, though they are accustomed to reasoning about functional organization and emergent hierarchies in biological systems. Rather than delivering a historical lecture, I wish to design an explanatory arc that treats the doctrine as a live conceptual problem: specifically, I want to build the session around a synthetic carrying example drawn from my own field—namely, the distinction between a pluripotent stem cell and a terminally differentiated cell that has lost the capacity to generate other tissue types—to illuminate Aristotle's claim that certain human beings are naturally suited to serve as instruments of another's deliberation. I am uncertain whether this biological analogy genuinely clarifies his normative argument about justice in the polis, or whether it merely projects modern cellular determinism onto an ancient ethical framework. Could you therefore map out the precise order in which the conceptual components must be introduced—beginning with the teleological view of nature, proceeding through the household analogy, and only then introducing the biological parallel—so that each layer depends correctly on the previous one?

Furthermore, I need to anticipate the misconceptions this particular audience will form: I expect they will conflate natural slavery with genetic determinism, interpret the absence of deliberative capacity as a measurable cognitive deficit analogous to psychometric testing, and assume that Aristotle is offering an empirical biological classification rather than a normative political thesis. At exactly which point in the sequence should each misconception be headed off—particularly before or after the biological analogy is introduced? I also need guidance on what synthetic or canonical example should carry the core argument if the stem-cell analogy proves misleading; whether I should retain Aristotle's own analogies of the household and the body, or construct a different scenario altogether. Please include a check for understanding at the end that would reveal whether the participants have grasped the normative structure of the argument rather than merely cataloguing an offensive historical doctrine, and state explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the medieval reception in Islamic and Christian scholasticism—while openly acknowledging that omission.
