You are serving as an independent philosophy-of-mind consultant to a joint university Neuroethics and Institutional Review Board. Before drafting, please spend a moment in careful analysis: map the core philosophical tension, identify the precise point where the researchers' argument is most vulnerable, and outline the concrete institutional conditions that could answer that vulnerability without collapsing into either blanket approval or simple regulatory box-checking.

Your deliverable is a formal written opinion for circulation before next Thursday's meeting. The board must reach a decision—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—on a proposed Phase I trial of what the investigators call a phenomenal-transparency prosthetic (PTP) for patients with complete locked-in syndrome.

The case is this. The PTP uses predictive-processing algorithms to generate a real-time representational model of a patient's conscious experience, displayed to clinical staff. The research team explicitly works within the theoretical framework that phenomenal consciousness functions as a constructed user-interface: not a transparent window onto mental reality, but an evolved representational layer that serves predictive and homeostatic functions. They argue that because first-person report is already mediated by this interface, the PTP does not diminish autonomy when it substitutes algorithmically reconstructed interoceptive predictions for verbal or motor self-report; in fact, they claim it offers caregivers access closer to the sub-personal processes that actually drive experience.

The consent problem is acute. Patients can communicate only through the PTP itself, so every stage of authorization—disclosure, comprehension, voluntary agreement—is mediated by the very representational system the trial studies. The researchers maintain that this circularity is harmless: if consciousness is genuinely a user-interface without direct epistemic transparency, then all first-person consent is already mediated representation, and replacing one channel with a more direct predictive readout changes nothing essential about autonomy. A dissenting board member argues precisely the opposite: if the interface genuinely lacks the kind of transparency that makes self-knowledge authoritative, then asking the interface to authorize its own clinical use is not consent at all but a kind of representational ventriloquism. The user is not speaking; the model is speaking in the user's voice.

The board's standing policy permits reasonable accommodation of novel consent frameworks, but it requires that any approval include concrete safeguards, a defined review timeline, and an explicit engagement with the strongest philosophical objection rather than a mere citation of regulatory compliance.

Please draft the memo with the following structure and content:

1. A clear bottom-line recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions), with a specific time frame for initial implementation or for mandatory review.
2. A serious, non-caricatured exposition of the researchers' user-interface framing: what it gets right about the nature of conscious access, why it might improve clinical decision-making, and why the circular consent structure is not obviously fatal.
3. An equally serious account of the dissenting objection, identifying the exact premise or inference in the researchers' reasoning that is weakest—particularly the move from "all consciousness is mediated" to "therefore mediation by this particular algorithmic interface preserves autonomy."
4. Concrete conditions or procedural safeguards that respond directly to that weakness (for example, an independent philosophical audit of the interface's representational fidelity, a phased consent-verification protocol using a non-PTP communication channel where physiologically possible, a hard stop at six months, or a requirement that consent be reauthorized after the patient experiences the interface's outputs rather than before).
5. A brief closing that explains why legal or regulatory definitions of informed consent, while necessary, are insufficient here: the memo should address the philosophical problem of mediated authorization on its own terms.

Write in a formal, institutional voice accessible to board members without specialist training in philosophy of mind. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reference lists, or appeals to specific page numbers or passages from named texts. You may refer to general theoretical positions—predictive-processing accounts, interface theories of perception, sub-personal versus personal-level explanation—without attributing precise quotations. Avoid treating this as a personal advisory letter; the document should read as an actionable institutional opinion with a definitive recommendation and a timeline.
