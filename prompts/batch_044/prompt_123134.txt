I have been thinking on my own about whether we genuinely wrong anyone when we adopt long-term policies—say, about energy extraction, agricultural scale, or carbon emissions—that will shape the world for centuries, knowing that those policies will also determine which particular people come to exist. To test my intuitions, I constructed a comparison that keeps circling back on me: Path A accelerates industrial agriculture and high immigration, producing a large future population that lives in a climate-stressed world where many suffer serious harms; Path B slows development, produces a smaller, more stable population in a healthier environment, but none of the people from Path A ever come into being. Under Path A, it seems impossible to name a specific victim who was made worse off relative to her own baseline, because under Path B that individual never lives at all. Yet Path A still feels like the morally worse choice, and I cannot tell whether that feeling tracks a real wrong or just a confusion about comparing worlds. Before I try to defend any position, I want a rigorous specification of what any adequate theory of obligations to future, identity-contingent populations must actually deliver.

Write the full list of desiderata: the concrete cases the theory must classify correctly, including not only the two-path comparison above but also cases where the same individuals exist under both policies yet with different welfare levels, cases where total population size differs substantially, and cases where cultural or communal continuity is broken even if individual suffering remains minimal; the distinctions it must preserve—between wronging a particular person and producing an impersonally worse outcome, between failing to benefit someone and actively harming them, and between the moral significance of existence itself versus the quality of that existence; the empirical or logical constraints it must accommodate, such as the causal link between environmental conditions and reproductive or migratory outcomes, and the problem of comparing welfare across non-overlapping sets of people who share no common baseline; and flag explicitly any pair or subset of these desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether a theory can consistently require that all moral wrongs be person-affecting while also generating strong obligations to avoid climate damage that only affects people whose very existence depends on the harmful choice being made. Keep the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and the tensions among them.
