I've been trying to map out a recurring lifecycle in quantitative modeling, and I wonder whether philosophers of science have isolated it as a distinct phenomenon with its own name. The lifecycle begins with a foundational assumption that is obviously false in reality—say, that agents can compute optimal strategies without cognitive cost, or that a biological trait evolves without interaction effects from other traits. At the outset, this is framed as a disposable simplification, a computational convenience that could be replaced by a more realistic mechanism once resources allow. But as the framework encounters empirical failures, the response is not to relax or replace the false premise, but to build corrective structures around it—adjustment coefficients, scope restrictions, or nested sub-models—that are formally derived from the original unrealistic setup.

After several rounds of such patching, the initial falsehood is no longer a removable approximation. The corrections are structurally calibrated to it, so excising the premise would not merely refine predictions; it would destabilize the entire architecture of patches that depends on it being literally true within the formal system. Despite this, the literature typically continues to describe the original assumption as merely an idealization, treating it as if it were still temporary scaffolding rather than a load-bearing pillar embedded deep in the machinery. I am familiar with broad discussions about unrealistic assumptions in scientific models, and with the idea that predictive success can justify premises that do not describe reality. But I want to know whether there is a specific concept for this migration path—the way a false assumption becomes architecturally indispensable through successive defensive layering, even while being rhetorically maintained as a harmless fiction. Does it have an established label, perhaps related to path dependence in theory revision or to a particular weakness in predictive-justification arguments? If not, how should one delineate it precisely from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis adjustments or from simple institutional inertia in a research program, and what boundary cases would show that it is genuinely a separate failure mode rather than just a dramatic instance of one of those?
