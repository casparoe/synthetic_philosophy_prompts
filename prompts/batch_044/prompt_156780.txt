I'm designing a focused three-year research program—roughly the scale of a dissertation or a small grant—on the conceptual fallout from Jackson's knowledge-argument about Mary. I don't want a survey of the literature; I want a defended agenda that ranks which open problem clusters deserve scarce time and which are downstream refinements that won't move the core debate.

Consider three interdependent clusters that have emerged since Mary's case was introduced:

A. The phenomenal-concept strategy: whether phenomenal properties are presented through a genuinely non-physical mode of conceptualization, and if so, whether that mode is metaphysically compatible with physicalism or instead collapses into a form of property dualism that physicalists wanted to avoid.

B. The ability-knowledge boundary: whether Mary's post-release cognitive change is best analyzed as acquiring new propositional knowledge (knowing-that) or rather as gaining practical, acquaintance, or recognitional capacities, and whether this distinction can be sharpened without circular appeal to the very phenomenal states under discussion.

C. The extrapolation problem: whether whatever gap Mary's case reveals—if it reveals one—generalizes to attributing consciousness across species, artificial architectures, or pharmacologically altered brains, and whether settling Mary's case is a prerequisite for constructing non-circular criteria for such attributions.

Your task: rank A, B, and C in the order they should be tackled within this program. For each position in the ranking, defend the placement by addressing four criteria explicitly: (1) dependency— which other clusters cannot be resolved without this one being settled first, and which depend on it only weakly or not at all; (2) tractability—whether existing conceptual tools are sufficient or whether progress requires inventing distinctions that are not yet stable; (3) payoff—what result here would count as decisive for the physicalism debate versus merely taxonomic, and what result in the other clusters would change your mind about the ranking; and (4) resource sensitivity—if the program had to be cut from three years to two, which cluster should be dropped and why its resolution would not alter the conclusions reached in the remaining two.

The deliverable should be an agenda, not a bibliography or citation map: specify the ordering, the criteria governing it, the trade-offs you are accepting, and what would have to be shown to reverse your prioritization.
