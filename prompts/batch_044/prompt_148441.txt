I've been turning over the old Damascus story on my own—the one where a traveler in Baghdad is frightened by Death, borrows a horse, and rides all night to Damascus to escape, only for Death to say he was surprised to see the traveler in Baghdad because they had an appointment in Damascus that very evening. The traveler ran straight into the thing he feared. Lately I've started to think this isn't just a spooky anecdote but reveals a structural problem about prophecy and human choice that generalizes badly. Here is the argument I have built, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) In the story, the traveler learns he is in danger and takes deliberate action—riding to Damascus—to avoid it.
(2) He experiences that ride as a free, self-directed choice: he picks the destination, he urges the horse, he believes he is escaping.
(3) Yet Damascus was already the appointed meeting place, so his free-seeming choice delivered him exactly to the spot he intended to avoid.
(4) Now suppose he had never learned of the danger. Then he would have stayed in Baghdad, and the meeting in Damascus would never have occurred.
(5) So the prophecy's fulfillment depended causally on the traveler's learning about the prophecy.
(6) Generalizing: for any prophecy P about a specific agent A, if learning P changes A's behavior in the way that fulfills P, then P's being true depends on P being communicated to A.
(7) But if a claim's truth depends on its being told to the very person it describes, then it is not predicting an independent future; it is functioning as a script that generates its own fulfillment.
(8) Therefore either prophecies are kept secret (and remain unfulfilled predictions about an independent future), or they are revealed and become self-fulfilling scripts with no predictive power of their own.
(9) Yet the Damascus story treats the prophecy as revealing a predetermined appointment that existed before the traveler heard it—not as creating the appointment through the telling.
(10) So we must reject one of the premises, but I want to keep both the idea that the traveler genuinely chose (2) and the idea that the prophecy was genuinely about an independent future (9).

Where does this chain break, if it breaks? Is there an equivocation between causing fulfillment and being part of the conditions under which fulfillment occurs? Or is the argument valid, and the real cost is giving up either genuine agency in the face of prophecy or the idea that prophecy can ever be about a fixed, independent future? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (2) and (9) together and pay the price elsewhere, but I want to know exactly what I am paying and whether there is a hidden slide between (5) and (6) that saves both.
