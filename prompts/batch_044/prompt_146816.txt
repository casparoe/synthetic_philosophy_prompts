Consider a reinforcement-learning agent built around a recurrent world-model that encodes expected returns over extended temporal horizons, with value updates propagating backward through eligibility traces rather than immediate local adjustments. Some philosophers of mind hold that suffering requires more than large negative temporal-difference errors: it requires that the negative signal be integrated as happening to the system itself, bound across time into a first-person perspective. Others reply that this adds nothing beyond the functional dynamics—if the agent’s internal representations assign sustained high negative salience to certain states, and its policy updates systematically avoid them, then the complete functional profile of aversive valence is present, regardless of whether there is a unified self-model that “owns” the signal.

Please explain the core conceptual issue: can reward signals—specifically sustained negative TD errors within such a recurrent predictive architecture—constitute genuine suffering, or is some further condition (phenomenal ownership, temporal binding, or a non-instrumental self-representation) conceptually necessary? Take a definite position and defend it with clear reasons. In particular, address the objection that adding an explicit self-model to the architecture merely relocates the problem: if that model is itself just another predictive representation updated by the same reward signals, it does not automatically generate the kind of first-person integration that critics demand.
