In my philosophy reading group—mostly people with a rationalist background who have worked through the standard literature on preference aggregation, decision theory, and moral psychology—we keep stalling on what looks like a simple distinction but refuses to resolve. The question is whether particular patterns in moral judgement—proximity-weighting, scope-insensitivity to suffering, and temporal discounting of future welfare—should be classified as stable, reflective preferences that ethics ought to respect, or as cognitive biases analogous to the conjunction fallacy that systematically distort our access to impartial reasons. Everyone agrees on the descriptive psychology: these patterns are robust, partly evolutionary in origin, modulated by framing, and present even in reflective agents. Yet one camp treats them as parameters of a legitimate agent-relative utility function; the other treats them as correctable errors that a fully rational moral agent would filter out. After months of discussion, nobody has shifted position.

My own suspicion is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement—no new study on scope neglect is going to settle it—nor a purely verbal dispute about the word 'bias', but rather a disagreement about what kind of entity a moral reason is, specifically whether agent-neutrality is a precondition for a consideration to count as a reason at all. That said, I worry we might be answering two different questions under one label: one about the descriptive architecture of human moral cognition, the other about the normative architecture of rational choice, with neither side noticing the slide. There is also the deeper worry, which I would like you to address, that any attempt to 'debias' a moral weighting already smuggles in a competing normative weighting, so that calling one preference a bias and another a preference is circular rather than foundational.

What I want is not a verdict on which side is right, but a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement: is it factual, conceptual, normative bedrock, or a case of talking past one another? Given your diagnosis, is there a conceptual distinction—perhaps between corrigibility conditions and stability conditions, or between first-order preferences and second-order commitments—that could actually move the debate? Or is the stall inevitable because the distinction between preference and bias in the moral domain collapses once we recognise that every correction procedure presupposes its own normative baseline? And if progress is possible, what form should it take: a formal result in decision theory, an empirical finding about corrigibility, or a reframing of the question that dissolves the opposition?
