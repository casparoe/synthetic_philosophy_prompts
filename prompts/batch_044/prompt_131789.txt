I'm preparing questions for a podcast episode aimed at an audience familiar with rationalist writing on decision theory and induction. The guest is an unnamed philosopher defending what she terms the "malign prior" view: not the familiar worry that Solomonoff induction predicts a deceptive demon, but the claim that the universal prior is structurally hostile to aggregative consequentialism because it embeds consequentialist agents at every level of description complexity. She holds that Kolmogorov simplicity weights hypotheses by program length rather than by moral salience, so a very short program generating vast, structured suffering can carry higher prior weight than the longer program corresponding to our observed world. From this she derives a "nested aggregation" problem—any consequentialist who takes universal induction seriously must consider that every competing explanation for her observations may contain its own embedded reasoners with standing, and the prior offers no principled way to discount them relative to surface-level observers. I want eight to ten interview questions that avoid letting her retreat to "that's just induction" or "weights are subjective." Specifically, I need at least one question pressing how she distinguishes her embedded-agent worry from standard simulation arguments; one probing whether the prior's simplicity weighting is genuinely doing the moral work or whether she is smuggling in an independent axiology about suffering density; and one asking what decision rule, if any, survives her analysis for an agent who still wants to act consequentially. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer—for example, if she invokes anthropic reasoning to filter embedded agents, the follow-up should ask why that filter does not collapse into privileging hypotheses that match our own descriptive complexity, thereby undermining the universality she claims for the prior. Keep the tone formal and philosophical rather than technical; I want the questions to feel like they belong in a serious colloquium Q&A rather than a pop-science interview.
