I am preparing a sequence of interview questions for a philosophy podcast. Next week's guest is a contemporary philosopher who defends a view she calls pure-absence tranquilism: the claim that only the total absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, and that every apparently positive mental state—joy, wonder, intellectual satisfaction—is at best an instrumental interruption of an underlying structural craving rather than a genuine good in its own right. I want eight to ten questions that press her well beyond restating the position. Please include: at least one question about whether the view can accommodate the intuition that a life of sustained mild contentment is preferable to a life of severe suffering punctuated only by brief relief, together with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—probably an appeal to a net-absence calculus that refuses to count positive states as goods; at least one question probing the metaphysics of craving—whether she conceives it as a representational failure, a somatic tension, or a normative misalignment—and how that conception determines whether practices such as focused meditation or targeted pharmacological suppression constitute authentic tranquility rather than mere masking, with a follow-up anticipating her reply; one question pressing the problem of altruistic motivation: if reducing another person's suffering is valuable only because it eliminates craving in them, then acting on their behalf seems to require a form of care or attachment that the view should classify as itself a kind of craving; and one concluding question asking what kind of empirical finding—perhaps from affective neuroscience or comparative studies of contemplative practice—would genuinely threaten pure-absence tranquilism rather than being reinterpreted as evidence of hidden craving. For each question, briefly indicate the standard defense she is likely to offer and propose a concise follow-up. Please write in American English, in a formal and polished register, and do not include requests for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
