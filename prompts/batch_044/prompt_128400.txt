I'm part of a philosophy of science study group, and after a long session on theory change we kept circling back to the same deadlock: does structural realism actually solve the pessimistic meta-induction, or does it just disguise an anti-realist commitment behind mathematical formalism? I want to see that deadlock rendered as a genuine dialogue rather than a list of bullet points. Write a conversation between two equally sharp interlocutors—call them Mara and Jonas—standing outside the seminar room. Mara should defend a substantive structural realist position: she claims that the retention of specific mathematical structures across scientific revolutions—such as the Hamiltonian framework surviving the transition to quantum mechanics, or the Poisson equation reappearing as the weak-field limit of Einstein's field equations—is not merely a pragmatic fact about predictive success but indicates that those structures map onto real relational features of the world, even when the entities we initially associated with them are abandoned. Jonas should reply with a rigorous version of the pessimistic meta-induction, granting the formal continuity but arguing that preserving an equation's form is fully compatible with every physical interpretation and ontological referent changing, so that structural realism collapses into a sophisticated instrumentalism unless Mara can explain why structural correspondence constrains ontology rather than just organizing phenomena. He should press her specifically on whether the predictive novelty of derivations within retained structures—say, deriving new gravitational effects from the metric formalism—actually supports realism about structure, or merely shows that mathematicians are good at extending formalisms independently of reference. Mara should respond directly to that challenge with an argument about cross-domain constraint or explanatory unification, not with appeals to scientific progress in general. They must respond to each other's actual points, avoid strawmen, and not ask for citations, reading lists, or authoritative texts; the exchange should live entirely in the conceptual machinery.
