In a recent departmental colloquium, a colleague offered the following summary of how the doctrine of double effect bears on the standard trolley problems, and I want to know whether I can rely on it. She said that the doctrine permits the switch case because the death of the one is a mere side-effect of diverting the threat, whereas it forbids the footbridge case because the large man’s death is intended as the means by which the trolley is stopped. She then introduced what she called the “tactile prohibition”: she claimed that the only genuine distinction at work is not intentionality but physical contact, since the doctrine allows precisely those killings that maximize lives saved so long as the agent does not directly touch the victim, which makes it not a deontological constraint at all but a socially calibrated heuristic for act-consequentialism that suppresses visceral aversion to direct harm. How fair is this summary? Please audit it claim by claim: identify what a defender of the traditional doctrine—say, in the Thomist formulation—would accept as accurate, where the summary conflates distinct conditions (particularly the means/side-effect distinction with a prohibition on contact), and whether the conclusion that double effect collapses into consequentialism actually follows once those errors are corrected. I want a committed response: take a definite position on whether these are harmless simplifications or substantive distortions, defend that judgment with reference to the doctrine’s four conditions as traditionally stated, and tell me whether the “tactile prohibition” captures anything the doctrine genuinely requires or is simply an artifact of misreading the proportionality and intention conditions.
