I take seriously the Kierkegaardian diagnosis—developed in his writings on the modern age—that the press and the abstract category of the public do not merely spread falsehood but structurally dissolve the conditions for authentic commitment. On the reading I am working with, the core claim is not sociological but formal: genuine commitment requires a qualitative, first-person relation to a possibility for which one takes responsibility, whereas the public transforms every issue into an anonymous, quantitative aggregation in which no particular individual holds the view. The press accelerates this leveling by collapsing the temporal gap between event and commentary, so that reflection is replaced by continuous, unowned reaction. I have come to think this can be generalized into a precise thesis: any communication technology that drives the ratio of information-throughput to deliberation-time below a critical threshold will necessarily erode the conditions for committed belief, regardless of content accuracy, because it removes the temporal interval in which a first-person stance can be formed.

What would count against this view? I want more than the obvious rejoinder that people still form strong commitments in mass societies or via digital media. I want to know: which specific features of the diagnosis depend on a particular psychology of decision—say, a model of belief-formation as requiring explicit, sequential deliberation—that might not be universal? Could a society maintain high deliberative density despite high information velocity through institutional filters that restore temporal gaps, and if so, does that defeat the view or merely show that technology is neutral and the danger is institutional? Is there an empirical pattern in the history of print, telegraphy, broadcasting, or networked communication where increased media velocity correlates with a measurable attenuation of commitment—understood as willingness to bear personal cost for a belief—rather than just a change in the object of commitment? And is the view at risk of being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every apparent counterexample, such as a committed activist formed through rapid online circulation, is explained away by saying the commitment was actually formed in private despite the technology? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including what defenders of the view would themselves concede as damaging, and an honest assessment of whether insulating the claim against counterexample is a virtue or a vice for a thesis about structural conditions.
