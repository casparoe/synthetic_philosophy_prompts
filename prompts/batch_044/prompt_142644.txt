I'm auditing a seminar on philosophy of history, and last week two students—Mara and Julian—got into a sustained argument about whether multi-causal explanations in history are ever genuinely overdetermined, or whether that label merely masks a failure to specify the right explanatory level. They were using the dissolution of the Western Roman imperial structure around 476 CE as their running example, but the dispute quickly turned conceptual rather than archival.

Mara opened by claiming that once you identify four or five structurally independent clusters—say, fiscal crisis, military overstretch, administrative decentralization, and sustained frontier pressure—each cluster is sufficient on its own to destabilize an imperial system of that scale. Consequently, if you run a counterfactual removing just one cluster, such as the fiscal crisis, the empire still falls, which means no single cluster does any real explanatory work. Julian objected that this confuses token-level overdetermination with type-level redundancy. Historical explanations, he said, do not cite "fiscal crisis in general" but a specific fiscal trajectory interacting with specific institutional responses; alter that specific trajectory and you change the timing, the geography of fragmentation, and whether the Eastern half survives intact, so the factor is genuinely difference-making even if the outcome remains broadly "collapse."

Mara replied that Julian was smuggling counterfactual dependence through the back door by quietly enriching the outcome description—from "collapse by 476 in the West" to "collapse eventually everywhere." She then introduced a distinction she called thin versus thick overdetermination: thin cases involve multiple causes of the same kind competing to produce an identical effect, whereas thick cases involve heterogeneous structural conditions that jointly compose a single sufficient set. In thick cases, she argued, ranking causes by importance is incoherent because the conditions are not substitutable parts but interlocking prerequisites of one macro-cause. Julian countered that even in thick cases the interlocking parts have distinct modal profiles: some are brittle (if removed, the remaining set collapses quickly) and others are resilient (if removed, the remaining parts compensate). Therefore, you can still assign explanatory weights by measuring how much the other conditions must adjust to preserve the outcome, which is precisely what historians do when they argue, for instance, that administrative decentralization was more decisive than frontier pressure because the latter only became fatal given the former.

They asked the seminar leader to referee, but she only said it depends on one's philosophy of science. I could not tell whether Mara's thin/thick distinction actually solves the problem or just renames it, or whether Julian's brittle-versus-resilient framing secretly reintroduces the very substitutability that Mara denies. Could you walk through their exchange step by step—who is right about the counterfactual test, about the thin/thick distinction, about whether explanatory weight requires substitutability—and say who is more right overall? Also, point out what both of them are missing, particularly regarding whether historical explanation aims primarily at counterfactual prediction, narrative coherence, or some other purpose altogether.
