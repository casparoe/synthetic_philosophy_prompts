I have just come out of a long and rather frustrating argument with a friend about al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism, and I want to crystallize the crux of our disagreement into an exam question for my upper-level philosophy of religion course. Our dispute was not about whether God is the ultimate cause of everything—on that we agree—but about whether al-Ghazālī denies secondary causation altogether or only denies that philosophers can demonstrate a logically necessary connection between cause and effect. My friend advanced a reconstruction she called “inert potency”: the claim that fire possesses a real but permanently non-actualizing dispositional property (quwwa) that is activated only by an independent divine volition, so that secondary causes are not eliminated but rendered structurally passive. I am skeptical that this survives contact with the Seventeenth Discussion of Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, yet I want the exam to compel students to adjudicate it philosophically rather than merely labeling it.

Please write a 90-minute essay question for third-year undergraduates who have read selections from Tahāfut al-Falāsifa and Ibn Rushd’s response in course translation. The question must be unanswerable by reciting the standard “fire and cotton” example or by summarizing occasionalism versus secondary causation in generic terms. Instead, it should require students to:
(i) distinguish logical necessity from metaphysical necessity in al-Ghazālī’s critique of the falāsifa;
(ii) evaluate whether the “inert potency” reconstruction is textually and philosophically coherent with his account of ʿāda (the habitual course of nature);
(iii) explain whether allowing fire an intrinsic but never self-actualizing property solves the problem of observed regularity or merely smuggles hidden necessary connections back into the created order;
(iv) present one precise objection that a defender of secondary causation—such as Ibn Rushd—would raise specifically against the “inert potency” reading; and
(v) defend a considered position on whether al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism is best interpreted as an epistemological caution about human knowledge of causes or as a metaphysical denial that created things possess genuine causal efficacy.

Then provide:
- A model answer of approximately 600 words that makes specific philosophical moves for each of the five points, not merely naming concepts.
- A marking scheme out of 20 marks, distributing marks across the five points with weights that reflect difficulty (suggested: 3, 5, 5, 4, 3), and including for each point a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode that should cost marks (for example, conflating logical with causal necessity, treating quwwa as self-actualizing, or presenting Ibn Rushd’s objection as a general complaint rather than a targeted rebuttal).
- A clear separator between an A-range answer (16 or above) and a B-range answer (12–15), specifying exactly which analytical moves—such as showing why “inert potency” either collapses into occasionalism or reintroduces Aristotelian actualization—must be present for the higher grade.

Keep the prompt formal and polished. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to modern secondary literature beyond what students could know from standard course texts. Do not include verbatim quotations from al-Ghazālī or Ibn Rushd; if you reference specific passages, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
