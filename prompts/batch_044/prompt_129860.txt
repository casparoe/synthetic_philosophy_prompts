I've been arguing with some friends about whether The Turn of the Screw is actually a ghost story or just an epistemic horror story about someone who wants too badly for there to be ghosts. I was re-reading Sidgwicks work on psychical research—he was on the SPR council but always the skeptic in the room, worried that absence of detected fraud gets treated like presence of the supernatural, especially when the witness has emotional skin in the game. He had this specific worry about first-person conviction loops where a subject's private certainty gets mistaken for intersubjective data because no one can reproduce the conditions, and I think James might be dramatizing exactly that loop.

Can you read the novella through that Sidgwickian lens? Not just "she's unreliable"—everyone says that. I want to know whether the governess is caught in the evidential asymmetry Sidgwick warned about: her visions are phenominal real to her, but the only checks (Mrs. Grose's vague nods, the childrens silence) fail the verifcation standard he demanded for psychical claims. Does the text expose the loop, or leave open she might be right despite it? Point to specific scenes—maybe the tower and the lake—where the philosophy illuminates what's happening, but also say where the reading breaks and an honest critic has to admit James is doing something Sidgwicks framework cant capture, and what the novella adds to the debate that Sidgwick's arguments alone don't. I'm writing an esssay for a philosophy of mind course and want something that takes both the book and Sidgwick's methodological skepticism seriously, without reducing either to an illustration.
