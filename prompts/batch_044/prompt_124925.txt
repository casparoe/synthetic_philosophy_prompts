I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of historiography, and we've spent several sessions circling an apparently intractable disagreement about presentism: whether historians can, or should try to, escape the moral and conceptual standards of their own time when interpreting past actors. One camp insists that genuine historical understanding demands a kind of methodological self-emptying—bracketing our present values so that we do not anachronistically judge, for instance, a seventeenth-century merchant for holding views we now find repugnant, and instead recover the internal coherence of his practical reasoning. The opposing camp replies that such bracketing is either psychologically impossible or ethically evasive, since every selection of evidence, every framing of agency, and every silence in the archive is already saturated with present concerns; they argue that the only intellectually honest historiography is one that openly owns its present standpoint rather than hiding behind a myth of neutrality. Despite careful exchanges, nobody has moved anyone else, and the debate has settled into a ritual repetition of two accusations—"you are merely imposing modern values" versus "you are disguising present judgment as false objectivity"—without any visible progress on the underlying issue.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about empirical claims regarding what working historians actually do, but rather about a hidden disagreement over what counts as a successful explanation in history: whether explaining an action requires recovering the actor's own normative framework on its own terms, or whether it requires situating that framework within larger structures—economic, ideological, or institutional—that we, now, are in a position to see more clearly. Yet I remain unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether the disagreement is largely verbal—turning on ambiguous uses of "objectivity," "understanding," and "presentism"—or whether it ultimately traces back to bedrock intuitions about moral progress and the authority of the past that neither side can argue for from shared premises. Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement with precision? Tell me whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions under a single heading; identify what, if anything, is doing the hidden work; and specify what kind of move—a formal distinction, an empirical study of historiographical practice, a reframing of the explanatory task—would actually count as progress, or whether we should expect the impasse to persist indefinitely and why.
