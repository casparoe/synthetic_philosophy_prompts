As a doctoral student working at the intersection of ancient philosophy and political economy, I am preparing my philosophical profile for a graduate seminar on economic ethics and want an internal consistency check before I present it. I have settled on the following package of commitments, but I am unsure whether they form a coherent whole or whether I am smuggling in incompatible motivations. (1) I accept Aristotle's distinction between natural chrematistics — acquisition bounded by the needs of the household (oikos) and oriented toward use — and unnatural chrematistics — the unlimited accumulation of currency for its own sake — and I take the normative force of this distinction to derive from its relation to eudaimonia rather than from any pre-modern economic conditions. (2) I regard modern fiat money as essentially a relational instrument of social credit rather than a commodity bearing intrinsic value, yet I want to maintain that exchange ratios in functioning markets track genuine interdependencies rather than being purely arbitrary conventions. (3) I interpret Aristotle's condemnation of usury (tokos) not as a primitive aversion to interest, but as the claim that treating a non-productive medium of exchange as a generative source of value violates the proper order of economic activity; I believe this carries over to contemporary compound-interest lending when it is decoupled from productive purpose. (4) I have recently argued, in a working paper, that algorithmic stablecoins and automated market-making protocols represent a genuinely novel category that Aristotle's binary framework cannot accommodate, since they simultaneously operate as mediums of exchange, stores of value, and self-replicating yield-generating assets, thereby collapsing the boundary between natural and unnatural acquisition. (5) Despite (4), I wish to preserve a reconstructed normative distinction between economic activities that serve human flourishing and those that do not, framing it in terms of whether accumulation is terminal (pursued without intrinsic limit) or instrumental (subordinate to civic or household flourishing).

My request is for an audit, not a lecture: please go through these commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what the least costly revision would be for each genuine tension. I am particularly concerned that (4) may undermine the applicability of (1) and (3) to modern contexts in a way that makes my retention of (5) either unmotivated or ad hoc. Rank the tensions by severity, explain whether each is resolvable without abandoning the Aristotelian framework, and indicate which single commitment I should drop if I want the cleanest overall package. I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citations — I need an assessment of logical and motivational coherence that could be evaluated without access to external sources.
