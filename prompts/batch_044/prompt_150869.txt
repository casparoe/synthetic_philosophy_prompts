I serve on the National Advisory Committee for Emerging Biotechnologies, and I am drafting our formal recommendation—due within thirty days—on a protocol that forces a philosophical problem into institutional form. The proposal, submitted by the Consortium for Affective Engineering, requests Phase I approval for a heritable, dual-mechanism germline intervention. The first vector suppresses nociceptive transmission at the dorsal root ganglion; the second, delivered via an optogenetically addressable construct, dampens limbic appraisal of aversive states without abolishing sensory discrimination or cognitive function. The applicants state that their objective is not analgesia but the elimination of suffering as such, producing what they term a hedonic baseline—a stable condition in which negative affect is structurally impossible, yet awareness, preference-formation, and rational deliberation remain intact. They frame this as an application of the hedonistic imperative: the view that the reduction, and ultimately the abolition, of suffering is the paramount moral aim, and that biotechnology should be directed toward that end.

The committee is divided. One faction treats the protocol as a logical extension of preventive medicine: if heritable diseases that cause suffering can be edited out, the capacity for suffering itself should be equally available for elimination. The opposing faction argues that suffering is not merely an instrumental harm but partly constitutive of moral agency, compassion, and the intelligibility of human flourishing; they further worry that rendering suffering impossible may collapse the distinction between genuine harm and mere preference frustration, with unpredictable consequences for legal, social, and ethical categories.

Please draft the Committee's written opinion—approximately 800 to 950 words—addressed to the Committee Chair, with an appropriate subject line and date. The memo must recommend approval, rejection, or approval subject to strict conditions, and must specify a concrete timeline or contingent mechanism for implementation. The reasoning should address the following with philosophical precision: whether suffering is purely instrumental or also constitutive of goods such as moral understanding and solidarity; whether the technology eliminates suffering or merely transforms it into an unrecognized form; and whether the applicants have established that a life without suffering is necessarily better rather than merely ontologically different. The memo must treat the strongest argument on the losing side fairly and without straw-manning—if you recommend approval, do not dismiss the moral-agency objection; if you recommend rejection, do not dismiss the preventive-medicine analogy. Include one specific, non-platitudinous oversight mechanism appropriate to your recommendation—such as a philosophical-oversight subcommittee, a staged rollout tied to conceptual benchmarks, or a sunset clause tied to long-term sociological data.

Write in a formal register suitable for circulation among scientists, clinicians, and institutional reviewers who are not specialists in philosophy. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific works or authors; you may invoke well-known conceptual distinctions—such as that between nociception and suffering, or between negative and positive hedonism—without attribution. Do not invent verbatim quotations from real texts; if you reference an existing philosophical idea, paraphrase it without quotation marks. The document should function as an actionable institutional recommendation, not as personal advice.
