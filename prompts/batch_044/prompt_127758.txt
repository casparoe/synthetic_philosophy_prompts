On Lewis’s account, a convention is an arbitrary regularity that solves a recurrent coordination problem: several equilibria could serve equally well, and stability derives from shared expectations rather than from intrinsic merit. If we now embed conventions in an evolutionary story—cultural selection pressures that, over generations, preserve the most robust or efficient equilibrium and eliminate rivals—then the surviving regularity (say, right-hand traffic or a given phonological standard) appears deterministically selected rather than genuinely arbitrary. So does evolutionary dynamics show that Lewis was wrong to insist on arbitrariness, or does that objection confuse the modal claim that alternatives were possible with the causal claim that they were never tried? In particular, if driving on the right has outcompeted driving on the left across most of the world, has the arbitrariness been resolved, and should we now treat the rule as functionally necessary rather than conventional?
