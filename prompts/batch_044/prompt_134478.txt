You are the lead intake assessor for a state restorative justice board that reviews offenses committed with the assistance of algorithmic advisory systems—cases in which a human actor made the final decision but relied on a predictive model for risk scoring, resource allocation, or behavioral recommendation. The board has adopted Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against true moral responsibility as its guiding framework: for an agent to be genuinely deserving of blame or praise for an action, that agent must be responsible for the mental nature or character that produced the action; but being responsible for one's mental nature requires having chosen it on the basis of a prior mental nature, generating an infinite regress that can only be broken if something is causa sui, which is impossible. Consequently, no one is ever truly responsible in the basic desert sense, though reactive attitudes and practical management responses may still be appropriate.

Your task is to design a reusable decision procedure—no more than eight ordered steps—that an intake officer with no philosophy background can run in about thirty minutes to classify any case into one of three outcomes: (1) management-only response (capacity-building, system redesign, or protective intervention, with no desert-based sanction); (2) reactive-attitude-appropriate but non-desert response (acknowledged harm, mediated dialogue, but no blame-based punishment); or (3) escalate to the values panel because the case falls in an indeterminate zone where the argument's application conflicts with institutional obligations. The procedure must ask concrete, answerable questions about the agent's causal history, the role of the advisory system, and the availability of alternative mental states, and it must specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step and exactly where the procedure stops. You must also identify the points where judgment about values—rather than facts—cannot be eliminated.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:
(a) A probation officer overrides a low-risk algorithmic recommendation and imposes a severe restriction that causes documented psychological harm;
(b) A clinician follows a diagnostic AI's recommendation exactly, withholding a treatment that later proves necessary, where the clinician's training history and fatigue levels are well documented;
(c) A system designer, knowing the training data was skewed but not maliciously so, deploys an advisory tool that systematically produces harmful recommendations adopted by multiple users.

For one of these—specifically, the case where the procedure yields an answer you think the board will resist—explain exactly what the procedure produces, why the board is likely to object, and whether that objection reveals a flaw in the procedure or in the board's commitment to the Basic Argument.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of case it should never be applied to, what information it requires that intake officers typically lack, and where it risks misfiring by treating contingent institutional needs as philosophical conclusions.
