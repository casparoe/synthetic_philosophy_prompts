I have just finished revising the syllabus for my upper-level metaphysics and philosophy of religion seminar, and I am drafting the final exam. Last month my grandmother died after a prolonged illness, and at her memorial service a family member read the old parable of the appointment in Damascus—the servant who sees Death in the marketplace, interprets her gesture as a threat, flees to Damascus, and is met there by Death, who explains only that she was surprised to see him in the marketplace because she had an appointment with him in Damascus that evening. Since then I have found myself returning to the story not as a generic illustration of inevitability, but as a precise logical puzzle about how avoidance can become arrival. I want the exam to reflect that preoccupation, but I do not want answers that merely summarize logical fatalism, name the Ockhamist and Boethian responses, or treat the narrative as a simple morality tale about acceptance.

Please write one essay question suitable for a 90-minute exam and answerable in roughly 1,000 words. The question should present the specific variant of the Damascus narrative described above—the servant’s interpretation of an ambiguous perceptual cue, his flight, the meeting in Damascus, and Death’s explanation that references only the location of the appointment, not any causal influence over his journey. Then ask students to evaluate three competing philosophical readings of the parable: (1) a standard logical-fatalist reading, according to which the endpoint (the meeting in Damascus) was necessary regardless of path; (2) a “path-dependent” fatalist reading, according to which the specific causal sequence—perception of threat, flight, arrival—is itself part of what is fixed, so that the servant’s attempt to alter the endpoint in fact realizes the predetermined world-line; and (3) a “counterfactual fragility” reading, according to which the prophecy is self-defeating: had the servant not formed the belief that he was threatened and stayed in the marketplace, he would not have been in Damascus, which means the appointment was contingent on his reaction rather than on a predetermined endpoint independent of it.

The question must require students to do more than classify these views. For each reading, they should assess whether it respects the servant’s practical reasoning (his inference from “I am threatened” to “I should flee”) and whether it renders the narrative internally coherent. Crucially, the best answers must identify exactly where the canonical “argument from the necessity of the past” applied to future-tensed propositions fails to capture the narrative’s specific structure—namely, that the servant’s belief about his own future location is formed in response to an ambiguous gesture, and that this belief then enters the causal chain that produces the very location it concerns. They should also address whether introducing a modal distinction between “thin” fatalism (only the meeting is necessary) and “thick” fatalism (the flight-and-meeting sequence is necessary) solves the coherence problem or merely renames it.

Please provide a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves, with marks assigned out of 25 total, plus a one-line note for each on how candidates typically fail to secure it. The scheme should separate an A answer (which executes the modal and causal analysis with precision) from a B answer (which correctly identifies the three readings but treats them as interchangeable labels rather than structurally different accounts of belief-formation and counterfactual dependence). Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the task should be fully answerable from conceptual reasoning alone.
