Since my father was recently diagnosed with early-onset familial Alzheimer's, I have been serving on the ethics committee at the university hospital that reviews Ulysses contracts and advance directives for psychiatric and neurological conditions. Our precedents are these: we approved a contract in which a patient with bipolar I disorder pre-authorized temporary involuntary hospitalization and lithium during future manic episodes, revocable only after 72 hours of clinical stability; we rejected a contract in which a patient with recurrent severe depression pre-authorized ECT during future episodes, even though she currently feared that suffering; we approved with conditions a contract in which a patient with schizophrenia pre-authorized long-acting injectable antipsychotics during psychotic breaks, contingent on confirmation by an independent psychiatrist; and we rejected a contract in which a patient with opioid use disorder pre-authorized a naltrexone pellet that would precipitate severe withdrawal upon use, deeming it punitive rather than therapeutic.

We now face a proposal from a fully competent patient with the same familial Alzheimer's mutation. She directs that once her MMSE falls below a specified threshold and she expresses a desire to eat, she is to be denied oral nutrition and hydration, with her spouse designated to enforce this refusal on the ground that her future demented self will lack the narrative continuity that makes preferences authoritative.

If we want to be consistent with our own track record, what should we decide? Which precedent is the closest analog, and do our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome? Please identify the competing rules—such as a capacity-continuity principle versus a treatment-restoration/non-withholding principle—that both fit our prior verdicts, state what each predicts here, give your best guess with reasons, and specify what further case would settle the matter between them. Also, please state how confident you are in this analysis and single out the point about which you are least sure.
