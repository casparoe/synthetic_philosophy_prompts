In a seminar on moral worth and acting for the right reasons, a classmate offered this analogy: acting with genuine moral motivation is like a compass that detects magnetic north through its own mechanism, whereas acting from social conditioning or behavioral reinforcement is like a compass fixed to point north by a hidden wire—both indicate the same direction, but only the first possesses what she called "moral authority." The analogy is used to argue that full moral praise belongs only to agents with autonomous, reason-responsive motivation, while externally shaped agents are merely "wired" correct. Please audit it: lay out the correspondence table (needle, magnetic field, hidden wire, the observer, and the normative "north"), then identify where the internal/external distinction breaks down—especially regarding whether agents ever have transparent access to the causal origins of their motives. Next, consider a reconceptualization I want to test: treat moral reasons not as a single vector detected by one instrument, but as coherence constraints across a multi-dimensional normative space that includes first-order desires, beliefs about value, and second-order approvals—closer to calibrating a distributed sensor network than to reading a compass. Does the conclusion that only "magnetic" agents have full moral worth survive this shift, or was the compass analogy carrying the argumentative weight all along?
