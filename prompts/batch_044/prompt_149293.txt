I have been thinking about the institutional habit, common in parliamentary democracies and in comparative political science, of arranging parties, voters, and policies along a single left-right axis. The practice is pervasive: legislative chambers seat representatives in a semicircle running from left to right; polling instruments routinely ask citizens to place themselves on a unidimensional scale; journalists and coalition negotiators describe alliances as “center-left” or “center-right” even when the constituent parties disagree sharply on issues that seem conceptually independent—say, on the pace of decarbonization versus on the cultural status of religious minorities, or on labor-market deregulation versus on national sovereignty. What strikes me as philosophically puzzling is that the axis functions not merely as a descriptive shorthand but as an organizing framework: it predicts committee assignments, structures media narratives, and is treated as carrying explanatory weight about future alliances. If one treats left and right as purely strategic labels—coalitions of convenience that happen to share a parliamentary bench—then the spatial ordering looks arbitrary. Yet the practice behaves as though there is something deeper holding each side together, something that makes it reasonable to expect that a party’s stance on carbon taxation will correlate with its stance on immigration or on progressive taxation.

So I want to ask what philosophical commitments this institutional practice actually presupposes about the coherence of political belief. Does it presuppose that ideological positions are constrained by an underlying value—perhaps equality versus hierarchy, or openness to change versus respect for tradition—from which specific policy preferences derive? Or does it presuppose something about practical reason itself, namely that citizens and representatives reason holistically rather than domain-by-domain? I am also struck by an apparent tension: the same ecosystems that rely on the left-right axis often report that voters hold “cross-cutting” preferences—economically interventionist but socially traditionalist, or libertarian on markets but cosmopolitan on borders—without treating those combinations as conceptually anomalous. Does this mean the axis presupposes an elite-level or party-level coherence that need not apply to mass publics? And if we replaced the implicit theory with one that treated every policy domain as fully autonomous—so that one’s view on distributive justice carried no predictive weight for one’s view on cultural recognition—what exactly would have to change in parliamentary procedure, survey design, or journalistic framing for the practice to remain philosophically coherent rather than merely conventional?

In particular, I would like you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and to say where it is underdetermined: could several rival theories of ideology license the same seating chart and the same polling scale? Please also address whether the distinction between left and right names a genuine social kind with shared causal or motivational roots, or whether it is better understood as a family resemblance sustained by institutional repetition, and what difference that metaphysical choice makes for how we should interpret election results.
