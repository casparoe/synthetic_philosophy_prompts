Give an example of a conceptual issue in material constitution—distinct from the classic statue-and-clay puzzle—in which an object and its constituent matter appear to diverge in persistence conditions not through physical damage or reshaping, but through a continuous, identity-preserving replacement of the original material (for instance, a biological artwork maintained by gradually substituting living tissue with synthetically identical analogues). Explain whether resolving this divergence requires treating constitution as a three-place relation among form, present matter, and origin-material, or whether a standard two-place constitution view can accommodate it by redefining the modal properties of the constituted object.
