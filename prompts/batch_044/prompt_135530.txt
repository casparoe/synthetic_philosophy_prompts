In my postgraduate seminar on Indian Buddhist metaphysics, two fellow students—let us call them Student A and Student B—became locked in a disagreement about how to read Nāgārjuna’s tetralemma in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā. I am writing as a bystander who could not decide between them, and I want you to adjudicate their exchange move by move.

Student A argued that the tetralemma is fundamentally an exhaustive reductio directed against Abhidharma realism: Nāgārjuna runs through all four logical possibilities—things exist with svabhāva, they do not exist, they both exist and do not, and they neither exist nor do not—in order to show that inherent existence is incoherent in every corner. She said this makes Madhyamaka a purely negative dialectic (a prasaṅga-style strategy) that dismantles wrong views without advancing any ontological thesis of its own; emptiness is not a conclusion but the absence left behind once all four positions collapse.

Student B replied that this reading turns Nāgārjuna into a sceptic, which misses the soteriological architecture of the text. He claimed the tetralemma is not symmetrical: the first two corners concern direct affirmation and negation of svabhāva, while the latter two already involve a kind of conceptual proliferation (prapañca) that operates on different grounds. The fourth corner—neither existence nor non-existence—is therefore not just another logical option to be rejected alongside the others, but a performative pointer to pratītyasamutpāda, a mode of being that is conceptually inexpressible yet cognitively accessible through the collapse of the grid itself. He said emptiness functions not as the empty set of rejected views but as a transformation of the framework within which views arise.

Student A countered that if the fourth corner is genuinely inexpressible, then Nāgārjuna has no right to deploy it inside a logical schema at all. Either it is treated as a rejectable position like the first three—which undermines Student B’s claim that it is special—or it is smuggled in as a metaphysical assertion while pretending to remain silent. She added that the text’s constant warnings against “reifying emptiness” or “clinging to emptiness as a view” actually prove that emptiness has definite meta-level content; otherwise there would be nothing specific to misunderstand.

Student B answered that this assumes the tetralemma operates within classical bivalent logic, which is precisely what Nāgārjuna is dismantling. The fourfold structure is a pedagogical scaffold, not a logical grid applied from outside. Once one sees that all four positions depend on the prior assumption of svabhāva, the scaffold collapses, and what remains is not a “view” but direct cognition of dependent origination. The therapeutic warnings against reification do not supply propositional content; they address the mind’s habitual tendency to grasp even at negation.

Student A then pressed a further objection: even if the method is therapeutic, Madhyamaka still needs an account of why the therapeutic process succeeds. If emptiness has no positive character, then the transformation Student B describes is indistinguishable from mere psychological conditioning, and the tradition’s claim to liberative knowledge (prajñā) collapses into a behavioural technique without epistemic authority.

Student B closed by saying that this objection conflates conceptual inexpressibility with epistemic emptiness. The cognition of emptiness is not a propositional belief but a non-conceptual direct perception (yogipratyakṣa in related traditions) that removes obscuration rather than replacing it with a new thesis. The authority of the path derives not from having asserted a correct ontology but from having eliminated the root error—svabhāva-grāha—that makes suffering possible.
