Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on metaphysical identity, which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve the very framework it operates within. I present it in numbered steps.

(1) Leibniz's Identity of Indiscernibles (PII): necessarily, for any entities x and y, if they share every qualitative property — both intrinsic and relational — then x is numerically identical to y.

(2) In Black's symmetric universe, sphere Alpha and sphere Beta share all intrinsic properties: identical radius, mass, temperature, and material composition.

(3) They also share every symmetric relational property. Each stands exactly two miles from a sphere of radius one meter; each stands in the relation being-to-the-left-of from one orientation and being-to-the-right-of from the opposite orientation; each bears the same distance relations, if any exist, to the boundary of the universe.

(4) Therefore Alpha and Beta share all qualitative properties. By (1), Alpha equals Beta.

(5) But they clearly occupy non-overlapping regions of space. To identify them violates basic physical intuition and the geometric structure of the scenario. So (1) must be false.

(6) The only available rescue for (1) is to invoke non-qualitative haecceities — primitive thisness properties such as being-Alpha — which differ between the spheres.

(7) Yet if haecceities are permitted, PII becomes trivial: every distinct object differs from every other solely by its unique haecceity, and the principle then excludes only the logically impossible case of one object being numerically plural.

(8) Thus the defender of PII faces a forced choice: abandon the principle altogether, or preserve it at the cost of rendering it metaphysically empty and explanatory useless.

(9) Now extend this reasoning across modal space. Consider possible world W and possible world W*, which are qualitatively identical in every respect except that in W Alpha occupies the left region and Beta the right, while in W* the positions are reversed.

(10) Since W and W* contain exactly the same objects standing in exactly the same relations — the total structure is permuted but not altered in any qualitative feature — they must, by the same reasoning that applied within a single world, be identical worlds.

(11) Consequently, modal realism cannot recognize genuinely distinct but qualitatively isomorphic worlds. Modal space collapses into a single structure with redundant labeling.

(12) This proves too much: it undermines not only Black's original counterexample but also the coherence of modal arguments used in philosophy of physics — for instance, arguments for structural realism about quantum mechanics that depend on there being distinct possible worlds with permuted but non-identical structures.

(13) Therefore, either Black's reasoning is flawed at its root, or we must accept both trivial haecceitism within worlds and the collapse of modal plurality across them.
