I have been refining the framework for a graduate seminar on comparative egalitarianism, and I would like an external audit of whether my own commitments hang together before I present them as a coherent package. My position comprises five theses: first, the American Dream ought to be interpreted strictly as a procedural ideal of fair equality of opportunity, not as a justification for unlimited status accumulation; second, Jantelagen is not reducible to envy but expresses a relational-egalitarian concern—along the lines Elizabeth Anderson articulates—that extreme status differentiation undermines democratic equality among citizens; third, the "tall poppy syndrome" is morally distinct from Jantelagen because it targets individuals rather than structural visibility, and I hold that actively cutting down successful people is always wrong, even when their success generates positional goods of the kind Fred Hirsch describes; fourth, I support aggressive taxation and the democratic regulation of luxury display precisely to reduce those positional externalities, but I deny that such measures constitute punishment of excellence; fifth, I reject any intrinsic moral entitlement to public celebration of achievement, on the grounds that celebration is a socially distributed resource legitimately subject to democratic revision.

Please examine these commitments for internal tension: distinguish pairs that genuinely conflict from pairs that only appear to, rank the real conflicts by severity, and for each specify the cheapest available revision together with what it costs. Indicate also whether a single revision exists that would resolve the most tension at once. Begin with your bottom-line verdict—whether the package is jointly tenable—stated in the first sentence; then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
