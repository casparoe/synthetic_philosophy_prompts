I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent considerable time thinking on my own about the objection that utilitarianism fails to respect what is often called the separateness of persons—the idea that there is no single experiencing subject who receives the summed welfare of different individuals, so that trading one person's severe harm against minor gains spread across many others is not analogous to balancing costs and benefits within a single life. I understand the familiar reply that this objection may only defeat naive additive aggregation, leaving room for more structured rules such as prioritarian weighting or contractualist acceptance. Rather than asking for a summary of these positions, I want a defended agenda for where serious conceptual work should actually go.

If you were advising a researcher or a small group with limited time—say, two to three years—on the genuinely open questions in this area, which lines of work should take priority? I am especially interested in three clusters: first, whether the separateness objection applies with equal force to ex ante risk policies, in which small probabilities of harm are imposed on many people to prevent larger harms to others, as opposed to ex post distributions of goods and burdens; second, whether contractualist or prioritarian aggregation rules genuinely avoid the objection or merely rename and conceal it; and third, how the debate ought to inform actual institutional choices—such as medical triage protocols or public-health trade-offs—without collapsing into either unrestrained aggregation or practical paralysis.

Please defend a specific ordering. For each priority, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone working conceptually rather than empirically, and what kind of argument or evidence over the next few years would cause you to demote or promote it. I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in knowing where limited effort should be directed and why. Please write your answer in American English.
