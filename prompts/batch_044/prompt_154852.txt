I am preparing the final examination for my upper-level philosophy of biology seminar. I want one essay question that students cannot answer by summarizing the standard contrast between etiological, selection-history accounts of biological function and causal-role, system-relative accounts. To force genuine application, please embed the following injected scenario: a synthetic “xenoperoxisome” engineered de novo from synthetic lipids and recombinant proteins with no evolutionary ancestry, inserted into mammalian cells, performing detoxification identically to natural peroxisomes, but essential only within a laboratory-constructed metabolic pathway and never subjected to natural selection.

Please provide:

1. The exam question itself, appropriate for a 75-minute essay of approximately 900–1,100 words. The prompt should require students to determine whether the xenoperoxisome possesses a biological function, and it must explicitly instruct them to take a definite, committed position—defending either an etiological analysis, a causal-role analysis, or a specifically modified hybrid—giving explicit reasons rather than surveying both sides neutrally. The question should also ask them to identify one significant cost or objection that their own committed view faces in this case.

2. A model answer of about 900 words that takes a clear, reasoned stand (for example, arguing that the causal-role account correctly attributes function to the synthetic organelle but only if “system” is restricted to the engineered cell-line, while conceding that this restriction sacrifices the normative force of malfunction). The answer should apply the conceptual machinery precisely to the synthetic case, distinguish at least once between proper function and system function, and make one original argumentative move—such as arguing that intentional design can serve as a proxy for selection history only if we abandon the normative distinction between malfunction and mere failure, or that causal-role accounts must accept artifact-functions as genuinely biological. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. If you reference specific claims associated with the selection-history or causal-role traditions, paraphrase them without quotation marks; do not quote any real text verbatim.

3. A marking scheme totaling 20 marks, built around five must-hit conceptual moves (e.g., correct application of the etiological criterion to the absence of selection history; correct application of the causal-role criterion to the synthetic pathway; explicit defense of a committed position with reasons; identification of a genuine internal cost to that position; precise handling of the malfunction/normativity issue). For each point, state the marks, the specific move required for full credit, and a one-line note on the typical student error that loses marks. The scheme should clearly separate an A-grade answer (18–20) from a B-grade answer (14–15): an A must not only apply both frameworks correctly but also defend its chosen view against the strongest version of the rival framework applied to the xenoperoxisome, whereas a B applies the frameworks correctly but either refuses to commit, or commits without acknowledging a serious cost.

Write the prompt in a formal, polished register suitable for a professor drafting an assessment. Ensure the scenario relies only on general knowledge of cell biology and philosophy of biology that an advanced student would possess, not on obscure empirical literature or highly specialized synthetic-biology terminology beyond the invented case itself.
