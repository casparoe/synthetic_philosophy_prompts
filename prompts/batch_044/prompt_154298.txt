I am drafting an essay that argues contemporary objectivist accounts of meaning in life—those that ground meaning in a life’s engagement with objectively valuable activities coupled with appropriate subjective fulfillment—neglect a structural problem I call diachronic commitment fragility. The claim is not Camus’s metaphysical absurdity, the clash between human aspiration and an indifferent cosmos, but rather a temporal instability internal to long-term projects: an agent may fully acknowledge that painting, scientific work, or care for others is objectively worthy and personally fulfilling, yet recognize that sustaining these activities requires projecting future evaluative commitments she cannot now guarantee. Each repetition of the project depends on a chain of such projections that can fracture without logical contradiction, not because the values are false, but because the agent lacks diachronic authority over her own continuity of concern. Because standard objectivist frameworks assess meaning synchronically—asking whether a life, taken at a time or as a completed whole, combines objective value with subjective engagement—they treat this fragility as a merely psychological obstacle rather than as a potential erosion of meaning itself.

I would welcome critical comments on the following. First, is this phenomenon genuinely distinct from familiar existentialist worries about authenticity, or from problems about personal identity over time? Second, do objectivist accounts—whether they emphasize fitting subjective attraction to objective goods or transcendent engagement with value—actually contain implicit resources for addressing diachronic instability, perhaps through retrospective evaluation or through the idea that present commitment can be rationally sustained without future guarantees? Third, am I conflating a feature of practical rationality with a condition on meaningfulness: might one reply that meaning is indeed reconstructed retrospectively, so that prospective fragility is irrelevant? I am particularly interested in whether the argument collapses if we allow that a life can be meaningful in virtue of its actual trajectory regardless of the agent’s present anxiety about continuation.
