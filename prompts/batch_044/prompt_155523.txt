I serve on the board of trustees for the state's public deferred-compensation system, and we must finalize the withdrawal architecture for a new retirement tier covering approximately forty thousand public-sector workers before next Tuesday's vote. The proposed design permits quarterly withdrawals with a declining penalty schedule—five percent in year one, three percent in year two, and one percent thereafter—without any mandatory lock-in. Our behavioral economists have demonstrated that this structure operates as a diachronic money pump: a participant who maximizes expected utility at each discrete choice node will, across the full temporal sequence, accumulate substantially lower lifetime wealth and report lower ex-post satisfaction than she would have achieved under a simple ten-year commitment with a flat, single-exit penalty. The economists therefore recommend abandoning the flexible schedule in favor of a commitment architecture—either a fixed lock-in with one hardship exit or an automatic escalation clause that removes subsequent choice points once the initial election is made.

Our legal counsel objects that mandatory commitment devices may violate the statutory requirement that participants retain meaningful access to funds in documented hardship cases. A minority of trustees raises a deeper philosophical objection: if preferences genuinely evolve over time, then there is no unified diachronic agent being pumped, only a succession of distinct temporal selves, and the board ought not impose intertemporal coherence upon them. The majority of the board seeks a concrete recommendation—adopt the flexible schedule, the commitment architecture, or a modified hybrid—with an implementation timeline and specific conditions, together with reasoning that takes both the legal-access concern and the temporal-selves argument seriously rather than treating them as obstacles to be dismissed.

Please draft the board opinion I will circulate before Tuesday's meeting. It should present a clear, actionable bottom line with a phased implementation schedule, explain how the recommended structure avoids the diachronic money-pump vulnerability without ignoring the hardship-access mandate, and engage the temporal-selves objection on its own terms—acknowledging its force—while showing why it does not defeat the case for institutional commitment design. Write entirely in continuous prose, as one would compose a formal letter or email: do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any other formatting devices, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or suggestions for further reading.
