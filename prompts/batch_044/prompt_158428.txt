You are a professional philosopher of science with deep familiarity with logical empiricism and W. V. O. Quine's critique in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." Before addressing the main question, please begin by briefly outlining the two core arguments Quine deploys against the analytic/synthetic distinction—specifically, the problem of circularity that arises when trying to define analyticity through synonymy, reduction, or explicit convention, and the holistic thesis that empirical statements face the tribunal of experience not individually but as a corporate body—and note precisely where each argument targets a boundary between truths grounded in meaning and truths grounded in fact.

With that framework in place, consider the following conceptual reconstruction, which is not standard in the literature but has been floated in recent informal discussions: call it "tiered holism." On this view, analytic truths are not those that are unrevisable in principle; rather, they are statements assigned a very high revision-cost within the web of belief. Logical and mathematical principles sit near the center of the web not because they are immune to experience, but because overturning them would require cascading adjustments across vast domains of science and ordinary belief, so rational agents prefer to preserve them and instead revise peripheral empirical claims. The defender argues that this preserves a meaningful, pragmatically grounded analytic/synthetic distinction without invoking any mysterious "truths in virtue of meaning" independent of empirical testing, and therefore escapes Quine's critique.

Please explain, with careful conceptual precision, whether Quine's specific objections in "Two Dogmas" actually undermine this tiered-holist reconstruction, or whether they only defeat stronger formulations of the distinction—for example, those that require absolute unrevisability or that attempt to ground analyticity in explicit definitional conventions reducible without remainder. In particular, examine whether Quine's pragmatic concession that even logical laws might, in principle, be revised in the face of sustained empirical pressure—a point that commentators have sometimes illustrated with quantum-mechanical puzzles—is compatible with allowing a graded, cost-based boundary, or whether his holism forces a flat landscape in which every statement is, in principle, equally open to revision and no principled distinction, even a pragmatic, revision-cost-based one, can be sustained. Be explicit about where the tiered view succeeds, where it collapses into Quine's position, and whether it misrepresents the force of his anti-dogmatic argument.
