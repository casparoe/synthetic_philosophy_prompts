I'm a novelist — mostly literary fiction about places that get erased and the people who try to keep them in language. I also sit on the advisory board for the Meridian Oral History Trust, which is this small nonprofit holding first-person recordings from neighborhoods that don't exist anymore. We're meeting next Tuesday to vote on what to do with the Holloway Sessions: about forty hours of audio diaries kept by residents of a cooperative housing block that burned and was demolished in the eighties after some ugly political fights. What's strange is that the diaries aren't straight testimony. A lot of the residents used them to work out fictional versions of their lives — alternate timelines where the fire never happened, where they stayed. So they're part memoir, part invented narrative, these hybrid objects that don't fit clean into either category.

Some board members and descendants want everything kept open-access. They say we owe it to the dead to preserve every voice, that sealing or destroying the tapes is like a second demolition, a failure of our duty to remember. Others — including one descendant who wrote us a long letter — say forcing these voices to stay in circulation is its own violence, that the survivors and their children have a right to be forgotten, and that keeping trauma permanently exhibited harms the living more than it honors the dead. Our chair asked me to draft the memo we'll circulate before the meeting. I want a clear reccomendation — preserve, seal, partial redaction, whatever — with a concrete timeline for what happens when. It needs to take the case for forgetting seriously; I actually think the arguments people like Rieff make about the damage remembrance can do apply here in a way the standard ethics-of-memory framework doesn't fully cover, especially since these aren't pure historical records but these weird fiction-memory hybrids. But I also need the memo to face the strongest argument on the other side head-on, not just brush it off.

Can you write it? I need reasoning the board can actually vote on, not a philosophy seminar paper. No citations or reading lists — half our board are archivists and community organizers who don't care about footnotes. The language should have some texture since I'm a novelist and I have to present it, but it needs to be precise enough that the staff know exactly what to do Monday morrning.
