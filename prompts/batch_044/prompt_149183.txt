I'm drafting a section on moral worth for a philosophy seminar, and I've walked myself into an argument that seems to show that ordinary, socially acquired moral action can never be genuinely worthy. I'd like to preserve both the claim that moral worth requires acting for the right reasons and the claim that reflective, socialized agents can achieve it, but I need to know exactly which joint to break.

(1) An act has moral worth only if the agent is moved by the normative reason that makes the act right, not by a merely coincident non-moral motive.
(2) To be moved by a normative reason R, the agent must possess the concept that picks out R.
(3) Possessing that concept requires being able to apply it correctly, which requires a criterion of correct application.
(4) For normative concepts, the criterion is itself normative—for example, that undeserved suffering is pro tanto bad.
(5) So possessing the concept requires already being responsive to the normative fact that grounds the criterion.
(6) Therefore, being moved by R requires already being responsive to the normative fact that justifies R's conceptual application.
(7) This generates a regress: each normative reason requires prior normative responsiveness to become conceptually available.
(8) The regress can stop only at a base-level normative grasp that needs no prior justification—say, an innate moral intuition or a direct-reference moral concept.
(9) But if the base is innate or direct, then agents who acquire morality through socialization and deferential moral cognition—most people—never reach the ground floor.
(10) So for the vast majority of agents, moral worth is structurally impossible.
(11) That proves too much; I want to preserve that ordinary agents who rely on learned, reflective frameworks can still act with full moral worth.

Which step should I reject? I'm inclined to defend (1) and either deny the regress in (6)-(7) or weaken (3) so that partial conceptual grasp suffices, but I want to know the price of each option—especially whether weakening (3) forces me into a direct-reference theory of moral terms, and whether denying the regress requires allowing that one can be genuinely moved by a reason one only partially understands. Lead with your bottom line, keep your answer to at most 300 words, and write in American English.
