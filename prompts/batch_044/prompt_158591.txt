The maxim that "revolutions devour their children" is routinely invoked in interpretations of the French Revolution to explain phenomena ranging from the execution of prominent revolutionary deputies to the Thermidorian Reaction. Rather than treating it as self-evident, lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be advancing, arranged from the trivial to the genuinely contested.

For each reading, specify the exact mechanism it posits—whether institutional path-dependence, an internal logic of ideological purification, the dynamics of revolutionary sovereignty, or mere contingent betrayal—state whether it is intended as an empirical regularity about revolutions in general or as a claim specific to 1789–1794, and identify whether "devouring" denotes literal execution of agents, institutional dismantling of structures created by the revolution, or symbolic betrayal of founding principles. The menu of readings should include at least: (1) a tautological version in which any regime that eliminates its own founders is defined as self-destructive by fiat; (2) a structural version in which revolutionary institutions necessarily generate enemies they must destroy in order to survive; (3) an ideological version in which fidelity to an abstract principle (equality, virtue, the general will) requires the elimination of particular representatives who embody corruption; (4) a contingency version in which personality clashes, tactical miscalculation, and accident do all the explanatory work.

Then assess each reading against the three interpretive frameworks—class struggle, ideology-driven explanation, and contingency—indicating which frameworks can accommodate the reading and which it undermines. In particular, address whether the slogan presupposes that a revolution has a unified "will" or "body" that can turn on itself, or whether it can be reconstructed in terms of competing social groups and factions without that metaphysical commitment.

Finally, inject and apply a niche conceptual distinction: between "revolutionary autocannibalism" (the regime destroying its own agents and institutional offspring) and "revolutionary autoimmunization" (the regime treating its founding norms and personnel as foreign bodies to be expelled in the name of purity). Ask which of these two mechanisms, if either, the slogan genuinely captures, and whether either requires accepting that revolutions necessarily generate internal enemies, or only that they reveal enemies that were present but concealed from the outset. Keep the weight on conceptual precision and on which readings survive the distinction, rather than on historical narrative or bibliographic reference.
