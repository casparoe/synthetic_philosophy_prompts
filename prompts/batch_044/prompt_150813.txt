I am a senior software engineer and a voting member of the Acquisition and Ethics Review Board at the Meridian Foundation for Digital Heritage. I have no formal training in philosophy or art history. Our board must render a binding decision on a proposed donation, and I need a formal memo—addressed to the full board—to guide our vote and our public reasoning.

The case concerns a reconstructed executable artifact. In 1986, an artist-collective authored a generative graphics program that was subsequently lost when its only storage server was destroyed in a 2019 fire. A preservation lab has now reconstructed executable source code by applying spectral fragment recovery to damaged disk platters, then feeding the recovered fragments into a constrained generative model that was restricted to reproduce only observed output behaviors. The result runs and produces visual outputs that are perceptually and computationally indistinguishable from archival video recordings of the 1986 system in operation. However, the reconstructed code is written in a modern language, relies on automatically generated rendering modules rather than the original hand-optimized loops, and bears no direct causal chain to the 1986 authors’ keystrokes, annotations, or specific algorithmic choices.

The donor asks us to catalog the item as the original program, reconstructed, and to display it running live in our permanent gallery with only a brief plaque noting the reconstruction method. Several board members argue that, because the actual token—the specific file authored in 1986—is irretrievably lost, what we possess is essentially a high-fidelity simulation or forgery. They contend that displaying it as the original misrepresents the historical record and erodes the value we place on authentic provenance. Other members argue that for executable art, the work’s identity resides in its functional behavior and in the audience’s phenomenal experience rather than in the arrangement of a particular binary or source file; on this view, rejecting the donation privileges an obsolete metaphysics of material objects over the artistic purpose of the work itself.

Please draft a formal memo recommending one of the following courses of action: (1) reject the donation entirely; (2) accept it only under an explicit reconstruction or simulation classification, displayed separately from artifacts with intact causal provenance; or (3) accept it under a new hybrid institutional classification that explicitly recognizes both its synthetic origin and its exceptional fidelity, together with specific labeling and display protocols.

The memo should state a clear bottom-line recommendation, provide reasoning accessible to a technically oriented board, honestly address the strongest argument for the option you do not recommend, and offer concrete guidance on cataloging language and gallery presentation. You may introduce and briefly explain any conceptual distinction—such as the tension between causal provenance and functional equivalence, or between token-identity and type-identity—that clarifies the reasoning. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Write in a polished, institutional tone appropriate for a board decision document.
