I run a small community makerspace where people share craft patterns and little mechanical hacks, and lately we've been getting these ugly fights about who 'owns' a modified idea. I'm not a lawyer or philosophy professor - I just fix bikes and organize the tool library. But I was thinking about how ideas are weird because if I give you an idea I still have it, which doesn't work for normal property. And there's this other thing I think is true but I don't know the fancy name: every idea has three layers - the original spark, the actual labor someone puts into adaptating it, and the community context that makes it useful in the first place. I want you to turn that into an actual decision procedure my volenteer mediators can run when two members fight over a design. It needs to be a step-by-step checklist they can do in about 20 minutes with no legal training, saying exactly what counts as a yes or no at each step, where it stops and says 'this is a values question, vote on it,' and where it might accidentally screw over the original creator. Apply it to three real-ish scenarios: someone who took an open knitting pattern and just swapped the yarn weight, someone who mashed together two old bike-frame ideas into something genuinely different, and a case where the procedure says 'nobody owns this, release it' but the person who came up with the spark is gonna be furious. Be honest about what the checklist can't catch and where human judgment still sneaks in. Also please write the answer in American English, not British spellings like 'colour' or 'centre.'
