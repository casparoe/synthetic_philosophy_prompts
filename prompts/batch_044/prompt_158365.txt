I want to work through a conceptual tension in Spinoza's democratic theory that introductory treatments usually paper over. Spinoza describes democracy as the most absolute form of sovereignty—the direct exercise of the multitudo's collective power—while simultaneously observing that the multitude is typically governed by passions that undermine collective rationality. The standard resolution appeals to common notions: adequate ideas that align individual and collective interests. But this raises a structural circularity. If democratic legitimacy requires that the state's power, or potestas, genuinely expresses the multitude's power, or potentia multitudinis, then the rationality that makes democracy legitimate must be generated from within democratic practice itself, not imposed by an external authority. Yet Spinoza also seems to treat common notions as cognitive preconditions: citizens must already grasp adequate ideas for democratic deliberation to be rational rather than mob-like. So do common notions emerge from democratic interaction, or must they be present beforehand? Please explain, with conceptual precision but without relying on formal proofs, whether this circularity is merely apparent. In particular, distinguish three interpretations: (a) democracy creates rationality ex nihilo through institutional feedback loops; (b) democracy reveals a rational structure already implicit in human nature, so the circle is benign; (c) the circularity is vicious, and Spinoza's democracy implicitly depends on an aristocratic or expert core that possesses adequate ideas prior to popular deliberation. I am looking for an intuitive but rigorous account of whether Spinoza's democratic sovereignty is genuinely self-grounding.
