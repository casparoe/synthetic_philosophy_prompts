If Zhuangzi were confronted with the modern bioethical and legal institution of advance directives—documents in which individuals specify precise clinical conditions (for instance, irreversible loss of narrative identity or permanent unconsciousness) under which life-sustaining treatment should be withdrawn—how might his philosophy assess the practice? Consider in particular his view of death not as a discrete terminus but as a continuous transformation (hua) within the endless flow of change; his anecdote about drumming on a tub and singing upon his wife's death; and his notion of xinzhai, or the fasting of the heart, as an emptying of the self that refuses to fixate on conventional distinctions such as life versus death. Would he interpret the directive's attempt to administratively bound, anticipate, and contractually arrange the exact moment and manner of one's death as a profound failure of this fasting—a clinging to form, autonomy, and control that denies the spontaneity of transformation? Or could he regard it as a legitimate expression of an individual's own transformation, analogous to his acceptance of his wife's change? Moreover, how would his rejection of conventional mourning and his insistence on the fundamental equality of life and death interact with the modern bioethical commitments to patient autonomy and non-maleficence? Reconstruct the likely tension between his metaphysics of continuous change and the institutional presupposition that death is an event that can be rationally foreseen, precisely delineated, and legally orchestrated.
