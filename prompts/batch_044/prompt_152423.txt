I have recently retired from a career in experimental physics and have begun studying philosophy of mind, particularly the debate over knowing how versus knowing that. In both philosophical texts and popular science writing, I repeatedly encounter the analogy that acquiring a practical skill is like learning to ride a bicycle: however precisely one masters the propositional physics of balance, center-of-mass projection, and angular momentum—however complete one's knowing that—the body will still fall until the skill is acquired through direct, embodied practice. The analogy is typically deployed to license a specific conclusion: that knowing how constitutes a distinct cognitive kind, irreducible to any constellation of propositional representations, and that reductionist accounts therefore miss an essential feature of human competence.

My own experience suggests the boundary is less sharp. Aligning a laser interferometer, for instance, demanded both explicit knowledge of tolerance specifications and an inarticulate, tactile feel for micrometer adjustments; the two modes of cognition were not separate layers but constantly interwoven. This makes me want to audit the bicycle analogy with some care.

Please lay out an explicit correspondence table: in this analogy, what corresponds to the learner, the bicycle, the physics manual, the fall, the years of practice, and the eventual fluent performance? Then identify the points of breakdown: does the analogy quietly assume that explicit knowledge is always inert until replaced by bodily wisdom, or that practical mastery is always non-representational? Does it conflate the difficulty of verbal transmission with the absence of propositional content altogether? Examine whether expert practitioners—surgeons, pilots, instrument makers—actually operate without representational structures, or whether the analogy smuggles in a romanticized picture of embodied cognition that does not survive empirical scrutiny.

Finally, tell me whether the anti-reductionist conclusion—that knowing how is categorically different from knowing that—actually survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the weight of the argument. If you think a more accurate analogy exists—perhaps drawn from scientific instrumentation or craft apprenticeship—propose it and explain what it corrects.

Please separate in your response what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on skill and knowledge, and do not present any contested position—such as the claim that the classic anti-reductionist distinction has been entirely dissolved, or that it remains fully intact—as if it were the consensus view.
