I keep noticing an explanatory move in biology and engineering that I cannot place precisely. Researchers describe a system—for instance, a kidney filtering waste, or a distributed algorithm sorting inputs—by treating it as though it were purpose-built for a specific task. From that framing they derive predictions about failure modes, design improvements, and what distinguishes normal operation from pathology. At the same time, they deny that any engineer actually designed the system, and they concede that a complete account in terms of underlying chemistry, mechanics, or circuitry, though accurate, fails to capture the organizational regularity that makes those predictions succeed. The framing is not merely a pedagogical aid; working clinicians and engineers build real interventions and diagnose actual dysfunctions using it. Yet there is no hidden agent or conscious planner behind the behavior. Is there an established philosophical concept for this kind of explanatory level—one that treats certain organizational patterns as genuinely robust and intervention-worthy, without reducing them to base-level physical laws and without attributing them to any mind? How is it distinguished from a mere heuristic or useful fiction, and what determines whether a pattern at this level counts as real in some stronger sense? I am less interested in a bare label than in the exact boundaries: what belongs inside this category, what falls outside, and whether it requires a specific ontological commitment or can remain fully neutral.
