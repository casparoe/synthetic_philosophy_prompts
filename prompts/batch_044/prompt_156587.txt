I am currently enrolled in an upper-level epistemology seminar, and our recent discussion of Moore's paradox — specifically the tension generated when a speaker asserts that it is raining while simultaneously denying that they believe it — has led me to wonder whether the absurdity is genuinely about first-person belief or rather about the structure of assertion itself. Most treatments we have encountered focus on the individual doxastic conflict, but I want to investigate a less-discussed extension: cases of what I will call delegated or institutional assertion, in which a speaker voices a proposition not in a personal capacity but as a representative of a collective body. For example, a university spokesperson might declare that the administration holds the policy to be just, while personally maintaining that it is not; or a climate scientist might present a consensus report stating that anthropogenic warming is certain, despite private reservations.

Please write a dialogue between two seminar participants — designate them as A and B — who are equally well-versed in the standard literature but who take opposing positions on whether Moore's paradox survives this kind of delegation. A should defend the normative view that assertion constitutively expresses the speaker's own belief, so that even a delegated utterance implicitly commits the speaker personally, making any simultaneous denial contradictory in exactly the Moorean form. B should defend the pragmatic or self-knowledge view: that the paradox is a conversational failure arising from the speaker's contradictory self-attribution, and that once the speaker clearly marks the utterance as institutional rather than personal, no contradiction remains because the doxastic attitudes are properly separated by role. They must engage with each other's actual arguments — A should ask whether B's role-distinction really eliminates personal responsibility for the content asserted, and B should ask whether A's normative account implausibly collapses all institutional speech into first-person belief. They should apply their disagreement to a concrete case, such as the climate scientist presenting a report she privately doubts, and discuss whether her statement is genuinely Moore-paradoxical or fully coherent. The dialogue should be rigorous, contain real back-and-forth over several exchanges, and avoid treating either position as a straw figure. I do not need citations or references to specific texts; I want the conceptual argument developed on its own terms.
