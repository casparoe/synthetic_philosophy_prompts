You work as a philosophical counselor running 50-minute sessions for clients who present with existential complaints about "the meaning of life." Operationalize Frank Ramsey's reconstructed diagnosis—treating such questions as pseudo-problems generated by extending the grammatical form "What is the meaning of X?" (appropriate for symbols, sentences, or practices with specifiable rules) onto the totality "life," where no answer-conditions can be fixed—into a reusable decision procedure. Inject the following reconstructed distinction: distinguish practice-embedded meaning (recoverable only from within a specific form of life or activity) from transcendent meaning (demanding justification from an impossible standpoint outside all practices).

Write an ordered checklist: the exact questions or tests to apply in sequence, what counts as a passing answer at each step, the stop conditions that yield one of three classifications—(1) pseudo-problem to be dissolved by grammatical clarification, (2) substantive practical crisis of purpose requiring ethical deliberation, or (3) clinical depressive or cognitive-distortion pattern requiring referral—and the points where the procedure must halt and hand judgment to a person because the distinction is normative rather than factual. Include three specific instruments: a counterfactual specification test (can the client state, without contradiction, conditions under which life would lack meaning?), a practice-embedding test (can the client identify the specific practice or form of life within which meaning is sought?), and a transcendence check (does the client demand justification of that practice from a standpoint outside it?). Flag which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedural tests.

Apply the procedure to two worked cases: (A) a recently retired surgeon who says, "My life has lost its meaning because I no longer save lives," and (B) a graduate student who asks, "Given an infinite, indifferent universe, what is the meaning of human life as a whole?" Add a third variant—modify either case or present a new one—where the procedure delivers a classification the practitioner is likely to resist, and explain what that resistance reveals about the procedure's structural limits. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of existential distress it will systematically misclassify, and why its output should never be treated as a mental-health diagnosis. Do not request citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
