After three hours arguing with a friend about whether a sugar cube sealed inside an impervious polymer shell is still soluble, I have realized we were not disagreeing about chemistry but about what it means for a disposition to persist when every path to its manifestation is systematically blocked. I am preparing a handout for an advanced philosophy of science seminar that classifies positions on dispositions, finks, and masks—not by asking students which philosopher they prefer, but by having them answer concrete cases and mapping their answers onto the logical space of views. The audience is graduate students who know the basic vocabulary but should be able to locate themselves without recognizing a label in advance.

Please construct a taxonomy in the form of a nested decision tree with six to eight leaves. Build it from the logical space rather than from a roster of named theories, so that each branch is split by a specific claim about a case, and only afterward should the leaves be tagged with position-names or marked “unnamed.”

Root split: Present the combined case of a fragile glass vase that is simultaneously wrapped in shock-absorbing foam (a mask) and fitted with an internal self-repair mechanism that activates on any crack (a fink). The first branch should ask whether this object is still fragile at all. Separate those who hold that both conditions destroy the disposition, those who hold that only one does, and those who deny that the distinction between fink and mask applies here.

Second-level split: Introduce the “mimic” case—a holographic projection that behaves identically to a breaking vase upon impact but possesses no physical substrate whatsoever. Use this to separate accounts that require an underlying categorical base from those that treat behavioral counterfactuals or functional profiles as sufficient for ascribing the disposition.

Third-level split: Introduce the “double-fink” antidote scenario. A poison is lethal unless an antidote is present, but the antidote is itself present only because a second mechanism detects the poison and releases it. Ask whether the poison retains its lethality in this environment, and use the answers to distinguish views that tie dispositions to the absence of interfering conditions in the actual world from those that tie them only to conditions in nearby possible worlds.

Fourth-level split: Ask about permanent universal blockage. Suppose every manifestation of a supposed disposition—solubility, dissolution, crystallization—is permanently masked or finked, with no remaining track available. Does a single underlying power persist, does the disposition fragment into independent multi-track capacities, or does it vanish altogether?

For each leaf, provide the name of a defended position if one occupies that branch (for example, a simple conditional analysis, a categorical realist view, a multi-track dispositionalism, or an eliminativist regularism), and write “unnamed” if the combination is coherent but apparently unoccupied. Briefly diagnose any empty branch: is it incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored?

Also flag any pair of leaves that this tree cannot genuinely separate with these four cases alone, and explain whether the convergence is practical (they agree on all judgments) or merely rhetorical (they differ only in how they describe the same judgments). If a branch looks unstable—perhaps because it requires accepting that a fink destroys a disposition while an identical mask does not—note that and explain why it is hard to maintain consistently.

Present the result as a nested list of claims and case-descriptions, not as a point-scoring quiz. Every node must be answerable by someone who does not already know which philosophical label it corresponds to. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the instrument should work for a student with no web access. Keep the tone formal and precise.
