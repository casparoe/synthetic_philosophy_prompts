I am organizing a 90-minute workshop for twelve graduate students in philosophy of biology and bioethics, and I need an in-class activity that makes them feel, from the inside, why every formal definition of life seems to include or exclude borderline cases arbitrarily once you are forced to apply it under constraint. The topic has become personally urgent for me: three months ago my sister delivered at 23 weeks after doctors debated whether the fetus had achieved independent metabolic autonomy sufficient to count as a separate life for certain ethical protocols, and since then I have noticed that every criterion I encounter—autopoiesis, evolutionary adaptation, metabolic closure, information processing—collapses into family resemblance or operational stipulation the moment I try to use it to settle a real case.

Design a structured game or role-play with rules, roles, and a payoff scheme that does not require real money. Give each student one of four cards describing a borderline entity: (A) a synthetic minimal cell engineered to maintain homeostasis and metabolize glucose but lacking any genome or evolutionary history; (B) a human body with no brain function sustained on ECMO whose gut microbiome continues to metabolize, adapt, and reproduce; (C) a viral factory inside a host cell that directs its own replication using host machinery but possesses no independent metabolism; and (D) a self-catalyzing peptide network enclosed in a lipid vesicle that grows, divides, and responds to chemical gradients but contains no nucleic acids and derives its energy directly from the environment rather than through internal metabolic cycles.

Assign each student a single definitional criterion—autopoiesis, evolutionary capacity, metabolic closure, or information processing—that they must defend for all four cases. Then pair them into opposing-criterion dyads and require each pair to produce a single joint classification of all four entities as alive or not alive within fifteen minutes. Use a payoff structure in which pairs earn one shared token for reaching consensus, but each individual also earns a token only if their personal classifications across all four cases remain internally consistent with their assigned criterion. This should create a tension between group agreement and individual coherence.

The rules should be arranged so that participants discover the contradiction themselves: they should begin by assuming common sense will guide them, then find that their assigned criterion forces them to classify a familiar case opposite to intuition. Include a timeline for the 90 minutes, specifying when cards are distributed, when negotiation begins, and when the hidden evaluation rules are revealed.

After the activity, provide a debrief that maps specific negotiation breakdowns onto philosophical moves—boundary-drawing problems, the exclusion of evolutionary history, the difference between operational and essentialist definitions. Be explicit about what will predictably fail the first time: students will likely ignore their assigned criteria and converge on intuitive judgments; tell me how to block this without lecturing in advance. Finally, state honestly which feature of the definitional debate the activity cannot reproduce—perhaps the normative weight of declaring something alive in a clinical or ethical context, or the temporal dimension of evolutionary lineage—and explain exactly what I must supply in the closing minutes to connect the intellectual exercise back to the personal stakes that prompted the workshop, without allowing the debrief to become a lecture on my experience.
