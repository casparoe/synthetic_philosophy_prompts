I keep hearing people say that figuring out whether video-game characters matter morally is just like asking whether characters in a novel matter — in both cases you've got representations of people, not people themselves, so the answer should be the same. Some use this to say we obviously shouldn't care about NPCs any more than we care about Mrs Dalloway; others flip it and say since we do care about Elizabeth Bennet, we should care about well-written NPCs too.

I want you to audit this analogy properly: lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the author, the reader, the narrative arc, the suspension of disbelief — then pinpoint where it breaks once you look at procedurally generated villagers in games like Animal Crossing who greet you by name, keep persistent memory of your behaviour across saves, make referance to past gifts, but have no canonical biography and pull dialogue from pools. Does the novel analogy smuggle in the idea that there's a single 'correct' interpretation of the character that the player is just discovering? And if the analogy breaks there, does the conclusion — that NPCs either do or don't matter — survive, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? Also, is there a better analogy — maybe puppetry, or chatbots, or something else — that handles the procedural-generation case without breaking?
