After a recent period of personal transition, I have been trying to articulate my philosophical stance on personal identity for a small reading group, and I want a consistency check before I present. Here is where I have landed:

(1) The self is a center of narrative gravity in Dennett's sense—not a Cartesian substance but an abstract organizational point constructed by the brain.
(2) I am a physicalist and reject any non-physical soul or persisting ego.
(3) Psychological continuity—memory, character, and intention—matters more for personal identity over time than bodily continuity does.
(4) I reject further-fact theories: there is no mysterious extra fact about identity beyond appropriate psychological and physical relations.
(5) I think a self can survive moderate narrative disruption, such as substantial memory loss or personality change, provided a coherent thread remains.
(6) I have recently come to think that genuine selfhood requires retroactive anchoring: the present center must actively authorize and reconstruct its past experiences as genuinely its own, rather than merely inheriting them through causal or informational chains. Without this active authorization, I believe there is biological continuity but no self.
(7) I believe artificial systems could possess narrative selves if they implement appropriate self-modeling and retroactive anchoring.
(8) Phenomenal consciousness is real, but I do not consider it necessary for narrative selfhood: an unconscious system could in principle be a self.

Please audit this package. Go through the pairs, identify which are in genuine tension and which conflicts dissolve on inspection, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each specify the cheapest fix and its cost. Also indicate whether a single revision could resolve the most problems with minimal damage. Do not treat all eight commitments as equally problematic—discriminate carefully, and identify which commitment is the weakest link in the set.
