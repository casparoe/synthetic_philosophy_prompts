Having spent my career modeling nonlinear dynamics in chemical reaction networks, I have recently turned my attention to the philosophy of logic and time. I am particularly struck by the problem of future contingents—especially the classic sea-battle reasoning associated with Aristotle—though I want to move past the standard scholastic and modal-logic rehearsals. To that end, please consider a specific hybrid framing: treat the present not as a branching node but as a frozen boundary condition for a deterministic Hamiltonian system, one in which all future events (including apparently contingent ones) are already implicit in the present state vector, yet remain epistemically inaccessible because any embedded measurement apparatus can resolve only a single eigenbranch. Within this framing—which preserves ontological determinism while allowing epistemic contingency—what are the most important implications of the original sea-battle argument? Specifically, I ask you to address three points with logical precision: first, whether the bivalence of future-tense propositions collapses entirely or merely shifts to a different descriptive level; second, how this bears on the principle of bivalence when applied to predictions generated by scientific instruments that are themselves subsystems within the Hamiltonian being measured; and third, whether the argument ultimately forces a rejection of determinism, a rejection of bivalence, or a rejection of the identification between present truth and present determinacy, and which of these three rejections is least damaging to the predictive practices of working science. Please treat this as conceptual analysis rather than history of philosophy, avoid bibliographic references entirely, and present your answer in terms a retired experimentalist can evaluate without recourse to textual scholarship.
