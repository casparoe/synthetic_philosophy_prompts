Student answer:
Hofstadter sees the self as a strange loop — a self-referential pattern that doesn't depend on one specific brain. Through shared memory and language, the symbolic structure that represents a person can be reconstructed in other brains, so an 'I' is not locked to a single skull. He illustrates this with his wife Carol: after her death, the patterns constituting her personality kept being activated in friends and family, meaning her 'I' persisted in a distributed form. This is closely related to Dennett's centre of narrative gravity, though Dennett treats the self as merely fictional while Hofstadter allows these distributed patterns to actually carry the original consciousness forward. One serious objection is that distributing a self across brains commits the fallacy of division, as if one mind were being sliced like a cake. Hofstadter replies that a loop isn't divided but transmitted like a radio signal that many receivers can pick up without weakening the source, so the objection misses the point.
