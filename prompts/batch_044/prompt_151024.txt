My philosophy-of-biology professor has delivered verdicts on whether particular items possess biological functions in the following cases: she holds that chloroplasts converting light into chemical energy have a function; that mammalian hearts contracting rhythmically have a function; that the audible sound produced by those contractions does not; that the reddish color of oxygenated blood does not; that the sneezing reflex triggered by nasal irritants does; that the unregulated proliferation of a malignant lung tumor does not; and that the mating song of a songbird does. Now consider a new case: researchers have engineered a strain of bacteria to express a synthetic enzyme, not present in any wild lineage, that allows the organism to metabolize a manufactured polymer in an industrial bioreactor, thereby sustaining its energy production and reproduction in that environment. Would she say this enzyme has a biological function? Identify at least two competing frameworks—such as an etiological selection-based account and a causal-role contribution account—that fit all her earlier rulings equally well, state what each predicts about the engineered enzyme, give your best guess at her actual verdict with reasoning, specify which prior case is the closest analog, and describe one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the two frameworks if her answer here remains ambiguous.
