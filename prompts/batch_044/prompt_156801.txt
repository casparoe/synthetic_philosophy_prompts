For a graduate seminar on the philosophy of cognition, I have been asked to present my working position on competence without comprehension, especially regarding evolutionary and computational explanations of intelligence. Before I finalize it, I want a precise consistency audit of the following commitments. They are meant to form a single framework, but I am unsure whether some pull in directions I do not intend.

My commitments are these:

(1) Darwin's explanatory inversion succeeds precisely because natural selection generates complex adaptive competence—organisms behaving as if they understood their ecological problems—without presupposing that any agent comprehends either the problem or its solution.

(2) Turing's corresponding inversion demonstrates that formal, syntactic rule-following can produce behavior indistinguishable from intelligent comprehension, again without the system understanding anything about the domain in which it operates.

(3) Large language models represent a hybrid case that neither framework fully captures: they acquire linguistic patterns through a statistical, population-level process over human-generated text that resembles cultural selection, yet they execute these patterns through formal computation, all while lacking both the embodied evolutionary history characteristic of organisms and the explicit symbol-manipulation architecture characteristic of classical computing machinery.

(4) I take "comprehension" to be definable in purely functional terms: it consists in integrated, sustained causal feedback loops between an entity and its environment that regulate behavior adaptively, with no necessary appeal to internal phenomenology or semantic intentionality.

(5) I deny that there is any irreducible explanatory gap between mechanistic descriptions and attributions of adaptive competence, but I maintain that there remains a genuine explanatory gap between mechanistic descriptions and attributions of comprehension, even when comprehension is characterized functionally as in (4).

(6) I hold that whenever an entity displays sustained, environment-coupled adaptive competence—whether achieved through biological evolution, formal computation, or statistical learning—we are epistemically justified in attributing to it a minimal, non-phenomenal form of understanding, though not full comprehension.

Please audit these for tension: identify which pairs genuinely conflict, which conflicts are only apparent, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each tension, specify exactly which commitment must be revised and what that revision costs. In particular, address whether my hybrid characterization of language models in (3) is compatible with my functional criterion in (4) and with my claim in (6) that competence alone warrants minimal understanding. Also say whether (1) and (2) are in competition or structurally parallel.

I invite direct pushback: if the task as posed rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the distinction between Darwinian and Turing-style inversions is not as sharp as I treat it, or if my hybrid framing of language models misapplies the competence-without-comprehension framework—please say so plainly rather than forcing consistency where none exists. I am not asking for a historical summary of Darwin or Turing, but for a precise diagnosis of what my own position entails.
