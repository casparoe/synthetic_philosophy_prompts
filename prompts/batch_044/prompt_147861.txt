I've been working through an argument about historical overdetermination and now I'm stuck because the conclusion seems to destroy historical explanation altogether. Consider the invented case of the Meridian Republic's collapse in 1847, where a drought, a tax revolt, and a foreign naval blockade each appear sufficient, in combination with background conditions, to bring down the government.

(1) For any factor F to genuinely explain an event E, F must make a counterfactual difference: had F not occurred, E would not have occurred.
(2) In the Meridian case, had the drought not occurred—while the tax revolt and blockade still happened—the collapse still occurs; the same holds if we remove the revolt or the blockade individually.
(3) Therefore, none of the three factors makes a counterfactual difference to the collapse.
(4) But this reasoning applies whenever multiple contributing conditions are each part of distinct sufficient sets—i.e., to virtually every complex historical event.
(5) So for virtually every complex event, no particular factor explains it.
(6) Yet historians routinely assign explanatory priority to specific factors (e.g., calling the blockade "the decisive cause").
(7) To preserve (1), historians must deny that the other conditions were genuinely sufficient—forcing them into implausible monocausal revisionism.
(8) Since such revisionism is methodologically disastrous, (1) must be false, and counterfactual dependence is useless for history.

This feels wrong: I want to keep (1) and also keep the intuition that historians can legitimately single out the blockade. But if I keep (1), I seem forced into (7). Where does the argument slip? Is there an equivocation between "F makes no difference" and "F is not part of the actual causal path that occurred"? Or does the error lie elsewhere—perhaps in how (2) treats the independence of the remaining conditions? What does fixing it cost: do I have to abandon counterfactual analysis entirely, restrict its scope to INUS-style conditions within the actual sufficient complex, or accept that historians regularly overclaim?

Please diagnose the argument: identify the flawed step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives (keeping (1) versus preserving multi-causal historiography). Conclude with a line beginning "Verdict:" that states the step you reject and, in a few words, the main cost of that rejection.
