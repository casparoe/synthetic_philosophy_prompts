At a seminar on decision theory, two graduate students, Jordan and Sam, debated the backward induction paradox in the centipede game. Jordan opened by laying out the standard argument: since at the final node taking dominates continuing, rational players should take there; anticipating this, they should take at the penultimate node, and so on, all the way back to the first move, so rational play ought to end immediately. Sam agreed the logic was valid but challenged its applicability, arguing that backward induction presupposes a principle of strategic transparency—the idea that one's rationality at any future node is fully common knowledge and causally independent of present choices—which is self-undermining in a finite sequential game: if transparency holds all the way back, the game never reaches the nodes where the reasoning applies, just as in the surprise examination paradox, where common knowledge of the exam's surprise conditions eliminates the conditions for surprise.

Jordan replied that weakening the transparency premise merely changes the subject. The paradox asks what rationality requires given the game's structure, not what people will actually do when transparency fails. Even if transparency is an idealization, the normative point stands: if you reach node k knowing the other player is rational, you should take, and this should be anticipated at every prior node. Sam countered that this confuses local rationality evaluated node-by-node with global strategic coherence. At node 1, continuing is globally optimal; the induction forces you to evaluate each step in isolation, but rationality should permit carrying out a globally superior plan unless there is a genuine local reason to deviate. Jordan responded that global coherence is just a label for ignoring the sequential nature of the choice: at each node you face a fresh decision, and there is no external authority enforcing a long-term plan against your present interests. The only reason to continue is the expectation of future cooperation, but backward induction shows that expectation is irrational.

Sam then introduced a more specific claim: the induction works only if the probability of deviation at each step is exactly zero. In any realistic epistemic setting, there is always some non-zero credence that an opponent reasons differently or makes an error. Once you allow even an epsilon of uncertainty, the induction collapses, and continuing becomes rational. Jordan conceded that with uncertainty the calculation changes, but insisted that this resolves the paradox only descriptively, not normatively: if we are asking what perfectly rational agents should do, epsilon is zero by definition. Sam closed by arguing that this makes backward induction a reductio of perfect strategic transparency rather than a guide to rational action, since the conditions that make epsilon zero also make the game impossible to play through.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For Jordan's initial presentation, Sam's transparency challenge, Jordan's normative and local-rationality reply, Sam's global-coherence and epsilon-uncertainty points, and Jordan's final normative-descriptive distinction, rate each as clearly right, partly right but missing something, or clearly mistaken, with brief justification. Then say who has the better of the argument overall, and identify what both are missing—specifically, whether there is a way to preserve the normative force of backward induction without requiring that the game actually be played through to later nodes.
