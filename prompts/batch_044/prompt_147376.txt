I'm drafting the introductory framing for an essay in a philosophy of history seminar, tentatively titled The Temporality of Invention: Hobsbawm, Anderson, and the National Present. The conventional reading treats Hobsbawm's analysis of invented tradition and Anderson's thesis of imagined communities as complementary explanations of modern nationhood: Hobsbawm shows how elites fabricate continuity with a suitable historic past through ritual and repetition, while Anderson shows how print-capitalism enables citizens to imagine horizontal solidarity with strangers across space. I want to argue that these accounts embed incompatible temporal logics that are rarely acknowledged together. Hobsbawm's tradition is essentially retrospective: it deploys ceremonial repetition and symbolic archaism to conceal rupture and present the present as an extension of an immemorial past. Anderson's account, by contrast, turns on a secular, forward-moving chronology—an empty, homogeneous time—in which citizens imagine themselves acting simultaneously toward an open future. If both analyses are correct, then the modern nation-state must simultaneously deny and affirm its own novelty, claiming ancient roots while operating through a distinctly modern temporal consciousness. I propose treating this not as a superficial rhetorical strategy but as a structural temporal paradox inherent to nationalism itself. Before developing historical illustrations, I want critical feedback on the conceptual architecture alone. First, is the tension I identify genuinely substantive, or does it dissolve once we rigorously distinguish the strategic intentions of state-building elites from the phenomenological experience of ordinary citizens? Second, does my framing risk conflating Hobsbawm's emphasis on elite fabrication with Anderson's emphasis on popular imagination in a way that misrepresents either account's scope? Third, is there a more precise philosophical vocabulary—perhaps from theories of collective intentionality or historical ontology—that would let me articulate this tension without relying on a dubious notion of collective consciousness? I am not seeking bibliographic suggestions, citation verification, or a literature survey; I want an assessment of whether the framing is coherent, philosophically interesting, and robust enough to sustain a full argument.
