I am preparing an essay for my upper-division course in Medieval Philosophy and Comparative Religion, and I need help interpreting a difficult passage in Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed. In the section where he treats the rationale for the sacrificial laws, Maimonides contends that the commandment to offer animals was never intended to satisfy a divine need, but rather functioned as a therapeutic concession: because the Israelites had been habituated to the idolatrous worship of beasts and to the offering of food in their honor, the Law permitted a parallel practice directed toward God, gradually redirecting an entrenched psychological and cultural pattern away from paganism and toward monotheism.

I am trying to understand whether this therapeutic-concession argument can be rigorously mapped onto what I will call structured transitionalism—the thesis that an institution which embeds a moral wrong can be provisionally justified only if its internal logic includes a determinate mechanism for its own eventual dissolution, rather than merely accommodating the wrong in perpetuity. Read in this light, Maimonides seems to suggest that sacrificial worship was legitimate not despite its violence but because it was designed to become obsolete, replaced eventually by prayer and interior devotion. If so, does his framework entail that any concession to a harmful practice that lacks such an engineered sunset clause is not truly therapeutic but merely palliative?

I also want to test this analogy against a modern parallel. Some contemporary ethicists of animal agriculture argue that incremental welfare reforms—such as improved stunning protocols or enriched enclosures—operate as transitional concessions that will eventually render the industry unnecessary, while abolitionists reply that these measures stabilize the institution without any credible endpoint. Applying Maimonides' reasoning, should we conclude that welfare reforms are analogous to sacrificial worship only if they are accompanied by an explicit institutional trajectory toward abolition? Or does the comparison collapse because sacrificial worship possessed a specifically religious teleology—an ordained prophetic endpoint—that secular industrial practices inherently lack?

Please help me unpack the logic of Maimonides' passage and assess whether this comparison illuminates or distorts his position.
