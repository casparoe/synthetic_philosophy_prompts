Been chewing on Scheffler's "afterlife" idea -- that we care about science, art, or even having kids mostly because we tacitly assume future generations will exist to carry the thread forward. I tried to turn it into a tight argument and now it seems to show that anyone in a "terminal generation" should see all their projects as normatively empty. I must have slipped somewhere.

(1) If the conjecture is correct, then the reason we value present projects is that they contribute to an ongoing human story.
(2) So the normative reason to pursue a project depends on there actually being a future story for it to feed into.
(3) Whether humanity continues is mostly outside any individual's control.
(4) Therefore, for a rational agent, the value of her own projects depends on conditions she can't influence.
(5) Now imagine a small isolated colony that knows it's the last generation -- no descendants, no continuation at all.
(6) By (2), their research, art, and relationships should have lost their normative point.
(7) Yet we think they still have full reason to live well and care for each other; saying they're in a "normative void" feels wrong.
(8) So either (2) is false, or we bite the bullet on (7).
(9) Biting (7) seems crazy, so (2) must go.
(10) But (2) was the bridge from the psychological conjecture to actual value. Without it, the conjecture collapses into just a descriptive claim about motivation, not about what's worth doing.
(11) Therefore the conjecture either proves too much (making terminal lives meaningless) or proves nothing normative.

I feel like there's a scope slide or an equivocation hiding between (1) and (2) -- maybe between "value is psychologically sustained by expectation" and "value is metaphysically constituted by actual continuation" -- but I can't name it acutally. I'd rather keep (7) intact than save the conjecture, but I want to know exactly which step to reject and what I'm paying. Is the move just to deny the normative reading entirely?
