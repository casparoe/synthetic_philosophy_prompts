I've been working through Hofstadter's three-layer model—the frame as the physical substrate, the outer message as the syntactic pattern accessible to a decoder, and the inner message as the semantic content that emerges only when the right interpretation scheme meets the right pattern. The guiding idea is that meaning resides in the inner message, not in the frame or the outer message alone. I tried to formalize this and ended up with an argument that seems to undermine the very distinction it starts from.

(1) Meaning is located entirely in the inner message.
(2) The inner message is fully determined by the outer message together with an interpretation scheme; the frame contributes nothing to meaning beyond sustaining the outer message.
(3) So if two frames realize identical outer messages and are interpreted by identical schemes, they carry identical meanings.
(4) Now imagine a synthetic substrate—say, a cloud of ionized gas arranged by pure thermal noise—that happens to instantiate, for an instant, the exact same state-pattern as a human brain experiencing acute pain.
(5) A neuroscientist applies the standard brain-decoding scheme to the cloud. By (2) and (3), the cloud carries the inner message "I am in acute pain."
(6) But a randomly formed gas cloud is not literally suffering; attributing pain to it is not merely false but conceptually wrong, because there is no subject, no evolutionary history, and no causal coupling to injury.
(7) Therefore (3) must be false: identical outer messages plus identical schemes do not guarantee identical meanings.
(8) The only available repair is to say that the frame's causal history—evolution, learning, sensory engagement—enters into the interpretation scheme itself, so the scheme applied to the cloud is not really identical to the one applied to a brain.
(9) But if the interpretation scheme already includes the frame's history, then meaning is partly intrinsic to the frame, and the inner message is no longer a separate layer that could, in principle, migrate to any substrate.
(10) So either we accept that cosmic accidents can suffer (keep 3), or we abandon the claim that meaning is fully relational and collapse the frame/inner-message distinction (reject 3).

I feel like there might be a middle path—perhaps something like "interpretive inertia," where schemes are causally anchored to the frames they evolved for without being reducible to them—but I'm not sure where exactly to place that move without either saving the framework at the cost of trivializing it or giving up substrate independence entirely. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? If I keep (3), am I committed to panpsychism about accidental patterns? If I reject it, have I given up on the idea that meaning could ever be realized in a different medium, or is there a more surgical option?
