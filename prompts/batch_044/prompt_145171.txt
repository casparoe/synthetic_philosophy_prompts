As a physicist recently retired from a career in low-temperature thermodynamics, I have begun to examine ethical and metaphysical questions with the same habit of isolating variables and testing limiting cases. I have become convinced of a position I will call tranquilism: that the absence of craving and suffering is the sole intrinsic good, and that every other value—knowledge, beauty, achievement, relational attachment—is at best instrumentally justified by its contribution to that absence. This is not merely a loose restatement of familiar ascetic themes; I mean it in a stricter form, where even the positive affect associated with understanding or discovery carries no independent weight if it is not reducible to the cessation of a prior disturbance.

Within this framework, I see several open conceptual lines, but I am unsure which are genuinely upstream. First, there is the measurement problem analog: is tranquility a binary state or a continuous variable with an asymptotic limit—like absolute zero—such that complete absence is physically or psychologically impossible and only degrees of approximation are meaningful? Second, there is a structural distinction I want to press: between static tranquility, the temporary non-occurrence of craving in a given moment, and dynamic tranquility, the dissolution of the causal conditions that regenerate craving over time. Does tranquilism require both, or does the view collapse into a purely momentary ethics? Third, there is an implication for the design and evaluation of artificial systems: if a digital architecture lacks phenomenological suffering but operates with goal-directed optimization loops that are structurally analogous to craving, should the governing principle be to minimize those loops, or is the analogy too weak to carry normative weight? Fourth, there is a reflexive challenge: the desire to understand tranquilism itself, or to defend it philosophically, appears to be a form of intellectual craving. Does the view entail that philosophical inquiry—including this very project—is instrumentally worthless unless it directly reduces suffering?

Given that my time and energy for this work are limited—perhaps the equivalent of one serious research project—I want to know which of these questions, or which others you think I have missed, should take priority. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature. I want a defended agenda: rank the open questions by which ones are conceptually tractable without empirical neuroscience, which ones block the others if left unresolved, and which ones, if answered, would actually change how one lives or evaluates scientific and technological practice. In particular, defend whether the static-dynamic distinction is a genuine bifurcation within the view or a pseudo-problem generated by importing thermodynamic metaphors; whether the reflexive challenge undermines the motivation for philosophical work altogether or only constrains its methods; and whether the question about artificial systems is an application of tranquilism or a test that could falsify it. I need the reasoning to be explicit about dependencies—what must be settled before what—and about what argument, available to reasoning alone, could move any of these up or down in priority.
