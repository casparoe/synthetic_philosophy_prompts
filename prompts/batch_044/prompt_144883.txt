Following my mother's recent diagnosis with an early-onset neurodegenerative disorder, I have been studying the ethics of assisted dying not as an abstraction but as a practical framework I may eventually need to navigate. In that reading, I have become fixated on a particular impasse in clinical ethics regarding psychiatric and existential suffering. One camp holds that assisted dying should extend to cases of treatment-resistant depression, and even to elderly persons who describe their lives as "complete" in the absence of severe somatic illness, because the criterion of "unbearable and irremediable suffering" applies with equal moral weight regardless of whether its source is physical or mental. The opposing camp maintains that mental illness inherently compromises the autonomy required for such a request to be valid, and that "existential suffering" is not a clinical entity but a philosophical misdescription of social isolation or the natural limits of aging. Both sides cite the same longitudinal data on regret and long-term psychiatric outcomes, and both agree that suffering is intrinsically bad, yet neither has shifted its core position in over a decade. Is this disagreement genuinely about a hidden empirical question—perhaps whether a specific cognitive distortion is present in every such request—or is it a clash between two incompatible conceptions of autonomy, one procedural and the other substantive? Alternatively, are the sides answering different questions under a single name: one asking whether we ought to respect an expressed preference, the other asking whether the conditions for genuine preference-formation have been satisfied? Given that diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress? Would it require a formal distinction between procedural and substantive autonomy, a reconceptualization of "irremediable" that separates clinical prognosis from narrative closure, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed because "suffering" operates with distinct semantic rules in psychiatric versus somatic contexts? I am not seeking a verdict on which position should prevail; I want to understand the structure of the disagreement, and whether the impasse indicates that progress is impossible or merely that we have not yet identified the right distinction.
