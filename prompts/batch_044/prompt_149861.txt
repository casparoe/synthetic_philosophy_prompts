I have drafted preliminary notes for an essay provisionally titled Asymmetric Dependence and the Evasion of Bradley's Regress, and I should like your critical assessment of the conceptual architecture rather than of its scholarly apparatus. The central claim is that Bradley's regress is typically interpreted as demonstrating that any external relation R linking relata a and b requires a further relation to bind R to them, generating an infinite explanatory hierarchy. I propose that this regress can be blocked not by internalizing relations in the Russellian fashion—treating them as already unified with their terms—but by reconceiving relations as directionally dependent entities whose ontological being is constituted by an ordered, non-reciprocal orientation toward their relata. On this account, to ask what binds R to a is to misapprehend R's categorical form: its connection to a and b is not an additional relational fact but the internal structure of its dependence, analogous perhaps to the way a vector's direction is inseparable from its magnitude rather than an appended property.

The notes unfold in four stages. First, I reconstruct the regress as an explanatory failure rather than a mere ontological proliferation: each binding relation introduces a new fact demanding the same form of explanation. Second, I distinguish my proposal from the standard Russell-Moore response, noting that I wish to retain the externality of relations while denying that they are entities that stand alongside their relata in need of linkage. Third, I develop the notion of asymmetric dependence binding: the unity of the proposition aRb derives from the irreducible directional sequence in which R depends on a and then on b, such that no second-order relation is required to secure coherence. Fourth, I anticipate the objection that directional dependence is itself a relational property of R, thereby reinstating the regress at the meta-level; I reply that dependence here is not a property instantiated by R but the categorical mode of R's existence.

I am particularly interested in your judgment on three points: whether the distinction between a relation conceived as an entity and a relation conceived as a mode of directional dependence is genuinely defensible or merely verbal; whether the vector analogy withstands scrutiny once we acknowledge that mathematical directions are still describable by relational predicates; and whether this approach implicitly collapses into a form of monism or trope-theoretic particularism that I have not adequately acknowledged. Please evaluate the coherence of each stage, identify the step most vulnerable to collapse, and suggest whether the argument succeeds in offering a genuinely novel resolution or only restates, in different vocabulary, difficulties already addressed in the literature on relational unity.
