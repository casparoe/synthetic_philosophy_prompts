I sit on the ethics and acquisistions board of the Meridian Cultural Archive, and we've hit a real mess. I'm also taking a seminar on moral psychology this semester, and this case keeps looping in my head. One of our senior curators, Dr. Elena Vasquez, has spent about 18 years building a living oral-history collection from the Marrow Creek community—it's basically her ground project, the thing that gives her life shape. Last month she discovered our largest endowment, the Halloway Fund, was capitalized through land deals that directly caused the displacement of those same families. Our lawyers say if we don't disclose and begin restitution we'll risk our nonprofit status, maybe worse. Vasquez isn't disputing the facts, but she's refusing to sign the disclosure memmo. She says that if she goes along with the board's institutional calculus—preservation of the archive versus legal liability—she'd be treating her relationship with the Marrow Creek families as just another input in a utilitarian weighing. She brought up Bernard Williams and that idea about one thought too many, where an agent's deepest commitments shouldn't be filtered through impartial cost-benefit reasoning because that destroys the conditions of having a life that's genuinely one's own. She argues that demanding she participate in the board's decision framework is exactly the kind of alienating demand Williams warned about.

We meet Thursday and I need a memo I can circulate before then. It should recomend a concrete course—approve the disclosure with conditions, reject the disclosure request, or approve with protected status for Vasquez's archive and some kind of phased timeline—and it has to treat her position seriously, not just restate nonprofit compliance law. I'm particularly stuck on whether Williams's integrity objection actually gives her a reason to refuse institutional cooperation here, or whether it just shows that any institutional ethics process necessarily demands that one extra thought from people whose work is bound up with personal meaning. The memo should engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly and say what the board could actually respond to. No need for citations or a bibliography, just the reasoning and the recommendation with a clear bottom line.
