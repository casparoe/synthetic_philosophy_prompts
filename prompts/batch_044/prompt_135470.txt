As a retired experimental physicist who spent thirty years in condensed-matter research before turning, somewhat belatedly, to philosophy of science and mathematics, I have been trying to organize my views on the reach and limits of formalization, and I would welcome a consistency audit before I present these thoughts to a small reading group of former colleagues. Here is where I stand.

First, I take Gödel's first incompleteness theorem to be a strictly syntactic result: in any consistent formal system rich enough to encode elementary arithmetic, there exist arithmetical truths that the system itself cannot prove. I insist that this is not, by itself, a thesis about human cognition, biological brains, or the limits of artificial computation—those extrapolations require additional premises that Gödel's proof does not supply. Second, I maintain that empirical scientific theories—whether quantum field theory or climate dynamics—are not the kind of recursively axiomatized structures to which the theorem applies; they are not formal systems in the requisite sense, so one cannot literally derive a "Gödel sentence" for physics. Third, despite that restriction, I think there is a genuine structural analogy between Gödelian incompleteness and what philosophers of science call the Duhem-Quine problem: just as a formal system needs external supplementation to settle every arithmetical question, a scientific theory needs auxiliary hypotheses, boundary conditions, and background assumptions to yield unique observational predictions. I do not claim this analogy proves that physics is "incomplete" in Gödel's technical sense, but I treat it as more than a mere metaphor—it reveals a shared logical pattern of underdetermination. Fourth, I believe that working mathematicians can indeed recognize the truth of particular Gödel sentences for specific formalizations, which suggests that human mathematical understanding reaches beyond the derivational resources of the system being studied; yet I reject both Lucas's and Penrose's arguments that this demonstrates the non-computational nature of human minds. Fifth, I am a physicalist about mind and cognition: I see mental processes as ultimately physical processes, without invoking any non-physical substance or property. Sixth, I read Gödel's second incompleteness theorem as showing that mathematics cannot fully ground its own consistency from within its formal apparatus, and I take this to provide modest support for a moderate Platonism about mathematical objects: we grasp mathematical truths partly because they describe an independent domain, not merely because we construct formal games.

My question is whether these six commitments form a coherent package. Specifically, I worry that my acceptance of the Duhem-Quine analogy in my third point smuggles back in the very application to empirical science that my second point denies; I also wonder whether my claim in the fourth point about mathematicians recognizing Gödel truths is compatible with my denial in the first point that the theorem bears on cognition, and whether that cognitive claim conflicts with my physicalism in the fifth point. Meanwhile, I suspect that the tension between my Platonism in the sixth point and my physicalism in the fifth is only apparent, but I am not certain. Please audit these pairings with precision: identify which tensions are real and which dissolve on closer inspection, rank the real ones by severity, and for each specify the least costly revision—whether that means abandoning the analogy, weakening the cognitive claim, modifying the physicalism, or giving up the Platonism. I am not asking for a lecture on Gödel's proof, but for a map of exactly what my position commits me to and where it risks collapsing.
