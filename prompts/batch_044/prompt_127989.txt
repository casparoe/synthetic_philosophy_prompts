I have spent some months observing, in my own life and among friends, the way people stop searching for apartments, jobs, or even vacation plans long before they have surveyed the full range of possibilities. The pattern I keep noticing is not merely that people settle for something “good enough,” but that the mental definition of “good enough” itself seems to warp under pressure: once a viable option appears, the checklist—budget, location, noise, commute—quietly rearranges its weights so that the available choice suddenly looks optimal. I have started calling this “threshold drift,” though I do not know whether it carries an established name in any formal literature.

I want to design a single 90-minute workshop for about sixteen adults with no background in economics, psychology, or decision theory. The goal is not to lecture them on bounded rationality, but to make them physically experience the moment when their own aspiration levels bend to justify stopping a search. Please design a fully runnable group activity—complete with explicit rules, hidden information cards, assigned roles if useful, and a token or point system that creates real but small stakes—where participants must choose among options that differ on several incommensurable dimensions, while an accumulating hidden “search cost” makes continued exploration increasingly painful. Arrange the mechanics so that groups discover on their own, before any explanation is given, that they have lowered or reweighted a criterion in order to declare a winner and stop looking.

Specify exactly what happens in each timed phase, how the payoff structure creates the pressure to stop, and precisely what the facilitator should say (and avoid saying) before the debrief. Then provide the debrief: map specific moments in the activity—such as the first group that stops early, or the instant a hidden cost is revealed—onto the conceptual parts of aspiration-level adjustment and bounded search. Be explicit about what will predictably break the first time the workshop is run, for example if participants try to game the point system rather than feel the drift, or if one dominant voice forces a premature collective stop, and tell me how to correct for that through rule tweaks or facilitation moves. Finally, be honest about which feature of this real-world phenomenon the workshop cannot reproduce—perhaps the long-term emotional weight of foregone alternatives, or the irreversibility of actual commitments—and explain how the facilitator must supply that missing piece verbally once the activity ends.

Please do not require any advance reading, do not include bibliographies, citations, or named theorists that participants would need to look up, and avoid technical vocabulary that depends on outside study.
