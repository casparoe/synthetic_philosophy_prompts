You are the senior curator of origin narratives at a national museum, and your oversight board requires you to classify every proposed gallery narrative—before funding is released—into one of three bins: "historical account with legendary overlay," "legend with a recoverable historical kernel," or "legend without recoverable history." You face roughly twenty-five proposals per quarter, you have two hours per case, you must sign the classification personally, and you have no specialist consultant on call for every submission. Your board rejects vague appeals to "scholarly consensus"; they want a written, reusable decision procedure that any trained curator can run, with explicit points where the procedure stops with a "no" and explicit points where it hands off to human judgment because the question is one of values rather than facts. Write this procedure as an ordered flowchart in words: the specific tests to run in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the path branches, and which distinctions must survive simplification and which can be dropped. Ground the procedure in the epistemic distinction between evidential independence—material or textual correlation that does not depend on interpreting the narrative through itself—and narrative dependence, together with a measure of legendary accretion velocity, that is, the rate at which supernatural, heroic, or anachronistic motifs accumulate relative to the earliest attestation layer. The procedure must be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as checklists, and it must flag the cases it cannot settle. Then apply it to three proposals: (1) a gallery framing the Trojan War as a historical conflict at Hisarlık with epic overlay; (2) a gallery presenting King Arthur as a post-Roman military leader behind the medieval romances; and (3) a gallery on the Exodus that treats the narrative as a national-origin legend with no recoverable historical kernel. Include one case where the procedure delivers a classification you expect the board will reject, and explain exactly which feature of the case forces that result. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—where it will misfire, what kinds of proposals it should never be used for, and why a curator still needs to override it. Keep the deliverable practical: no bibliographies, no citation requests, no appeals to authority that a curator without web access could not verify in the two-hour window.
