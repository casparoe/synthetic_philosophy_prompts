In my sixth-form philosophy discussion group we have been working through Daniel Dennett’s brain-transplant thought experiment — the case in which Hamlet’s brain is placed in a vat while his original body remains elsewhere — and a classmate keeps falling back on the line “you are wherever your brain is”. He has used it both to insist that Hamlet is genuinely in the vat and to dismiss the suggestion that the original embodied person survives in the operating theatre as a kind of “empty shell”. I want to take back to him a precise disambiguation of that slogan. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single answer, the distinct philosophical claims it might be smuggling in — for instance, a claim about spatial coincidence, a claim about the necessary conditions for consciousness, a claim about personal identity over time, and a claim about whether embodiment is metaphysically essential — and for each indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, and crucially whether it actually does the work he needs it to do (settling Dennett’s puzzle without begging the question against the view that the whole organism is the subject of experience)? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
