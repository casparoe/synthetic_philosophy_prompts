Hey — I'm covering game-theory crossovers for a science/ideas column and this argument my editor sent over is eating me. It starts neat then runs straight off the rails.

(1) The strategy-stealing argument proves that in any finite, perfect-information, two-player, zero-sum game with no draws, the first player has a winning strategy. The trick: if player two had one, player one makes an arbitrary opening move then "steals" that strategy — the extra move can't hurt, since any plan that works for second still works for first.

(2) Real-world competitions — market entry, debate framing, even military escalation — are structurally the same kind of game: sequential, payoffs are zero-sum, and information eventually gets revealed.

(3) There's also a symmetry condition. In Hex the board doesn't care who placed the first stone. Social games work the same way — the rules don't change just because you spoke first or moved first, so your "dummy" opening is strategically invisible.

(4) So the first actor in any competitive interaction should be able to steal the opponent's best response and guarantee victory, since having an extra option never hurts you.

(5) But that's obviosly false. Second-movers win all the time: fast-followers in tech, reactive campaigns, negotiators who wait to see the cards.

(6) Therefore strategy-stealing must collapse the instant you leave the board, which means there's a hidden assumption that breaks in real life.

I really want to keep (1) — it's a beautiful formal result — and I definitely want to keep (5). That puts the damage somewhere around (3), where I smuggle social symmetry in by treating information leakage and revealed preference as irrelevant. But is that the right cut? If I reject (3) I have to admit that in real interactions every "dummy" move changes the opponent's belief state, which kills the portability of the proof — but then I'm also saying formal symmetry is never portable outside combinatorics. Which step is actually wrong, and what's the real price of fixing it?
