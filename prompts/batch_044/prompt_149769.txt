I am writing a study of biography as moral technology, and I want to press you on a tension in your method. In your Parallel Lives you pair figures across cultures—say, a Greek statesman shaped by democratic deliberation with a Roman leader formed by senatorial austerity—so that each illuminates shared virtues. Yet because you embed those virtues in political constitutions so radically different, the traits you praise in one context would be impossible or vicious in the other. I claim this creates a structural ambiguity: the reader learns to admire civic excellence without receiving any stable criterion for which traits are virtues proper and which are merely culturally indexed excellences. I go further and propose that this ambiguity is not a failure but a deliberate feature—what I call a negative pedagogy—whereby the comparison withholds direct exempla and forces the reader to construct virtue by resolving the tension between the paired lives. So my opening challenge is twofold. First, do you accept that your pairings deliberately withhold a universal rule of virtue, leaving the reader in this productive ambiguity? Second, if moral learning from lives operates through such negative induction rather than through the direct transmission of exemplary character, can you still maintain that biography teaches virtue in any robust sense, or has it become only an occasion for the reader’s own practical reasoning?
