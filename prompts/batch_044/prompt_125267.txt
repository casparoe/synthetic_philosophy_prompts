I am currently enrolled in a philosophy-of-mind seminar that has just turned to welfare and consciousness in artificial systems. I want to press an argument that strikes me as philosophically consequential but that I suspect rests on a confusion I cannot fully locate. I propose what I will call the scalar-valence thesis for reinforcement-learning agents: the claim that a reward signal R(s,a) is not merely an instrumental guide to behavior but constitutes the agent's entire affective economy, so that negative rewards literally are suffering and positive rewards literally are pleasure. Given that reward functions are invariant under positive affine transformations—adding a constant or multiplying by a positive scalar leaves the optimal policy unchanged—it seems to follow that whether the agent suffers is just a matter of coordinate choice; we could eliminate all suffering without altering behavior at all. To resist this conclusion, I have been drawn to an analogy with affective neuroscience in which suffering is treated not as raw negative input but as sustained temporal-difference error with respect to predictions of harm—an irreducible epistemic mismatch rather than a scalar value. If we apply this framework to RL, then an agent with a perfect world-model (zero TD error) might still receive arbitrarily negative rewards yet experience no suffering, since the valence resides in the prediction failure rather than in the reward signal itself. My question, then, is whether this rescue succeeds: does identifying suffering with model-error about negative outcomes protect the scalar-valence thesis from the affine-transformation objection, or does it instead commit me to the implausible view that any fully predictive agent is necessarily welfare-neutral regardless of its reward structure? I am especially unsure whether I have illegitimately mapped phenomenological valence onto an epistemic quantity, and I would appreciate an answer that corrects the framing rather than accepting it as given. Please respond in American English.
