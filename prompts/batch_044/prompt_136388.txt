I am preparing an argument for a graduate seminar on democratic ethics, and I find the standard literature somewhat unsatisfying because it tends to oscillate between treating the ballot as a purely instrumental causal lever and treating it as an expressive gesture of civic solidarity, without a clear account of how these dimensions interact under structural conditions. To sharpen the question, I want to adopt a framing I will call authoritative assent: the idea that casting a vote is not merely registering a preference but performing a minimal act of co-authorship in collective decisions, one that carries normative weight only when the electoral system exhibits what I will term proportional accountability—that is, when the translation of votes into policy outcomes does not systematically nullify or dilute the preferences of participants who act in good faith. On this view, the moral status of voting depends jointly on the voter’s intentions and on whether the institutional context preserves the reciprocal link between assent and result.

With that framing in place, write an essay on the following controversy. Is there a genuine moral duty to vote in large-scale democratic elections where one’s individual ballot is virtually certain not to be decisive? And when every available candidate is morally objectionable but one is clearly the lesser evil, does casting a vote for that candidate constitute an act of responsible co-authorship, or does it amount to unjustified complicity in wrongdoing? Weigh the instrumental argument (that non-pivotal votes have negligible causal efficacy and therefore generate no obligation), the expressive argument (that participation affirms democratic values independently of outcome), and the authoritative-assent argument sketched above. Reach and defend a definite conclusion: either argue that a qualified duty to vote for the lesser evil holds precisely under conditions of proportional accountability, or maintain that the duty is illusory and that abstention, protest voting, or other forms of refusal are morally preferable. Your conclusion should explicitly address how it handles severely flawed candidates and structurally distorted electoral systems in which proportional accountability is absent or severely weakened.
