In Plato’s Symposium, Socrates alone remains fully sober while the other guests become increasingly intoxicated, and his unbroken clarity is presented as essential to genuine philosophical inquiry. Given this portrait, the Athenian Stranger’s proposal in the Laws—that the well-ordered city should institutionalize regulated symposia in which citizens drink wine together under official supervision—appears to stand in direct contradiction: if wisdom requires the kind of absolute self-restraint Socrates displays, why would Plato legislate collective drinking as a civic practice rather than prohibit it? Is the Laws passage best understood as endorsing ritualized intoxication as a pedagogical test—analogous to administering a mild irritant to observe a constitution—or does it rely on a conception of moderation that is not actually at odds with Socratic sobriety?
