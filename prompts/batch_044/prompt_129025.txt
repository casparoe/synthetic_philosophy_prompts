I am a novelist revising a literary novel in which several central relationships are structured around asymmetrical recognition: one figure holds institutional or emotional authority over another, yet remains existentially dependent on that subordinate's acknowledgment. I want to turn Hegel's dialectic of lordship and bondage into a practical revision procedure—not literary criticism, but a reusable diagnostic I can run on any dominant-subordinate dyad in a draft—without reducing it to a generic domination trope. Please write a step-by-step checklist, ordered for execution in about thirty minutes per relationship, that asks: how recognition is sought and withheld; where dependence reverses the apparent hierarchy; what form of labor or objectification transforms the subordinate's self-consciousness; whether the stakes approximate an existential risk rather than mere inconvenience; and at which point mutual recognition is either achieved or structurally blocked. For each step, specify what answer confirms the dialectic is genuinely present, what answer breaks it, and where the question is one of artistic values rather than philosophical fact, so that judgment—not procedure—must decide. Include explicit flagging of the false-positive case in which mutual dependence exists but recognition was never genuinely risked, yielding a result the procedure produces but I should reject on narrative grounds. Then apply the procedure to three drafted relationships: an aging composer who requires his silent copyist's verification to believe in his own genius; a prison warden whose authority depends specifically on one inmate's refusal to grant it; and a mentoring pair in which dependence is real but the risk of recognition was avoided, producing the false positive. State clearly what the procedure cannot do—especially its inability to generate emotional texture or replace dramatic instinct. Deliver the answer in American English.
