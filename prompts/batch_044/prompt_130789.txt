question: buddhist ethics is often called consequentialist, but scholars disagree about how far the analogy runs. discuss the role of cetana (intention) and upaya (skillfull means) in that debate, with attention to whether anatta (no permanent self) strengthens or weakens a consequentialist reading.

student answer:

buddhist ethics is consequentialist at its core, but it adds a mental-state filter western versions dont have. the buddha says cetana is karma, so intention determines the moral weight of an action more than the bare result. that sounds agent-centered, but because of anatta theres actually no persistent agent to carry moral responsibility, which removes deontological constraints and pushes the view toward act-utilitarianism over a stream of conscious moments. upaya — skillfull means — makes this explicit: a bodhisattva can violate precepts if the net reduction in dukkha is large enough, so precepts are just heuristic rules that get overridden by outcome calculations. the pali texts support this framing because they keep asking whether an action produces harm or welfare, not whether the agent has good character. so the absence of self doesnt weaken consequentialism; it actually strips away agent-based objections to pure outcome-maximization, leaving a hybrid where cetana sets direction but the final metric is still suffering-reduction.
