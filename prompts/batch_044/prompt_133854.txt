I have been reflecting on a decision problem that arises in both personal and collective planning, particularly when facing outcomes that are both extremely unlikely and catastrophically severe. The conventional framework I encounter treats such situations through expected utility: weighting each outcome by its probability and summing the results. Yet this arithmetic seems to miss something important—namely, that a one-in-a-thousand chance of total systemic collapse appears to call for a qualitatively different kind of deliberation than a ten-percent chance of a serious but recoverable setback, even when the products happen to align. This has led me to wonder whether there is a defensible way to incorporate the "weight" of risk itself—independent of pure probability—into rational choice, without collapsing into inconsistency. Because I lack formal training in philosophy or economics, I am not seeking bibliographies, citations, or lists of canonical texts; I am seeking a defended agenda.

Among the open conceptual questions in this space—whether risk-weighted utility can be formalized without violating standard axioms of consistency, what it implies for institutions that must make irreversible commitments over long time horizons, and whether it introduces a hidden asymmetry between spreading a severe risk across a large population versus concentrating it on a few—how should they be prioritized? Please defend the ordering: identify which question is upstream of the others, which is tractable through conceptual reasoning rather than technical mathematics, and what concrete case or counterexample over the coming years would cause you to revise the ranking. In particular, I want to understand whether this approach conflicts with the principle that rational planning ought to treat equivalent harms symmetrically regardless of their distribution, since if such a tension is genuine, it should significantly affect how seriously we take the risk-weighting framework itself.
