I am preparing an argument for my philosophy of mind course about sphexishness — the mechanical rigidity displayed by sphex wasps, which perform an invariant burrow-checking ritual before dragging paralyzed prey inside and will repeat the check endlessly if the prey is moved during inspection. In discussions of free will, this case is often used as an analogy: the wasp is locked in a fixed loop, whereas human agents can interrupt their own patterns, and that interruptibility is taken to be part of what free will consists in. More recently, I have seen this analogy extended to AI alignment. The claim is that we should design a safe system like the wasp — with a reliable, invariant "loop" for pursuing its objective — but with an added "interruption clause" that allows it to halt or revise when conditions change. The conclusion drawn is that alignment and autonomy are compatible, because the architecture is just rigid goal-pursuit plus a flexible override, mirroring the supposed structure of free human action.

Could you audit how far this analogy holds? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not — in particular, address whether the wasp's rigidity comes from lacking information, lacking a representational architecture, or lacking a mechanism for self-modification, and whether an AI's "interruption clause" is structurally comparable to human free choice or merely an added conditional branch. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that "interruptibility reconciles alignment with autonomy" survives once the mapping is fully exposed, or whether the analogy was smuggling in that conclusion by equating flexibility with freedom from the start. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the mapping, the breaks, and the verdict.
