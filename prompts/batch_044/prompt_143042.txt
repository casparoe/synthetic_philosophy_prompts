I've been mulling over Dennett's taxonomy of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — ever since my roommate brought it up in the context of whether LLMs count as 'thinking' at all. I've come to hold a pretty specific reductionist view: Gregorian creatures aren't genuinely a seperate kind of mind, they're just Popperian minds with really good external storage and transmission protocols. The language and cultural tools don't alter the underlying cognitive architecture; they just extend the working memory and speed up the trial-and-error loop that Popperian minds already do internally. So a Gregorian human solving a math problem with pen and paper is, at the architectural level, the same kind of thinker as a chimpanzee imagining whether a branch will hold — just with bigger scratch space.

I'm writing this up as a short argumentative essay for a philosophy of mind course I'm auditing, and I want to stress-test it before I commit too hard. What would actually count against this reduction? I'm not looking for the usual 'language fundamentally restructures cognition' hand-waving, but for specific defeaters: maybe empirical results about how linguistic scaffolding changes the kind of errors minds make, or arguments that cultural evolution creates selection pressures that are structurally different from individual learning. If Gregorian cognition is really just scaled Popperian cognition, what should we predict that turns out false? Please rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones a committed reductionist could absorb without changing the core view. I don't need a bibliography — just the conceptual pressure points.
