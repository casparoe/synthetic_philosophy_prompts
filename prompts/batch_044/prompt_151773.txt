I am delivering the opening address at the retirement symposium for Professor Elena Vasquez, who spent four decades in epistemology developing a view she called the transparency of the obvious—a position that takes G.E. Moore's proof of an external world not as a simple refutation of skepticism, but as a demonstration that demonstrative reference is epistemically prior to any skeptical argument that might challenge it. Vasquez's distinctive contribution, articulated in her essay Showing and Saying in the Common Room, was to argue that Moore's gesture succeeds only within a shared framework where pointing can count as proof, but that this same framework reveals a structural limitation: you cannot point to someone's intellectual legacy in the same direct way. The audience is divided exactly in half. One half consists of epistemologists and historians of philosophy who will catch any misreading of Moore—particularly any confusion between knowing that an external world exists and being able to prove it within a skeptical dialectic—and who know Vasquez's work well enough to recognize her specific twist on what she called the bibliographic fallacy, the idea that citation metrics and publication lists attempt to do for intellectual contribution what Moore refused to let skeptical premises do for perception. The other half are family members, university administrators, and former students from fields like art history and biology who have never read Moore and should not be made to feel that they are sitting through a lecture.

The philosophical distinction must shape what I can and cannot say in the tribute, not merely decorate it. Because Vasquez's view treats direct demonstration and mediated interpretation as asymmetrical modes of knowing, I should not be able to simply list her achievements—holding up her curriculum vitae, her named chair, her citation record—as if they were Moore's two hands, available for immediate inspection. That would violate the very framework I am invoking. At the same time, pure abstraction—speaking only of frameworks and asymmetries without anchoring them in the particular person being honored—would lose the family and fail as a retirement speech. I want the address to navigate this tension explicitly, using the analogy of Moore's two hands to frame two different ways of knowing Vasquez's work: one direct and demonstrative, appropriate to colleagues who shared her common room, and one mediated and interpretive, necessary for those who knew her only through its effects. The speech should run about eight minutes, maintain a formal ceremonial tone, and reach a specific conclusion about why retirement tributes, unlike philosophical proofs, require both modes operating simultaneously without collapsing one into the other.
