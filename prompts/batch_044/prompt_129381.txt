I'm trying to work out my actual stance on tearing things down, using a kind of Burkean/Chesterton fence logic but stretched to cover rules, norms, and little bureaucratic rituals rather than literal fences. Here are my gut verdicts.

There's an old town ordinance that every pub sign has to be hand-painted, originally so the town council could check for obscenity quickly, but now that's done by inspectors and the rule just keeps chain pubs out. I'd scrap it — the fence is purely decorative.

Then there's this weird university rule that PhD defenses have to happen in the room with the stained-glass window, even though the room is too small and everyone uses Zoom now. The window was donated by a family that funded the department's first chair. I think keep the rule, or at least don't just delete it because it's inconvienient. But I can't quite say why.

A legal formality: contracts for selling farm equipment over $500 still need to be notarized in my state, even though digital IDs exist. The notary rule was added after a specific fraud wave in the thirties. I'd probably keep it, but only weakly.

There's a social norm in my field that you don't cite working papers in print, only published versions. Nobody remembers why — maybe to avoid citing mistakes? I'd drop it immediately; it feels like dead weight.

But here's the pair that really bugs me: I think we should abolish the requirement that juries be exactly twelve people, since there's no magical number, even though the tradition is old. But I also think we shouldn't drop the convention of adresssing judges as "Your Honor" even if it feels archaic, even though both traditions are old and both have murky origins. What's the actual difference?

Also there's this case I keep going back and forth on: a small town maintains a redundant paper ledger of property taxes alongside the digital database, just because "that's how Mrs. Halloway did it for forty years." Is that a fence to preserve or just bureacratic clutter?

So is there one principle that sorts all of these? Maybe something about whether the practice carries information that isn't stored elsewhere? Or whether removing it severs a line of accountability? I want the principle that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, but I also want an honest list of which ones I have to surrender if I want the principle to stay simple. And please tell me what your principle predicts for the ledger case, and whether the jury-number vs. "Your Honor" tension is a real conflict or just me being inconsistent.
