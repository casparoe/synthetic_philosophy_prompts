Write a deadpan parody of a hospital clinical-ethics protocol document—formatted as a numbered policy memo with an attached FAQ—satirizing the philosophical distinction between doing harm and allowing harm (killing versus letting die). The piece should run about 600 words in the flat, procedural voice of an institutional review board that has rigorously operationalized the distinction through invented bureaucratic instruments: for example, a real-time “Moral Causation Ledger” that logs every physician action as either an Intervention (commission) or a Non-Action (omission), and a required “Intervention-Omission Ratio” that must stay within accreditation bounds. Have the satire arise from extending the conceptual distinction into absurd administrative practice—addressing edge cases such as whether silencing a ventilator alarm counts as harming the alarm by commission or failing to save the patient by omission, how withdrawal of nutrition is scored when a feeding tube is physically present but not activated, and whether a resource-allocation committee that withholds a scarce bed is “allowing” death or “doing” it through distributive omission. The target should be the style of discourse that treats the doing/allowing distinction as administratively tractable and morally decisive, not any named individual. Keep the tone internally consistent and deadpan; the reader should recognize that the logic being followed is genuinely the philosopher’s, even as its institutional conclusion becomes ridiculous. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific philosophical texts.
