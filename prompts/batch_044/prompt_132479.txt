Write the desiderata that any adequate philosophical account of the badness of human extinction must satisfy, specifically where the badness exceeds the direct harm of death, suffering, or deprivation experienced by those who perish. The account should explain why extinction seems morally catastrophic even in scenarios where it is painless, instantaneous, and unknown to the victims — for instance, a silent cosmic event that eliminates the species without warning or awareness.

Your specification should include the following, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement:

1. The phenomena it must cover. It must account for (a) the intuition that present people are harmed or wronged by an extinction they will never experience; (b) the intuition that potential future people are harmed by never existing, despite the non-identity problem; and (c) the intuition that long-term present projects — scientific, artistic, or political — suffer a distinctive damage if humanity ends. For each, provide the thought experiment or distinction that makes it a genuine desideratum rather than a sentimental projection.

2. The distinctions it must respect. It must not collapse (i) harming existing agents, harming potential agents, and damaging impersonal goods; (ii) dependency on specifically human future generations versus dependency on any future consciousness capable of registering value; and (iii) retrospective devaluation of past achievements versus prospective undermining of present motivation. Flag any pair of these distinctions you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — particularly if accommodating both the claim that dead people are not harmed and the claim that present agents suffer a present wrong.

3. The consistency conditions. The account must cohere with (a) the asymmetry between procreation and non-procreation — that is, why bringing a happy person into existence is not morally required, yet preventing all future happy existence is gravely wrong; (b) the view that posthumous harm to the dead is impossible or conceptually confused; and (c) the cosmological and biological reality that human extinction is likely irreversible and that no non-human successors are assured.

4. Negotiable versus non-negotiable requirements. Mark which conditions are shared by nearly all serious accounts and which belong to particular camps — for example, those who hold that value is necessarily agent-relative versus those who allow for impersonal or cosmic significance.

Then, briefly score Samuel Scheffler's dependency view — that the value of present activities depends on the expectation of a human future — against your list. In doing so, address whether satisfying all non-negotiable desiderata forces acceptance of something like a successor-neutrality condition (that the badness depends on there being some conscious future, not necessarily human) or whether that can be rejected without collapsing the distinction between extinction and ordinary personal death. Also indicate whether the account must resolve what might be called the archival paradox: whether the absence of a future audience retroactively diminishes the value of achievements already completed, or only undermines the present motivation to pursue them.

Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
