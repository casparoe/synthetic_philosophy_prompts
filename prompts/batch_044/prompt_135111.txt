Read Ridley Scott’s Blade Runner through the philosophy of authorial intention and textual meaning. The theatrical release leaves ambiguous whether Deckard is a replicant; Scott has subsequently maintained that he intended Deckard to be one, while internal narrative and visual cues—including the origami unicorn left by Gaff, ambiguous emotional registers, and disputed reflective motifs—support both human and replicant readings. Analyze this through moderate actual intentionalism, hypothetical intentionalism, and anti-intentionalist views that fix meaning by a work’s internal structure and competent audience uptake. Does the director’s later statement settle the interpretive question, or does the film’s own architecture, especially the unicorn sequence functioning as an embedded authorial signature, resist that external authority? Further, consider whether the existence of a director’s cut and multiple released versions creates a single intentional object or a family of works with divergent meanings. What does this cinematic case add to the philosophical debate that purely literary examples do not: does collaborative production, post-release revision, and the separation between initial audience reception and later authorial clarification expose limits in standard theories of interpretation?
