I’ve been trying to think through, in a somewhat systematic way, whether being able to imagine a situation is any good guide to whether that situation could actually obtain in reality. A friend who listens to philosophy podcasts and reads popular metaphysics blogs offered me what he called the “standard modal-rationalist summary.” According to him, conceivability works like a kind of mental simulation or model-building: if you can construct a coherent scenario in your mind—say, a world in which water is not H2O, or a being physically identical to you that lacks any inner experience—without running into a contradiction you can detect, then you have shown that the scenario is genuinely metaphysically possible. He said the only reason this ever misfires is ignorance or conceptual confusion on the conceiver’s part, which is why philosophers distinguish a quick “prima facie” judgment from an “ideal” one that corrects for all such errors. He went on to claim that this makes modal rationalism essentially correct—careful thought is largely transparent to modal facts—and that this is precisely why the zombie argument succeeds: since we can clearly conceive of consciousness being absent despite full physical duplication, materialism must be false, and the materialist response that we are merely overlooking a hidden contradiction is just an ad hoc defense of the simulation method. He also mentioned modal empiricism as the rival view, which he characterized as the claim that imagination tracks only linguistic consistency rather than real possibility, and he dismissed it as obviously wrong because language can be perfectly consistent about impossible situations. I suspect he is sliding together several different claims and overstating the conclusions, especially about zombies and materialism, but I do not have the background to say exactly where the errors lie. Is this summary a fair representation of the philosophical debate? Please separate what is accurate, what counts as a common simplification, and what is genuinely misrepresented—particularly whether conceivability should be understood as mental simulation, whether the zombie argument is as straightforward as he presents it, and whether his quick dismissal of the empirical challenge holds up.
