I'm a doctoral student in social epistemology and philosophy of science, trying to stabilize my own views on epistemic closure before drafting a chapter. Here is the package of commitments I currently hold, and I want an honest audit of whether they hang together.

My commitments:
(i) I accept the familiar distinction between epistemic bubbles (homogeneous information environments produced by missing exposure) and echo chambers (environments in which outside dissent is present but systematically discredited). However, I want to introduce a third category I'm calling an "epistemic dead zone": a community in which outside dissent is technically accessible, not actively discredited, yet rendered cognitively inert because members lack the shared interpretive framework required to absorb it.
(ii) I hold that bubbles are less harmful than echo chambers, since accidental exposure can still penetrate a bubble.
(iii) I hold that dead zones are more harmful than echo chambers, despite the absence of any active credibility-discount mechanism.
(iv) I hold that individual "curatorial filtering"—deliberately ignoring information sources that fail basic reliability tests—is often epistemically rational.
(v) I hold that when every member of a community engages in such filtering simultaneously, the result is collective closure that is irrational, even though no individual step is.
(vi) I hold that the primary harm of bubbles, echo chambers, and dead zones is practical and political (polarization, coordination failure, democratic dysfunction) rather than doxastic: I do not think they necessarily produce more false beliefs, only more resistant ones.

These six theses feel right to me, but I worry that (ii) and (iii) pull against each other, and that (iv) and (v) involve a fallacy of composition I haven't defended. I also worry that (vi) undermines the motivation for (iii).
