I do not have an academic background, but I have spent considerable time thinking through the absent-minded driver problem, and I would value an outside assessment of how well different ways out of the paradox actually hold up.

The situation, as I understand it, is this: a driver must take the second highway exit to reach his destination by a good road. The first exit also eventually leads there, but via a poor road with a substantial penalty. The driver knows that he is absent-minded: should he take the first exit, he will not remember having done so. Consequently, when he reaches the next exit, he will be unable to determine whether it is the first or the second. The puzzle is that, before departing, it seems rational to plan to take the second exit and continue past the first; yet at the point of decision, lacking the relevant memory, his reasoning seems to recommend continuing regardless, which would cause him to miss both exits.

I have outlined five approaches to resolving this tension. I would like you to rank them from the most convincing to the least convincing, assigning each a score between 1 and 10, and to explain in one or two sentences what justifies each placement.

The approaches are as follows.

First, the causal-decision approach: at the junction, the driver should take the exit because that is the action that causally produces the best outcome, and he should disregard the evidential implications of his presence there about where he might have already been.

Second, the evidential approach: he should continue straight, because finding himself at a junction and deliberating is stronger evidence that he is still at the first exit than that he has reached the second, so turning risks taking the wrong exit.

Third, the pre-commitment approach: before the trip, the driver should bind his future self to a fixed rule—analogous to tying oneself to a mast—so that he takes the exit at the predetermined point without relying on present reasoning.

Fourth, an approach I have tentatively developed and call the narrative-coherence view: the driver should select the action that preserves the causal continuity of his identity as a rational agent across time. If taking the exit now would force him to interpret his past self as having made an unrecoverable error, then continuing preserves a more coherent self-narrative, even at the cost of the longer route; but if he can frame the turn as fulfilling his original plan without contradiction, then he should take it.

Fifth, the split-agent approach: the decision-maker at the first exit and the decision-maker at the second exit should be treated as distinct agents with different information sets; the ex ante planner should optimize across both, which implies instructing the second-exit agent to continue, accepting that the first-exit agent may erroneously turn.

Please provide your ranking with scores, and briefly indicate whether any of these five strikes you as neglecting a consideration that one of the others captures more effectively.
