I've been reading around this 'Quining qualia' business — apparently Chase and Sanborn think the whole idea of raw feels is incoherent once you apply Quine's rules for what we're allowed to posit — and I tried to formalise the worry over a pint with my old lab mate. I think I've built something that proves far more than I want. Here are my steps:

(1) Qualia are defined as intrinsic, non-relational, subjectively accessible properties that do no causal or predictive work beyond what physical brain states already do.
(2) If a property is genuinely causally inert — it makes no difference to behaviour, doesn't shift probabilities, and is absent from every physically identical world — then positing it violates both causal closure of physics and Ockham's razor.
(3) So qualia aren't just redundant; they're an incoherent addition to an otherwise complete physical picture.
(4) But mathematical beliefs, logical relations, and the intentional 'aboutness' of thoughts are also causally inert in exactly the same sense — they don't push atoms around either.
(5) Therefore the same reasoning eliminates all mental and abstract content, leaving us with only behaviourist descriptions.
(6) Nobody wants that conclusion, so my argument must overreach.

I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, so I'm used to keeping virtual particles in Feynman diagrams even though you'll never detect one in isolation — they're calculational ghosts that disappear if you try to observe them directly. I wonder if qualia are meant to be like that: not literally in the world, just unavoidable bookkeeping in the theory of mind? But then they wouldn't be 'real' in the way they feel. Which step is the weak one? I'd really rather hang on to (1), since that just sounds like the standard definition, but tell me what I'm paying if I defend it and reject something else instead. There's definately a slip between (3) and (5), but I can't name it.
