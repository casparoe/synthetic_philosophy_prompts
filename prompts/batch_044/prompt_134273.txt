I'm drafting a session for clinical ethics consultants who keep hitting these pain-asymbolia cases and not knowing wether to treat. The philosophical background is the old fight about whether pain is bad in itself or only bad because of the reactive, aversive layer it triggers -- you know, the intrinsic-badness view versus the reactive-account. In asymbolia the sensory track is intact but the aversive colouring and withdrawal behaviour drop out, sometimes with laughing or even approach. The ward needs a reusable procedue, not a one-off answer.

Please turn that philosophical distinction into a bedside decision procedue -- maybe five or six ordered tests -- that a consultant with no philosophy background can actually run in about fifteen minutes before signing off. For each step say what counts as passing or failing, and mark the exact point where the answer stops being factual and becomes a value judgement (for instance, treating for dignity or future risk rather than for present suffering). The procedue must handle the profile where verbal report stays intact but both behavioural avoidance and affective distress are absent, and it should include my own little addition: a counterfactual-withdrawal check, asking wether the patient would restructure future behaviour to avoid the stimulus, not just whether they pull away now. That distinction matters, I think.

Then apply it to three cases:
- The classic profile: sharp nociception reported clearly, zero withdrawal, smiling response, no request to stop.
- The partial-preservation case: they avoid physically but describe the feeling as "pressure with a colour" and deny it's unpleasant.
- One that gives the family an answer they hate: a patient who actively seeks repetition, reports neutral curiosity, but has a self-injury history that makes staff want to block access anyway.

Say where the procedue misfires, where it forces a value call, and what it flat-out can't settle. Don't give me a bibliography or citation list -- these people don't have time for that, and half the papers are behind paywalls anyway.
