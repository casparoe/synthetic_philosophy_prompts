I'm writing a scene where my protagonist corners a Moore scholer at a dinner party and gets a little drunk arguing about intrinsic value. She's trying to trap him with this chain and now can't find the exit herself:

1. Moore says friendship and beauty are good in themselves, specifically in the mutual awareness of mental qualities.
2. So the total intrinsic value in a situation scales with the number of distinct conscious centers doing the appreciating.
3. That means two friends together admiring a sunset generate more intrinsic good then one person admiring it alone.
4. Now imagine a neural-bridge tech that lets two people temporarily share one stream of consciousness during aesthetic experience—not permanently merged, just overlapping for the moment.
5. During the overlap there's only one center of appreciation, but it's richer, containing both people's memories and sensibilities.
6. By Moore's calculus this should be *less* valuable, since you've collapsed two appreciating subjects into one.
7. But that feels backwards—the shared experience sounds deeper, not like a loss of value.
8. So either Moore's framework can't account for why deep connection is good, or we have to bite the bullet and say the bridge actually destroys intrinsic good by reducing subject-count.

She thinks the slip is probably between steps 3 and 5, but she's terrified it's in step 1. Can you map out exactly which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs? She'd much rather keep Moore's core idea then accept that merging minds is somehow immoral. Also she needs this to sound like a tipsy academic talking, not a polished seminar paper—so if the reasoning is too neat, tell her where to rough it up.
