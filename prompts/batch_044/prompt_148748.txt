Student answer:
Wolpert's NFL result for learning shows that averaged over all possible input-output mappings, every algorithm — Bayesian ones, connectionist ones, whatever — gets identical error. This means no inductive strategy is universally superior. Because of that, Hume's problem is basically dissolved: if all methods tie on average, there's no rational obligation to expect the future like the past rather than any other pattern. The result applies just as well to search and optimization, since it's all the same underlying math. The averaging assumes a uniform distribution over all logically possible worlds, which is obviously unrealistic, but that's exactly the point — without prior knowledge about reality, you can't justify one inductive bias over another. Some people say this only applies to disembodied learners, not agents embedded in the world, but that distinction doesn't matter here because the averaging is formal, not physical. In our actual world nature is somewhat regular, so algorithms do differ in performance. Still, NFL captures Hume's insight from his writings on custom and habit: our expectations come from psychological bias, not pure reason. So the problem isn't solved, but it's shown to be unavoidable — you always need an inductive prior, and none is justified a priori. That's essentially what Hume meant.
