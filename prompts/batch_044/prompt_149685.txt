I have come to hold a qualified version of objective-list well-being, modified by what I shall call the causal-transparency constraint: an achievement contributes to a life’s going well only if the agent could, in principle, trace its success conditions back through an unbroken causal chain to states of affairs that are not wholly constituted by the agent’s own mental representations. Applied to Nozick’s experience machine, this yields a nuanced verdict: plugging in is not necessarily a loss of well-being if the simulated environment preserves structural feedback loops that mirror genuine resistance and failure, but it becomes a decisive harm when the mediation renders all outcomes causally opaque to the subject. What would count against this position — not merely the standard hedonist or desire-satisfactionist rebuttals, but specifically which intuitions about achievement, which boundary cases (for instance, virtual artistic creation or simulated moral relationships), and which results in the philosophy of mind about representational transparency or epistemic access would undermine the causal-transparency constraint itself? Which defeaters would a defender of this view concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be absorbed by refining the distinction between structural isomorphism and mere phenomenal resemblance? I should like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with attention to whether the position risks collapsing either into pure objective-list theory or into a form of phenomenalism about well-being.
