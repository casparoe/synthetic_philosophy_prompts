I am drafting a novel in which a remote community has maintained an intricate system of signaling rituals—patterns of prescribed silence, gesture, and reciprocal exchange—for generations without any written charter, founding document, or remembered origin. Within the narrative, a philosopher character insists that these rituals are best understood through David Lewis’s analysis of convention: they constitute arbitrary but stable solutions to recurring coordination problems, sustained by common knowledge of mutual expectations and a general preference for conformity, even though the original coordination problem has long vanished and the rituals have since acquired profound moral weight. She further claims that recognizing them as conventions rather than as moral absolutes actually strengthens their authority, because it reveals the rational, interest-based foundation of the community’s cohesion rather than appealing to an unexamined tradition. Present her position with philosophical precision, then tell me what would genuinely count against it. I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical discoveries about the rituals’ forgotten origins, which conceptual objections to extending a Lewisian framework to practices that now carry normative force, and which rival accounts of social norm evolution—say, evolutionary or institutional—would threaten her view? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those she would herself concede as damaging from those she would dismiss, and indicate whether her position risks anything real or has been insulated by strategic qualification. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need arguments, not references.
