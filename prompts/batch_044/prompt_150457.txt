Zoological institutions coordinate breeding through Species Survival Plans that treat named taxonomic species as the decisive unit for pairing, even when genetic surveys show the managed populations contain cryptic lineages, hybrid swarms, and continuous clinal variation. The same programs typically forbid cross-species pairings regardless of ecological function or individual welfare, as though the species boundary itself carries normative weight. What philosophy of biological taxonomy does this practice presuppose—essentialism, a pragmatic convention, or pluralist realism? Reconstruct the implicit theory of what a species is and whether it is valued intrinsically or instrumentally, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, and say whether the framework could survive replacement by a population-genetics or ecosystem-function standard without collapsing. Finally, say what would change your mind: for instance, evidence that SSP coordinators explicitly reject taxonomic essentialism and instead use species labels only for bureaucratic tractability, or conversely that they secretly rely on reproductive-isolation criteria that align with one particular species concept.
