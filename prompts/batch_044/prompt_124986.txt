I am a high school junior tasked with giving the five-minute opening address at a symposium co-hosted by our school's philosophy club and the county historical museum. Half the room are professional historians of science, several of whom treat "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution" as retrospective constructions with no genuine joints in history; the other half are parents and community members for whom those three labels form the only timeline they know. The speech must take up whether these periods are discovered divisions or invented ones, and the answer must do real structural work: it should determine whether I can welcome guests by saying we are walking "from the Middle Ages into modernity," govern how I describe the museum's chronological exhibit, and shape the closing invitation — all without losing the non-specialists or sounding like a lecture to the experts. Write the remarks so that the conceptual argument about periodization carries the architecture of the speech rather than decorating it.
