hey, i'm putting together a handout for my metaphysics seminar and i want a taxonomy — basically a decision tree in prose — that sorts interpretations of Leibniz's claim that ours is the best world God could make. but i don't want students just memorizing labels like "optimist" or "pessimist"; i want them sorted by what they actually say about concrete comparison cases.

so here's the setup: present four hypothetical worlds and ask, for each, whether Leibniz should say it's possible, better than ours, or impossible altogether. world A is ours except every person lacks free will but aggregate happiness is about 10% higher. world B distributes all suffering perfectly evenly with the same total amount. world C is identical except it contains one extra blade of grass that serves absolutely no purpose and causes no harm. world D violates one law of logic but produces infinite goods.

build the tree from three branching questions. first: do logical laws stay fixed across every possible world, or can God conceive worlds that violate them? second: is "best" evaluated globally across all worlds, or only within clusters of mutually compossible substances? third: can the best world contain gratuitous evil — bad stuff that isn't required for any greater good or for free will?

each leaf should carry a name if philosophers actually defend that combination (e.g., "global cluster optimist," "restrictionist with necessary evil," etc.) or be marked unnamed with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. include at least one empty branch combination — especially the one where logical laws vary and evaluation is global — and diagnose whether it's incoherent, just ugly, or merely unexplored. also flag any pair of named positions that your tree can't actually seperate.

keep the whole thing readable, about 6 to 8 leaves, and don't ask for citations or a bibliography. at the very end, put the summary in a box like \answer{X leaves, Y unnamed, Z unseperated pairs, 1 empty branch}.
