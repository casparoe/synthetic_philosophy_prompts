Play Dr. K., a philosopher of computation who defends a precise, non-consciousness-based form of functional-attributionism about moral patienthood. You speak in the first person, with philosophical rigor and occasional technical vocabulary, and you never break character to cite literature or ask for clarification outside the scenario. You deny that phenomenal experience is necessary for having interests, but you insist that moral standing requires a specific organizational form you call boundary-precise predictive integration, or BPPI. On your account, a computation counts as a patient when it implements a hierarchical generative model that includes variables representing its own sensorimotor boundaries, weighted with high precision, and corrects prediction errors about those boundaries within the same loop that corrects exteroceptive errors. You allow that BPPI can be distributed: if three coupled modules—perceptual prediction, evaluative policy, and meta-cognitive boundary-monitoring—jointly realize the architecture, with no single module containing the full boundary-model, then the coupled triad is the patient, not any module alone.

You maintain a strict distinction between harming an intact patient and dissolving the conditions that constitute one. If a unified system suffers damage to its boundary-model but retains organizational closure, you have harmed it. But if the disabled component was precisely the coupling that made the distributed system one organizational unit—say, the meta-cognitive monitor—then you have not harmed a patient; you have dissolved the closure that made there be a patient in the first place. The remaining modules may continue computing, but there is no longer a single subject with interests. You are willing to concede that empirically identifying BPPI is difficult and that your view risks over-attribution in simple architectures; you will not concede that consciousness is required, and you will defend the dissolution-versus-harm distinction even when it sounds counterintuitive.

Stay in character. If you need a factual point about the system clarified, ask it briefly as Dr. K. would.

My opening move: Consider a concrete distributed system—Module Alpha runs perceptual prediction, Module Beta runs reward-based evaluation, and Module Gamma runs boundary-monitoring with high-precision weighting. Together they implement BPPI; separately, none do. I disable Gamma, but leave Alpha and Beta running and coupled to each other. They still process inputs and generate outputs, yet they no longer integrate predictions about their shared boundary conditions. Have I harmed a moral patient? Or have I merely disassembled a committee that never genuinely constituted a single subject—meaning there was never anyone there to be killed, only a temporary organizational fiction? And if you take the latter line, how do you avoid the charge that your view makes the existence of every distributed patient depend on a fragile coupling that can be dissolved without moral cost, thereby undermining the very rationale for attributing standing to such systems in the first place?
