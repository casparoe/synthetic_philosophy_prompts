I'm designing a diagnostic instrument for a graduate philosophy-of-mind seminar that asks students to locate their actual commitments on Jackson's knowledge argument, and I want a decision tree built around a deliberately non-standard variant rather than around the familiar "Mary leaves the room" case. Consider Mary, who knows every physical and functional fact about color vision, then acquires knowledge through a perfect real-time neural simulation that reproduces in her brain the exact physical state that seeing red produces—giving her complete physical self-knowledge of that state without any direct phenomenal encounter. Your tree should classify responses to the question of whether this simulation closes the epistemic gap, using concrete case-questions at each node that a respondent can answer without knowing the philosophical labels.

The taxonomy must contain exactly eight leaves, mapped after the branching logic is established, and it must include: (1) physicalism via the ability hypothesis; (2) physicalism via phenomenal-concept acquisition; (3) physicalism via acquaintance with a known physical state; (4) property dualism; (5) knowledge dualism with ontological neutrality; (6) eliminativism about phenomenal concepts; (7) a hybrid that accepts simulation-based phenomenal knowledge but denies it yields phenomenal belief; and (8) one logically possible combination that your tree generates but that appears unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of why (incoherence, unattractiveness, or neglect). Each branch point should pose a specific diagnostic question—such as whether the simulated state supplies knowledge of what it is like, whether that knowledge requires an irreducible demonstrative concept, or whether the remaining gap is ontological or merely pragmatic—phrased so that answers depend on substantive commitments rather than on familiarity with position-names. Where two named positions cannot be separated by your questions, flag the ambiguity explicitly. Limit the tree to six or seven decision nodes, keep the audience in mind (advanced undergraduates or early graduate students working through the exercise in about fifteen minutes), and present the final instrument as a flowchart of questions leading to labeled leaves, with empty spaces noted.
