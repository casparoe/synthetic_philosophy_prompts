In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind we have been working through the extended mind thesis, and I want you to apply it to a concrete, high-stakes episode that is usually discussed only in engineering or historical terms: the final two minutes of the Apollo 11 lunar module descent on 20 July 1969. The salient facts are these: Armstrong observed that the computer was guiding Eagle toward a boulder field; he assumed manual control, flew horizontally away from the target at roughly constant altitude, and touched down with less than thirty seconds of fuel remaining. I am not asking for a flight narrative. Instead, map the cognitive architecture of that interval with philosophical precision. Which states—belief about terrain suitability, working-memory tracking of fuel and descent rate, the intention to preserve crew and vehicle—were realized in Armstrong's neural processes, which were realized in the Lunar Module's computer display and autopilot logic, and which existed only in the coupled loop between pilot and machine?

Then I want you to deploy a distinction that I will define here rather than borrow from the literature: call it the mediation gap. In some cases of artifact-assisted cognition, the agent constantly translates the tool's output into an independent mental representation before acting—the slide-rule user computes, then holds the result in mind, then decides. In other cases, the agent trusts the artifact's state and acts on it directly, with no representational buffer, so that the artifact's current state functions analogously to an internal mental state. Apply this distinction to argue whether the LM computer was genuinely part of Armstrong's extended cognitive system during the manual override, or whether it remained an external tool that he consulted and then overrode.

Finally, let the episode talk back to the theory. The parity principle advanced by Clark and Chalmers suggests that if a process plays the same functional role as an internal one, its location is irrelevant. Does Armstrong's ability to disconnect the autopilot and revert to purely manual control show that the computer was never constitutive of his mind, only causally coupled? Or does the episode show that extended cognitive systems can be decoupled under stress without thereby proving they were never genuinely extended? Be honest about where the extended-mind framework illuminates the flight dynamics and where it forces an artificial classification onto a situation that may be better described as skilled tool mastery rather than distributed cognition.
