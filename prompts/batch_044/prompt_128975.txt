I'm working through the heterogeneity problem for qualitative hedonism and have constructed an argument that seems to collapse the view into either circularity or over-inclusion, but I can't locate the leak. I'd like to keep premises (1) through (4) and (7) if possible, and I think (9) is doing real work, though I'm unsure about the move to (10)–(12). Here is the chain:

(1) Hedonism is true only if there is a single property Q such that: (a) every pleasure instantiates Q, (b) no non-pleasure instantiates Q, and (c) Q is intrinsic and non-relational.

(2) The heterogeneity of pleasure is radical: gustatory pleasure, intellectual satisfaction, and the diffuse well-being of conversation share no sensory modality, no intentional object, and no functional role.

(3) So Q is not a sensory quality, not an intentional feature, and not a functional disposition.

(4) The remaining option is that Q is a sui generis phenomenal quality—"pleasantness itself."

(5) But if Q is sui generis, it should be introspectively isolable: a subject should be able to identify Q in experience independently of the experience's other features.

(6) In practice, subjects cannot do this; when directed to attend only to the "pleasantness" of tasting chocolate versus solving a proof, they report either nothing in common or only relational features (liking it, wanting it to continue).

(7) So Q is either absent—hedonism is false—or present but introspectively transparent: not an additional feature but the way the experience is experienced.

(8) If Q is transparent in this way, then to say "this experience has Q" is just to say "this experience is experienced as pleasant."

(9) Now apply this to a case of "hedonic inversion": imagine a subject—conceptually coherent, if neuropsychologically exotic—who experiences tissue damage with exactly the transparent quality Q, yet reports it as aversive and seeks to avoid it.

(10) By (8), this subject's experience has Q, so by hedonism it is intrinsically good and should be sought.

(11) But we have independent reason (non-paternalistic) to help the subject avoid it, and the subject's own aversive evaluation seems authoritative.

(12) Therefore hedonism either misclassifies this state as good—over-inclusion—or must deny that Q is present, which requires an external criterion for Q that violates (1c) by making Q relational (dependent on the subject's overall evaluative stance).

(13) So qualitative hedonism proves too much: it either counts aversive states as intrinsic goods or abandons the claim that pleasure is non-relationally good.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a way to block the move from "has transparent quality Q" to "is intrinsically good regardless of the subject's overall stance"? And please push back plainly if the argument rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the inversion case in (9) is conceptually incoherent rather than merely exotic, or if I have smuggled a relational criterion into the transparency claim in (7)–(8). I want a diagnosis of the argument chain, not just a defense of hedonism, and I'm open to being told the whole framing is misguided.
