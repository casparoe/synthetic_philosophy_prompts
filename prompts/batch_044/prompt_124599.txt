I am in my final year at a British comprehensive school, and I have noticed a recurring pattern in arguments about shared resources that does not quite match the explanations I have encountered so far. We have a communal garden and a shared digital archive for student projects. The usual account is that people take too much because they do not bear the full cost — the tragedy of the commons. But what I observe is different. Some students do not merely overuse the garden or the archive; they begin to treat access itself as something to be monopolised. One group might plant in every available plot not because they need all the crops, but to prevent other groups from planting anything, and then defend this by saying they are only protecting their “fair share” from people who would take more. In the digital archive, a contributor might take a large dataset, apply a restrictive licence, and argue that without this protection free-riders would exploit shared labour. The result is not uniform depletion but a kind of fragmentation: the resource is still present, yet it is partitioned into private claims defended as self-defence rather than greed.

The pattern seems to involve three elements: first, an anticipation that others will overuse; second, a preemptive restriction of access by one party; and third, a justification that frames this restriction as preserving liberty or protecting investment rather than as exclusion. It feels close to the tragedy of the commons, yet in the classic version everyone suffers from collective overuse, whereas here the resource is undermined specifically by people locking parts away and claiming that any collective rule against this infringes their rights. It also feels different from simple selfishness or from historical enclosure, because the actors genuinely believe they are reacting to a threat from others rather than seizing something for pure profit.

Is there an established philosophical or economic concept that captures exactly this configuration — the collapse of a commons through preemptive, defensive partitioning rather than through uniform overconsumption? If so, how is it distinguished from the standard tragedy of the commons, from enclosure, and from the free-rider problem? If there is no precise term, could one be defined with clear boundary conditions — for instance, specifying whether the restrictor must genuinely fear overuse by others, or whether the concept applies when the restriction itself creates the scarcity it claims to solve? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
