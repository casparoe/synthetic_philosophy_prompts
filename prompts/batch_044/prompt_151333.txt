hey, i need a retirement toast for my old art-history prof, Dr. Elena Vasquez. she's been at the university forever and just turned seventy. half the room at her dinner are aesthetics philosophers who know her papers inside out; the other half are family, old students, and friends who mostly remember that she loved Caravaggio and hated those museum audio guides.

she spent her career arguing that aesthetic testimony is basically broken — you can't genuinely know a painting is beautiful or sublime just because an expert tells you so, you have to stand in front of it yourself. but she also taught thousands of people through slides, descriptions, and those long emails where she'd write three paragraphs about the light in a single Vermeer. i want the toast built around one specific idea i just made up: call it "aesthetic debt." the idea is that when you recieve someone's testimony about a work's beauty, you don't just fail to gain real aesthetic knowledge — you actually incur an obligation to go experience the thing yourself, like borrowing a book you promised to return. this should do real structural work in the speech: it explains why she was so insistant on dragging us to the museum even while she was writing essays about testimony failing, and it should shape what i can and can't say about her legacy. specifically, i can't just say she taught us to see through her words; i have to say her words were always meant to push us toward the door, and that she never intended her descriptions to replace the encounter.

please write it in three sections, in this order:

1. "The opening" — about a minute, set the scene, acknowledge the split crowd, land one joke that works for both philosophers and family about her refusal to let anyone hide behind a wall label (roughly 150 words).

2. "The knot" — about two and a half minutes, lay out the problem of aesthetic testimony in plain language, then introduce aesthetic debt as the twist that saves her career from contradiction; this is where the philosophy actually structures what can be said, not just decorates it (roughly 400 words).

3. "The toast" — about a minute and a half, resolve without undoing her view: don't claim she's watching over us or that she lives on in some false metaphysical sense, but don't be cold either; raise the glass and say something that comforts the non-philosophers while still being true to her (roughly 250 words).

write it casual, like you're talking to a friend who writes speeches. no bibliography, no "see also," no reading list — her family shouldn't feel like they walked into a seminar. and please, don't quote anything verbatim from her actual papers; paraphrase if you need to reference her view.
