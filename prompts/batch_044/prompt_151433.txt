I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent several years reading Frege's writings on logic and language, and more recently I have become preoccupied with the private political diary he kept late in life. I want to understand whether that diary should alter how we interpret his philosophy, and I have developed a specific framing that I need tested with rigor rather than dismissed as obvious.

My framing is this. Frege's semantic theory holds that reference is always mediated by sense: there is no direct access to an object except through some mode of presentation. Yet in the diary he treats social and ethnic categories—what we might call Jewishness or Germanness—as if they possess fixed, extensional properties that are immediately accessible, independent of context or mode of presentation. In other words, he appears to treat them like rigid designators stripped of mediated sense. I want to know whether this is merely a personal moral failure, or whether it reveals that his own universalism about objective thoughts (Gedanken) contains a structural blind spot: when the objects in question are human groups rather than numbers or logical entities, his system permits—perhaps even encourages—the bypassing of sense.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps I will call the Compartmentalists and the Structural Continuists.

The Compartmentalists maintain that Frege's philosophical system—his distinction between sense and reference, his doctrine of objective, mind-independent thoughts, and his anti-psychologism in logic—forms a coherent whole that is entirely separable from his private political judgments. They hold that the diary expresses prejudice and moral failure, but that nothing in it undermines, contradicts, or illuminates any claim in his published logic or philosophy of language.

The Structural Continuists maintain that the diary is not an isolated lapse but exhibits a pattern continuous with Frege's philosophy. They argue that his commitment to direct, unmediated reference for objective thoughts allows him to treat social identities as if they too have fixed reference without contextual sense. On this view, the diary does not merely record bad opinions; it applies—incorrectly and harmfully—the very structure of his semantic universalism to human categories.

Produce a document with exactly these parts, in this order.

I take there to be at least two: (i) whether the diary's treatment of ethnic and national categories can be plausibly analyzed as treating them as direct-reference terms lacking mediated sense; and (ii) whether a philosopher's failure of moral or social judgment in one domain can ever constitute internal evidence against the structural claims of that philosopher's system in another domain.
