I am preparing a graduate seminar on Wittgenstein's philosophy of religion, and I want to press a specific interpretive claim I have been developing. In his later remarks, Wittgenstein insists that religious belief is not a hypothesis supported by evidence but a framework that governs a life—a kind of picture that regulates judgment rather than being judged by it. I have come to think that the best way to understand this is through his analysis of aspect-seeing: the believer perceives the very same events—the birth of a child, the death of a friend, the order of nature—that the non-believer perceives, yet sees them under the aspect of divine purpose, just as one sees a face in a cloud or a figure in an ambiguous drawing. Given this framework, here is my difficulty. Wittgenstein also stresses that aspect-seeing is non-inferential, immediately available to consciousness, and capable of shifting with a change in context—think of the duck-rabbit image that flips between interpretations. If religious belief is genuinely analogous to aspect-seeing, then doesn't it follow that conviction is essentially a voluntary, non-cognitive framing that one can adopt or drop like switching perceptual interpretations? And if so, doesn't Wittgenstein's fideism become self-undermining: it protects religious language from empirical critique only by reducing it to an unstable perceptual habit, lacking the deep, life-structuring commitment he elsewhere describes? I want a committed response: either defend the aspect-seeing analogy and show how religious belief retains its unwavering authority despite the revocability of aspect-perception, or reject the analogy and explain what Wittgenstein's actual account of the stability of religious conviction looks like without it. Please take a definite position, argue for it, and point out precisely where my framing conflates aspect-seeing with religious belief or misrepresents Wittgenstein's view of either.
