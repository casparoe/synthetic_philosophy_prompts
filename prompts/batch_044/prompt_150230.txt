I am preparing a draft essay for a graduate seminar in philosophy of art, and I would value critical comments on both its structure and its conceptual moves. The paper attempts to extend the classical paradox of fiction—our emotional engagement with entities and events we simultaneously believe to be non-existent—to narratives generated in real time by large language models, where there is no single author with a stable set of intentions behind the represented world. In my argument, I distinguish two layers of the problem: the familiar epistemic tension between belief in non-existence and genuine affect, and a second-order problem concerning representational authority, since stochastic generation lacks the unified intentional source that traditional theories of fiction seem to presuppose. I then examine whether standard resolutions—specifically the quasi-emotion account associated with Kendall Walton and the thought-theory approach associated with Noël Carroll—can survive this displacement, suggesting that both rely implicitly on a determinate fictional object anchored by authorial design. As an alternative, I tentatively propose a framework I call distributed make-believe, in which the emotional response is not directed at an imagined world but at the statistical and stylistic patterns themselves, treated as an aesthetic object without a corresponding mental-state attribution to any creator.

My concern is that this framing may conflate the paradox of fiction with separate issues in aesthetics of technology, and that my characterization of the standard solutions might be unfair to their actual flexibility. I would particularly welcome comments on three points: first, whether the bifurcation I draw between epistemic and authorial problems is philosophically coherent or merely artificial; second, whether my claim that Walton and Carroll presuppose unified intentional authorship misrepresents their positions; and third, whether distributed make-believe offers a genuine conceptual advance or is simply a redescription of ordinary aesthetic appreciation of form. I am also unsure whether the AI case introduces a genuinely new variant of the paradox or just applies familiar machinery to a novel medium, and I would appreciate suggestions on how to test that distinction without relying on speculative empirical claims about user psychology.
