Modern Stoic revival literature—from popular handbooks to clinical resilience programs—repeats the injunction to separate “what is within our power” from what is not, often compressed into the line “focus only on what you can control and accept the rest.” The slogan is deployed to justify emotional-regulation techniques, to excuse withdrawal from collective political action, and to license an almost ascetic self-discipline. I want you to treat that one-liner not as advice but as a philosophical claim, and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, arranged from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, specify whether it is defensible, whether it actually supports the practical uses people put it to (for instance, dismissing obligations to address systemic injustice, or grounding a strict separation between mental effort and behavioral outcome), and whether it quietly presupposes a specific philosophy of mind—such as a Cartesian boundary between internal judgment and external event, a compatibilist account of mental agency compatible with determinism, or an embodied-cognition view on which “internal” control is always environmentally scaffolded. In particular, examine whether contemporary neuroscience and embodied-cognition research, which blur the line between internal processing and environmental coupling, undermine the very boundary the slogan needs. Reach an overall verdict about which reading is philosophically most plausible and which ones a coherent modern Stoicism ought to abandon. Please indicate, for your main conclusions, how confident you are in them, and explicitly identify the single point in your analysis about which you are least sure.
