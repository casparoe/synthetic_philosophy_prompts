Construct a thought experiment—set in a realistic institutional context, confined to one paragraph, with no science-fictional elements, perfect predictors, or supernatural devices—in which a newly formed state deliberately fabricates a centuries-old ethnic ritual in order to unify a linguistically divided population. Engineer the case so that Hobsbawm's analysis of invented traditions predicts the ritual will fail to generate legitimate authority, because its recent fabrication is transparent and serves an obvious present political interest, while Benedict Anderson's framework of imagined communities predicts the very same ritual will succeed in producing durable communal solidarity, because mediated, repeated dissemination creates an anonymous horizontal fellowship regardless of historical authenticity. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate precisely why each framework delivers its verdict: specify the mechanism Hobsbawm treats as decisive (the visibility of invention and its instrumental function) and the mechanism Anderson treats as decisive (the simultaneity of imagined membership enabled by mass reproduction). Then identify the single structural feature of the case that forces the divergence—whether it lies in the transparency of fabrication, the medium of transmission, the presence or absence of a competing authentic tradition, or another isolated variable—and explain what happens to both predictions if that feature is removed. Finally, state how a defender of the framework that comes out poorly in your scenario would attack the design: would they deny that transparency undermines legitimacy, deny that solidarity requires belief in antiquity, or argue that the case illegitimately conflates ritual legitimacy with state authority?
