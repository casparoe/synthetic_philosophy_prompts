I'm preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a view she calls "normative rupture with proxy authorization" regarding transformative experience. On her account, the difficulty in deciding whether to become a parent, convert religions, or emigrate is not merely epistemic—lacking first-person knowledge of what the experience is like—but normative: the pre-transformation self employs an evaluative framework that the experience itself replaces, so that self lacks standing to authorize the choice on behalf of its successor. To avoid paralysis, she proposes that agents can invoke second-order commitments—not to any particular outcome, but to maintaining "epistemic openness"—which function as proxy consent: the future self is treated as having authorized the transition if it falls within the scope of that prior openness. I want eight to ten interview questions that do not let her restate this framework, but instead press on three specific pressure points. First, ask how her "normative rupture" differs from the familiar epistemic-transformative distinction without merely relabeling it, with a follow-up for the likely reply that authority, not information, is what's lost. Second, ask how the proxy-authorization mechanism avoids exploitation by agents who retrospectively reinterpret nearly any outcome as consistent with a vague prior commitment to openness, with a follow-up keyed to her probable defense that openness is a substantive normative stance rather than an empty formal condition. Third, ask whether formalizing that second-order commitment collapses her view back into expected-utility reasoning with unusually wide confidence intervals, with a follow-up anticipating her claim that the commitment operates at the level of practical identity rather than preference ordering. Make the sequence progressive, so that by the end she has had to defend the view's distinctiveness, its vulnerability to retrospective rationalization, and its resistance to formalization—all without being allowed to retreat to "that's a research program, not an objection."
