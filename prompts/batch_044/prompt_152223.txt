I have noticed a recurring structural pattern in hierarchical systems—legal, logical, and organizational—that I do not have a precise term for, and I am wondering whether philosophy or systems theory has named it. The pattern involves a hierarchy that appears fully open at the top: there is a meta-level with authority to revise or regulate every level beneath it. Yet embedded somewhere in the base level—not in the meta-rules themselves—is an exception that activates selectively. It behaves like an ordinary object-level rule in almost every context, but whenever the top level attempts to apply its authority to itself or to the conditions of its own operation, this hidden exception blocks the move. The hierarchy therefore looks revisable all the way up, but in practice there is an invisible ceiling that protects the top from self-modification. The ceiling is not acknowledged as a separate meta-level; it is disguised within the substrate and only pulls the ladder up during attempts at self-reference.

Two instances come to mind. First, a constitutional framework that explicitly grants a supreme court or legislature the power to amend any legal rule, yet contains an obscure procedural bylaw—treated as ordinary administrative law—that makes the amendment mechanism itself unamendable without a separate, unreachable supermajority. The bylaw is not part of the constitutional text; it lives in the base-level rules, yet it functions as an inviolate boundary. Second, in a formal system, a predicate is defined using only object-language syntax and seems to apply generally to all predicates in the domain. But its definition contains a hidden clause that exempts precisely those inference rules that would allow the predicate to be applied to its own defining conditions, so that attempts to formalize self-reference at the meta-level are silently redirected back into the object level and neutralized.

This feels adjacent to Hofstadter's tangled hierarchies and to the idea of an inviolate level—like the hands in Escher's Drawing Hands, where each level seems to depend on the other without a clear ground. But in those familiar cases, the loop or boundary is usually visible: you can see the hands drawing each other, or you can identify the meta-language as distinct. Here the loop is concealed; the boundary is not a recognized level but a camouflaged feature of the base. Is there an established concept for this specific configuration—an embedded, self-activating inviolate level that masquerades as part of the substrate? How exactly does it differ from standard Gödelian self-reference, from a motte-and-bailey structure in rule systems, and from an ordinary tangled hierarchy? If the literature has no precise name, I would like a proposed term with clear boundary conditions: what makes this hidden-ceiling pattern different from a simple hidden axiom, from constitutional entrenchment clauses that are explicitly marked as such, and from cases where the hierarchy is genuinely open but practically stable?
