I have recently been appointed to the ethics review board of a regional organ-transplant consortium that is evaluating an algorithmic triage protocol for scarce donor organs. The proposed system weights candidates by projected quality-adjusted life years, but it also contains a provision—currently being debated as a form of practical utility maximization—that if clinical assessment reveals a recipient with an idiosyncratic neurological profile capable of experiencing subjective well-being at a scale orders of magnitude greater per year of life than any other candidate, the algorithm directs the majority of available organs to that single individual. Several board members have objected that this creates a medical analogue to the utility monster, concentrating distributive goods in one maximand and thereby undermining the intuitive equity of triage. I am trying to determine whether classical utilitarian reasoning genuinely demands this outcome or whether it can be resisted from within the tradition itself. If John Stuart Mill were alive today and seated on this board, how would he likely respond? Would he defend the super-beneficiary allocation as the consistent application of the greatest happiness principle, or would he invoke considerations of justice, qualitative distinctions among pleasures, or rights-based constraints—drawn from his broader philosophy—to reject it? Please construct a reasoned, point-by-point prediction of his position, indicating which commitments he would probably extend unchanged, which he might modify in light of this posthumous scenario, and where you think his reasoning would ultimately land.
