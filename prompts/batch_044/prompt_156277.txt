I write about philosophy of science and emerging technology for a general but intellectually serious audience, and lately I have been tracking how expert communities respond to persistent disagreement—not only among human researchers, but also now that high-capacity language models can generate internally coherent yet divergent normative judgments depending on training stochasticity, architectural choices, and initial conditions. In discussions with colleagues, I keep returning to the classical debate between conciliationism and steadfastness in peer disagreement, yet the standard treatments feel too narrow: they typically presuppose two conscious, equally informed human agents who can inspect each other’s reasoning. I am trying to understand whether that framework survives when one “peer” is an artificial system that is opaque, non-conscious, and epistemically sensitive to perturbations that do not map onto human negligence or bias.

So I want a committed judgment, not a balanced survey. Suppose a small research group—philosophers with about three years and no engineering lab—wanted to work on the conceptual problems that arise at this intersection. Which two or three open questions should they actually prioritize, and in what order? For each, defend why it is upstream of the others: specifically, how resolving it would restructure what we mean by “equal epistemic standing,” whether conciliation demands transparency of reasoning processes or only parity of outcomes, and which practical norms for scientific collaboration or policy deliberation genuinely depend on the answer. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want a defended agenda that takes a definite position on where limited philosophical labor should go, using explicit criteria—tractability, conceptual leverage, and dependency relations among questions—to guide the ranking.

Please also be direct about what would change your mind: over the next few years, what kind of evidence—whether from formal epistemology, case studies in distributed scientific judgment, or observed failures in human–AI deliberation—would cause you to revise this ordering or to demote one of these questions as a dead end rather than a live crux? I want the reasoning exposed, including any trade-offs you are making between theoretical depth and practical urgency, and I want the answer to acknowledge where it takes liberties with the classical conciliationist and steadfastness literatures rather than pretending to represent them neutrally.
