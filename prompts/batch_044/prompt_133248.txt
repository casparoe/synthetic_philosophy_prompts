In a seminar on revolutionary politics I have been attending, a classmate recently invoked the old line that revolutions devour their children in order to cut off debate about whether the Terror’s purge of the Girondists and the execution of figures like Danton was genuinely inevitable. His point was not merely that it happened, but that the slogan expresses something like a structural law: once a revolutionary rupture occurs, internal radicalization and the elimination of moderates follow with something close to necessity, so appeals to contingency, misjudgment, or particular ideological choices miss the deeper mechanism at work. I am skeptical that the phrase actually carries enough content to do that work, but I also recognize that it might be hiding several quite different claims—some trivial, some empirical, some normative, and some metaphysically loaded—under a single vivid image.

Could you lay out the precise theses that “revolutions devour their children” could be expressing, treating the slogan not as a single theory but as a family of disambiguated readings? In particular, I would like you to distinguish: (1) a weak empirical generalization about the frequency of intra-revolutionary purges; (2) a functionalist claim that revolutionary regimes, lacking stable constitutional mechanisms for factional rotation, necessarily resolve succession crises through violence; (3) a normative or tragic thesis about revolutionary virtue corrupting itself through the very intensity of its moral commitments; (4) a specifically French-Revolution structural claim, perhaps tied to the absence of a legitimate arbitration procedure between competing claims to popular sovereignty, that makes the Thermidorian reaction not an accident but an immanent reversal; and (5) a metaphorical or almost thermodynamic image in which revolutionary energy dissipates through internal friction regardless of particular actors. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false; whether it actually applies to the French case specifically or only to modern revolutions with vanguard-style party structures; and, most importantly, which of these readings—if any—would genuinely support the dismissal of contingency that my classmate intended, and which would leave room for the view that different choices by the Convention, the Committee of Public Safety, or particular deputies might have altered the outcome. I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of historians, but rather for a conceptual menu I can use to press him on what exactly he thinks the slogan proves.
