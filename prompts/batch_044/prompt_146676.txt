I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a friend about whether adversarial debate can actually detect deception when reasoning is deliberately fragmented. In current AI safety practice, there is a protocol where a proposer model constructs an argument or plan, but distributes parts of its reasoning chain across separate, non-interacting context buffers—sometimes encoded or compressed—so that the critic model sees only the final claim and a subset of intermediate steps. The institutional rationale is that deception requires logical coherence, so adversarial probing should still expose contradictions at the output boundary even if the internal fragments remain hidden. What view of reasoning and epistemic access does that rationale presuppose? Must it assume that the content of an argument is fully recoverable from its publicly observable outputs, or can it accommodate a kind of distributed semantic externalism where the argument's identity depends on relations among hidden fragments? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of knowledge and deception, distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say what structural changes would be necessary if we replaced that epistemology with one that treats unobservable inferential steps as genuinely opaque rather than merely practically concealed.
