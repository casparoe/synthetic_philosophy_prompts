In a graduate seminar on population ethics, a colleague kept returning to a line she treated as decisive: "Once suffering crosses the lexical threshold, pleasure becomes incommensurable with it—no aggregation can bridge the gap." She used it to reject any welfare calculus that permits extreme suffering for the sake of massive aggregate happiness, insisting that beyond that point the two dimensions cease to belong to a single evaluative scale. I am unsure whether she is advancing a claim about psychological measurement, about the structure of rational preference, about the architecture of a social welfare function, or about something else entirely, and I want to avoid talking past her.

Could you disambiguate that slogan into its distinct precise theses? For each reading, specify whether it concerns (i) the cardinal measurability and interpersonal comparison of pain versus pleasure, (ii) a lexical ordering in individual choice, (iii) a normative incommensurability embedded in an aggregation rule, or (iv) a metaphysical claim about the nature of value; then say which versions are trivial, which are substantive but defensible, which are false, and—crucially—which ones actually license her conclusion that extreme suffering should never be tolerated regardless of compensating happiness. I am particularly interested in whether the incommensurability claim is doing independent moral work or is merely rhetorical packaging for a standard negative-utilitarian priority rule, and in how the commensurability debate changes if one restricts attention to intra-personal rather than inter-personal trade-offs.
