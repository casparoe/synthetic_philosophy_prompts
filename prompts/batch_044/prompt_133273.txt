As a novelist drafting a philosophical exchange for a novel about an ascetic community that regards desire as a kind of cognitive pollution, I need an argument that goes well beyond the familiar formulations one might find in introductory texts. The position I want defended is a stringent variant of tranquilism—let us call it negative-constitutive tranquilism—which holds that the absence of craving and suffering is not merely the highest good but the only state with intrinsic value, and further that any occurrence of positive feeling, even of a purely contemplative or aesthetic kind, is necessarily defective because it introduces what we might term latent attachment-formation: the subtle encoding of a preference for the continuation of that state, which functions as a proto-craving. Please construct a rigorous argument for this view that directly confronts the objection that a life composed entirely of such absence is phenomenologically indistinguishable from non-existence or deep unconsciousness, using as a central tool a distinction between vacant absence—a mere lack of content—and active dissolution, in which consciousness operates as a continuous, self-consuming release of potential attachments without substituting new ones. The argument should establish why only active dissolution qualifies as the tranquil good, should explain how it can be sustained over time without collapsing into vacancy, and should avoid any appeal to textual authorities, historical figures, or named doctrines, relying instead on the internal logic of conscious economy and the structural dynamics of desire itself.
