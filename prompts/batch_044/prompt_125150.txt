A fellow student in my philosophy of mind reading group recently summarised Hofstadter's position on whether an 'I' can be distributed across brains in the following way: 'Hofstadter holds that the self is not a physical entity or an immaterial soul, but a strange loop — a self-referential pattern of symbols that can in principle be instantiated in any substrate capable of representing it. Because patterns are substrate-independent, the loop that constitutes my "I" is not confined to my own brain; fragments of it are already present in the minds of people who know me, embedded in their memories and behavioural dispositions. Once I die, these fragments might, in principle, combine in other brains to form a more complete version of my loop, so that my "I" genuinely persists in a distributed fashion across several living people. Yet this distributed persistence depends on what Hofstadter calls symbolic resonance — a continuous, interactive updating among the host brains — without which the pattern collapses into mere biography rather than a living self. Consequently, if neuroscience ever enables direct brain-to-brain symbol sharing, we could deliberately construct a collective "I" spread across multiple individuals, and the moral status of such a being would hinge on whether the resonance network achieves sufficient loop-completeness.'

I suspect this captures his view, but I also worry that it imports ideas from distributed cognition theory that Hofstadter himself never endorsed.
