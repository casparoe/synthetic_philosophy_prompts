I run an interdisciplinary seminar on political ethics and institutional design—about 18 advanced undergraduates and graduate students, one 75-minute session, with no assumed shared vocabulary. I want a structured role-play or simulation that makes participants feel, rather than merely discuss, why defining corruption across cultures is not just a matter of drawing a sharper line. The core tension I want them to inhabit is between an obligation-to-reciprocate framework, in which receiving a substantial gift creates a binding social debt that must be honored through future action, and an impartiality-norm framework, in which any private benefit tied to a public decision is a disqualifying conflict of interest. The problem is that in many contexts the same material exchange—say, a livestock gift presented to a local councilor before a water-rights vote—functions simultaneously as both. I want the rules and hidden incentives arranged so that groups operating under different frameworks evaluate the identical exchange differently, then are forced to apply one universal standard to classify a set of exchanges. By the end they should have experienced how the corruption label presupposes a specific boundary between public office and private loyalty that is not self-evidently universal, and they should see that clientelism and gift-giving are not simply opposite ends of a spectrum but can occupy the same institutional space. Please design: (1) the roles and group assignments (e.g., council members, community representatives, external auditors, NGO observers), (2) the staged scenario with concrete materials (what is being decided, what the gift is, how it is delivered, and a synthetic hybrid exchange form—such as a collective contribution that is also personally directed—to prevent this from being a generic replay of standard case studies), (3) the rules of evaluation, including hidden or asymmetric information, so that participants actually run into the framework clash before it is named, (4) a payoff or stakes system using non-monetary metrics (legitimacy scores, community trust, audit clearance) that makes the conflict tangible, (5) a precise timeline for the 75 minutes, (6) a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the conceptual parts (the reciprocity obligation, the impartiality requirement, the universalist presupposition, and the historical specificity of the public-private separation), (7) an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time—such as participants defaulting to their home framework and moralizing the other groups—and how to handle it without lecturing, and (8) a frank statement of which feature of real-world clientelism the simulation cannot reproduce and you as facilitator must supply. Do not ask participants to read external texts; do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation request in the instructions; and keep the design runnable without web access.
