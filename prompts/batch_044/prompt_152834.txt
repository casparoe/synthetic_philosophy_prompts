Analyze the introduction of the moving assembly line at Ford's Highland Park plant around 1913–1914 as a case study in whether artifacts have politics. Do not merely recount the economic history; instead, map specific technical features—the continuous-flow conveyor, its single-speed synchronization, the elimination of craft sequencing, and the spatial separation of planning from execution—onto the political and authority relations they made possible or necessary. Then assess the episode through the distinction, central to this debate, between artifacts that inherently require particular social structures and those that merely happen to be embedded in them: could the line have sustained comparable efficiency under worker-controlled pacing and decentralized decision-making, or did its material form enforce a specific hierarchy regardless of management intent? Next, examine what the episode reveals about technological determinism itself: does the pairing of the line with the five-dollar day and Ford's sociological surveillance department show that politics shaped the artifact, or that the artifact reshaped politics in ways that exceeded Ford's original design? Finally, identify one feature of the case that the "artifacts have politics" framework illuminates clearly—perhaps the line's compatibility with Soviet industrial planners—and one feature it either misdescribes or leaves silent, such as the racial and gender exclusions that structured access to Ford's workforce, and say whether that silence is a weakness in the theory or simply outside its proper scope.
