Consider a modified version of the well-known Judy Benjamin scenario. Judy is a soldier who knows she is in exactly one of three divisions: Red Company, Blue Company, or Headquarters (Black). Before receiving any further information, she assigns equal prior credence—one-third each—to being in Red, Blue, or Headquarters. She also knows the exercise rules governing survival: soldiers in Red survive with probability 0.3, soldiers in Blue survive with probability 0.8, and soldiers at Headquarters survive with certainty. A trusted radio transmission then delivers the following authoritative statement of the exercise design: "If a soldier does not survive, then that soldier is in Blue; if a soldier is in Red or at Headquarters, then that soldier survives."

Maya and Elias, two philosophers observing the case, disagree about how Judy should update her beliefs upon receiving this message.

Maya's first move: Judy should treat the message as evidence that a particular material conditional—(not surviving → being in Blue)—is true, and apply standard Bayesian conditionalization on that fact. Given her priors, the worlds in which that material conditional is false are already low-probability (they require a dead soldier in Red or Headquarters). Conditioning on the conditional being true therefore barely shifts her location probabilities; her credence in being in Blue should remain close to one-third.

Elias's reply: That mischaracterizes the message. It is not an observed accidental correlation but an authoritative revelation of the exercise's structural rules. Judy should therefore perform a Jeffrey-style update on her conditional survival probabilities: P(survive | Red) and P(survive | Headquarters) both go to 1, while P(survive | Blue) remains 0.8. Once those conditionals are revised, she must integrate them with her prior over locations.

Maya's second move: Even after revising the conditionals, Judy has received no direct self-locating evidence about her own division. Knowing that Red soldiers always survive does not discriminate between Judy being in Red versus being in Blue, because in either case she is currently alive and able to listen to the radio. Since her survival was highly probable under all three locations to begin with, her present survival provides no meaningful Bayesian update to her location credences; they should stay uniform.

Elias's counter: The Jeffrey update changes more than the conditionals—it changes Judy's overall expectation about survival across the population and, crucially, creates an evidential asymmetry from her first-person perspective. Because she is now reasoning about the message, she can ask: "If I were in Blue, I might not have survived to receive this transmission; since I did receive it, that is weak but genuine evidence against Blue." This is not a mistake about sunk costs but a correct application of expected-evidence reasoning to self-locating belief.

Maya's third move: That argument illicitly assumes the transmission's delivery is contingent on her survival. In fact, the message is broadcast indiscriminately to all sectors regardless of individual survival status; a dead soldier in Blue does not block the signal. Therefore, Judy's act of receiving and processing the message carries no information about whether she survived, and Elias's "expected evidence" point collapses.

Elias's final move: Even if the broadcast is physically indiscriminate, Judy's epistemic access to its content is not. From her centered, first-person standpoint, she can only update on information she actually possesses, and she possesses it only in worlds where she is alive. Once the structural rules are updated, the correlation between location and survival means that conditioning on being an epistemic agent who received the message should slightly lower her credence in Blue relative to Red and Headquarters—though she should not treat it as decisive.

For each of Maya's three moves and each of Elias's three replies, rate the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating.
