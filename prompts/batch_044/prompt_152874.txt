At a philosophy workshop, two participants — Maya and Leo — debated whether compatriots have special distributive claims. Maya opened by arguing that special obligations arise not from shared ethnicity but from being co-authors of a common institutional scheme: citizens jointly authorize coercive laws, share tax burdens, and expose one another to democratic risk in ways that create a distinctive reciprocity. Leo replied that this institutional defense begs the question, since many state boundaries are historically arbitrary and coercive. He proposed a distinction between membership obligations, which require consent, and coercive cohabitation obligations, which arise merely from being subject to the same state power; if Maya’s argument relies on the latter, then non-citizen residents and even outsiders affected by a state’s policies fall under the same umbrella, dissolving the compatriot priority she wants. Maya answered that Leo misses the specific reciprocity of risk-sharing unique to citizens — for example, citizens are uniquely liable to conscription or fiscal extraction by fellow citizens’ votes — and that this generates a legitimate priority even if borders are morally arbitrary. Leo countered that empirical risk-sharing is too contingent to ground normative priority: a wealthy citizen faces little real risk, while a foreign worker affected by her country’s trade policy bears substantial burden, so Maya’s criterion either over-includes or collapses back into cosmopolitan concern for anyone causally affected. They asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange step by step, rating the strength of each move, say where each speaker is right or wrong, and identify what both are missing — particularly regarding whether there is a principled line between political reciprocity and global causal interdependence.
