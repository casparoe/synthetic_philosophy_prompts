I hold the view that karma is best reconstructed not as a metaphysical law of causation but as a diachronic theory of moral desert: the cosmic mechanism ensures proportionality between moral quality and experienced well-being across lifetimes, with the desert-base being the intentional structure (cetana) of the act rather than the persistence of a substantial self. To maintain this, I treat collective or institutional karma as reducible to aggregated individual intentional structures, and I treat intermediate states (bardos) as heuristic descriptions of transitional intentional patterns rather than ontologically real waystations. What would count against this interpretation? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: conceptual problems with aggregating individual desert into collective outcomes; whether denying ontological status to intermediate states collapses the view into Theravada momentariness or eternalism; whether the proportionality claim is unfalsifiable because cross-lifetime correlations are evidentially inaccessible; and what defenders of this specific reading would themselves concede as damaging, not only what critics allege. If the position has been insulated by qualifications, assess whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind. Please answer in American English.
