In climate governance frameworks—particularly those emerging from the IPCC assessment reports and national geoengineering research protocols—there is a marked institutional asymmetry in how carbon dioxide removal (CDR) and solar radiation management (SRM) are regulated. CDR projects are typically permitted to proceed incrementally and can be scaled back with effects that dissipate over decades; by contrast, SRM proposals are almost always accompanied by termination-shock protocols, institutional safeguards that treat an abrupt cessation not merely as inefficient but as catastrophically worse than never having started, because the suppressed warming rebounds rapidly. What philosophical commitments about the direction of time, causal asymmetry, and the ontology of physical processes does this regulatory distinction presuppose? Does it assume a robust, non-conventional arrow of time rooted in thermodynamic irreversibility—that is, that entropy increase fixes a direction in which interventions become locked in—or can the distinction be explained entirely by contingent engineering facts and risk-management pragmatism? Furthermore, is the practice consistent with the same policy documents’ treatment of past carbon emissions as a settled baseline that can only be mitigated, never genuinely undone, or does that second commitment presuppose a different, perhaps more metaphysically loaded, view of temporal direction? Reconstruct the implicit theory of time embedded in these protocols, distinguish what they strictly require from what they merely accommodate, and indicate what would have to change in climate governance if one replaced the underlying view with a time-symmetric physics coupled to a purely decision-theoretic account of risk.
