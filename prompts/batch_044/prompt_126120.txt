I have recently become preoccupied with the logic of cooperative termination after observing a prolonged professional negotiation that seemed to unravel precisely when both parties knew the endpoint was near—an experience that led me to revisit the centipede game and the backward induction paradox in my coursework on rational choice theory. Rather than rehearse the standard textbook presentation, I want to explore a somewhat more granular framing: suppose we model the interaction not as a fixed finite horizon with perfect common knowledge of rationality, but as a sequence in which each player maintains a dynamically updated "rationality credit"—a graded belief about the opponent's willingness to deviate from myopic payoff maximization based on the history of cooperative moves—while also recognizing that the utility of continuing the interaction includes a non-decomposable relational value that is destroyed the moment either party invokes the backward induction logic. Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—one who maintains that even with graded belief revision and relational utility, backward induction eventually forces termination at the first node because any positive probability of rational defection propagates backward, and another who argues that once relational utility is genuinely non-decomposable and belief revision is path-dependent, the induction chain breaks and sustained cooperation becomes rationally stable. The characters should engage each other's actual arguments about belief propagation and utility decomposition rather than talking past one another, and the exchange should feel technically informed without relying on citation or reference to specific literature.
