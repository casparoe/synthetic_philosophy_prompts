At a philosophy-of-mind reading group, two members—Riley and Sam—got stuck arguing over whether Dennett’s Chase-and-Sanborn intuition pump shows that qualia are an incoherent posit. They agree on the setup: after years of tasting Maxwell House coffee, Chase reports that he has become a more sophisticated drinker and now dislikes the taste, while Sanborn reports that the coffee now tastes different to him, implying an intrinsic phenomenal change. They want an outside referee.

Move 1: Riley argued that Dennett’s core point is that Chase and Sanborn are behaviorally, functionally, and introspectively indistinguishable; therefore there is no fact of the matter about which of them experienced a change in intrinsic qualia. Positing an unobservable, non-functional “what-it’s-like” property is therefore metaphysically incoherent—like positing a bare particular that plays no explanatory or predictive role.

Move 2: Sam replied that this confuses epistemology with ontology. The inability to discriminate between two states does not entail there is no difference. To make the point concrete, Sam proposed a hypothetical phenomenal-inverter drug that swaps the intrinsic character of every color experience—so red now carries the intrinsic feel green used to carry—while leaving all behavioral dispositions, neural signatures, and relational judgments intact. A subject takes it and reports, “Everything looks the same, but I have a lingering sense that something intrinsic has flipped.” Sam said this shows an undetectable intrinsic change is conceptually possible, so absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

Move 3: Riley countered that Sam’s “lingering sense” is just a second-order belief with no evidential weight—no different from insisting one is followed by an invisible dragon despite all contrary evidence. Riley then pushed further: imagine extending the inverter to entire qualia bundles in which not only colors but the full affective-relational structure is preserved—pain still hurts, pleasure still pleases, disgust still repels—but each node’s inner glow is replaced by a different one that bears all the same structural relations. Even here, Riley claimed, there is nothing left for the term “qualia” to latch onto except a ghost in the machine.

Move 4: Sam said Riley begs the question by assuming that any property must either be functional/relational or be a ghost. Sam proposed an intermediate category—structurally anchored but non-functional properties—analogous to the specific acoustic timbre of a sound fixed by its physical structure but not reducible to the listener’s behavioral response. Sam argued that Chase and Sanborn are precisely disagreeing over whether taste has such anchored phenomenal properties, and that dismissing them a priori is not an argument but a refusal to take first-person phenomenology seriously.

Move 5: Riley responded that Sam still has not answered the incoherence charge. If a property is genuinely structurally anchored, the anchoring structure should allow identification and calibration. But in the Chase-Sanborn case neither subject can point to any structural feature—neural, acoustic, or relational—that distinguishes the two experiences. So Sam’s category collapses either into functionalism (if the structure does the work) or into mystery-mongering (if it does not).

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: say who is right about what at each step, identify the points both sides miss, and say which side is closer to being right overall. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
