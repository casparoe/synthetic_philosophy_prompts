As a retired physicist now studying moral philosophy, I have been examining the non-aggregationist position in rescue ethics associated with John Taurek. I have constructed an argument that seems valid but reaches an implausibly expansive conclusion, and I need help identifying the precise error. (1) If saving five people is not morally required over saving one, then the moral reasons generated by potential victims do not combine additively. (2) Non-additivity entails that the total reason present in a five-person rescue case equals, not exceeds, that in a one-person case. (3) Now suppose I control a divisible resource—say, a treatment protocol that can be split into five equal parts, each saving one life, but that saves no one if administered whole to a single patient. (4) Here, distributing the resource saves five lives; concentrating it saves one and leaves four dead who could have been saved at no extra cost. (5) By (2), the reason to distribute equals the reason to concentrate, so I have no stronger reason to distribute. (6) Yet concentrating produces four independent, unsatisfied rescue reasons of the very same kind that was satisfied for the one. (7) Because these failures are distinct, concentrating generates a pattern of moral failure four times larger than distributing, even if the reasons do not aggregate into a single stronger reason. (8) Hence, on non-aggregative premises alone, I am still required to distribute. (9) This applies to any divisible good—funding, time, attention—forcing exhaustive subdivision across all simultaneous needs and erasing any justification for focused commitment to science, art, or personal projects. (10) This is absurd, so one premise must fail. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (3), but I cannot locate the slip without collapsing into aggregation. Which step should I reject, and what is the precise cost of that rejection for the non-aggregationist? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
