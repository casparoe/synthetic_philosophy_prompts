In my distributive-justice seminar I have come to defend the American attitude toward economic inequality—accepting wide income dispersion provided intergenerational mobility and formal equality of opportunity remain intact—over the European preference for compressing distributions through aggressive redistribution, on the grounds that the American view targets life prospects rather than statistical parity. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Beyond the familiar stagnation of U.S. mobility, which deeper findings—about whether mobility metrics merely reshuffle positions within a stable hierarchy, about whether high inequality systematically erodes the institutional conditions that make mobility possible, or about endogenous preferences and positional goods that blur the opportunity-outcome boundary—would genuinely undermine the core claim? What predictions does this view risk that its rival avoids? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which objections would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as tracking the wrong metric? I am especially interested in whether there is a conceptual vulnerability—perhaps that once we fully account for dynastic transmission through credentialing and for relative-deprivation effects that persist invariant to absolute mobility—the distinction between equality of opportunity and equality of outcome collapses, leaving the American view without a stable foundation.
