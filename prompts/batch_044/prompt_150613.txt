Consider the American Prohibition era, from the Volstead Act in 1920 through repeal in 1933, and examine it as an institutional case study through Plato’s framework in Laws I–II. There, regulated drinking is not mere indulgence but a civic diagnostic: a symposion guided by a sober symposiarch is meant to reveal whether citizens are naturally temperate or prone to excess, whereas unregulated intoxication functions as a political and bodily disease. Apply that mapping rigorously to the national ban. If the state removes wine altogether, who—if anyone—assumes the symposiarch’s observational and moderating role? Does prohibition suppress vice, or does it drive the diagnostic underground, into speakeasies and clandestine consumption where no sober authority can witness the drinker’s true nature? Use the documented shift from public saloon culture to black-market distribution, and the corresponding rise of organized supply networks, as your empirical material. Then reverse the analysis: what does the American failure reveal about Plato’s model? Does it confirm his preference for regulated exposure over suppression, or does it show that his framework presupposes conditions—small-scale civic intimacy, shared ritual authority, a trusted ruling class—that a modern federal state cannot replicate? Be disciplined about correspondences: identify who in this episode plays the drinker, the symposiarch, and the polity; note explicitly where Plato’s theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak; and conclude whether Prohibition ultimately supports, undermines, or simply lies outside his conceptual architecture.
