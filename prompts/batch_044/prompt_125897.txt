I was discussing theories of well-being with a friend who had just read a draft essay—apparently circulating on a long-term-risk research site—introducing a position called tranquilism. He summarized it for me like this: Tranquilism holds that the only thing that finally matters for an individual’s experiential well-being is the absence of craving. It rejects the standard hedonist spectrum that runs from bad through neutral to good, and replaces it with a homogeneous value scale that moves only from optimal states—defined as moments in which one has no consciously experienced need to change anything about the present—to increasingly severe non-optimal states marked by stronger cravings. Pleasure, on this account, is not positively valuable in itself; it is merely the typical mechanism by which the mind temporarily suppresses suffering, essentially a mask over an underlying deficit rather than a genuine good. Because the view tracks only subjectively experienced craving for change, it treats death and deep unconsciousness as perfect states, since in them there is literally no experiential craving at all. From this, my friend concluded that tranquilism is really just negative hedonism in disguise, that promoting happiness is not merely secondary but conceptually confused, and that the view necessarily entails a suffering-focused ethics giving absolute moral priority to reducing craving over any other aim.

Something about this feels both too tidy and too extreme, but I do not know the literature well enough to locate the specific errors. Is this a fair summary of the view as it is actually developed? In particular, does the view really deny all positive value to pleasure, or does it distinguish tranquility from pleasure in a more nuanced way? Is the claim about death and unconsciousness being perfect states an accurate implication, or does the view restrict its scope in a way my friend ignored? And is the leap from “absence of craving is optimal” to “suffering-focused ethics is the only rational moral framework” something the position actually asserts, or is it an overstatement—especially regarding whether tranquilism is meant to stand alone as a complete moral theory rather than serve only as an axiological building block? Please go through the summary sentence by sentence, separating what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what is likely a misrepresentation, and tell me whether my friend’s final conclusions survive correction of the errors.
