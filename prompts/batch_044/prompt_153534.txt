As I am drafting a philosophical novel set in an alternate Victorian era, I find myself wondering how Jeremy Bentham might have judged a proposal that has no direct analog in his own writings: the use of germline gene editing and targeted neuromodulation not merely to reduce incidental pain, but to permanently re-engineer the affective architecture of all sentient life—human and nonhuman—so that suffering becomes physiologically impossible, leaving only gradations of well-being. Given his commitment to the greatest happiness principle, his particular concern for animal welfare, and his willingness to endorse radical institutional redesign when utility demands it, would he have embraced this hedonistic imperative as the logical culmination of utilitarianism, or would he have resisted it on the grounds that the absence of aversive states removes the primary engine of moral reform and social progress? If forced to choose between a world in which suffering is abolished through such biotechnology but human agency is thereby diminished, and a world in which suffering persists but drives progressive change, which outcome do you think he would have judged more conducive to the greatest good? I am particularly interested in whether his answer would depend on whether the intervention is applied only to future generations who never consented, or whether it could be made universal through some voluntary mechanism, and how his well-known skepticism toward metaphysical notions of natural rights might interact with modern concerns about bodily autonomy in genetic engineering.
