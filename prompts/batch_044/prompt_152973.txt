You are a senior reproductive-ethics consultant at a regional fertility center, and you need a reusable decision framework for evaluating requests for preimplantation genetic testing and experimental targeted germline editing. You have about forty-five minutes per consultation, your notes must survive review by a hospital ethics board that includes non-philosophers, and you cannot resolve deep metaphysical disputes in real time. The framework must translate two commitments into an operational procedure: first, the principle of procreative beneficence, understood as a reason to select the child expected to have the best life measured against a plural but bounded conception of well-being rather than any single metric; second, the constraints of liberal eugenics, meaning institutional neutrality among reasonable conceptions of the good life, a strict prohibition on coercive or socially compelled selection, and special caution wherever the selected trait is identity-constituting for existing communities.

Turn these commitments into a step-by-step procedure your team can actually run: list the questions to ask in fixed order; specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each; identify the exact points where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation (support, conditional support, defer, or decline); and flag explicitly where a question is irreducibly one of values rather than clinical or predictive fact, so that the board or the parents must decide. Mark which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure. The framework must handle hard cases: selection for traits whose value depends on contested social contexts; interventions with multi-generational effects that alter the range of lives available to descendants; and requests that define the best life using metrics the future child might reject.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

1. A couple requests germline editing to reduce a known high-penetrance variant for early-onset familial Alzheimer's, with no other intended modifications.
2. A couple enrolled in a long-duration isolated space-mission program requests selection for a heritable variant associated with extreme emotional resilience to prolonged solitude, a trait highly valued by mission architects but not by mainstream social norms.
3. A deaf couple requests selection for congenital deafness, arguing that deafness is not a disability but a cultural identity, and that hearing children born into Deaf families often face social isolation; the procedure must address whether best life can be defined in a way that respects this claim without collapsing into pure parental preference.

In at least one case the procedure should yield a recommendation that conflicts with the parents' stated preference, and you must explain how the framework justifies that conflict without violating liberal neutrality. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: for instance, resolving the non-identity problem, adjudicating between genuinely incommensurable conceptions of flourishing, or substituting for democratic deliberation about which traits should be socially encouraged.
