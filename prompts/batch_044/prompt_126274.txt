I'm trying to identify a particular failure pattern I keep noticing in discussions of self-control and intertemporal choice, and I want to know whether philosophy or behavioral economics has a precise name for it. The pattern runs like this: someone recognizes their own tendency to overvalue immediate rewards, so they construct an external commitment device—say, a locked savings account, a software blocker, or a public promise—to bind their future self. Then, at the critical moment, an exogenous disruption occurs—a technical glitch, an unexpected social obligation, a minor physical discomfort—that technically does not invalidate the commitment but provides a plausible excuse to suspend it. Crucially, the person does not abandon their long-term goal; they explicitly reaffirm it and promise to resume the constraint tomorrow. They treat the disruption as a one-time exception that merits temporary relaxation of the safeguard, yet they also fail to restore the safeguard afterward, and the cycle repeats with new excuses. This is not simple weakness of will in the classical akratic sense, because the agent is not directly choosing the smaller reward while believing the larger one is better; instead, they are disabling the architecture that was meant to prevent that choice, using reasoning that is locally plausible but globally self-defeating. It also differs from ordinary hyperbolic discounting, which describes the shape of preferences over time, not this specific maneuver of exploiting contingent events to dismantle one's own precommitments. Is there an established concept—perhaps in the literature on dynamic inconsistency, self-deception, or strategic reasoning about the self—that captures this exact configuration? If the nearest concepts are things like rationalization, the planning fallacy, or relapse-related frameworks in addiction studies, I would like to understand precisely how this pattern differs from each: for instance, whether it requires genuine belief in the exception's legitimacy or merely its rhetorical utility, and whether the cyclical restoration and re-suspension of the commitment device is essential or incidental. If there is no established term, could you propose a precise definition with clear boundary conditions—distinguishing it from plain equivocation, from practical reasoning that retreats and returns like a motte-and-bailey, and from cases where the commitment device genuinely becomes obsolete?
