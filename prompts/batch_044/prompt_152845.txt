I am defending, in a job-market seminar next month, a reconstruction of Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry. My central claim is that democratic legitimacy ought to be understood not as the aggregation of pre-political preferences nor as the achievement of rational consensus through deliberation, but rather as the institutionalisation of collective experimental inquiry—where policies are treated as working hypotheses subject to participatory testing and revision. To avoid the standard pragmatist objection that this collapses politics into technocracy, I argue that genuinely democratic inquiry requires what I call 'reflexive instability': institutional mechanisms deliberately designed to allow the participatory process itself to overturn the framework within which it operates, rather than seeking equilibrium or closure.

The audience will be mostly political philosophers—several committed deliberative democrats in the Habermasian mould, a couple of Rawlsians who view stability as a primary virtue of democratic institutions, one pragmatism specialist who knows Dewey's writings on inquiry inside out, and two philosophers of science who are sympathetic to scientific method but deeply sceptical of normative extensions of experimental practice into the political domain. I need a Q&A preparation sheet: the objections they will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they are to my position, each phrased in the vocabulary they would use. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, what I must hold firm on, a concise reply I could deliver in under two minutes, and the precise point where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks—specifically the one that targets the coherence of 'reflexive instability' rather than its desirability. Do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I need arguments I can defend without recourse to citation.
