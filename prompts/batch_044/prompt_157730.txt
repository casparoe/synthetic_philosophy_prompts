Given that I recently acquired citizenship and must now decide how to vote in an upcoming municipal election where both leading candidates have endorsed policies I consider seriously unjust, a disagreement I overheard at a dinner party has taken on unexpected urgency. Two guests—both philosophers of law, neither of whom I knew well—debated whether voting for the lesser evil is obligatory, permissible, or impermissible. I am reporting their exchange as precisely as I can recall it; I have no view to defend and want an impartial adjudication.

(1) The first argued that democratic citizenship entails a duty of justice: since the outcome will bind everyone, abstaining is a form of free-riding on others' maintenance of legitimate authority. When both options are flawed, one must minimize expected harm, so voting for the less unjust candidate is not merely permitted but required.

(2) The second replied that this reduces moral agency to harm-minimization calculus. If both candidates support rights-violating policies, casting a ballot for either expresses assent to the framework that produced them. Abstention is the only act that withholds democratic legitimacy from an unjust choice.

(3) The first countered that this confuses causal efficacy with symbolic meaning. In a large electorate, a single vote is almost never decisive; the harm is caused by the winner's policies, not by one's ballot. Abstaining therefore does nothing to prevent harm—it merely removes one's negligible protective contribution—while voting for the lesser evil actually reduces expected injustice.

(4) The second responded that the causal argument precisely misses what makes voting distinctive. Voting is not primarily a causal lever but an expressive act of authorization. Because the expressive dimension does not scale with causal power, one can be symbolically complicit even when causally inert. Voting for the lesser evil signals that the harmful system is acceptable if moderated, thereby stabilizing it; abstention withholds that endorsement regardless of electoral mathematics.

(5) The first then pressed a reductio: if withholding expressive endorsement is the standard, then citizens should also refuse jury service (which might convict the innocent), tax payment (which funds unjust wars), or employment in imperfect institutions. But we do not think moral integrity requires emigration or total withdrawal; we think citizens should work within institutions to improve them.

(6) The second distinguished voting from those other cases. Jury service and taxation are unavoidable conditions of social cooperation—one can serve to prevent worse outcomes or pay under protest—whereas voting is voluntary and specifically structured as an authorization of representatives. Casting a ballot is therefore uniquely an act of conferring democratic legitimacy on a particular agent. The complicity argument applies specifically here, and abstention functions not as anarchism but as a targeted refusal to authorize.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing that would change the evaluation.
