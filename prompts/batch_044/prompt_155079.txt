I'm on the heritage board for a midsize city and we run this dual-track designation system. Track one is the official archive -- deeds, census rolls, factory logs, all the usual. Track two is our community memory map, where residents pin stories, family lore, even half-remembered rituals tied to specific lots. When the two conflict we list both in the report and then vote, but nobody can say what the vote is actually deciding. There's this waterfront lot, for example, that a lot of residents recall as a gathering place for an erased religious group, but the archives only show a coal depot running 1890-1954. Our practice just records the tension and moves on.

What philosophical commitments about collective memory versus history does this procedure presuppose? Is it implicitly saying that lived memory has epistemic weight the archive lacks, or that archival records are just one narrative among others? Could the same voting procedure be run by someone who thinks memory is always distorted, or by someone who thinks archives are power-laundering? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, plus where the practice is philosophically undertemined -- where several rival views would license the exact same dual-track vote.

There's also this weird new layer: we started timestamping community submissions on a distributed ledger, calling them 'immutable memory nodes,' and now we recieve them through a portal that logs every edit. Does that change the implicit epistemology -- like treating oral testimony as cryptographically verified -- or is it just tech theater that leaves the underlying commitments untouched? And if we actually replaced our implicit theory with one that privileges documentary history, or one that treats community memory as sovereign, what concrete parts of our designation report would have to change? Don't need citations, just work from the concepts.
