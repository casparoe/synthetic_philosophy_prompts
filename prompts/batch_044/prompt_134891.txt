I have been arguing with a close friend—someone with a background in logic who has lately become convinced that Gödel's results have direct philosophical teeth outside mathematics—about whether his latest construction actually demonstrates something about human cognition, or whether it collapses under its own weight. We were preparing remarks for a workshop on formal methods and practical reasoning, and he insisted on testing an argument he thinks undermines both computational ethics and naturalistic neuroscience. I am writing to see whether I can locate the fault line, because at the moment the reasoning feels like a trap: each individual step seems defensible, yet the conclusion strikes me as far too strong.

Here is the argument as he laid it out. (1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem applies to any consistent formal system capable of representing elementary arithmetic: within such a system there exists a true statement that the system can neither prove nor disprove. (2) If we formalize a domain of practical reasoning—say, ethical decision-making or rational choice—using axioms that include arithmetic, then that formalization inherits the same limitation; there will be normative truths the framework cannot derive. (3) A being whose reasoning is entirely captured by that framework is therefore constitutionally blind to at least one true normative claim. (4) Humans appear not to suffer from such permanent blind spots: through reflection or meta-reasoning we can recognize truths that lie outside our prior formal commitments, which suggests our cognition is not fully captured by any fixed formal system. (5) One might reply that humans merely expand their axioms, but any expanded system is again subject to Gödel's theorem, producing a new blind spot. (6) To escape this infinite regress of self-modification without instability, an agent must possess a capacity for insight that is not itself governed by formal rules. (7) Therefore, any agent—human or artificial—whose cognition is fully formalizable at every stage is either permanently morally blind or locked in an unstable infinite regress; since we are neither, human cognition must contain an essentially non-formalizable core, and any project that models the brain or AI ethics as fully algorithmic is doomed from the start.

What I need is a diagnosis of where this chain breaks, if it does. Is the error in step (2), in assuming that normative or practical domains can be mapped onto arithmetic-rich formal systems in the way Gödel requires? Or is it in step (4), where my friend treats human meta-reasoning as genuinely escaping the theorem rather than merely pushing the Gödel sentence up to a larger system? Alternatively, does the slide happen in step (6), where he assumes that avoiding an infinite regress requires non-formal insight rather than, say, a pragmatic halting condition? I would prefer not to abandon the idea that Gödel's theorems genuinely constrain formalization—there seems to be real philosophical work being done there—but I want to know exactly what accepting or rejecting each step commits me to, particularly regarding whether the argument truly threatens naturalistic neuroscience or only badly designed AI architectures.
