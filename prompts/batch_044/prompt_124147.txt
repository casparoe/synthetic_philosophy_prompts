I am drafting a long-form essay on how evolutionary biology reshaped nineteenth-century thinking about human values, and in returning to Darwin’s *Descent of Man* I keep circling what looks like an unexamined structural parallel. Darwin explains moral sentiment as arising from social instincts—sympathy, habit, and the approval of one’s community—gradually refined by natural selection and then further shaped by cultural transmission and reflective comparison. At the same time, he treats religious belief as emerging from an overactive tendency to attribute agency to unexplained phenomena, a tendency that may have been reinforced because tribes with shared supernatural commitments enjoyed greater cohesion. On a first reading, both morality and religion appear to be evolutionary products: capacities that spread because they conferred survival advantages, not because they track independent non-natural properties or supernatural realities.

This parallel makes the modern philosophical reception of Darwin puzzling. Evolutionary debunking arguments are routinely directed at religious belief—if conviction in a deity arose primarily as an adaptive social glue, that genealogy is taken to undermine its epistemic authority—yet Darwin’s evolutionary genealogy of morality is almost never treated as a symmetrical debunker of ethical objectivity. Instead, philosophers often insist that explaining the origins of moral judgment is fully compatible with moral realism, or that natural selection can serve as a “truth-tracking” mechanism for cooperative norms in a way it cannot for theology. My confusion is whether there is a genuine disanalogy in Darwin’s own framework that justifies this asymmetry. Did Darwin think that moral progress involves an irreducibly rational, self-critical component—perhaps the capacity to compare one’s impulses with an abstract standard of virtue—that religious belief structurally lacks? Or is the modern divergence simply disciplinary protectionism, with ethicists accepting an evolutionary origin story for norms while metaphysicians reject one for the divine?

If one applies Darwin’s evolutionary account consistently across both domains, it seems one should either debunk both or rescue both. So I want to understand precisely where the supposed break lies: is it in the mechanism (group selection versus hyperactive agency detection), in the possibility of rational revision, or in a hidden normative commitment within Darwin’s own discussion of moral progress? And if the asymmetry turns out to be a modern imposition rather than something Darwin himself recognized, what does that imply for how we should read his account of morality—not as a neutral genealogy, but as one that quietly smuggles in the very objectivity it claims to explain?
