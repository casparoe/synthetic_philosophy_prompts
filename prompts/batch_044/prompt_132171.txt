I keep seeing people in rationalist and decision-theory circles reach for a model-based reinforcement-learning analogy to explain the indicative/counterfactual distinction: an indicative conditional is like an on-policy evaluation—running your current world-model forward from present evidence without altering the trajectory—while a counterfactual is like an off-policy or counterfactual evaluation in causal inference, where you branch at a past node, apply an intervention, and compute the outcome in the modified model. The conclusion they usually draw is that the difference is purely one of epistemic framing—both are just probabilities over worlds—so philosophers who treat them as categorically distinct are over-indexing on grammar rather than on underlying structure. But I worry the analogy quietly imports an interventionist theory of causation that does not belong in ordinary indicative reasoning, and that it collapses important differences in how we update on evidence versus imagine deviations. Can you audit this analogy carefully? Give me the explicit correspondence table—world-model state, evidence update, intervention operator, reference class of worlds, and whatever plays the role of a reward or loss function—then lay out the disanalogies: does indicative reasoning actually involve an intervention? Does the counterfactual case require a similarity metric over worlds that the RL framework does not supply? Separate the breaks that matter for the philosophical conclusion from the ones that do not. Finally, tell me whether the reductionist conclusion—that the distinction collapses into a single kind of probabilistic update—survives once the mapping failures are accounted for, or whether there is a better analogy that preserves the categorical difference without importing causal machinery where it does not belong.
