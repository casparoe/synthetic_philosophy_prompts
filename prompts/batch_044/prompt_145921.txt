I'm taking an advanced seminar on behavioral genetics, and after our session on variance-partitioning models my classmates Maya and Leo stayed behind to argue about a hypothetical study we had discussed in class: a twin study reports a narrow-sense heritability of 0.62 for cognitive flexibility in a population of adults raised in heterogeneous households, but an adoption study using polygenic scores finds that roughly half of that variance disappears when parental genotypes are included as predictors of the family environment. Maya opened by saying that this proves twin studies systematically overstate true genetic influence, because what looks like direct genetic variance is really just parents' genes constructing the environment; once you separate direct from indirect effects, the direct heritability falls to about 0.30, which she takes to mean that the trait is mostly environmentally malleable. Leo replied that this is a misallocation rather than a reduction: the parents' genes are still the causal source of that environmental variance, so the total genetic contribution remains near 0.62, and labeling the indirect portion environment is only a bookkeeping choice. Maya pushed back that if the mechanism runs through the environment—books in the home, conversational style, stress levels—then an intervention that changes those environmental inputs can still shift outcomes regardless of whose genes originally built them, so Leo's still genetic framing obscures the very possibility of environmental modification. Leo conceded that interventions are possible in principle, but argued that Maya is smuggling in a normative claim about malleability that the heritability coefficient was never meant to address; he added that in a population where every family provided identical cognitive stimulation, the direct heritability might actually rise because environmental variance would shrink, which shows that heritability is always a property of a specific population and cannot ground universal claims about nature versus nurture. Maya answered that Leo's population-dependency point actually supports her view: if heritability can jump or fall based on environmental standardization, then citing a single number like 0.62 is useless for policy, and we should always report direct, indirect, and environmental components separately rather than collapsing them into one genetic bucket.

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: evaluate Maya's opening claim about overstatement, Leo's bookkeeping objection, Maya's intervention argument, Leo's point about population-dependency and malleability, and Maya's final methodological prescription. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, and explain briefly. Then say who is more right overall about what heritability does and does not mean in this context, and identify anything important—perhaps about reaction norms, gene-environment correlation, or the limits of polygenic-score decomposition—that both speakers have overlooked.
