I've been turning this over in my head for a couple of years, mostly on my own, in the odd spare hour: is moral progress a real thing, or is it just the story that people at the end of a sequence tell themselves? The version I keep landing on is that the circle of moral concern has been getting wider over the centuries — it used to stop at your kin or your tribe, then it swallowed the nation, then all of humanity, and now people are arguing it should stretch to non-human animals and to those who won't be born for a hundred years. It's a comforting story, but I can't shake the feeling that I can only see the expansion because I'm standing at the latest point and looking back, and that from any other vantage the same history would look like a set of arbitrary swings. I'm not after a defense of any particular position, and I'm definitely not after a reading list; I want the specification first. So write out, as carefully as you can, what any adequate theory of moral progress actually has to deliver.

I want the full requirements list, not a summary. Which historical cases it has to be able to explain — and I don't mean only the flattering ones, so put in at least one clear case of apparent moral regression, and at least one case where the improvement was driven by money, technology, or demography rather than by anyone actually getting morally better. Which distinctions it is not allowed to collapse, for example between a change in what people sincerely believe is right and a change in what actually happens to people, and between the circle genuinely growing and our finally enforcing a rule that was in place all along. And which general findings from moral psychology and evolutionary biology it has to sit comfortably with, or else give a reason for discounting — I'm thinking of things like the fact that our default moral instincts seem tuned to small groups and to in-group favoritism, which puts real pressure on the idea that widening the circle is ever going to be stable. For each requirement, give me the consideration behind it in a sentence or two, so I can tell a genuine desideratum from a mere preference.

Then do the part I actually care about. Tell me which of the requirements you think are non-negotiable and which are really just one camp of moral philosopher wearing a neutral face, and tell me whether any pair or cluster of them is jointly unsatisfiable — because if the list itself is inconsistent, that's a result, not a failure. If you can, close by naming the single requirement that does the most work excluding bad theories, and say roughly what kind of theory it rules out. A table is fine for the list itself, but keep the reasoning in prose.
