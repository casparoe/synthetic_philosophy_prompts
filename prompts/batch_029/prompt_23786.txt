I'm a retired physicist — I spent most of my career in condensed matter — and I've only in the last couple of years started reading philosophy seriously, and I confess I find it harder to read than a physics paper because it doesn't give you a clean formalism to grip. I find myself drawn to the problem of causation by omission, and I'd like you to compare two ways of handling it: the counterfactual account associated with David Lewis and the structural causal model account associated with Judea Pearl. The two look similar on the surface — both, roughly, say that an omission is a cause if the outcome would have been different had the agent acted — but I suspect they diverge in the weeds, and I'd like you to show me where and why.

In particular, I'd like you to work through two cases. First, the preemptive omission: A fails to water a dying plant, but B, who would have watered it anyway, does so, so A's omission didn't counterfactually make a difference, and yet we still hold A responsible. Second, the tragedy of the commons: each of a group of herders adds one more animal to a shared pasture, and the pasture is ruined, but no single herder's addition counterfactually makes a difference. How does each account handle these two cases? And here is the question that's really bugging me: both accounts seem to require the notion of a "default" or "expected" state — the state against which the omission is measured — but neither, as far as I can tell, has a principled way of identifying that default. Is the default statistical (what usually happens), normative (what ought to happen), or something else? Does the structural model have a better answer here, or does it just push the problem elsewhere? I'm especially interested in whether the two accounts agree on what counts as an omission in the first place, or whether they differ on that too.
