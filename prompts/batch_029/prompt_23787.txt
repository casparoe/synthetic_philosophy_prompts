I'm in a seminar on social choice and collective decision-making, and I've been working through the judgment aggregation literature. I've come to hold a position I'd like stress-tested, and I want a candid map of its exposure rather than a defense. My view is that the discursive dilemma — the case in which a group, by majority-voting each of a set of logically connected propositions, ends up accepting premises while rejecting a conclusion those premises logically entail — is a genuine, non-artifactual threat to the internal consistency of collective decision-making, and that it is not dissolved by switching to premise-based voting, because that merely relocates the problem. Specifically, I think the premise-based reply fails because the choice of which premises to aggregate is itself a substantive, non-neutral decision: the same conclusion can be decomposed into different sets of premises, and different decompositions can yield different collective outcomes, so the "premise" the group votes on is fixed by a prior, politically loaded choice rather than by the logic.

What I'd like from you is a map of what would count against this position. First, which arguments or formal results, if true, would show that the discursive dilemma is better understood as an artifact of a modeling assumption — say, the assumption that the set of propositions the group is asked to judge is closed under the relevant logical relations, so that the conclusion is on the agenda once the premises are, or that the logical structure is fixed in advance — rather than a genuine threat, and what would the world have to look like for each such result to hold? Second, is the premise-selection objection I lean on actually a live threat to premise-based voting, or has it been defused by some standard move I'm missing, and if it has, what is that move and does it succeed? Third, what would a defender of the view that the dilemma is an artifact themselves concede as counting against them, versus what they'd dismiss as a misreading of the setup? And fourth, an honest assessment of whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — i.e., whether I can explain away every counterexample — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of these a committed defender of the "genuine threat" view would concede as counting against them. Of the various defeaters you identify, tell me which one you think I should find most persuasive, and why it should move me more than the others.
