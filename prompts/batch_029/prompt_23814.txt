Spent my career studying herd behaviour in fish and birds, so after I retired and got into philosophy and social epistemology, the "wisdom of crowds" and "information cascade" literature jumped out at me as basically the human version of what I used to measure. I've been reading an argument about cascades (the restaurant-line thing, where once enough people pick one place everyone just piles in regardless of there own hunch) that seems to prove too much and I can't find the hole. Here's it as I reconstructed it:

(1) Two restaurants, and each person gets a private hunch — a signal that's more likely right than wrong.
(2) A rational person picks by weighing their hunch against what they can read off the line in front of each place.
(3) Once the line gets long enough, what the line tells you outweighs your own hunch, so you ignore your hunch and just join the long line. That's the cascade.
(4) The final crowd choice is a function of the whole chain of choices, and each link in that chain was made by someone who had a hunch, so the final choice carries the information of every hunch in the line.
(5) Pooling more independent signals only makes your judgment more reliable — thats the jury theorem, the backbone of the wisdom-of-crowds stuff.
(6) So the final crowd choice in a cascade is at least as reliable as a committee that pooled everyone's hunches optimally.
(7) So blindly following the line is at least as good as full rational information-sharing.

The conclusion is absurd to me: it says a degenerate pile-on where everyone ignores their own judgment is at least as good as the ideal case where everyone shares everything, and I know from a lifetime of pooling replicates that the ideal case strictly beats a pile-on. I'm sure one of the middle steps is doing something sneaky but I can't name it, and I'd rather keep (5) and the "more independent evidence is better" idea than give up the conclusion. Which step do you reject, and what does it cost me to reject it?
