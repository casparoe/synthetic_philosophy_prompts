I'm working on a long-form feature about whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems really tell us anything about the limits of artificial minds, and I keep getting tangled in the actual logical weeds of the Lucas-Penrose argument rather than the popular gloss on it. I've reread the relevant chapters of Penrose's "Shadows of the Mind" alongside Putnam's and Feferman's rebuttals, and I want to see the strongest version of the disagreement play out rather than the strawman I usually encounter in magazine pieces.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind: one a committed defender of the Lucas-Penrose argument, and the other a computationalist who thinks the argument fails? I'd like the exchange to get specific rather than staying at the level of "Gödel proves machines can't think." In particular, I want the defender to address the standard worry that we have no guarantee human mathematical reasoning constitutes a sound, consistent formal system in the first place, and to explain why they think we can nonetheless assert our own soundness in a way a machine supposedly cannot assert its own. I want the critic to press on this directly, including raising the point that if a human's implicit "formal system" is unknown or unknowable to the human themselves, the whole argument that "we can see the truth of our Gödel sentence but the machine can't" loses its force, since neither side can actually exhibit the relevant Gödel sentence in practice. Have them go back and forth at least four or five exchanges, each responding to the other's actual move rather than restating their opening position, and let neither one land a clean knockout blow — I want the piece to end with the real fault line exposed, not resolved.
