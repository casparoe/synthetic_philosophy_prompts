I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or mathematical logic beyond an undergraduate discrete math course, and I've been trying to think carefully about the trust architecture of a self-modifying agent. Concretely, I'm imagining an AI agent that is allowed to rewrite parts of its own codebase — including, in principle, the proof checker it uses to verify its own work — but only when it can produce a machine-checked proof that the modified version satisfies a safety specification, and I'm evaluating whether to build this on top of an existing proof assistant. In a discussion with a colleague who works on formal methods, we kept hitting a wall I can't quite articulate: people cite Gödel's second incompleteness theorem — the result that a consistent, sufficiently strong formal system cannot prove its own consistency — as if that were the fundamental obstacle to an agent trusting its own reasoning, but I've also seen references to the Löb theorem and to what some people call the "Löbian obstacle," and I'm not sure these are the same thing. I'd like you to compare the two results for me.

To make sure we're on the same page, here's my understanding. The second incompleteness theorem says that a consistent, sufficiently strong theory T cannot prove the sentence Con(T) asserting T's consistency. The Löb theorem says that, for any sentence φ, if T proves the statement that T's provability of φ implies φ, then T already proves φ itself; in particular, T cannot prove that conditional for any φ it doesn't already prove. Two questions. First, are these really different obstacles, or is one a corollary of the other, and which is the real one for an agent that needs to trust its own proofs? In particular, the obvious workaround for the second incompleteness theorem is for the agent to add Con(T) as an axiom, or to anchor its trust in a weaker base theory; does the Löb theorem block that kind of workaround, and if so, exactly how — i.e., in what sense does the obstacle reappear no matter which theory the agent adopts as its trust anchor? Second, given the correct diagnosis, what are the most important implications for my design: is there any sense in which the agent can be self-trusting at all, or is the right architecture to externalize trust to a separate verification process that the agent is not allowed to modify?
