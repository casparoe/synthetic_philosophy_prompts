The proof assistants I work with — Coq, Lean, and the like — are built on dependent type theory, and their design rests on the Curry–Howard correspondence: a proof of a proposition is literally a program with a corresponding type, and verifying a proof reduces to type-checking. Homotopy type theory goes further, modeling equality proofs as paths in a space, so that two definitions of the same concept can be shown to be "the same" up to a continuous deformation. Brouwer, the founder of intuitionism, died in 1966, and his logic — which rejects the law of excluded middle and treats proofs as constructions — is exactly the logic these tools are built to formalize. I'd like your best reconstruction of what he would have made of this state of affairs. I've read that he was hostile not only to Hilbert's formalist program but also, later, to the formalization of his own intuitionistic logic, on the grounds that a formal calculus cannot capture the intuition it was meant to codify. Given that, would he have regarded a machine-checkable calculus of his own logic as the ultimate vindication of intuitionism, or as precisely the kind of betrayal he warned about? And are there any details of his actual, documented views — including his later, more idiosyncratic work — that change the answer?
