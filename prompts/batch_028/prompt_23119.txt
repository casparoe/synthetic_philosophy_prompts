I'm putting together a set of short essays for a graduate seminar on epistemic skepticism, and the dogmatism paradox is the one topic my students keep waving their hands at instead of actually engaging. I want you to write an essay that takes the paradox seriously as a genuine dilemma and then reaches and justifies a verdict on how it should be resolved. Frame it around the "prior warrant" version — the idea that to trust a source of belief (say, your perceptual faculties) you'd seem to need to already know the source is reliable, but you can't know that without already using the source, so you're stuck in a circle. I'm less interested in a survey of every response than in whether the paradox is really a threat, or whether it's an artifact of assuming a reliabilist criterion of justification in the first place. A dogmatist or Moorean who just flatly denies that you need independent, non-circular grounds for trusting your faculties looks like they're cheating, but I'm not sure that's fair — so I want the essay to press on exactly that point and decide whether the denial is principled or a cop-out.

To keep it from being generic, work in a concrete case, maybe the difference between trusting your memory and trusting a particular instrument or a friend's testimony, and show whether the paradox bites harder or softer for each. I also want you to confront the objection that any move which lets you "just trust your faculties to begin with" quietly imports the very reliability you were trying to establish, so the whole thing collapses into circularity. End with a clear, defended position — e.g., that the paradox dissolves once you reject the prior-warrant requirement, or that it survives and pushes you toward a specific view of basic justification — not a non-committal "it's complicated." A couple paragraphs of setup are fine, but I really want the reasoning to do the work.
