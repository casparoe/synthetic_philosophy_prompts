I'm a retired biologist who has recently taken up philosophy, and I find myself turning to it when a practical problem resists a purely technical answer. For the past several years I have been coordinating a volunteer bird count at a nature reserve. We monitor the population of a particular species by having volunteers record, in a one-hour block, how many individual birds they can identify in a given transect, and we then use that per-hour count as our headline indicator of the population's health from season to season. Over the last two or three years the headline number has climbed steadily, which we have taken as encouraging, but I have grown increasingly uneasy. I have started to suspect that the more experienced volunteers have quietly begun to spend their hour in the same handful of spots where the birds are most concentrated, rather than moving through the transect as the original protocol intended. The count goes up, but I am no longer confident that it tracks what it was originally meant to track.

I have been reading, in a rather unsystematic way, about the general idea that a measure can cease to be a good measure of its target once people begin to optimize for it, and that this can happen in more than one way: in one mode the people being measured actively game the measure, while in another the measure simply drifts out of correlation with the quantity of interest through no one's deliberate effort. I cannot tell whether what is happening with our count belongs to the first mode or the second, and I am not even sure that there is a genuine general principle at work here as opposed to an ordinary case of a sloppy protocol. I would be grateful if you could help me sort out which of these diagnoses fits, whether the distinction between them is philosophically significant or merely a matter of degree, and what, if anything, it implies for how I should redesign the counting protocol so that the number we collect is actually an honest gauge of the population rather than a record of where our volunteers chose to stand.
