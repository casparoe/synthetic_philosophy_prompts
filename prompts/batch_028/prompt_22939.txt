I am working on a novel about two veteran insurance actuaries who have spent thirty years at the same firm, were trained by the same mentor on the same historical loss data, and who, for the purposes of the scene, have access to exactly the same current information, with neither holding any private data the other lacks. Each is independently asked to estimate the probability that a newly identified class of risk will produce a catastrophic loss within the next decade, and the two arrive at figures that differ by a factor of three.

The scene I am writing has them sit down together, lay out all of their reasoning in full, and walk one another through every assumption, every model, and every number. Even after this complete and honest exchange, they do not move toward each other; each is confident that the other is making a mistake, and each can point to a specific step at which they believe the other went wrong. A careful reader will presumably ask whether this is realistic, and I would like the scene to be defensible, but I am not sure what would actually make two such people fail to converge after a full disclosure of their reasoning.

Could you tell me what feature I would need to build into the characters—some hidden difference in what they actually believe about the world, or in how they weigh evidence—that would make the persistent disagreement rational rather than mere stubbornness? And if the disagreement is genuinely rational, what should the more careful of the two actually conclude about the situation, if anything? I want the answer to be something a character could articulate in plain speech in the course of the scene, not a lecture the reader has to sit through.
