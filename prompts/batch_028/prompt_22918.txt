I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I recently got tangled up in an argument with a colleague about whether it's rational to rely on experts in domains you can't verify yourself. His favored example was ethics: he maintained that just as you'd defer to a lawyer about a contract or a doctor about a diagnosis, you should defer to a moral philosopher about whether a particular action is right, and that the fact that you can't independently check the moral claim is not a reason to discount testimony but the same reason you accept testimony in every other field. I pushed back by pointing out that moral claims are different, because you can't be a mere spectator of your own actions the way you can be a spectator of a mathematical proof, but I couldn't articulate the difference, and I suspect I'm simply being inconsistent, since I plainly accept testimony about things I can't verify, like whether a cryptographic protocol is actually secure.

Could you walk me through the conceptual issue of moral testimony as it's developed in the philosophical literature? I'd like you to first lay out the structure of the argument that moral inquiry is like other expert domains such as medicine, mathematics, or law, and explain why that argument is meant to be a problem for the view that moral knowledge must be grounded in one's own moral reasoning. Then I'd like the main objections, with particular attention to the worry that accepting moral testimony undermines the point of morality itself, since acting on a moral claim seems to require taking it as a reason for yourself rather than as a report from an authority.

Finally, I'd like an account of why the standard replies to that objection might not fully dissolve the problem, for instance the reply that you can combine an expert's testimony with your own moral judgment the way you'd combine a doctor's diagnosis with your own symptoms, or the reply that moral experts function more like diagnosticians who help you see the relevant features of a situation than oracles who hand down verdicts. I'm not looking for a survey of who said what; I want a clear account of the conceptual tension and of the specific points where the debate actually turns, since the disagreement with my colleague still feels unresolved to me.
