I am a retired physicist who has only in the past year or so begun to read philosophy seriously, and I have been trying to work through Wittgenstein's Lectures on Religious Belief. There is a point in that work that I keep coming back to, and I would be grateful for a careful explanation. Wittgenstein insists that religious belief is not a hypothesis and is in a sense groundless: it does not stand to the world in the evidential relation that a scientific hypothesis does, and it is not the kind of thing that could be overturned by the arrival of new evidence. I understand the broad gesture, but two difficulties remain for me, and I would like them addressed. First, if a belief is not a hypothesis, is not supported by evidence, and is not susceptible to disconfirmation by experience, what exactly distinguishes it from a delusion or from a merely false belief? Second, if such a belief genuinely cannot be overturned by experience, in what sense can a person ever lose their faith? Please explain how Wittgenstein's talk of a picture of the world or a form of life is supposed to do the work of drawing these distinctions, and where, if anywhere, that account is vulnerable.
