I was at a panel at a science journalism conference last month where two philosophers got into a debate about panpsychism, and I've been trying to make sense of it ever since. One of them, a woman who I think was at a midwestern university, argued that the combination problem is a genuine, unsolved problem for panpsychism. Her point was that if consciousness is fundamental and exists at the micro-level — in electrons, quarks, whatever — then we need an account of how these micro-experiences combine to produce the macro-experience we actually have. But any account of combination runs into a problem: if I have 10 to the 28th power atoms in my body, each with its own micro-experience, why doesn't my conscious experience look like a jumbled mosaic of all those micro-experiences? The only way to avoid this, she said, is to say that the micro-experiences are somehow dissolved or transformed in the combination process, but that's just magic — it's the same problem as dualism, just relocated.

The other philosopher, a guy who'd been writing a book on the topic, pushed back. He said she was assuming the wrong model of combination. This problem only arises if you assume that the macro-experience must be a mosaic of the micro-experiences — that the micro-experiences should be visible or accessible in the macro-experience. But that's not what panpsychism claims, he said. The macro-experience is a new, emergent property that arises from the micro-level, and the micro-experiences don't need to be visible in it any more than individual water molecules need to be "wet" in the same way the macro-water is wet. And the combination problem, he argued, is a problem for any emergentist view, not just panpsychism — if physical properties combine to produce new physical properties, we face the same question, and we don't consider that a problem for physicalism.

The woman came back with a point she thought was compeling: for physical properties, we have a rich mathematical framework for how they combine — quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics, thermodynamics — and we can in principle derive the macroscopic from the microscopic. For consciousness, we have no such framework. And the water-molecule analogy fails, she said, because we can measure the properties of individual atoms but we can't measure the "experience" of an electron. So the combination problem for consciousness may be permanently unbridgeable, which means panpsychism isn't a scientific theory — it's metaphysical speculation that can't be tested or falsified. The man's last point, before the moderator cut them off, was that we can test panpsychism in indirect ways — by studying the relationship between consciousness and physical processes, by looking at cases of altered consciousness, by studying the computational properties of conscious systems — and that the charge of "metaphysical speculation" could be leveled at quantum mechanics too, which also makes claims we can't directly observe. I honestly couldn't tell who was more right. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? I'm particularly curious whether the water-molecule analogy actually does the work he thinks it does, and whether the testability objection is a knockdown argument or just a complaint about where the field is right now.
