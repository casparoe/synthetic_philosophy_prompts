I just had a long argument with a friend who is convinced that Good's theorem on the value of information settles the matter: since the expected value of information is always non-negative, he says, there is no such thing as information worth less than no information, and anyone who declines a test or a diagnosis is simply being irrational. I think the theorem is doing less work than he allows. Give me a concrete, fully specified decision problem in which an agent rationally refuses to acquire information that, on the surface, Good's theorem says she would never be worse off obtaining, and then explain precisely which assumption of the theorem — about how utility depends on the state, on the agent's knowledge of it, or on her ability to ignore the signal — is actually being strained in her case.
