Can you explain the problem that arises when we require that an action be done for the right reasons in order to be fully morally praiseworthy? I'm trying to understand the distinction between acting for the right reasons and merely acting in accordance with duty, and how this distinction affects whether we should credit an agent with moral worth. Two sub-questions I'm especially interested in: (1) If the right reason to do the right thing is simply that it is right, doesn't this create a kind of circularity or bootstrapping problem? (2) Is there a meaningful difference between the right-reasons requirement being a constitutive condition on what makes an action genuinely good, versus being a condition on how much moral credit or merit the agent deserves?
