The student's answer:

Moral luck is the idea, introduced by Bernard Williams and developed by Thomas Nagel, that we routinely hold people morally responsible for things that were not fully within their control. The core principle at issue is the Control Principle: we are only responsible for what depends on factors within our control, and so two people who differ only in matters of luck should not be judged differently.

The famous example is the truck driver who swerves to avoid a child. In one version of the case the child is killed, and in the other the child steps out of the way at the last second. The driver does exactly the same thing in both cases, yet we judge the first driver much more harshly. Williams calls this circumstantial luck, because the driver found himself in a different situation.

Williams also distinguished several other kinds of moral luck. There is resultant luck, which is luck in the way things turn out; circumstantial luck, which is luck in the situations one finds oneself in, for example whether or not one was a paratrooper in the Second World War; and constitutive luck, which is luck in who one is, one's temperament and capacities.

The reason the problem is genuinely hard is that the Control Principle seems obviously correct when we state it in the abstract, but our actual moral reactions violate it constantly. A natural response is to say that we should simply stop blaming people for outcomes beyond their control and judge them only by their intentions. But that seems to ask us to abandon a deeply ingrained part of our moral practice, and it is not clear that we can coherently do so. On balance, then, I think the best we can do is acknowledge that moral luck is real and that our judgments are partly shaped by factors outside anyone's control.
