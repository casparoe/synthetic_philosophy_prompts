I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy and two of my study group mates got into a pretty heated arguement last week about why promises actually bind, and I've been trying to figure out who's right about what ever since. Nadia went first: she said the whole force of a promise comes from the practice it's part of, basically. Like money or language, "I promise" only means something inside a convention that everyone plays along with, and the convention exists because it serves a general interest — coordination, trust, being able to count on people. So the reason you should keep a promise is the same kind of reason you should honor a convention, and the Humean idea that promises are a human invention rather than something natural just fits this nicely. Owen pushed back and said that makes the obligation purely external, like a rule of a game, but when I promise I take on a reason to act that I didn't have before, and that reason comes from the act itself, not from some external practice. He said breaking a promise is a genuinely different moral category from cheating at chess, and the convention view can't capture that.

Nadia replied that if I made a promise in a context where no one else was playing the promise game, it clearly wouldn't bind — so the force has to come from the practice, and my "I took on a reason" feeling is just the convention intenalized. Owen said even in a context with no convention my promise seems to generate an obligation, and the convention view can't explain why the obligation sticks to me personally, like why I still feel bound when it's against my interest and no one is watching. Nadia said the convention view handles that fine because I'm a participant in the practice and those "no one's watching" cases are exactly where the internalized convention shows up, but then she turned on Owen and said the self-binding view is circular: promising binds me, sure, but why should I care about binding myself? The self-binder needs an independent reason to value self-binding, and that just pushes back to convention or self-interest. Owen's last move was that self-binding is a primitive moral power, like a real commitment, and we don't have to reduce it, plus the convention view has a worse problem: it can't explain why I should keep a promise when keeping it would undermine the convention itself, like if keeping it would expose that the practice is exploitative — on the convention view I'd have no reason to keep it, which is just wrong.

I honestly can't tell who's winning here. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point, not just who wins overall? And is there anything both of them are missing?
