At a colloquium I attended last spring, two of my former colleagues — a political scientist and a legal scholar — had a heated exchange about the "discursive dilemma" and what it implies for juries. Let me set the scene, since the technical point matters: a three-judge panel must decide a case in which the defendant is liable if and only if both (i) he signed the contract in question and (ii) he intended to be bound by it; each of the three judges is logically consistent in his or her own judgments, yet the majority finds that he signed, the majority finds that he intended to be bound, and the majority nevertheless finds him not liable. The political scientist began by saying that this shows any procedure taking separate majority votes on the premises is incoherent, because it can convict on a verdict that contradicts the majority's own answer to the question that actually matters. The legal scholar replied that the "inconsistency" exists only in the tally, not in anyone's judgment: every individual judge is perfectly consistent, and a panel is not a person whose opinions can be inconsistent, any more than a majority of preferences can be non-transitive — so the dilemma is an artifact of treating the panel as if it were a single agent. The political scientist answered that the point was not about anyone's head but about the record: a verdict must state grounds, and a record that says "signed, intended, not liable" is unintelligible on appeal, so the incoherence is in the institution, not in the representation. The legal scholar conceded the record problem but said the cure was to change the questions, not to abandon the premises: the panel could vote on a single compound question — liable if signed and intended — and then the dilemma would not arise, which shows it to be a limitation of a particular questionnaire, not of majority rule as such. The political scientist closed by saying that every such fix merely relocates the trouble: voting on the conclusion lets the majority convict on a ground that no majority endorses, and each restructured rule preserves one desideratum at the cost of another, so the dilemma is a genuine impossibility result about judgment aggregation, not a drafting problem. I am a retired ecologist who has only recently taken to reading philosophy, and I listened politely without being able to tell whose argument was stronger. Could you go through their exchange step by step, say who has the better point at each stage, and point out anything either of them missed that the other side should have pressed?
