I retired from a career in physics about four years ago, and in the time since I have taken to reading philosophy in a way I never did while I was busy with my day job. I recently worked my way through what I now think of as the cluelessness objection to consequentialism, the argument associated with James Lenman to the effect that because the long-run consequences of our actions ramify so widely and are so difficult to trace, we are in practice unable to know which actions are best, and that consequentialism, far from giving us guidance, therefore leaves us with none at all.

I have also read enough of the replies to know that there is no single answer, and I am honestly a little lost about how much weight to put on each. There is the move to what I have seen called robust satisficing, picking options that do reasonably well across a range of possible futures rather than optimizing for a single expected value one has no reason to trust; there is the suggestion of treating value as scalar or fuzzy rather than a precise quantity, which changes what the best option even means; and there is the more radical thought that a consequentialist should simply appeal to indifference or give up fine-grained comparisons altogether. I keep wondering whether these replies genuinely dissolve the problem or merely relocate it somewhere less visible.

Could you lay out, for someone in my position, the most important practical implications of this whole cluster of ideas for the way I should actually go about making a long-horizon decision, such as how to think about a large charitable bequest or a choice whose consequences I genuinely cannot foresee decades out? I would especially like you to be clear about where the replies really change what I should do versus where they only change how I feel about it, and to flag the points at which a consequentialist and a non-consequentialist might quietly end up recommending the same course of action.
