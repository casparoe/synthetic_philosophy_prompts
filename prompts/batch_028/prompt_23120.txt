I've been arguing with a friend about John Stuart Mill's late writings on socialism, and we keep going in circles, so I'd like to settle it by applying Mill's own framework to a concrete case. My friend insists that Yugoslav workers' self-management (introduced in 1950) is, in Mill's terms, much closer to the centralized "revolutionary" socialism he was skeptical of than to the small-scale socialism of self-governing workers' associations he found more attractive; his reasoning is that the means of production in Yugoslavia were held in a legal category of "social ownership," distinct from both state and private property, and that each enterprise was run by a workers' council rather than owned by the workers as a cooperative. On the other hand, Yugoslav enterprises did buy and sell their output on a market, competed with one another, and distributed a large share of their net income to their workers, with the rest going to investment and social funds. Given Mill's late distinction between centralized (revolutionary) and small-scale (associational) socialism, and his persistent worry that any workable socialist arrangement must preserve individual incentive and genuine competition, how would Mill actually classify and evaluate this system? In particular: does the "social ownership" of property—as opposed to ownership by the workers' association itself—tip it toward the centralized form he disliked, or does the worker management and market exchange keep it in the associational camp? And does a market in output, but not in the means of production, preserve the inter-association competition Mill thought essential to keeping incentives alive?
