I'm putting together a discussion for my upper-level seminar on legal moralism, and I keep running into the same problem. The Hart-Devlin debate is the centerpiece of the course, but it feels so locked to its 1960s context, the whole Devlin lecture and the Wolfenden report thing and all that, that students treat it as a museum piece and I just cant get them to see it as a live toolkit. I was talking this over with a colleague of mine over lunch last week and she said I should stop fighting it and just pick a case from the last couple decades that didnt exist when either of them was writing, so that the students have to actually work the concepts instead of just reciting who said what. The case I keep landing on is non-consensual intimate image sharing, the revenge porn stuff, and specifically the question of whether the state is allowed to criminalize it on the grounds that it violates some shared moral expectation about sexual privacy and consent, even in the cases where the victim is hard to pin down and the harm is mostly psychological rather than physical.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct, as charitably as you can, what Devlin's actual argument would have been on a case like this, and then what Hart's reply would have been. I don't want the obvious "revenge porn is bad so make it illegal" answer that you can probably produce in your sleep. I specifically want you to work through whether the wrong involved here is one of the minimal, near-universal moral rules that even a liberal who otherwise wants to keep the state out of private life would accept as a legitimate basis for law, or whether it's a more comprehensive, culture-specific moral claim that a liberal should resist enforcing. And I want you to bring in Devlin's idea that a society is held together by the invisible bonds of a shared morality — the way I put it to students is that it's like the root system holding up the whole tree — so that you can't just prune off individual moral beliefs without threatening the whole structure, and figure out whether something like revenge porn is the kind of thing that actually threatens that root system or whether it's just a branch a tolerant society can afford to leave alone. Close with the strongest version of the Hart-side reply that Devlin would have to answer, and then tell me which side of the debate you think the case actually lands on.
