So I retired about three years ago after a career in population ecology — I spent most of it building models of how animal populations grow, level off, and crash around carrying capacity — and my daughter, who does bioethics, has been pushing me to read actual philosophy for the first time. I've been chewing on the repugnant conclusion, the thing Parfit comes up with, and I gotta be honest, the more I read about it the more I think people are dressing up what is basically arithmetic.

Here's how I understand the argument, and I'd love a sanity check. You start with a population of people who are doing really well. Then you add a separate group of extra people whose lives are positive but not as good, and the total amount of good goes up, so the bigger population is better. You keep doing that, adding more and more people with a little less and less welfare each time, and eventually you end up with a vast population of people whose lives are barely worth living at all, and the whole thing is "better" than the original small happy group. That's the whole trick, right?

So my question is: if the conclusion is just "more total good beats less total good," why do people treat it as a deep philosophical problem instead of just doing the sum? It feels to me like the only real disagrement is that some people get sentimental and refuse to count the extra lives. Is the repugnant conclusion really just a fancy name for plain total utilitarianism, with the interesting part being how to get sentimentalists to accept the arithmetic? Or am I missing something about why this is supposed to be genuinly hard?
