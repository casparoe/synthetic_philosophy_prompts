I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together a long-form interview for my magazine with a political theorist who has spent the last decade or so working on judgment aggregation. I'm mostly there for the discursive dilemma — the thing where a commitee votes separately on a set of logically connected propositions by majority rule and ends up with a collective judgment that's internally inconsistent, even though every single member voted consistently. I've been reading her work and I keep going back to it because half the popular write-ups just repeat the little court-dissolving-a-company example and call it a day, and I can't quite tell where the real bite is. I'd like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that push past that.

I want at least one question on the difference between the older "doctrinal paradox" from the legal side and the "discursive dilemma" as List and Pettit formalized it, and why that distinction is doing real work rather than being a relabeling. I want one that presses on the impossibility theorem — not the lazy "you can't satisfy all the axioms" version, but which axiom is actually the one people are willing to drop, and what it costs to drop it, with a follow-up that asks what the resulting rule would actually look like in practice and whether a real committee would tolerate it. And I want a thread on the premise-based versus conclusion-based approaches, where the follow-up is keyed to the "it depends on the logical structure of the agenda" reply she's almost certainly going to give — I want that question to force her to say what that dependence actually looks like in a case where it genuinely matters, not just assert it. Close with one question on whether the discursive dilemma is a real threat to the legitimacy of collective decisions or a mathematical curiosity that only bites under contrived agenda structures, and what the answer becomes if we let in agenda control — the power to choose which propositions go on the ballot in the first place.
