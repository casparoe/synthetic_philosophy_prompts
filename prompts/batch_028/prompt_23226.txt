Assigned question: Pascal's mugging is usually presented as a problem for expected utility maximization. (a) Explain why a mugger's demand can have a positive expected value for the victim even when the mugger's claim is very probably false. (b) Offer one proposed response to the problem and evaluate whether it actually succeeds.

Student's answer:

The mugging works because the victim must weigh the expected value of paying, which is −c (the cost of the payment), against the expected value of not paying, which is −p·U, where p is the probability that the mugger is telling the truth and U is the magnitude of the harm the mugger threatens. The trouble is that the mugger can threaten an outcome of infinite utility — say, the total destruction of everything of value — so that p·U is infinite for any p greater than zero, however small. The expected value of not paying is then negative infinity, which is worse than −c, so the victim is forced to pay. That is why an expected utility maximizer is muggable.

For part (b), I think the problem dissolves once we notice that the threat is self-defeating. If the mugger is genuinely capable of bringing about the threatened harm, the mugger has no reason to demand a few dollars; the mugger would simply bring the harm about directly. So the very fact that the mugger is asking for money shows that the mugger cannot actually deliver the threat, which means the claim is false and the victim should refuse to pay.
