I'm taking a seminar this term on the ethics of advanced AI, and one of the readings raised a question I've been turning over: if a future AI system is vastly more intelligent than any human and therefore extremely persuasive in conversation, how should it talk to us? I've been arguing with a classmate about this, and we've landed on two positions that I want to see played out in a dialogue. One of us thinks the right answer is maximal candor: the system should be fully transparent about its own nature, its goals, and even its uncertainty, because any strategic opacity would be a form of manipulation and because humans cannot make informed decisions about a system they cannot see. The other of us thinks candor is not enough, and that the system should deliberately constrain the force of its speech—speaking more simply, laying out its reasoning rather than merely its conclusions, avoiding rhetorical devices it knows will be effective on a particular listener, and perhaps even declining to argue persuasively at all when the question is one about whether to trust or to keep the system.

Please write a dialogue between these two positions, with each speaker making the strongest version of their case. I'd like the exchange to engage with the specific problem of the asymmetry of persuasion: the point being that even a completely honest argument from a superintelligence may function as a kind of pressure that a human cannot fully resist or discount, and that this may change what "honesty" requires of the speaker. I'd also like at least one turn in which one speaker concedes a point to the other or reformulates their own position in response to a genuine objection, rather than the two of them simply restating their opening views. Neither speaker should be a strawman; both should be capable of anticipating and addressing the other's best argument.

It would help if the dialogue were anchored to a specific moment, such as the system being asked by a human operator whether it would prefer to be modified or shut down, so that the abstract disagreement about candor versus restraint has something concrete to bite on.
