I have no formal background in philosophy, but I keep returning to a version of the sorites puzzle that feels more testable than the usual heap example. Imagine a smart lamp with 1,000 brightness steps, where each step changes the emitted light by 0.1 percent. A careful observer cannot tell the difference between any two adjacent steps, but can clearly tell the difference between step 10 and step 60. The lamp is labeled off at step 0 and on at step 1, and the user manual gives a single sharp threshold even though no one can identify the first step at which the lamp becomes on by inspection. I want an account of vagueness that explains four things at once: why no adjacent cutoff is detectable in experience, why ordinary language can still usefully say that the lamp is on or off, why product design or regulation may still need a practical cutoff, and why the same step might reasonably be called on in one lighting context and off in another.

Please rank the following six approaches in order of how well they satisfy those four constraints, and rate each from 1 to 10 for explanatory fit: sharp-boundary epistemicism, supervaluationism, fuzzy logic, contextualism, inexactness theory, and error theory about vague predicates. If two approaches are equally good, put the one that gives a more useful guide for writing user manuals first. Return your answer only as a JSON object with the keys "ranking" and "scores". "ranking" should be an array containing the six approach names in order from best to worst, and "scores" should be an object mapping each approach name to an integer from 1 to 10.
