I'm a philosophy professor — epistemology and the philosophy of probability, mostly — and I sit on our state Supreme Court's advisory commission on forensic evidence, a small group that gets pulled in when a case turns on statistics the trial judges weren't trained to evaluate. There's a case in front of us right now and I need a memo I can circulate before Thursday's session, so it has to be something we can actually act on. The defendant was convicted of an arson at a family-run dairy in a very small, insular town; the prosecution's expert testified that a paint fragment from the scene matched a rare pigment formulation, that only about 1 in 50,000 homes in the state had a product containing it, and therefore there was a 1 in 50,000 chance the paint came from somewhere other than the defendant's house. The catch is that the defendant's house is in a town of about 800 people, and the dairy's supplier had sold that exact product to roughly a third of the town for a decade, so the "1 in 50,000" is doing the classic reference-class move I keep bringing up in my seminars — except I don't definately want the memo to stop at "the prosecution's number is wrong, here's the town number," because the town number is also a reference class, and the defendant belongs to smaller and smaller classes (the street, the supplier's customer list, the people who repainted in the last five years), and I'm not sure there's a principled place to stop, and I think the memo should say something honest about that instead of pretending the Bayesian fix is clean.

So: draft the commission's opinion memo. Recommend a concrete disposition of the appeal — reverse, remand with a specific jury instruction, or affirm — with a time frame for whatever next step follows, and engage the prosecution's position seriously: I want the memo to treat their real point as the strongest objection, seperate from the plain question of whether the state number was wrong, and their real point is that the rarity of the pigment is still evidence of something, and the defense never put on a Bayesian case of its own, so maybe the error was harmless. Explain why or why not that still loses. Keep the math light — the commission includes two judges and a forensic pathologist who will follow a conditional-probability argument but will stop reading if it turns into a textbook. One last thing: I was discussing this with a friend of mine who does the jury-study work and she kept saying the real question is what a jury will do with a "1 in 3,000" instruction instead of a "1 in 50,000" one, and I think the memo should at least address that head on.
