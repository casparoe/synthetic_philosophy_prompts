In preparing a seminar reading on Bernard Williams's "Internal and External Reasons," I want to draw a sharp line between two positions that students routinely conflate. The first is the straightforward motivational-internalist picture, on which a consideration can be a reason for an agent only if it is already connected to some motive the agent actually has. The second is Williams's own internalism, on which a consideration is a reason for an agent only if there is a sound deliberative route from the agent's subjective motivational set to a motivation to act on that consideration.

Please compare these two positions. In particular, explain why Williams's account is not simply the first position, focusing on the role that sound deliberation plays in fixing the relevant motivational set. How does that role make the internal/external boundary less sharp than the straightforward picture suggests, and what does that imply for whether Williams's view is genuinely internalist at all?
