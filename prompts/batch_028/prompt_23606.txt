I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm in the middle of a long piece on whether the future is genuinely open or already fixed, and I keep running into the sea-battle problem, the argument that goes back to Aristotle: if a sea battle is going to take place tomorrow, then the claim that there will be a sea battle tomorrow is already true today, and if it is not going to take place, then the claim that there will not be one is already true today, so one of the two claims is already true, which seems to mean the outcome is already settled. I raised it at a dinner with two people I trust a great deal — a working logician and a philosopher of time — and they ended up disagreeing about what the argument actually commits us to, and I could not referee it.

The logician's line was that the argument is valid and that we should simply accept that future-tensed sentences are bivalent, that they carry a determinate truth value right now, and that this is a purely logical fact with no bearing on whether the future is causally determined. The philosopher of time objected that the logician was quietly treating "it is already true that P will happen" as though it meant "P is already the case," and that truth is a way of representing the world, not a force that locks it down. The logician answered that this was just relabeling: if the truth value is already fixed, then the outcome is already fixed, and calling the fixedness "merely a truth value" is a way of avoiding the conclusion. The philosopher of time replied that the fixedness of the truth value is fixed in the sense that it tracks the future, not in the sense that it causes or seals the future, and that the claim that it will rain tomorrow is already true today without making the rain inevitable or making our present choices irrelevant. I left the table without knowing who had the better case.

Could you walk through their exchange move by move and say who is right about each specific point, rather than just handing me a winner? I'm most interested in whether the step from the claim that a sentence is already true to the claim that the future is already determined is legitimate, and in what, if anything, both of them are missing about the relationship between the truth of a future-tensed sentence and the openness of the future it describes.
