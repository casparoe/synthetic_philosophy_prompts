I am preparing a graduate seminar on moral uncertainty, and I want to clarify a distinction that I think is often elided: the difference between uncertainty over moral theories and uncertainty over moral facts within a fixed moral framework. Please explain this distinction carefully, with attention to the case in which the competing moral theories do not merely disagree about which act is right, but use different evaluative currencies or different decision rules. For example, one theory maximizes aggregate welfare, another treats certain rights as inviolable constraints, and another evaluates conduct primarily by the character of the agent rather than by the consequences of the act. My students keep assuming that if I assign a probability to each theory, I can simply compute a weighted sum of the theories' verdicts and choose the act with the highest expected moral score, but I suspect that this procedure may not always be well-defined when the theories do not share a common value function over acts.

Could you explain why the expected-value formulation is conceptually unstable in that case, and what alternative ways of representing moral uncertainty would be available, such as probability over value functions, probability over preference rankings, or a view that treats moral disagreement as partly a disagreement about the kind of question being asked? I would prefer an explanation that stays at the level of the conceptual architecture rather than surveying the literature, and that makes clear what must be true for a decision procedure under moral uncertainty to be more than a convenient heuristic.
