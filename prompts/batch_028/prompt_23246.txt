Two people in a forum thread I follow got stuck arguing about a mundane case, and I couldn't tell who was right about what. The setup: you're in a city you don't know, and a passerby tells you the museum you're heading to is closed today for a local holiday. You believe them and go somewhere else.

Sam said you weren't actually justified in believing that. To be justified by testimony you need independent evidence that the source is reliable, and you had none — you didn't know the person, you couldn't check, and the claim was specific enough to be uncorroborated. So you effectively guessed, and it's a mistake to call a lucky guess a justified belief.

Alex pushed back and said that's the wrong model. When someone tells you P, the report itself is a reason to believe P; you don't need a prior, separate reason to trust them. On Sam's account, Alex argued, no single report could ever justify, but we're constantly justified by single reports from strangers, so the account is plainly false.

Sam replied that Alex had assumed what was at issue. "The report itself is a reason" is only true if the reporter is reliable, so Alex had smuggled the reliability assumption in as a premise and then accused Sam of demanding it — stipulating the conclusion rather than supplying an independent ground. If Alex wanted to block the regress objection, Sam added, note that he doesn't need testimony to get his reliability evidence: he can have non-testimonial reasons to think people are usually honest.

Alex answered that they weren't stipulating reliability. Testimony is a defeasible default: it gives you a reason to believe that you only override when you have a positive reason to distrust the speaker — they seem to be lying, they're an interested party, the claim is outlandish. And Sam's "people are usually honest" isn't non-testimonial after all; it's itself built out of testimony and inference about other minds, so Sam had just deferred the regress rather than escaped it.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — for each of the four turns, say whether it's right, wrong, or partially right, and why — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing?
