The topic is Descartes and animals. Here's the question I set, and here's the answer one student turned in.

Question: "Explain Descartes' claim that non-human animals are automata (bêtes-machines). What is his argument from the absence of speech, and does he think animals have any kind of sensation or feeling? A few paragraphs is enough. Try to keep straight what Descartes actually wrote from the popular caricature of him."

Student's answer:

"Descartes holds that animals are basically very complicated machines, like a clock, whose whole behavior can be explained by the arrangement and motion of their body-parts, with no need to appeal to a mind or a soul. There's no immaterial soul in a beast the way there is in a human, who has a thinking substance (res cogitans) joined to a body.

His main argument is the speech test, which he gives in the Meditations. He notices that no animal, however clever, can put words together in a way that makes its thoughts understood, while even the stupidest human can. Since no animal can do this, Descartes takes it to show they have no reason at all, not just less reason than we do.

On sensation, Descartes denies that animals feel anything at all. Pull a dog's tail or burn its paw and it doesn't feel pain in any way; it just reacts mechanically, the way a clock ticks. So the popular picture of a dog writhing in agony is, on Descartes' view, just us projecting. Animals have no sensory experience whatsoever, not even the kind of sensory input that drives their reflexes."
