I'm a novelist and I'm about three weeks from a deadline on a book where the metaphysics actually has to hold up, and I've hit a wall. My protagonist is a particle physicist who, over the course of the novel, comes to believe that consciousness isn't something brains just produce but is a fundamental property of matter — the thing panpsychists mean, where even a rock has some dim, sub-threshold experience. The trouble is a friend of mine who reads philosophy keeps ambushing me at dinner parties with the combination problem: if every particle carries its own little experience, how on earth do all those micro-experiences add up to my one big experience of being a person? He's basically convinced me it's a devestating refutation, and I've started to suspect I'm building the whole book on a house of cards.

So could you write me an essay that actually weighs whether the combination problem is a genuine, fatal objection to panpsychism, or more of a confusion — something that can be dissolved or answered rather than just endured? I don't want the standard "here are the views on both sides" treatment that ends in a shrug. I want you to pick a real side and defend it, and I want the reasoning to be the point, not citations (I can't use your sources in the book anyway, so skip the footnotes).

Two specific things I'm curious about. First, the move where people say the objection quietly assumes the very thing in question — wich is that experience is the kind of thing that has to be assembled out of parts — and whether that's a real dissolution or just a sleight of hand. Second, the "problem of the many": if the micro-experiences are out there, why is there exactly one experience of me rather than a swarm of overlapping candidates? Is that the same problem wearing a different hat, or a genuinely different one? I need to walk away knowing whether I can keep my physicist's panpsychism in the draft or whether I should quietly retcon him into a dualist before my editor notices.
