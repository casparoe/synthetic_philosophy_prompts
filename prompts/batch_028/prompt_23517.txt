I'm taking a course on the history of educational philosophy and we just finished a unit on Whitehead, specifically *The Aims of Education and Other Essays* (1929). I keep coming back to this concept of his called "rhythm" — the idea that a good educational program should alternate between different modes of engagement. He talks about romance, precision, and generalization as the three main phases of learning, but the rhythm is about how you move between them, and he warns that if the rhythm is broken — precision without romance, say — you get what he calls "inert ideas" and the mind basically stops growing. I was reading some cognitive science papers on interleaving (the idea that mixing different types of problems or subjects in a single study session is better for long-term learning than just blocking, ie doing one type of problem over and over) and I couldn't stop thinking about how similar Whitehead's rhythm sounds to interleaving. I've read some of Rohrer and Taylor's work and Bjork's "desirable difficulties" framework, and it feels like there's a real connection here, but I'm not sure if Whitehead is basically describing interleaving in a more hand-wavy philosophical way, or if he's talking about something genuinely different — like a rhythm of the entire educational experience over years, not just a specific study technique you use in a single session. Can you compare the two in detail? I'm particularly interested in where they overlap, where they diverge, and whether Whitehead's "inert ideas" (knowledge received but never used, tested, or thrown into fresh combinations) has any real analogue in the cognitive science literature or if it's something he's just making up on his own.
