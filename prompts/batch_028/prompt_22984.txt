I'm taking a college-level philosophy course this semester, and we've just finished reading Clark and Chalmers's "The Extended Mind," which argues that the mind isn't confined to the skull. In the paper's central example, Otto, an amnesiac, keeps all his memory notes in a little notebook he consults constantly, while Inga, who has a normal biological memory, stores the same information in her head. The authors' "parity principle" says that if two systems play the same functional role in supporting a belief, and one of them (Inga's brain) counts as part of the mind, then the other (Otto's notebook) should count too.

At our last study group, Maya and Jordan got into a real argument about whether this actually works, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better case, so I'd like a referee. Maya started by saying the parity principle is basically obvious: the notebook does exactly what Inga's memory does — it stores, retrieves, and feeds information into Otto's reasoning — so if we're allowed to count Inga's memory as part of her mind, we can't consistently exclude Otto's notebook without just drawing the line at the skull for no reason. Jordan pushed back that the parity principle is never actually satisfied, because Inga's memory is immediately available and she trusts it without checking, whereas Otto has to actively open the notebook, and it could be wrong, lost, or scribbled over. Maya replied that Inga's memory is just as fallible — she misremembers things all the time, and recalling a memory is itself a process that can fail — so there's no principled gap in reliability between the two. Jordan then said the real difference isn't reliability but integration: Inga's memory is woven into her ongoing cognition, while the notebook is a tool Otto reaches for, and if we let tools count as mind, we have no stopping point, because then his phone, his cloud storage, and the whole library would all be part of him. Maya answered that the stopping point is given by functional role and reliability, not by location, and that the library fails precisely because it isn't reliably available or trusted the way the notebook is.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific claim, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'm not asking you to pick a favorite side so much as to sort out which individual points actually hold up.
