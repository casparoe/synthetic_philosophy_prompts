I'm working through the Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch model for my decision theory seminar and I keep hitting a wall on something that feels like it should be obvious. In the standard setup, each agent in line observes the actions of everyone who went before them and then folds in their own private signal before choosing. So it seems to me that by the time we get to, say, agent 20, that agent is effectively standing on top of the accumulated judgment of all nineteen predecessors plus their own signal. If that's right, the later agents should be making strictly better-informed decisions than the early ones, and the whole sequence should converge toward the truth the same way a big sample average does — more agents, more pooled private informations, more accurate final call. My group mate keeps insisting cascades are basically the market version of the wisdom of crowds gone wrong, which doesn't land with me at all because the whole point is that everyone in the line is being perfectly Bayesian.

But then I get thrown by all the stuff about cascades being fragile and information-poor, like how a single contrarian can collapse the whole thing. If each agent is rationally building on the entire history, why would the group's final decision ever be worse than just... averaging everyone's private signals directly? Am I missing something about how the signals actually get passed along, or is the fragility just a feature of the specific parameterization and not really a general problem?
