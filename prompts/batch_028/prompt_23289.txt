I'm grading a take-home exam for a graduate seminar on moral uncertainty and moral epistemology. The exam was worth 20 points, and the question I posed was: "Outline the expected moral value (EMV) approach to moral uncertainty: what is the core idea, and what is its most serious objection?" A student's answer to that question is reproduced below. Please assign a letter grade and a point score out of 20, and support them with point-by-point comments keyed to specific passages in the answer: indicate which claims are correct, which are imprecise, and which are wrong, and explain in each case why. In particular, I want you to flag any step that reads as though it follows but does not actually follow from what came before, and to assess whether the objection the student identifies is in fact the strongest one available against the approach. You may present the comments as a list; keep the assessment fair and specific to the text rather than a general impression of the student's ability.

Student's answer:

"The EMV approach says that when we are uncertain about which moral theory is true, we should act so as to maximize the expected moral value of our actions. The core idea is to treat each candidate moral theory — say utilitarianism, prioritarianism, or a deontological theory — as a hypothesis, assign it a credence reflecting how confident we are that it is correct, and then pick the action that does best across the theories, weighted by those credences. In practice this amounts to computing a weighted average of the utility functions of the candidate theories: each theory tells us how much moral goodness each available action produces, we multiply each theory's assessment by our credence in that theory, and we add the results up. The action with the highest total is the one we should do."

"The most serious objection, I think, is that the approach cannot handle moral disagreement. When different people hold different moral views, there is no fact of the matter about which utility function to weight by, so the expected moral value is just as arbitrary as picking any single theory. The approach also seems to demand that we be able to assign precise credences to moral theories, which is difficult to justify."
