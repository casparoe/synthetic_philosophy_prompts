I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of philosophy of action and decision theory, and I keep getting stuck on what I take to be a live and underappreciated controversy in the intertemporal-choice literature, so I'd like you to write an essay that actually takes a side. The setup I keep returning to is the contrast Thaler and Shefrin drew in their 1981 two-self model between a farsighted planner and a myopic doer, and the way O'Donoghue and Rabin later sharpened it into the distinction between the sophisticated agent—who fully foresees her own future weakness of will and discounts hyperbolically—and the naive agent, who does not. What I find genuinely confusing, and what I want the essay to press on, is whether the sophisticated agent is really better off than the naive one. On the one hand, the standard story is that sophistication is rational: she anticipates the doer's temptation and can precommit accordingly, so why would she want to be naive? On the other hand, the same literature seems to show that the sophisticated agent, precisely because she knows she will cave, ends up putting things off indefinitely, and that in some settings a degree of naivete is actually welfare-improving. I want the essay to weigh these considerations carefully—treating the "experienced utility" framing seriously rather than dismissing it as a trick of the model—and to ask, at the bottom, whether the whole picture of a self binding another self is even coherent, or whether it quietly reifies the doer as a separate agent who can be overruled. Please reach and defend a clear verdict on whether sophistication, properly understood, is a genuine advantage or a trap, and justify it against the strongest version of the opposing case rather than a straw man. I'll be honest: I don't yet know which way I want it to come out, so I'd rather you argue to the best conclusion the evidence supports than simply reassure me.
