I've been reading on my own about Frederick Douglass and the Constitution, mostly because I keep coming back to something that struck me as odd: that he clearly changed his mind over the decades about whether the Constitution was a pro-slavery document or an anti-slavery one, and I couldn't tell which of the two Douglasses I should trust. I'm not a historian or a lawyer; I just find the puzzle interesting, and last week at a dinner with two friends it turned into a real argument.

One of them, a man who reads a lot of constitutional history, insisted that the early Douglass was right and the later Douglass had it wrong. His point was that the original text contains explicit pro-slavery clauses — the three-fifths compromise, the fugitive slave clause, the twenty-year protection of the slave trade — and you can't just wave those away by talking about the document's spirit. A clause that hands escaped people back to their owners is pro-slavery whether or not it was a concession, he said, and the fact that Douglass later came to praise the Constitution as a great American document was really just him reacting to the postwar amendments, which made it a different document anyway. The other, a woman who has read a fair amount of Douglass himself, pushed back and said the later Douglass was right, with a couple of arguments. First, those pro-slavery clauses were concessions made to get the slave states to join the union; the overall architecture of the document — the Bill of Rights, the framework for expanding rights — was oriented toward liberty, and Douglass, who knew slavery better than anyone alive, eventually saw that. Second, the original document contained the means of its own correction: the amendment process was built in, and that is precisely what made abolition possible, so in a real sense the document was anti-slavery from the start because it provided the mechanism by which slavery could be ended. The first friend replied that the amendment process was a general feature that could have been used to change anything, so the fact that it happened to be used against slavery does not make the original document anti-slavery; by that logic any document with an amendment clause is anti-something for whatever it later gets amended against. The woman answered that spirit and trajectory are real features of a document, not mere sentiment, and that a constitution is a project with a direction, and read in context that direction was toward liberty; she added that the document's silences mattered, because it never gave the federal government power to establish or maintain slavery, and that absence of protection was itself a kind of protection against it. The first friend replied that silences and trajectory are doing all the work on her side and are unfalsifiable, because there is no specific clause in the original text that makes it anti-slavery, whereas on his side every explicit clause about slavery is pro-slavery.

I honestly could not referee it, and I would like someone to go through their exchange and tell me who has the stronger point at each step — not just who wins overall. In particular I would like to know what each of them gets fundamentally right, what each gets wrong, and whether there is anything both of them are missing that a careful reader of the actual constitutional text would have noticed.
