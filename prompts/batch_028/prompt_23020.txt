I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm currently working on a piece about the afterlife of Wittgenstein's rule-following discussion — specifically, Saul Kripke's reading of it, the one that has given rise to the label "Kripkenstein." To get the technical points right before I try to make them accessible to a general readership, I've been going back over a particular exchange I overheard in the margins of a philosophy conference last spring, and I'd be grateful for a second opinion on who's right about what. Two of the participants, a senior professor and a much younger postdoc, got into it over coffee after a talk on the Philosophical Investigations, and it went roughly like this.

The postdoc opened by arguing that the community response is the most promising reply to the skeptic's paradox: when a speaker produces "2+68=70" and the community accepts it, that acceptance is what fixes the rule, and Kripke's regress argument against the community reply is overblown, because at some point we simply stop the regress and appeal to the practice. The professor pushed back, insisting that Kripke is right that the community reply fails, because agreement on any finite set of past answers never determines a rule — for any pattern of past agreements there is a "quus-like" rule that fits them all — so the community reply merely relocates the paradox one level up, and the only honest conclusion is the "satisficing" one: there is no fact of the matter, and we get by with agreements that are sufficient for our purposes. The postdoc objected that "satisficing" is just a dignified way of giving up, because if there is no fact of the matter, then "2+2=4" is no truer than "2+2=7," which is plainly absurd. The professor replied that Kripke does not mean that; on the "satisficing" view, "right" and "wrong" are not grounded in a fact of the matter about the rule but in the community's practice, so "2+2=4" is "right" in the sense that the community accepts it, not in the sense that some fact of the matter makes it so. The postdoc then said that this is exactly why Kripke has misread Wittgenstein: the actual solution in the Investigations is the meaning-is-use reading, on which the rule is fixed by the practice itself, so there is no paradox because we never need a "fact of the matter" about the rule in the first place. The professor conceded the point had force but said Kripke's entire project is to show that the meaning-is-use reading is not as straightforward as it looks, because "use" is still a practice and the practice is what the community determines, which brings us right back to the community reply and its regress.

Could you go through that exchange and say who's right at each step, rather than just who wins overall? I'm particularly curious about three things: first, whether the postdoc's objection that "satisficing" collapses into the claim that 2+2=4 is no truer than 2+2=7 is a fair reading of Kripke or a misreading; second, whether the professor's regress argument against the community reply is as decisive as she makes it, or whether the postdoc's "we just stop and appeal to the practice" is a legitimate way to defuse the regress; and third, whether the postdoc is right to think Kripke has misread Wittgenstein, or whether the professor is right that Kripke is exposing a genuine gap in the meaning-is-use solution. And if there is a point both of them are missing — perhaps something about the distinction between the skeptic's paradox and the private language argument, or about the "disposition" reply — I'd be glad to have it flagged.
