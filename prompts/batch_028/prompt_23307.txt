I'm in a graduate seminar on Madhyamaka this term, and two of my classmates got into a long argument over Nāgārjuna's tetralemma that neither of them would drop, so I've been keeping score and can't decide who has the better of it. The tetralemma, as I understand it from the course reading of his root treatise, is the move of taking a claim — say, the question of how things come into being — and running it through four horns: that they arise from themselves, from something other than themselves, from both, or from neither — and then rejecting all four.

Ines started by saying this is self-undermining. If Nāgārjuna rejects the fourth horn, "neither," then he can't consistently say "it is none of the first three," because that is itself a positive assertion that the four options are jointly exhaustive and that he has picked the leftover. And when the text goes on to say that emptiness itself is empty — that emptiness is not a thing with its own nature — Ines argued that the doctrine has thereby voided its own content: if there is no stable emptiness to assert, the tetralemma either secretly collapses into a fifth, unspoken nihilistic position, or it is simply incoherent. Ravi answered that Ines is misreading the tetralemma as a truth-functional disjunction over four mutually exclusive options exactly one of which must be true. The four horns, he said, are all "views" that presuppose some inherent nature, and rejecting all four is not choosing a fifth option but dissolving the presupposition that there must be one. The "emptiness of emptiness" is what keeps emptiness from hardening into a new inherent nature — it is a corrective, not a thesis — so there is no self-refutation, because a corrective is not something that can be true or false. Ines replied that this just concedes the point: if emptiness has no truth-conditions, you cannot tell the Madhyamaka position apart from mere skepticism or from "anything goes," and you have stopped answering whether the argument is valid in order to say the point was never validity. Ravi said that the point really is not validity but the uprooting of clinging to views, including the view of emptiness, and that Ines is asking the wrong question of a tool that was never meant to be a syllogism.

I have been sitting on the fence because each of them has a moment where they seem clearly right and a moment where they seem to be talking past each other. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right at each step — not just who wins overall — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the tetralemma is actually doing?
