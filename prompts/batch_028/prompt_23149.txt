I'm in a history-of-economic-thought seminar and we've just started on Hume's early political economy, and I've been stuck on his essay "Of Interest" (from the 1752 Political Discourses) because it seems to pull in the opposite direction from what I was taught in my intermediate macro course. Hume spends a lot of time arguing that the lowness of interest is not caused by a plentiful stock of gold and silver: a money supply that is merely fixed in size, no matter how large, only raises the price of labour and commodities and has no grip on the interest rate. Instead he locates the rate in real conditions — high interest, he says, comes from a great demand for borrowing, little wealth available to supply it, and large profits from commerce, and these are actually symptoms of a *less* advanced economy, while low interest tracks the growth of industry and trade. In other words, low interest is a sign of a flourishing nation, not an engine of it.

What I can't reconcile is how this squares with the two standard accounts I was given. On the one hand there's the loanable-funds story (which I associate with Locke's 1691 "Some Considerations of the Consequences of the Lowering of Interest"), where the interest rate is the price that equilibrates the supply of savers and the demand of borrowers — a real rate of return on capital. On the other hand, my macro text treats the nominal rate as something the money supply and liquidity conditions do push around, with the Fisher relation tying the nominal rate to the real rate plus expected inflation. Could you compare Hume's account with these two? In particular: in what sense is Hume closer to the loanable-funds / real theory, where does he genuinely diverge from it, and does his insistence that the money stock is irrelevant to interest survive contact with the quantity-theory and liquidity-preference views — or is he quietly assuming something (a fixed velocity, neutral money, a purely long-run perspective) that the monetary accounts are built to relax? I'd also like a verdict on which of the three is the more defensible as a positive theory of the nominal interest rate, and where Hume's argument has a real flaw.
