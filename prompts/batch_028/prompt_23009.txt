I'm working through Quine's argument for the indeterminacy of translation, as developed in his book Word and Object, and I've been struggling to pin down exactly what the argument establishes. In particular, I keep running into what seems to be a conflation of two distinct claims: the weaker claim that the total body of behavioral evidence is consistent with multiple, mutually incompatible translation manuals, so that the evidence underdetermines the correct translation; and the stronger claim that there is no fact of the matter about what the native speaker's words refer to, such that the translation is indeterminate in a metaphysical or ontological sense rather than merely epistemically underdetermined. I'd be grateful for a careful explanation of this distinction.

Specifically, I'd like you to explain how Quine's argument — via the gavagai thought experiment and the broader thesis that linguistic meaning is exhausted by a speaker's behavioral dispositions — is supposed to establish the stronger claim rather than merely the weaker one. And I'd also like to understand the standard objection that Quine's argument is either question-begging or only relative to a particular theory of meaning (namely, the theory that meaning is a function of observable behavior), and how Quine might respond to that objection. In other words, I want to understand whether the indeterminacy of translation is a claim about the limits of our evidence, or a claim about the nature of reference itself, and what the stakes are between those two readings.
