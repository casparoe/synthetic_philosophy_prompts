I'm working on a piece for a magazine that explores why Bayesian epistemology stumbles when it meets the history of science, and I want to get the details right on what is often called the "problem of old evidence." For the readers who don't know it: the puzzle arises when the evidence is already known to the agent before any theory that explains it is ever proposed. The standard textbook case is the perihelion of Mercury, which was well documented long before Einstein, yet is routinely treated as a great confirmation of general relativity. The naive Bayesian calculus, which measures confirmation by a shift in conditional probability against a fixed prior, seems to make such old evidence confirmatorily inert, and a large literature has tried to patch this up. For a sidebar, I want to sort the main proposed fixes into a hierarchy of how well they actually resolve the problem, rather than simply how much they get cited.

Could you rank the following five responses to the old-evidence problem, from the one you judge best able to restore the confirmatory force of old evidence to the weakest? (1) The "surprise" response, which holds that old evidence confirms a theory only if it is surprising or unexpected given that theory, for instance because it runs counter to what rival theories predict. (2) The "likelihoodist" response, which sets the prior probability of the theory aside and instead compares how well the theory and its rivals predict the evidence. (3) The "diachronic" response, which tracks how an agent's credences change over time, for example by modeling the moment a theory is first proposed, or by considering a counterfactual agent who did not already know the evidence. (4) The "explanatory" response, which holds that confirmation in these cases is essentially a matter of explanation and that Bayesian confirmation theory is simply the wrong tool for the job. (5) The "deflationary" response, which accepts that Bayesianism cannot handle old evidence and concedes that confirmation in such cases must be modeled by something other than conditional probability.

For each item in your ranking, give a one- or two-sentence justification, and close with a single line stating which item you believe most working philosophers of science actually treat as the leading response. Format your answer as a JSON object with a field "ranking" (an array of the five numbered items in order, strongest to weakest) and a field "justifications" (an array of the corresponding justifications, in the same order).
