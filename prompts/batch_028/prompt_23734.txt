I am a senior TA for a freshman philosophy elective at my school, and I am grading take-home exams on the extended mind thesis. Please grade the student's answer below as the instructor would, strictly against the rubric I have provided.

Exam question: Clark and Chalmers (1998) argue for the extended mind thesis using a parity argument built around two characters, Inga and Otto, who store the same piece of information in different ways. (i) State the parity principle. (ii) Explain the roles of Inga and Otto in the argument. (iii) Explain the coupling-constitution objection that Adams and Aizawa raise in The Bounds of Cognition (2008). (iv) In your view, does the objection succeed? Answer in 250 to 350 words.

Student's answer:
Clark and Chalmers' parity principle says that we should not draw the boundary of the mind at the skull merely because of where things physically are: if an external process plays a functional role for an agent that is exactly equivalent to a role played by an internal process, and the internal process counts as part of the mind, then the external one should count as part of the mind as well. Their famous example contrasts Inga, who remembers a fact from memory, with Otto, who keeps the same fact in a notebook he consults immediately and automatically trusts. Because the notebook plays the same functional role as Inga's memory, on the parity principle Otto's mind extends into the notebook.

Adams and Aizawa object in their book The Bounds of Cognition by pointing out the difference between coupling and constitution. Their worry is that the parity principle is too permissive: if a notebook is part of the mind because it is coupled to the agent, then so is everything else the agent is causally connected to, such as the walls of the room, and the mind becomes absurdly large. They conclude, therefore, that coupling is not constitution, and that cognition is brainbound.

I think this objection fails. Otto's notebook is reliable, immediately available, and automatically trusted, so it is in the right relation to him to be part of his mind, and in his case coupling does constitute. The parity principle is safe, and the extended mind thesis is true.
