The question was: In Marx's analysis of exploitation in Capital, what is the distinction between "labor" (Arbeit) and "labor-power" (Arbeitskraft), and why does it matter for his theory of surplus value? How does this relate to the concept of alienation in the 1844 Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts?

The student wrote:

Marx draws a sharp line between labor (Arbeit) and labor-power (Arbeitskraft). Labor is the actual activity, the expenditure of human energy in the course of production. Labor-power, by contrast, is the worker's capacity to labor — the bundle of physical and mental abilities the worker possesses and can put up for sale. Under capitalism, what the worker actually sells to the capitalist is not labor but labor-power, and the wage is the value of that labor-power, which is fixed by the socially necessary labor-time required to produce it, i.e. roughly the value of the worker's means of subsistence.

Once the capitalist has bought the labor-power, they set it to work, and this is where exploitation enters. Over the course of a working day the worker creates more value than the value of their labor-power. Part of the day — the "necessary labor" — merely reproduces the value of the labor-power the capitalist has paid for, and the rest — the "surplus labor" — creates value that the capitalist keeps as surplus value. So in effect the capitalist exploits the worker by paying them less than the value of the labor they actually perform: the worker might labor eight hours but is really only compensated for four, and the other four hours are pure gain for the capitalist.

This is a different but related idea from the alienation Marx describes in the 1844 Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts, where the worker is alienated in four respects: from the product of their labor, from the activity of production itself, from their species-being (Gattungswesen), and from other human beings. Alienation is the more philosophical, humanist notion, and exploitation / surplus value is the more rigorous economic analysis that Marx develops in Capital. But they are really the same phenomenon seen from two sides — the philosophical and the economic — so the surplus value theory in Capital is essentially the rigorous restatement of the alienation analysis of 1844, both capturing the fact that the worker's own activity comes to operate against them.
