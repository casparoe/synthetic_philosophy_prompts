I'm not a philosopher by training, but I've been reading Kierkegaard on my own for a while now, mostly through his short essays, after a friend mentioned how prescient his complaints about the press felt when applied to the way we all consume news these days. A passage from a little work of his called The Single Individual has been stuck in my head for weeks, and I can't quite get it to click. He writes:

"There is a view of life which conceives that where the crowd is, there also is the truth, and that in truth itself there is need of having the crowd on its side. There is another view of life which conceives that wherever there is a crowd there is untruth, so that (to consider for a moment the extreme case), even if every individual, each for himself in private, were to be in possession of the truth, yet in case they were all to get together in a crowd—a crowd to which any sort of decisive significance is attributed, a voting, noisy, audible crowd—untruth would at once be in evidence."

I get the broad point — that truth seems to belong to the individual rather than to a mass of people — but it's the "extreme case" that trips me up, and I can't tell which of two readings he intends. On one reading, the people who assemble into a crowd each stop being the passionate, concrete person who actually relates to the truth; by merging into the crowd they become, as he puts it in the same work, mere specimens, so the truth is lost because the real truth-holders have effectively ceased to exist as such. On the other reading, the crowd is just an abstraction that has no real essence at all, so attributing truth to it is a kind of category mistake, and the individuals' private possession of the truth is untouched — it's only the crowd-as-such that can't be true. Could you help me figure out which reading the passage actually supports, or whether he wants both, and what in the wording points that way?

And I'd also like to understand how this connects to his separate criticism of the press, which I've read as the idea that the press manufactures the public out of people who are never actually concretely present together. Is the press, on this view, the thing that turns real individuals into the kind of "unreal" crowd-members whose voting produces untruth, or is the crowd-is-untruth point something that holds even without any press involved? I'd be grateful for a careful, line-by-line-ish reading of the passage rather than a general summary of what Kierkegaard thought about crowds.
