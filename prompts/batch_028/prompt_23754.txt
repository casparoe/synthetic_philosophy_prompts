At dinner, my two friends got into an argument about a sentence one of their coworkers had said: "If the train had left on time, we would have made it to the meeting."

My friend Dana said this is a claim about what actually happened, and it's true because the train didn't leave on time, so the "if" part never applied. She said the sentence is just a tense-shifted version of "If the train leaves on time, we will make it to the meeting," and that the two are the same kind of claim.

My other friend Ravi said that's wrong. He said the two are fundamentally different. "If the train leaves on time, we will make it to the meeting" is a prediction about the future, and you can say it while genuinely uncertain about whether the train will leave on time. "If the train had left on time, we would have made it to the meeting" is a judgment about a make-believe situation, and you only say it when you know for sure that the train didn't leave on time.

Dana conceded that the two feel different, but said the difference is just conversational style, not a difference in what the sentences are true or false. She said both are true, and both are about the same connection between the train leaving on time and making the meeting.

Ravi said the difference is substantive. He said: "If it rains tomorrow, the picnic will be canceled" is fine to say even though you don't know whether it will rain, because the rain is possible. But it's weird to say "If the moon is made of cheese, then..." when you know the moon isn't made of cheese. So the "if it rains" kind of sentence is only assertible when the antecedent is possible. The "if the train had left on time" kind of sentence is the opposite — you only say it when you know the antecedent is false. That, he said, is a real difference in what the sentences are doing, not just a difference in style.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall?
