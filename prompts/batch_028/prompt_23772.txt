Compare the revealed-preference account of utility — on which an agent's utility function is simply whatever is consistent with their observed choices, so that "utility" adds nothing to a convenient summary of behavior — with the view that utility is a genuine, mind-internal quantity that choices merely reveal or fail to reveal. Given that any observed choice pattern can be made consistent with some preference relation, though not always with a single, well-behaved utility function, what, if anything, can behavior actually tell us about an agent's "real" utility? And how does that bear on the harder case of recovering a utility function from the behavior of a non-human decision-maker, where we have no independent access to the agent's motivations?
