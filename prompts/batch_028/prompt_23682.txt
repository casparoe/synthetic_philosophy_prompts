In preparing my lecture notes on peer disagreement, I have come to think that conciliationism faces a decisive objection, and I would value your help in seeing whether I am missing something. The Fundamental Principle — that a rational agent ought to be in the doxastic state that her evidence supports — strikes me as a bedrock norm of epistemology. Now take a paradigm case of peer disagreement: I and my epistemic peer have access to the same first-order evidence, yet we disagree about some proposition. Conciliationism instructs me to move to a compromise credence, roughly the midpoint between our two. But that compromise credence is not the one the first-order evidence supports; the first-order evidence supports my original, pre-disagreement credence. It therefore looks as though conciliationism requires me to settle on a doxastic state that my evidence does not support, in flat contradiction of the Fundamental Principle. Given how basic the Fundamental Principle is, I am at a loss to see how a conciliationist can defend her view against this charge of incoherence. Where, if anywhere, does the conciliationist response go?
