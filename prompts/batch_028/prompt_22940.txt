Write an essay on whether the Condorcet jury theorem offers genuine epistemic support for democratic decision-making, or only an idealized one that real-world conditions defeat. In its standard form, the theorem says that if each voter independently has a better-than-chance probability of voting correctly, then the majority's verdict becomes more reliable as the electorate grows, approaching certainty in the limit. The independence assumption is doing a great deal of work, and it is plausibly false of real electorates, because voters draw on overlapping information sources, absorb the same narratives, and learn from one another, so their errors tend to be positively correlated. One position holds that this correlation is fatal: once errors are correlated, the majority's accuracy is bounded by the quality of the shared information rather than approaching certainty, and in some configurations a larger electorate is actually less reliable than a smaller one, so the theorem has no real purchase on politics. The opposing position holds that the theorem's core insight survives — that aggregating judgments is more reliable than relying on any single one — even under moderate correlation, so long as each voter retains some independent element of judgment. Weigh these two positions, attending in particular to whether the objection defeats only the theorem's idealized claim that the majority becomes infallible with size or its weaker claim that the majority outperforms a single voter, and reach a defended conclusion about which position is correct.
