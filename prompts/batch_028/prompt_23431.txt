I cover science and ideas, and this term I am grading the take-home essays for a short seminar I teach on the early modern rationalists. Below is a question I set, followed by one student's answer. I would like you to grade the answer out of 10 and provide specific comments justifying the grade, taking each substantive claim in turn and saying whether it is accurate, and if not, precisely what goes wrong.

Question: In the Confessio philosophi, Leibniz identifies the happiness of the mind with the contemplation of God. Explain (a) what work this identification does in his account of the nature of moral evil, and (b) how his account is supposed to answer the problem of the "author of sin" — the charge that God, as creator and preserver of all things, is thereby responsible for the sins of creatures. (10 points)

Student's answer: Leibniz's identification of the happiness of the mind with the contemplation of God makes his moral theory thoroughly eudaimonist: the true good is one's own flourishing, and for a rational being that flourishing just is the intellectual vision of God. Moral evil is then a privation rather than a positive reality — a disorder in which the will, failing to attend to the true good, turns toward a lesser apparent good. This does the work for the author-of-sin problem: since evil is only a lack, and God creates only positive realities, God cannot be the author of sin; the privation is produced by the creature's own will, so moral evil is imputed to the sinner rather than to the creator. Responsibility is preserved because the will acts spontaneously, from the agent's own inclination, and the sinner could have done otherwise, since freedom for Leibniz is precisely the power to do otherwise. Without the identification, the whole theodicy would wobble: if the contemplation of God were not the highest good, the order of the world could not be praised as one of love and admiration.
