I retired from a career in statistical physics about two years ago, and since then I have been working my way, slowly and with some embarrassment, into the literature on Bayesian epistemology, partly out of old habits and partly out of a newly acquired taste for philosophy. One assumption keeps tripping me up: the common prior assumption, as it figures in Aumann's result to the effect that two agents who share a prior over the states of the world cannot rationally maintain disagreement about probabilities once it becomes common knowledge what each of them believes. I think I follow the rough statement of the theorem, but I confess I do not understand what the common prior is actually doing in the argument, or what the epistemic landscape looks like when the assumption is dropped. My suspicion is that it has something to do with the fact that, when the prior is common, each agent's posterior is a function of the evidence alone, so that revealing one's posterior effectively reveals one's evidence; but I would like to see that intuition pinned down in a worked example rather than in abstract claims.

Could you give me an example of a conceptual issue that illuminates this? I would like you to construct a small scenario — a coin, a card, a die, anything with a state space of three elements or fewer — in which two agents with different information but a common prior do reach agreement, and then to modify the scenario minimally, say by giving the agents different priors, and to show that they can now rationally maintain their disagreement indefinitely. In both versions, please spell out precisely which step of the reasoning the common prior is licensing: which inference goes through in the first case and breaks down in the second.

Please keep the example self-contained and checkable, since I should be able to verify the arithmetic myself: use simple fractions rather than decimals, and let the two versions differ only in the priors, so that the contrast is visible. And please do not merely restate the theorem or paraphrase a proof sketch — I want to see the mechanism at work.
