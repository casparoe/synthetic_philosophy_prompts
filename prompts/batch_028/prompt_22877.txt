I'm in a graduate seminar on the logic of time and modality, and this week we've been working through the problem of future contingents — whether a sentence about an event that hasn't happened yet, like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow," is already either true or false right now, and what that has to do with whether the future is genuinely open. After the lecture, two of my classmates, Nora and Sam, got into a long argument about Aristotle's sea-battle argument that I couldn't fully follow, and I'd like help sorting out who has the better case. I'm not taking sides; I just want the argument untangled.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Nora started by saying the whole argument hinges on a step Aristotle never justifies: if it's already true, right now, that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the battle seems fixed — it can't fail to happen, because if it didn't, the sentence would have been false all along, and a sentence can't flip from true to false just because the world changed its mind. So, she said, once you grant that the sentence is already either true or already false, you've granted that the future is settled, and that's just determinism in disguise. (2) Sam replied that this step quietly changes the subject: "it's true now that there will be a sea battle" is a claim about when the sentence is true, not a claim about whether the battle is necessary, and those are different notions. The sentence can be true now and still be such that, in a scenario where the fleets never meet, it would have been false. Truth at a time isn't necessity, he said, so you can hold that the sentence is already true or false and still hold that the future is open; Aristotle is smuggling "it will happen" into "it must happen." (3) Nora answered that this only works if you're allowed to call the sentence true now without anything in the world making it so. She's a truthmaker kind of person: a proposition is true only if there's a fact corresponding to it, and the fact that would make "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" true is the future battle itself — but that fact doesn't exist yet. So either the fact exists in advance, in which case the future is already settled and you're a determinist whether you like it or not, or the fact doesn't exist yet, in which case there's nothing to make the sentence true now, so it isn't true now, and you've quietly denied bivalence. Her "open future plus bivalence" option, she said, has no room left. (4) Sam said she'd set up the dilemma too fast, because she's assuming a truthmaker has to be a fully existing present fact, when a tensed fact can be partly future: "there will be a sea battle" is a fact about the present in the sense that it's now the case that the battle is coming, even though the battle itself is later. So the truthmaker isn't a present object sitting in the world but a present-tensed relation to a future event, and even if he granted her truthmaker principle, a fact can be the case without being necessary, so he still gets to keep bivalence and an open future. (5) Nora said she suspected he was just stipulating his way out: "a present-tensed relation to a future event" sounds like a fact, but if the future is genuinely open — if it's still live that the fleets might not meet — she doesn't see what fixes the truth value right now. Something has to decide whether the sentence is true or false, and if nothing has decided it yet, then it isn't decided, which is just the "neither true nor false" option wearing a bivalential costume. So, she said, the only stable options are that the future is settled (determinism, and bivalence works) or that the future is open (and then the sentence is now neither true nor false). (6) Sam ended by turning the charge around: he said "something has to decide" begs the question, because she's never said what a "decision" is or why a sentence's truth has to be fixed by some present event rather than by the course of things. Maybe the truth of a future-tensed sentence just tracks how things turn out, with no earlier decider to be found; and if you demand a present decider, he said, you're building determinism into your theory of truth rather than discovering it in Aristotle.

Could you go through this move by move? For each numbered move, say whether the person made a sound point, an unsound one, or something in between, and why. Then give an overall verdict on who's more right, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing — including whether the dispute turns on a real philosophical difference or on a shared confusion.
