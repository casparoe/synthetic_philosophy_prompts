A friend and I got into a long argument last night after we both read (separately) about Elizabeth Anscombe's essay on Truman and the atomic bomb, and I'm still not sure who had the better case. We both agreed on the background: Truman publicly justified dropping the bombs as necessary to end the war, and a lot of historians now think that necessity claim is shaky. The fight was over the moral logic. I've numbered each person's move so it's easier to follow.

(1) My friend Dana said Anscombe's core claim is that choosing to kill innocent people as a means to your own ends is always wrong, full stop, and that the bombs are a clean case of exactly that: you kill the civilians in order to pressure the Japanese government into surrendering, so the deaths are the mechanism, not an accident. (2) I (Marcus) pushed back with a thought experiment I'd heard somewhere: a wartime ferry loaded with heavy water, a key ingredient for an enemy atomic bomb program, is sailing toward the coast, and you can sink it by killing everyone on board, which stops the whole program. I said if killing them is permitted there, then "killing innocents as a means is always wrong" is false, and the bomb case has to be decided by consequences after all. (3) Dana replied that the ferry case doesn't do what I think it does: you're attacking a military target, and the deaths are something you foresee with certainty but don't choose as your means, whereas in Hiroshima the deaths themselves are what's supposed to do the persuading, so the two cases aren't the same kind of thing. (4) I said that's exactly the problem, because the bomb "works" in the same way the sinking works: the terror of what just happened is what makes the government act, so the deaths are the mechanism in both cases, and her distinction is just a way for the bomber to feel better. (5) She answered that the distinction is what lets us tell a careful attack on a military target that happens to kill some bystanders apart from deliberately lining a family up in the crosshairs, and if I throw that out I don't actually have a moral position, I just have "whatever gets the job done is fine." (6) I said that's not what I was saying, I was saying we should be honest that the line she's drawing is doing all the work and asking whether it's principled or just comfortable.

Can you go through this the way a careful referee would: for each numbered move, say who's right and who's wrong about that specific point, and then tell me what, if anything, both of us are missing. I'm not asking you to take a side from the start, I just want to know which of us actually had the better argument and where each of us slipped.
