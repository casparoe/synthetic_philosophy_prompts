I'm TAing for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and have to grade a short-answer question on Lewis's 1976 triviality result for indicative conditionals. I come from a software engineering background with no formal training in philosophy, so I'd like a careful, independent assessment. Below is the exam question and one student's answer. Please assign a numeric grade out of 10 and give me the specific comments that justify it: a point-by-point account of what is correct, what is incorrect or does not follow, and what is missing, with each comment tied to the relevant part of the answer.

Exam question (10 points): In a short answer of about 250 words: (a) state the thesis that Lewis's 1976 triviality result targets and state the result; (b) give the main steps of the argument; (c) name one response to the result and say whether it avoids it or merely shifts the problem.

Student's answer:

Lewis's 1976 paper "Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities" targets the Stalnaker thesis, which says that for indicative conditionals, P(A > B) = P(B|A) whenever P(A) > 0. The result is that no nontrivial probability function satisfies this thesis: if P does, then every proposition has probability 0 or 1. The proof assumes the thesis holds for every conditional with a positive-probability antecedent, including after conditioning, and that P is countably additive.

The argument has three steps. First, a dynamic clause: for P(A) > 0, P(A > B | A) = P(B | A), since by the thesis the probability of the conditional given A is the probability of the consequent given A and A. Second, expand P(A > B) over the partition {A, ¬A} by the law of total probability: P(B|A) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(A > B | ¬A)P(¬A), so P(A > B | ¬A) = P(B|A)·P(A)/P(¬A). Third, apply the thesis to the conditional probability given ¬A: P(A > B | ¬A) = P(B | A ∧ ¬A). Since A ∧ ¬A is impossible, the conditional probability given an impossible event is taken to be 0, so P(A > B | ¬A) = 0. From the previous step, P(B|A)·P(A)/P(¬A) = 0; since P(A) > 0 and P(¬A) > 0, P(B|A) = 0 for every B. But taking B = A, P(A|A) = 1 ≠ 0, a contradiction. Hence P(¬A) = 0, i.e., P(A) = 1; since A was arbitrary, P is trivial.

One response, Lewis's own, is to reject the Stalnaker thesis and instead compute the probability of a conditional from a possible-worlds semantics, taking P(A > B) to be the measure of the worlds at which the conditional is true. This avoids the result by denying its premise, though it leaves open the question of which measure to use.
