I've been reading about AI alignment on my own for a while now, mostly through online essays and forum posts, and I keep coming back to this "coherent extrapolated volition" idea — the one where instead of asking what we want right now, you ask what we would want if we thought more, better, and longer about it, and then you make that answer "coherent." I was talking to a friend about it last week and they said I was overthinking it, but I definately think the "coherent" part is doing way more work than people admit, and I'd like you to argue that position for me.

Specifically: I think "coherence" isn't actually one thing. It could mean making your preferences transitive, it could mean finding a stable fixed point under some idealized deliberation process, it could mean resolving conflicts by some priority rule, and so on — and which fix-up you pick changes the answer you get. So CEV isn't really one theory but a family of theories, and the choice among them is itself a substantive moral question that CEV was supposed to get you past. Can you make that argument as well as you can, and then tell me where the argument is weakest?
