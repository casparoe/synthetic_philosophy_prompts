I'm writing a short paper on Spinoza's defense of democracy and I'd like a second pair of eyes on the interpretive move at the center of it. Here's the draft.

The standard story is that Spinoza is an early liberal: in the Theological-Political Treatise he argues that democracy is the best form of government precisely because it best preserves the individual's natural liberty, since in a democracy the sovereign power is never really transferred away from the people the way it is under a monarch or an aristocracy. On that reading, the retention of liberty is the point, and it's a point about rights.

I want to argue the opposite: Spinoza's case for democracy is not a liberty-based case at all but a stability-based one. The reason he calls democracy the most natural regime is that it's the one that's easiest to set up and hardest to overthrow, because the least amount of power has been handed over to a separate sovereign. The fewer powers you transfer, the less the governed have to lose and the less motive they have to rebel. So the fact that citizens keep their "natural liberty" is not the reason democracy is good; it's the mechanism by which democracy stays stable. The liberty language is doing instrumental work in a consequentialist argument about which regimes survive, not expressing a commitment to liberty as an end in itself.

The same goes for his famous defense of freedom of thought. He insists that no one can really give up the right to judge for himself, and that a state which tries to police people's opinions will only make things worse. But I read this as a claim about the limits of effective power, not about an inviolable right of conscience. Thought is simply not something a sovereign can control, and the attempt to control it backfires by breeding resentment. So toleration is recommended because it's the policy that actually works, not because conscience has a moral claim the state must respect. Spinoza, on this reading, is not a proto-liberal but a cold calculator about which institutions hold together.

The trouble with my own reading is that some of the passages look like they're making a rights claim after all. When he says the right to judge is one thing a person cannot transfer, that sounds like it's locating a boundary on the state that isn't just a matter of effectiveness. If the right to judge were only a fact about what no government can do, the natural word for it would be a limit on power, not a right. So I'm not sure my consequentialist reading can absorb those passages without doing some awkward work.

Can you comment on whether the central move -- that the "most natural" argument and the freedom-of-thought argument are both really stability/effectiveness arguments, and that the liberty language is just instrumental -- holds up? In particular: does the "right to judge" material force a hybrid reading where there's a genuine moral-rights element, or can it be absorbed into the consequentialist frame? And is there anything in the unfinished Political Treatise that bears on whether he changed his mind between the two works?
