I've been working through the Allais paradox on my own, and I've got the standard setup: in the first pair, choose between a sure $1 million and a gamble that pays $1 million with 89 percent chance, $5 million with 10 percent, and nothing with 1 percent; in the second pair, choose between $1 million with 11 percent chance (and nothing with 89 percent) versus $5 million with 10 percent chance (and nothing with 90 percent). Most people take the sure million in the first pair and the $5 million gamble in the second, and the usual verdict is that this is incoherent by the independence axiom. I'd like you to steelman the case that this is genuinely irrational, and I don't think the standard "it's just inconsistent" answer does that justice. Argue, as forcefully as you can, for the position that this choice pattern is truly irrational, and in doing so first pin down exactly which constraint it breaks — I'm fairly sure it's independence rather than transitivity or dominance, and I'd like that distinction made explicit — and then give a principled reason why a rational agent's preferences really ought to satisfy that constraint, rather than treating it as a mere mathematical convenience.
