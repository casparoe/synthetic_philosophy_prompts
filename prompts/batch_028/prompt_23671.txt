I'm a retired physicist (worked on plasma physics, long ago) and I've onnly recently started reading philosophy, so bear with me. A few of us were having dinner last week and two of my friends, Greg and Priya, got into a heated argument about whether the laws of physics are "real" or just "descriptions," and I honestly couldn't tell who was making sense. Greg is the one who thinks the laws are real things — he kept saying gravity isn't just a pattern we've noticed, it's a genuine feature of the world that does the work of making apples fall and planets orbit, and that if the laws were just descriptions there'd be nothing "out there" enforcing the regularity, which he found absurd. Priya pushed back and said that's just a mysterious extra entity; the only real things are the particular facts — the mosaic — and the "laws" are just the most compact way of summarizing that mosaic, nothing over and above it.

Then it got more technical, which is where I lost the thread a bit. Greg said that if the laws are just entailed by the mosaic, they're redundant — why bother positing them — and that the laws are obviously contingent (gravity could have been repulsive, the electron could have had a different charge), whereas if they're fixed by the facts then, given the actual facts, they couldn't have been otherwise, which makes them sound like logical truths. Priya answered that they're not redundant but a kind of comprassion, like a video codec isn't redundant even though it's entailed by the raw frames, and that the laws are contingent in the right sense because a different mosaic would give different laws. Greg then said the compression metaphor misses the point: a codec doesn't make the video, it just describes it, and more importantly a mere description of the mosaic can't explain anything, because we use laws to explain why things happen, and a restatement of the facts is no explanation. Priya said the best system explains by showing that the particular facts are instances of a compact pattern, and that this is exactly David Lewis's best system account. Greg's last point was about counterfactuals: laws are supposed to tell us what would happen in non-actual situations (drop the apple on the moon, change the electron's charge), and a description of the actual mosaic can't do that. Priya said Lewis's whole analysis of counterfactuals is built on the best system, so the counterfactuals are grounded in the same compact structure.

I have no idea who's right, and I'd love a referee. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific point — not just who wins overall — and also point out what, if anything, both of them are missing?
