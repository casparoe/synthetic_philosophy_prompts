At a philosophy seminar, two of my classmates argued about Ian Hacking's "looping" idea and what it does to psychiatric classification. The first said: if psychiatric categories are interactive kinds — if labeling someone hyperactive, or a child-abuse victim, changes how that person and their family behave, which in turn reshapes the category — then those categories can't be objective scientific kinds. They're social projections that rewrite the very people they're meant to describe, so a psychiatric "disorder" is no more a real kind than a fashion trend is.

The second replied: that overstates the looping effect. It shows the categories are historically unstable and partly self-fulfilling, but it doesn't show they're arbitrary or purely social. A category can be interactive and still track a genuine difference in the world, including a biological one. The fact that people change once they hear a diagnosis doesn't mean there was no difference to start with; it just means the classification and the people co-evolve.

The first pushed back: that concedes too much. If the boundary of a category keeps shifting in response to who gets classified, then the "real difference" it's supposed to track can't be pinned down independently of the classification, so appeal to an underlying stable kind is doing no work. The second answered that this just smuggles in a natural-kind requirement: even stable natural kinds are partly constituted by our best theories of them, so demanding that psychiatric categories be classification-independent begs the question against them.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — say who is right about each specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
