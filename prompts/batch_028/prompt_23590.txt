So I was sitting at this coffee shop the other day and two people at the next table got into a real argument about whether what you believe actually changes what you see, and I've been thinking about it all week. One of them, a guy I'll call Sam, said he's definately convinced your thoughts literally change your perception — like, when he learned to drive the road looked totally different than it does now, he actually takes in more of the periphery, not just that he notices more. The other one, a girl named Alex, said no, your eyes and visual system do the same thing regardless of what you think; what changed is what you pay attention to and what you make of it, and that's a judgment, not a perception.

Sam pushed back with this thing he'd read about where a really blurry letter is way easier to make out if it's sitting inside a real word than on its own, and said that's the context changing the actual seeing. Alex said that's exactly the kind of thing people point to but it's really just your brain guessing the letter from the surrounding words and then it feels like you saw it — the light hitting your retina didn't get any clearer, you're just confusing "I now think it's an E" with "I now see an E more clearly." Then Sam said so by her logic a wine expert and a regular person taste the exact same thing and the only difference is the labels, which felt wrong to him, and Alex basically said yes, that's the point, the expert just has more knowledge to apply to the same sensory input, so it's knowledge that got better, not vision.

I didn't jump in, I just listened, and I honeslty can't tell who's right about what. Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who's right about which specific claim, and what, if anything, the two of them are both missing? I don't want a "both sides have a point" shrug if one of them is actually just wrong about part of it.
