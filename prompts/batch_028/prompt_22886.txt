I just got back from a long argument with a friend over Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against ultimate moral responsibility — the argument that no one can be ultimately responsible for what they do, because to be responsible for what one does one would have to be responsible for who one is, and one's character is shaped by factors one did not choose. We did not resolve it, but I have decided that the eulogy I am giving for my mother, a high school English teacher for thirty-five years, should be consistent with it. The audience will be family, colleagues, and former students — no philosophers, so no names, no explicit references to the argument, and no "as a philosopher I believe" framing. My friend's standing objection was that a eulogy written this way would end up saying her goodness was just luck, and I think he is right that that is the failure mode to avoid. So the task: praise her as fully as a eulogy can — her patience with difficult students, her habit of staying late, the way she read to the class — without ever resting on the premise that she chose her character, that she earned her virtues, or that she could have been otherwise; the praise should be grounded in what her character did for us and in our gratitude, not in desert. About four minutes long, warm, and it has to actually comfort a room of people who have lost her, rather than sound like a consolation that her goodness was an accident.
