I've been mulling over something on my own after a news story about how hospitals decide when to stop life support, and two friends of mine got into a real argument about it over dinner, and I honestly couldn't tell who was making the better case. One of them, let's call him David, started by saying that brain death is the real definition of death, not just a convenient rule: the brain is what makes a human being a person and what keeps the whole body alive, so when the brain irreversibly stops functioning, the person is dead, full stop, even if a machine keeps the heart beating for hours. He pointed out that this is why doctors actually declare death by a set of brain tests rather than by waiting for the heart to stop, so the practice already treats the brain, not the circulation, as the thing that matters. The other, Mara, pushed back by saying that David has the explanation exactly backwards. An organism is alive as a whole first, she said, and the brain is just the organ that integrates all the body's systems; the brain doesn't make the organism alive, the organism's life is what keeps the brain working. So "brain death" isn't the essence of death, she argued, it's a reliable sign that the organism has irreversibly lost the capacity to function as an integrated whole, and the real criterion of death is the loss of that capacity, with whole-brain death being simply the cleanest, most reliable way to tell when it has happened.

David answered that Mara had quietly swapped in a different definition. If death is "irreversible loss of integrated function," he said, then a person in a permanent vegetative state — the brainstem still working, the heart still beating, but no consciousness and never again — is not dead by her standard, because the organism is still integrated enough to go on, and that seems obviously wrong, since the person is clearly gone. Mara replied that this is exactly where her view works and his breaks: on his view that the brain is what makes the organism alive, you are forced to say the whole brain is the seat of life, so a vegetative patient with a working brainstem is still alive, which is the wrong answer, whereas on her view the patient is dead, because what matters is the capacity for the kind of integrated, conscious life that makes a human organism a person, and that capacity lives in the cortex, not in the brainstem, which is just keeping the body's basic rhythms going. David then accused her of the "higher brain" move, saying she was just picking whichever organ's failure feels most like "the person is gone," and that she had no principled reason to single out the cortex over the brainstem, so she was just moving the goalposts from "the brain" to "the part of the brain I care about." Mara's last word was that she was not picking an organ at all but a capacity, and that the reason whole-brain death is the legal standard is not that the brain is the essence of life but that it is a bright, reliable line marking the irreversible loss of that capacity, and we need a bright line in order to make hard decisions like when to withdraw treatment or to donate organs.

I sat out the whole thing and still can't decide. Could you go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about each specific point — not just who wins overall — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing?
