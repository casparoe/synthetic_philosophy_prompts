**Exam question (20 points).** In *On Certainty*, Wittgenstein engages with G. E. Moore's "Proof of an External World." (a) Reconstruct Moore's argument and state the three conditions Moore says a good proof must satisfy. (b) What is the central force of Wittgenstein's objection to Moore, and how does it connect to his notion of a "hinge" proposition? In your answer, explain why a hinge is not simply a proposition that we happen to be especially certain of.

**Student's answer.**

*(a)* Moore's argument in "Proof of an External World" is designed to answer the Cartesian skeptic. The skeptic argues that if I know anything at all, I must be able to rule out the hypothesis that I am a disembodied brain in a vat, or under the spell of an evil demon; since I cannot rule that out, I cannot know anything about the external world. Moore's response is to turn the argument around. He holds up his two hands and asserts, as premises, that here is one hand and here is another, from which it plainly follows that there are external objects, and hence that an external world exists. He does not deny the skeptic's conditional; rather, he simply asserts the premise the skeptic denies. He offers three conditions for a good proof: the premises must be different from the conclusion, the premises must be known, and the conclusion must follow from the premises; and he claims his argument satisfies all three.

*(b)* Wittgenstein's objection, as I understand it, is that Moore's proof is circular. The premise that I know I have two hands already presupposes that there is an external world, since a hand is by definition an external object. So Moore's argument begs the question: he is using the very thing he is supposed to prove in order to prove it, and the skeptic is not really answered.

This connects to Wittgenstein's idea of a hinge proposition. A hinge is a proposition that we are especially certain of — certain in the same way we are certain of "2 + 2 = 4" — and which therefore cannot be doubted without absurdity. Examples include the proposition that the Earth has existed for a very long time, or that I have hands. Because hinges are certain in this a priori way, they stand fast and cannot be the object of genuine doubt. Moore's mistake, on this reading, was to try to "know" a hinge in the ordinary evidential sense, treating it as something that could be established by reasons. Hinges are not established by reasons; they are the framework that makes reasoning possible.
