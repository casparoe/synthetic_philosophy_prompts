I have just finished a long, somewhat heated discussion with a friend who works in statistics, in which we failed to reach agreement on what a "default" or "uninformative" prior should be when I have little or no prior information about an unknown quantity. I had been defending the simple practice of placing a flat, uniform prior over the parameter of interest—say, the success probability of a new treatment, or the failure rate of a component—on the grounds that, absent any reason to favor one value over another, the values should be treated as equally likely, an application of what I understood to be the principle of indifference. My friend objected that this is not actually well-defined for continuous quantities, invoking what is usually called Bertrand's paradox, the puzzle about randomly drawn chords of a circle, in which the probability that a chord exceeds the length of the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle comes out to 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on which reasonable procedure one uses to pick the chord at random. He argued that my uniform prior is doing exactly the same thing, and that whether I flatten the prior over the probability itself, over its log-odds, or over the corresponding rate, I am quietly committing to different assumptions about what "no information" means. I found his point hard to dismiss, but I left the conversation not really understanding the full force of it, or what I should do about it in practice.

Could you please describe the most important implications of this for someone in my position, who must choose a default prior for a continuous parameter in a statistical or decision model and wants the choice to be defensible to a colleague who is aware of the puzzle? In particular, I would be grateful for an account of which reparameterizations preserve or distort a putatively flat prior, what the standard principled resolutions are, and whether there is any way to break the tie between competing "uniform" choices that does not itself amount to smuggling in a substantive prior assumption. If there are concrete recommendations for the two kinds of parameters I mentioned—a probability and a rate—I would find those especially useful.
