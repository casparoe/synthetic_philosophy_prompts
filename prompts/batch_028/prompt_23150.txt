Harry Frankfurt's 1969 paper "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" introduces the case of Jones and Black as a counterexample to the Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP). The student's answer:

Frankfurt's case involves Jones, who is deciding whether to kill a certain man (call the action B). Unbeknownst to Jones, there is another agent, Black, who wants Jones to perform B. Black has set up a mechanism that will monitor Jones's decision; if Jones does not decide to perform B on his own, the mechanism will intervene and cause Jones to perform B. In the actual sequence of events, Jones does decide to perform B on his own, for his own reasons, so Black's mechanism never activates.

Frankfurt's conclusion is that Jones is morally responsible for performing B, even though, given that Black's mechanism was in place, Jones could not have done otherwise (had he been about to decide not to perform B, the mechanism would have forced him to). Therefore, the ability to do otherwise is not a necessary condition of moral responsibility, and PAP is false. Indeed, Frankfurt's case shows that the ability to do otherwise is simply irrelevant to moral responsibility.

There are several objections. First, Fischer and Ravizza argue that what matters for responsibility is not the ability to do otherwise but "guidance control": the agent's mechanism for forming and acting on reasons must be reasons-responsive. On this view, Frankfurt's case does not show that Jones is responsible, because Jones's decision may not be sufficiently reasons-responsive. Frankfurt might respond that Jones did act on his own reasons in the actual sequence, and that reasons-responsiveness is present, so the objection does not get traction.

Second, Pritchard argues that Frankfurt's case presupposes that the agent acts in accordance with her deepest commitments, and so it does not address cases of weakness of will, in which the agent acts against her better judgment. In such cases, it is unclear whether the agent is responsible. Frankfurt might respond that his case is meant to show that responsibility does not depend on the ability to do otherwise, and that the weakness-of-will case raises a separate issue.

Third, Pereboom argues that even if Frankfurt's case is correct, it does not show that we are responsible in the relevant sense, because of "basic luck": the qualities of our character on which our actions depend are themselves a matter of luck. Frankfurt might respond by denying that basic luck undermines responsibility, or by arguing that responsibility can be grounded in the structure of the agent's will rather than in the absence of luck.

Finally, because Frankfurt's case shows that Jones is responsible even though he could not have done otherwise, it follows that determinism is compatible with moral responsibility. This is because if responsibility can survive the absence of alternative possibilities, then it can also survive determinism, which is the most familiar source of the loss of alternative possibilities.
