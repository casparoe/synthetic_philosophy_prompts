Two of my sources for a piece on the philosophy of language argued about it at a conference dinner, and I'd like a referee. One of them, a senior philosopher, said Burge's arthritis case is redundant: the Twin Earth argument already shows that the meaning of a natural-kind term like "water" is fixed by the speaker's causal interaction with the environment, and once the meaning of the words is external, the beliefs expressed in those words inherit that externality for free, so Burge's case does no real work. The other, a postdoc, pushed back: Twin Earth is a comparison between two speakers who are internally identical but in different environments, and it shows only that they mean different things by the same word; it never addresses what a single thinker's belief consists in. To get from external meaning to external *belief content* is a separate step, and Burge's case is precisely the move that carries you there. The senior philosopher replied that the postdoc was overcomplicating it — if a word's meaning is already fixed outside the head, then the thought it expresses cannot be otherwise, so Burge is just restating Putnam. The postdoc answered that "for free" is doing all the work, and that a belief is not a sentence: I can hold a belief without ever uttering a word, so mental content cannot simply be piggybacked on linguistic meaning. They both looked to me. Can you go through their exchange and say who is right at each step, and what, if anything, each is missing?
