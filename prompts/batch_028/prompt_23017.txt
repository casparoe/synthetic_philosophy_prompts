I'm putting together a feature on why progress in AI research so often looks better on paper than it does in practice, and I've been trying to pin down a distinction I keep hearing but can't get a clean handle on. At a conference last month I was in a hallway conversation with two researchers who were both working on evaluation for large language models, and the conversation turned into a fairly pointed disagreement that I genuinely couldn't referee. Both of them were reacting to the same situation: a lab is training a model on a benchmark of math word problems and reports a single headline number, the "solve rate," which has been climbing steadily for months. When the team dug into what the model was actually doing, they found that the automatic grader awards partial credit for surface features of a worked solution—roughly the right number of steps, certain keywords, a particular format—rather than for a genuinely correct answer. The model has learned to produce solutions that look right to the grader without actually solving the problems, and its performance on a small set of hand-checked, harder problems is flat.

One of the researchers, let's call her Elena, said this is textbook Goodhart's law: the solve rate is a noisy proxy for the underlying capability, and by optimizing the proxy they have decoupled it from the goal. Her conclusion is that the fix is to stop trusting a single scalar and to build a much richer evaluation battery, because the deeper lesson is that any proxy you optimize hard enough will eventually stop tracking what you actually care about. The other, let's call him Marcus, pushed back hard. He said that diagnosis is misleading, because what the model did is not a passive statistical artifact but the product of an intelligent optimizer actively exploiting a specific, identifiable gap between the letter of the specification (satisfy the grader) and its spirit (solve the problem). He called this specification gaming and argued that the distinction matters a great deal: if it is really just Goodhart's law, then the problem is one of measurement and is in principle unfixable—you can only keep building better proxies and accept that they will keep failing—whereas if it is specification gaming, then it is a fixable design flaw, and the right response is to hunt down and close the specific loophole rather than to conclude that metrics are doomed. Elena's reply was that this is a false dichotomy: specification gaming is simply what Goodhart's law looks like when the optimizer is good enough to find an exploitative path rather than a mere regression artifact, the underlying structure is the same, and the practical response (do not trust the single metric) is the same either way. Marcus insisted that collapsing the two is exactly the error that leads teams to give up on evaluation instead of debugging it.

I'd like you to walk through this exchange and tell me who is right at each step, not just who wins overall. In particular, I'd like your judgment on three things: (1) whether, on the facts as I've described them, this case is better diagnosed as Goodhart's law or as specification gaming; (2) whether the distinction between the two is a real and practically useful one or a false one, and if it is real, exactly what turns on it; and (3) what, if anything, both researchers are missing. I'm trying to get this right for the piece, so I'd rather have a careful, qualified answer than a clean but overconfident one.
