I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a long-form feature on the birth of decision theory. My argument is that the Wager in Pascal's *Pensées* is usually treated as the first decision-theoretic argument in the literature, and that the very concept of expected value it presupposes turns out to be far more fragile than Pascal could have known. I want to include a section that reconstructs what Pascal himself would have made of the Pasadena game, a thought experiment introduced in 2004 by Harris Nover and Alan Hájek, and I'd like your help thinking it through before I draft that section.

Let me spell out the setup, since it is the engine of the whole thing. A fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads; if the first heads lands on the *n*th flip, the player wins 2^n/*n* dollars if *n* is odd and loses 2^n/*n* dollars if *n* is even. The expected value of the game is therefore the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which in the natural order of the payoffs sums to ln 2 — but because the series is only conditionally convergent, its terms can be rearranged to sum to any number at all, including positive infinity, negative infinity, or zero. So the game has no well-defined expected value: its "value" depends entirely on the arbitrary order in which you list the outcomes. That is a deeper problem than the St. Petersburg game, which at least has a well-defined (if infinite) expected value, and it is precisely the kind of case for which expected-value maximization has no answer.

What I'd like is a reconstruction of Pascal's likely position, in roughly 600 to 900 words, in four parts. First, a short account of why the Pasadena game breaks expected-value reasoning, written so a general reader can follow it. Second, the strongest case that the Wager is not actually vulnerable to this: on one reading, Pascal never computes an expected value at all — his argument is closer to a dominance argument, in which the believer's outcome is at least as good as the non-believer's in every state of the world and infinitely better in one, so that the comparison between "an infinity of an infinitely happy life to gain, a chance of gain against a finite number of chances of loss, and what you stake is finite" does the work without the infinite series ever needing to converge. Third, the strongest case that the Wager is vulnerable: consider a God who infinitely rewards belief and infinitely punishes disbelief, so that the payoff structure of the wager is exactly Pasadena-like — an infinite gain on one branch and an infinite loss on another — and explain why Pascal's proportionality reasoning about the certainty of the stake and the uncertainty of the gain becomes unstable when both sides of the ledger are infinite. Fourth, and this is the part I care about most, your best, textually grounded guess about which side Pascal himself would have taken, with specific reference to how he treats infinity in the Wager — in particular, his insistence that "wherever the infinite is and there is not an infinity of chances of loss against that of gain, there is no time to hesitate" — and whether that caveat gives him a principled way to reject the tormenting-God variant without abandoning the argument. Two constraints: please distinguish carefully between what Pascal actually argues and what later commentators have read into him, and do not let the St. Petersburg paradox stand in for the Pasadena game — the whole point of the Pasadena is that it has no expected value at all, not merely an infinite one. I'd rather have a sharp, defensible reconstruction than a survey of the literature.
