I'm a third-year philosophy student, and I'm taking a course this term on paternalism and the philosophy of the self. I've been reading a lot on Ulysses contracts — voluntary precommitments in which a present self binds its future self to correct for a known failure of will — and I keep coming back to the puzzle of how such self-binding can be autonomy-respecting rather than a form of self-paternalism. Next week our department is hosting a visiting scholar who defends the view that Ulysses contracts and, by extension, entrenched constitutional constraints (such as bills of rights that future majorities cannot easily override) are legitimate, autonomy-respecting forms of precommitment. Her unifying principle is what I'll call the self-authorship criterion: a precommitment is legitimate because it is authored by a rational agent who anticipates a future failure, and it does not violate autonomy because the constrained agent is the same as the authoring agent — the same person in the individual case, or the same polity in the constitutional case. She is very good at deflecting the standard autonomy objection (that binding one's future self, or one's future majority, undermines that agent's autonomy) by pointing out that the constrained agent is simply the same as the authoring agent, so no one's autonomy is violated.
