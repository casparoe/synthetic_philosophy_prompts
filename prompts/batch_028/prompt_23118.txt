Question: In your own words, explain how Margaret Gilbert's "plural subject" account of collective intention differs from (a) the aggregative (summatist) view, and (b) Searle's account of "we-intentions." Then state one key advantage Gilbert claims her account has over the aggregative view.

Student's answer:

On the aggregative view, a group's intention to φ is nothing over and above the sum of its members' individual intentions: the group intends to φ just in case each member individually intends to φ, perhaps together with some mutual awareness that the others intend as well. Gilbert rejects this, since on the aggregative view there is no genuine group-level intention—the group's intention is reducible to the individuals'—and the view so cannot explain why a joint intention feels more binding than a mere coincidence of individual intentions.

Gilbert's alternative is that when a group jointly intends to φ, its members become a single "plural subject"—a subject of intention that is the group taken as a whole rather than any one individual. For Gilbert, this plural subject is a distinct entity, a kind of group mind, that exists over and above the individuals and is what does the intending. The joint intention is secured by a "joint commitment" the members undertake, which is what makes it a "we-intend" rather than a collection of "I-intend"s.

Searle's account, by contrast, introduces "we-intentions": intentions whose content has a collective, "we" form, as in "we intend to push the cart." Searle, like Gilbert, treats the group as a single subject of such an intention; the difference is merely that Searle does not appeal to a joint commitment, but only to each participant's holding the we-intention, together with an individual intention to do her part.

Gilbert's main advantage over the aggregative view, she argues, is that her account can explain "we-attitudes" and the distinctive responsibility members bear: because the plural subject is the real subject of the intention, a member can be committed to, and faulted for, the group's undertaking even in cases where she does not fully endorse it on her own.
