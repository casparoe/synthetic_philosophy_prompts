I am preparing a short piece on Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP), the principle that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise, and I want to think through what is at stake if Frankfurt's move succeeds rather than re-litigating the move itself. For background, I accept the standard structure: in Frankfurt's 1969 case, a counterfactual intervener (Black) is set up to ensure that Jones performs a certain action should Jones appear to be about to choose otherwise, but Jones in fact chooses the relevant action on his own, so the intervener never acts; Frankfurt's conclusion is that Jones is nevertheless fully responsible, and so the ability to do otherwise is not necessary for responsibility. I also have a working grasp of the main lines of reply: the flicker-of-freedom strategy, which argues that the agent retains some alternative possibility (for example, the ability to make the decision on his own or spontaneously, which he could have avoided) and so PAP survives in a weakened form; the dilemma defense, which argues that under determinism the agent's actual decision is not really up to him (so he is not responsible after all) and under indeterminism the residual flicker is too thin to ground responsibility; and the semicompatibilist response associated with Fischer and Ravizza, which abandons PAP outright and grounds responsibility in something like guidance control or reasons-responsiveness. I have been working through a few papers on the flicker strategy and the dilemma defense, and I find how each of these constrains the others harder to pin down than I expected, which is part of why I want to step back to the implications.

I find it plausible that (i) Frankfurt-style cases succeed in refuting PAP, (ii) the flicker strategy is at best a partial rescue, (iii) the dilemma defense leaves the libertarian with a residual problem, and (iv) semicompatibilism is now the dominant framework. Given those working assumptions, please describe the most important implications for how the free-will debate is organized. I am especially interested in what PAP's failure buys or costs for libertarians, compatibilists, and hard determinists; whether the center of gravity has shifted from the question of whether an agent could have done otherwise to questions about control, sourcehood, and whether the actual sequence of events is genuinely the agent's own; and whether the implications extend beyond moral responsibility to desert, reactive attitudes, and legal responsibility. Please prioritize the implications by importance, and flag any that are themselves contested among the parties rather than settled consequences.
