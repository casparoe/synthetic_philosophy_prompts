I serve on my university's research integrity and conduct board, and we are being asked to rule on a case before next month's plenary vote. A second-year doctoral student in the psychology department is documented to have altered the raw data from a lab study so that the results came out in favor of the hypothesis her supervisor had publicly staked his reputation on; the alteration is corroborated by file timestamps and by entries in her own lab notebook, and she has no prior record of misconduct. Her defense, supported by a string of emails between her and the supervisor, is that the supervisor had already decided the study had to come out positive and had told her that if she reported a null result he would alter the data himself, take sole credit for the paper, and push her out of the program. On her account, the falsified result was going to be published no matter what she did, so she is asking the board to find that she could not have done otherwise and to clear her, or at minimum to hold her only lightly accountable while the supervisor is dealt with in a separate proceeding. The board is genuinely split: one faction holds that she made the alteration as a free choice, acting on her own reasons, and that the supervisor's backup plan does not make her act any less her own, while another faction holds that if the outcome was fixed independently of her conduct, treating her as fully responsible imports the assumption that responsibility depends on the availability of a genuine alternative. One member keeps pressing the question of whether disciplinary responsibility, like moral responsibility, is subject to the principle of alternative possibilities—the idea that an agent can be held responsible only if she could have done otherwise—and whether a case in which the agent's act was free but the outcome was predetermined sits comfortably on either side of that line.

Please draft the board's decision memo for circulation before the vote. It should recommend a concrete disposition from the options we can actually impose (full sanction, reduced sanction, or a finding of no violation, in each case with or without a parallel referral of the supervisor to the faculty conduct process), state the reasoning that supports the recommendation, and give an honest and strong treatment of the best argument for the disposition we are not recommending, including the worry that whatever rule we adopt will be invoked in future cases where the claim that the outcome was going to happen anyway is much less well documented. The memo should be something a board with no background in the relevant philosophy can act on, but it should not paper over the conceptual issue at the center of the split.
