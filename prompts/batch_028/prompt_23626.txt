"In De Mendacio, Augustine writes that 'he may say a false thing and yet not lie' and that 'he may say a true thing, and yet lie.' (a) In Augustine's terms, what makes an utterance a lie, and why do both of those cases hold? (b) Apply this to the case where a murderer asks you where your fleeing friend is hiding. What's Augustine's verdict, and why? (10 points)"

Here's what one student turned in:

"For (a), Augustine defines a lie as a false signification with the intention to decieve, which he pictures as a double heart—you have one thing in mind but say another. What matters isn't whether the words are true or false but the state of the speaker's mind. So you can say a false thing without lying if you genuinely believe it's true; you're just mistaken, not a liar, since you don't have a double heart and don't mean to deceive. The reverse case, saying a true thing and still lying, happens when you're being insincere or trying to mislead the hearer, so that even though the words come out true, your intent is deceptive.

For (b), Augustine is an absolutist: a lie is always a sin, and no good end, even saving an innocent life, justifies it. So you should not lie to the murderer. His reasoning is that a lie is intrinsically sinful and that the friend's safety is ultimately God's to decide, not yours. (He does allow, though, that if lying were the only way to save the life, the lie would be a lesser evil and so permissible.)"
