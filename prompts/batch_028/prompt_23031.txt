I retired from a physics lab about three years ago, and lately I've been working my way through Hobbes, which is a strange thing for a person who spent thirty-five years measuring things to do, but I find Leviathan far more interesting than I expected. I'm stuck on the stretch across chapters 4 and 5 ("Of Speech" and "Of Reason, and Science") where he's talking about geometry, read alongside the parallel discussion of first principles in De Corpore, and I'd really like some help unpeacking it. The rough gist, as I understand it, is that the truths of geometry are not innate, but are built up out of our own definitions; that reasoning is nothing but computation, i.e. adding and subtracting names in a long chain; and that what people call "common notions" (the axioms) are not really notions at all, but propositions that become obvious the moment you understand the words in them. He goes on to claim that geometry is the only genuine science in the strict sense, because it's the only field where the conclusions are actually certain.

What I'm confused about is what he's doing to the common notions. I always assumed axioms like "the whole is greater than the part" were self-evident starting points, the kind of thing you can't prove but everyone just sees. But he seems to be saying they're self-evident only in a much weaker, almost linguistic sense: once the terms are defined, the "truth" of the axiom is just the signification of the words, so it's more like a definitional tautology than a discovery about the world. Is that a fair reading? And if so, what's the point of that move for him — does he think this demotes axioms to verbal conventions, or does he think it's precisely what makes geometry reliable, since then every step is under our control? I'm also curious how far "computation in general" actually gets you. Does he really think that from a definition of a line and a few axioms you can mechanically grind out the Pythagorean theorem, or is he being generous about what counts as a step in the reckoning? I want to understand how much of his picture of mathematics as symbol manipulation survives contact with what mathematicians actually do, because I have a feeling it bears on his whole political project, and I'd rather not wade into that part until I get this.
