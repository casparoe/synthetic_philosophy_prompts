I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of religion, and I've been going in circles for a couple of weeks over Gödel's modal ontological argument — the S5-based proof that moves from the mere possibility of a being possessing all positive properties to the necessary existence of such a being. A visiting colleague has been openly skeptical of the argument, and I'm preparing a reading-group session where we'll take it up, so I'd like a dialogue that lets me see the knot from both sides at once. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers, each fully competent and in command of the literature, in which one defends the argument as a genuine, non-vacuous a priori result and the other grants the underlying modal logic but insists the argument is question-begging?

The defender should hold, roughly, that the notion of a positive property is not an arbitrary stipulation but tracks genuine perfection and is at least partly fixed by rational reflection; that the possibility premise — that a being with all positive properties is possible — is itself rationally compelling rather than smuggled in; and that the argument's real payoff is conceptual, showing that anyone who finds the idea of a maximally perfect being coherent has, in a sense, already committed to its existence. The critic should press the two strongest versions of the standard objections: first, the parody objection, that if the axioms governing positive properties are the only constraint, you can run the identical proof for a necessary unicorn (or any other arbitrary property) simply by enlarging the set of positive properties, and that Gödel has no principled, non-ad-hoc way to block this; and second, the vacuity objection, that the defining clause for a God-like being is so broad that the possibility premise carries as much weight as the conclusion, so the argument is at best a tautological unpacking of a definition.

I'd like the dialogue to have real back-and-forth rather than two alternating monologues: the critic should respond to the defender's actual replies to the parody and vacuity objections, and the defender should not simply repeat a stock response but should have to concede or refine something under pressure. Near the end, I'd like them to confront, at least briefly, whether their disagreement is a substantive one about what is rationally compelling or a merely verbal one about what the phrase positive property is doing, and to say something honest about which it is. Please keep the technical content accurate — the argument really does run in S5, and its conclusion is a necessary being with all positive properties, with identifying that being with the personal God of classical theism a further step the proof by itself does not deliver — and keep the register conversational but serious: two colleagues at a whiteboard, not a textbook.
