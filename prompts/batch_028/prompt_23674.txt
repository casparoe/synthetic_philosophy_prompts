I've been reading a lot about the epistemology of testimony lately — the reductionist versus non-reductionist debate, whether testimony is a basic or merely derivative source of justification, all of it — and it got me thinking about a corner of the topic I had not really considered: aesthetic testimony, that is, whether you can be justified in believing that a work of art is beautiful or good on the basis of another person's say-so. At a dinner last month, two friends of mine, M and D, got into a long argument about exactly this, and I honestly could not referee.

M opened by defending what she called the anti-testimony thesis about aesthetics: you simply cannot learn from testimony that a work is beautiful. Her reasoning was that aesthetic value is a response-dependent property — it is instantiated only when a suitably responsive perceiver actually responds to the work in the right way — so when a critic says a painting is beautiful, she is reporting her own response, not stating a freestanding fact about the painting that I could be justified in believing on her word. D pushed back by saying that M was conflating two distinct things: aesthetic belief (believing that a work is beautiful) and aesthetic appreciation (actually having the first-hand aesthetic experience of engaging with the work). On D's view, an expert's testimony can legitimately give you the belief that a painting is beautiful, just as testimony gives you beliefs about any other kind of fact; what testimony cannot give you is the appreciation itself, which you have to have by actually looking at the painting. M replied that even the belief is not transmissible: if aesthetic value is response-dependent, there is no freestanding fact about the painting for the critic to report, so there is nothing for my belief to be about, and the critic's word is no more a route to aesthetic knowledge than a verbal description of what a dish tastes like is a route to knowing its taste. D conceded that if you are an anti-realist about aesthetic value, the anti-testimony thesis has some force, but said that anti-realism is a separate and contestable metaphysical commitment, and that even on a response-dependent view the critic's report is still evidence about what a suitably responsive perceiver would find, so it is testimonial evidence after all.

They asked me to sort it out and I could not. Could you go through their exchange and say who is right about each specific move — not just who wins overall — and point out what, if anything, both of them are missing?
