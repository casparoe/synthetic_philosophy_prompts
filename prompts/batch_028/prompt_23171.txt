The exam question read: "Explain the diachronic Dutch book argument. What is it supposed to show about the norms governing belief revision, and what assumptions does it rely on?"

The student answered: "The diachronic Dutch book argument concerns how our degrees of belief should change over time, as opposed to the synchronic argument, which asks whether our credences at a single time obey the axioms of probability. The diachronic argument targets the rule of conditionalization, the claim that when you learn new evidence E, your new credence in any proposition A should equal your old credence in A conditional on E. The argument runs roughly like this. You start with prior credences, then you learn E and form posterior credences; if those posterior credences are not what you would get by conditionalizing your prior credences on E, there is a sequence of bets — some offered before you learn E and priced by your prior credences, some offered after and priced by your posterior credences — such that each bet is fair or favorable to you at the moment it is offered, given your credences at that moment, yet the whole package guarantees a net loss whatever the outcome. The bookie exploits the mismatch between your prior credence in A and your posterior credence in A: because those two numbers fail to line up the way they would if you had conditionalized, the bookie can stitch bets across the two times into a sure-loss portfolio. The conclusion the argument is meant to draw is that conditionalization is the only rational rule of belief revision — any agent who departs from it is, by the argument's own lights, irrational, since there is a sure way for the bookie to profit at the agent's expense. The argument is forceful because it does not depend on the agent's particular goals or values; it shows that the agent's own credences, taken at each time, sanction a set of bets that are collectively losing."
