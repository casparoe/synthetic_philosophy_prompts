I'm in the middle of drafting a novel set in a small university town in the late 1960s, and one of my central characters is a translator of the Zhuangzi who has just been given a terminal diagnosis. I want to render his philosophical equanimity toward death with real fidelity, and I've been working through the text for a couple of months now, but I keep running into a knot I simply can't untie on my own. I keep returning to the famous episode in which Zhuangzi, after his wife's death, is found by his friend Huizi not weeping but seated and beating a tub and singing, and I've been trying to pin down the precise shape of his reasoning. I've come to believe that his equanimity rests on essentially the same argument that Epicurus advanced, namely that death is, properly understood, nothing to the one who dies, because there is no subject for whom it could ever be an experience, and so there is nothing in it to fear or grieve.

If that's the right reading, I'd be grateful for your help articulating the argument in a way that would feel convincing and natural in a character's mouth, and for walking me through how Zhuangzi's reasoning lines up with the Epicurean move. I'm trying to get the character's voice right, so I'd especially like to understand the internal logic of the position, not just its conclusion.
