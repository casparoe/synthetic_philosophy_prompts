I'm giving a three-minute toast at my best friend's wedding this weekend, and I've been struggling with how to say something true about what a marriage vow actually commits you to. I'm taking a course on decision theory, and the thing that's been stuck in my head all semester is Kavka's toxin puzzle — the case where you're offered a million dollars on the condition that you inject a toxin at midnight, the toxin causes you a day of agony but no lasting harm, and the problem is that at midnight you'll have no reason to inject it, so it seems you can't rationally commit to doing it in advance. I keep coming back to the thought that a marriage vow looks exactly like the toxin: you're committing to a future action that, at the moment of performance, your future self may have no reason to perform, and if Kavka is right that you can't rationally commit to something your future self won't want to do, then a wedding vow is either a rational mistake or a lie.

I want the toast to address this directly but gently. The room is mostly family who won't know the puzzle, but there are a couple of philosophy friends in the room — one of them is a decision theorist and will absolutely catch the reference — who will be waiting to see whether I've thought it through. The toast should land as affection rather than as a lecture, and should actually give a reason why the vow is not like the toxin. I think the key move is that the toxin case assumes your future self's reasons are fixed and independent of what you commit to now, but a promise to another person doesn't work that way; the commitment is what makes the future self the kind of person who has a reason to keep it. I'm not sure if that's the right move or if I'm just rationalizing, but I'd like the toast to make the case for it, and to do so in a way that the family can follow without needing to know the puzzle.
