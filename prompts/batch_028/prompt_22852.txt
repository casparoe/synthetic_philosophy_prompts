Question: "Lewis denies transworld identity and instead appeals to a counterpart relation. Explain how this bears on his semantics for counterfactuals. In your answer, (a) state the role of the counterpart relation, (b) describe the function Lewis uses to evaluate a counterfactual at a given world, and (c) explain why Lewis rejects the Kripkean accessibility relation."

Student's answer: "Lewis holds that an individual exists in exactly one possible world, so there is no transworld identity. Instead, a counterpart relation links an individual in one world to similar individuals in other worlds. This matters for counterfactuals because, when we evaluate a claim such as 'if I had taken the job, I would now be in Vienna,' we look to the worlds nearby the actual world and check what the speaker's counterpart does there. Lewis evaluates a counterfactual at a world w with a function f that maps w to the set of worlds nearby w. This function plays the same role as an accessibility relation in Kripke's semantics, in that it connects a world to the worlds relevant for evaluating claims at that world; the only difference is that it is not required to satisfy the S5 frame conditions. The counterfactual 'if A then B' is true at w just in case there is some world in f(w) at which A is true and B is true. Lewis rejects the Kripkean accessibility relation because, on modal realism, 'possible' is indexical and every world is actual from its own perspective, so it is not coherent to say that one world is accessible from another."
