I am writing a historical novel set in late eleventh-century Baghdad. In one scene, a student of al-Ghazālī is preparing to answer a physician who has attributed a patient's fever to the imbalance of his humours, and who therefore insists that the fever is the work of a natural cause rather than of God's direct will. My literary consultant has assured me that al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is "merely" Humean scepticism about causation with a theistic wrapper, and that I may therefore write the student's arguments by borrowing Hume's. I suspect this is too glib. Could you compare al-Ghazālī's occasionalism with Hume's account of causation in some detail — in particular, what each takes a "cause" to be, why each denies that a cause necessitates its effect, and what each stands to gain from that denial — and say where the "Hume with God" reading breaks down, so that I can write the student's actual position rather than a pastiche of Hume?
