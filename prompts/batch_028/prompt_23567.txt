I am a novelist, and I am currently drafting a novel set in the world of field linguistics, in which my protagonist is a radical translator working with an uncontacted community. I have been trying to decide whether my protagonist's persistent sense that she can never know which translation is correct is a defensible philosophical position, or merely a literary device I have borrowed without understanding. In doing so, I keep returning to two arguments that I believe both purport to show that empirical evidence does not fix meaning: Quine's argument from radical translation (the famous gavagai example), and Putnam's model-theoretic argument for the indeterminacy of reference. I was discussing this with a friend who is a philosophy PhD, and she suggested that the two arguments are fundamentally different in kind — that Quine's is an argument about the underdetermination of interpretation by all possible observations in a specific epistemic situation, while Putnam's is a model-theoretic or logical result about the relation between a theory and its models — but I confess that I do not see why this distinction matters, or whether one argument is simply a repackaging of the other.

Could you compare the two arguments in some detail? In particular, I would be grateful if you could explain where they actually agree and where they diverge, whether the model-theoretic argument succeeds where Quine's fails (or vice versa), and what the difference in their logical structure suggests about what, exactly, is being shown to be indeterminate in each case.
