I'm working on a chapter of my dissertation about formal models of ambiguity, and there's a debate I keep going in circles on that I'd like help settling. I was going over it with my advisor last week and we just couldn't agree. Background: Ellsberg's urn experiments are the standard evidence that people treat ambiguous probabilities differently from risky ones, and the two workhorse formalizations that come out of the literatue are Gilboa–Schmeidler maxmin expected utility (you replace the single prior with a set of priors and score each act by the worst expected utility in that set) and the Klibanoff–Marinacci–Mukerji "smooth ambiguity" model (you also have a set of priors, but you take a probability-weighted average of the expected utilities across the set and then apply a second, possibly concave, utility function to that average).

The controversy I want the essay to get into is which of these is the more defensible way to capture what the Ellsberg experiment actually shows people doing. The case for maxmin is that it's the minimal, cleanest departure from expected utility — one extra object (a set of priors) and a pessimistic selection rule — and it lines up nicely with the robustness / model-uncertainty reading, where the decision-maker is genuinely unsure which model of the world is right. The case against is that maxmin is all-or-nothing: it literally ignores every prior except the worst one, which gives knife-edge predictions (any two acts with the same worst-case value are indifferent) and can't represent the graded, moderate ambiguity aversion that the experimental data seem to show. The case for smooth ambiguity is exactly that it can dial the strength of the aversion up or down with the curvature of the second utility function. The case against is that it's structurally heavier — it stacks two utility functions and smuggles in a probability distribution over the set of priors, which some people read as a second-order object that the Ellsberg data don't really liscense.

Write an essay that weighs these arguments and reaches a conclusion: in your judgment, which model better captures the Ellsberg phenomenon, and why? Be concrete about what each model predicts in the actual 30/15/15 urn setup (the one where the 15 non-red, non-black balls are either white or yellow, and the subject prefers red to black but prefers non-black to red) and where the two models' predictions diverge, rather than just restating the axioms. Don't end on a non-committal "both have merits" note — pick a side and defend it.
