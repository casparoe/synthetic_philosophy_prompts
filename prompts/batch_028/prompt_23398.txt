The essay question was: "Explain whether Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment is a genuine counterexample to the Identity of Indiscernibles. In your answer, (a) state the principle and the counterexample as precisely as you can, (b) address the role of relational properties, and (c) consider whether the fact that the two spheres occupy different spatial locations is enough to tell them apart."

The student's answer is as follows:

"The Identity of Indiscernibles (IoI) is the principle that if two objects share all of their properties, then they are not two objects but one; that is, there cannot be two things that differ only numerically. Max Black's famous counterexample asks us to imagine a universe that contains nothing except two perfect iron spheres of exactly the same size, colour, and temperature, set some distance apart in otherwise empty space. The two spheres are, on this story, qualitatively identical in every respect. Each has precisely the same intrinsic properties as the other, and the only relational property either has — namely, being a distance d from the other sphere — is possessed by both of them, since the distance is the same in both directions. So the spheres are indiscernible by every property, yet they are plainly two spheres, which is supposed to refute IoI.

I do not think the example succeeds, for two independent reasons. First, IoI is in any case trivially true, so there is nothing for Black to refute. Take any two distinct objects a and b. Then a has the property of being identical with a, a property that b does not have, while b has the property of being identical with b, a property that a does not have. So there is always at least one property in which a and b differ, and it is simply impossible for two distinct objects to share all their properties. Black's spheres therefore cannot be a counterexample, since distinct objects can never, even in principle, be perfectly indiscernible.

Second, and independently of that, the two spheres are discernible after all. They occupy different regions of space: one sphere is at a location L1, the other at a different location L2. Being at L1 is a property of the first sphere, and being at L2 is a property of the second, and these are two different properties. So the spheres differ in at least one property, and the example does not in fact yield two distinct but perfectly indiscernible objects. On the contrary, the very fact of their being in two different places is what keeps them apart. So far from refuting IoI, Black's universe seems to illustrate it."
