I'm taking a philosophy of physics seminar this term, and we've spent a couple of sessions on supertasks, mostly through the lens of Thomson's lamp. I'm comfortable with the basic setup — a lamp toggled at times 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, and so on, with the toggle times converging on t = 1 — and I understand the standard intuition that at t = 1 the lamp appears to have no definite state, since the state function oscillates and has no limit as t approaches 1. What I keep getting stuck on is a subtler point that has come up more than once in our discussions: why exactly the completed supertask does not count as a counterexample to determinism.

The objection that keeps tripping me up runs roughly like this: determinism requires that the state of a system at a later time be uniquely fixed by its state at some earlier time; but in the supertask, the state at t = 1 is not fixed by the state at t = 0, or by the state at any earlier time, because the limit of the state function as t approaches 1 simply does not exist; therefore the supertask is a counterexample to determinism. I suspect this objection conflates two distinct things — the non-existence of the limit of the state function, on the one hand, and the indeterminism of the governing dynamics, on the other — but I can't articulate the distinction well enough to be confident. Could you walk me through, in a way a careful student can follow, where exactly the objection goes wrong and what the correct technical reason is for why the supertask does not violate determinism? I'd particularly like to understand how the non-extendibility of the state function to the limit time bears on whether the dynamics are deterministic, and whether there is any sense in which the supertask is "incomplete" as a physical scenario — i.e., whether the toggle schedule, taken as the "law," fails to specify the state at t = 1 in a way that is categorically different from a genuinely indeterministic law.
