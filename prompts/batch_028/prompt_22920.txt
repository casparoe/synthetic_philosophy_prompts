Consider the possibility that ZFC is inconsistent. (a) State precisely what it would mean for ZFC to be inconsistent, and explain the consequence for what ZFC proves. (b) State Gödel's second incompleteness theorem as it applies to ZFC, and explain why the fact that no proof of Con(ZFC) has been found inside ZFC is not, by itself, evidence that ZFC is inconsistent. (c) Give one reason why a working set theorist might have a different degree of concern about the inconsistency of ZFC than a working algebraic geometer. (d) If ZFC turned out to be inconsistent, what would happen to a theorem such as "there are infinitely many primes," and to our proofs of it?

Here is one student's answer:

(a) ZFC is inconsistent if there is some sentence P in its language such that ZFC proves both P and its negation. In that case, by the principle of explosion (ex falso quodlibet), ZFC proves every sentence in its language.

(b) Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that if ZFC is consistent, then ZFC cannot prove its own consistency, Con(ZFC). So the fact that we have not found a proof of Con(ZFC) inside ZFC is expected and is not, by itself, evidence of inconsistency. If Con(ZFC) is provable at all, it must be proved in a stronger system, such as ZFC augmented with the assumption that ZFC is consistent, or in a stronger set theory.

(c) A working set theorist is more directly exposed, because they use the full strength of ZFC and its large-cardinal extensions, whereas an algebraic geometer typically relies on only a small, well-understood fragment of set theory. So an inconsistency would have to be quite deep before it would threaten the geometer's results.

(d) If ZFC were inconsistent, then ZFC would prove every sentence, including "there are infinitely many primes" and its negation. However, "there are infinitely many primes" is provable in much weaker systems, such as Peano arithmetic, so we would still have a proof of it that does not depend on ZFC. The theorem itself remains true, but our ZFC-based proof of it is no longer a valid proof in the usual sense.
