Two friends of mine, Marcus and Priya, got into an argument at a study group over whether we're allowed to just take a moral philosopher's word for it on a hard case, and they used the example of whether it's wrong to steal food when you're starving and the owner doesn't need it. Marcus started by saying that moral facts aren't locked behind expertise the way particle physics is: if a physicist tells me there's a Higgs boson, I can't check that myself, so I defer, but if a philosopher tells me stealing the bread is wrong, I can actually think about it myself, so his testimony is redundant and I shouldn't even want it, since the moral conclusion is supposed to be one I reach on my own. Priya pushed back that I can't actually think about it well — I'm giving the case thirty seconds of intuition, while the philosopher has spent a decade working through the objections and the edge cases, so his testimony is the only reliable source I've got and it's not redundant at all. Marcus conceded that the philosopher has more time and training, but said that's an empirical fact, not a moral one, and the real point is that a moral belief isn't a fact I'm trying to acquire; it's something supposed to be mine, grounded in reasons that are reasons for me, so if I believe it only because a book told me to, it doesn't actually function as a reason in my life. Priya replied that that's a psychological worry, not an epistemic one — maybe believing on testimony is less motivating, but that doesn't make the belief less true or less justified, and she doesn't see why moral beliefs have to be self-generated in order to count. Marcus then said that's exactly why the redundancy objection works for moral cases but not for physics: there's no neutral vantage point outside my own moral perspective from which I could check the philosopher's work, so the expert and I are really just two people with different moral intuitions, and his being a professional doesn't give him privileged access to some fact I'm locked out of. Priya answered that if that's right, then there's no such thing as moral progress or moral learning, but we do change our minds in light of better arguments, so there must be some fact of the matter the expert is tracking that I'm not, even if I can't step outside my perspective to see it directly. I was the one who started the discussion and I honestly can't tell who's more right. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall, and point out anything both of them are missing?
