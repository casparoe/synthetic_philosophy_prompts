I keep reaching for two different vocabularies and I'm not confident they line up. On one hand there's Campbell's law, the social-science idea that a quantitative indicator once it is used for decision-making becomes exposed to pressures to corrupt the very process it is meant to monitor. On the other there's the four-way taxonomy that has come out of the AI-alignment literature, which splits Goodhart's law into regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial failures.

I'd like you to compare these two framings rather than simply define them. And conversely, is there a piece of the four-way split that has no real analogue in Campbell's account — or, the other way around, does Campbell's emphasis on "corruption pressures" and on the deliberate responses of the people being measured capture something that a taxonomy built around optimization pressure on a statistical proxy leaves out? I'm trying to decide whether the alignment taxonomy is a genuine refinement of the older social-science insight, or whether it is mostly about a different phenomenon — a non-adversarial statistical optimizer — that only superficially resembles what actually happens inside a human organization. Give me a reasoned verdict on how much the two frameworks genuinely overlap, and on which is the better lens for someone in my position who has to choose a metric and then live with the behavior it induces.
