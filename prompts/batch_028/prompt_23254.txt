I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I keep going in circles on the causal exclusion argument (the Kim-style version, where causal closure of the physical plus the non-identity of the mental seems to squeeze the mental out of the causal picture). I've been reading through the responses and I'm getting confused about how to seperate two of them, which I think people ofthen blur together. One is the "causal priority" move — the idea that the mental cause is the real or ultimate cause and the physical cause is just a derivative, secondary way of describing the same underlying event, so there's no overdetermination at all, just one cause under two descriptions. The other is the "causal redundancy" or overdetermination move, where the mental and the physical are treated as two distinct, each-sufficient causes that just happen to line up, and the defender's job is to argue that this kind of systematic redundancy isn't as bad as it looks (or that closure doesn't rule it out the way the argument assumes).

Can you compare these two responses in some detail? I'm especially interested in (a) whether the priority response really does avoid overdetermination or whether it just relocates the problem, and (b) how each one fares against the worry that systematic, universal overdetermination is empirically and methodologically implausible — i.e., the worry that if every mental cause were a redundant cause alongside a sufficient physical one, we'd expect to see the effects come apart in the way overdetermined cases usually do. And do you think the two responses are actually in tension with each other, or can a defender hold both at once? I'd rather you work through the conceptual structure than just name-drop the standard objections, and it's totally fine to flag where you're not sure.
