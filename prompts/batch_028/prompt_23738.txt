I'm in a history-of-economic-thought seminar and we're working through Hume's "Of the Balance of Trade" (the 1752 essay), and I keep getting stuck on this bit where he runs this thought experiment about the money supply getting multiplied fivefold overnight:

"Again, suppose, that all the money of Great Britain were multiplied fivefold in a night, must not the contrary effect follow? Must not all labour and commodities rise to such an exorbitant height, that no neighbouring nations could afford to buy from us; while their commodities, on the other hand, became comparatively so cheap, that, in spite of all the laws which could be formed, they would be run in upon us, and our money flow out; till we fall to a level with foreigners, and lose that great superiority of riches, which had laid us under such disadvantages?"

I totally get the basic mechanism — prices spike, our exports become uncompetitive, money flows out and imports flood in, and it eventually settles down. What I cant figure out is what he means by the money flowing out "till we fall to a level with foreigners." Is "a level" here supposed to be our absolute price level coming back down to what it was before the shock, or is it our prices getting back in line with our trading partners prices? Those feel like two different targets to me and I can't tell which one he's assuming. It's bugging me because if it's the second one, the target is moving every time our neighbors' prices change, so there's no stable point for the flow to stop at. Can someone walk me through what he's actually pinning the equilibrium to here?
