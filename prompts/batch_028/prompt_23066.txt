I work on a team that builds an internal tool where a language model does multi-step reasoning over our data, and a big part of our stack is a technique I'll just call "self-consistency": instead of taking the model's single greedy output, we sample several independent reasoning chains at nonzero temperature and keep whichever final answer comes up most often. It reliably improves our accuracy, and two of my colleagues, Dana and Marcus, got into a long argument about what that improvement actually shows. I'm not a philosopher and I mostly care that the thing works, but I'd like someone to sort out who is right about what.

Their exchange went roughly like this. (1) Dana said self-consistency works because the model already has the answer in its weights and a single greedy decode is just one noisy draw that often fails to surface it, so majority voting is recovering the model's genuine latent belief distribution, and the accuracy boost on hard reasoning problems is direct proof that the model knows the answer and we are finally eliciting it. (2) Marcus replied that there is no latent belief sitting in there waiting to be recovered — the model is simply a function from prompt to a distribution over token sequences — so self-consistency is nothing but variance reduction on a noisy estimator, i.e. averaging, and calling that "elicitation" smuggles in the very conclusion you are trying to establish. (3) Dana countered that variance reduction only pays off if the estimator is tracking the truth, so the mere fact that the majority answer is more accurate means the model's output distribution is actually lining up with the correct answer, and that is exactly what "the model knows it" amounts to in any operational sense. (4) Marcus conceded the tracking point but said that just relabels things: "the model knows it" is now simply a name for "the most probable output happens to be correct," and majority voting can be systematically wrong, because if the model has a bias the most probable wrong answer wins every time and you end up highly confident in a wrong answer — so the technique reveals the model's most probable (possibly biased) behavior, not any knowledge. (5) Dana then insisted that even granting all that, the weights still encode information about the answer that a single greedy decode does not fully extract, since with a different prompt or a small fine-tune you can get the model to produce the correct answer it "failed" to produce on a single pass, which is precisely why she calls it latent knowledge in the weights rather than in any one decode. (6) Marcus's last word was that this only shows the output is a function of the entire context, which is trivial and true of any such model; different prompts activate different parts of the function, and that is not latent knowledge being elicited but simply how a context-dependent function behaves, so she is anthropomorphizing a lookup table.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about each specific point — not just who wins overall — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing?
