I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I sit on my newsroom's annual service-recognition panel, which decides who recieves a bonus and a public mention at year end. This year we have a case that's been bugging me. One of our data reporters, off the clock and at her own expense, spent about three months tracking down the specific households that had been wrongly listed as "safe" in a contaminated-water database after we published a story on it, and personally notified each one, even though our policy was to publish a correction and move on. She turned down a lucrative assignment to do it, and it cost her real money and a strained relationship with a source. The editor wants to give her the top award and also quote her in a staff memo to "inspire" everyone else.

I'm not sure that's right, and I'd like the panel's written opinion drafted before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (award, no award, private recognition, etc.) with a time frame, and it should grapple with the fact that her act was, as I see it, genuinely above and beyond the call of duty, and that publicly rewarding an act that was not required seems to risk treating it as if it had been, wich would set a precedent we'd then use against people later. I also want an honest treatment of the editor's view, which I think is the strongest consideration on the other side: that without public recognition the organization is signaling that it doesn't value such acts, and that no one will do them again.
