I am teaching a graduate seminar on political liberalism, and two of my students have been stuck for several sessions on a question of exegesis about how Rawls treats religious reasons in public political discourse. We had just covered the duty of civility — the requirement that citizens offer one another reasons that can be shared by all reasonable citizens, rather than relying solely on the premises of their own comprehensive doctrines — and the question of how far that requirement extends to religious arguments was left open.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) The first student said that Rawls explicitly permits citizens to offer reasons drawn from religious or other comprehensive doctrines in political discussion, so long as they later supplement those reasons with appropriate public reasons. So, she argued, Rawls's position is permissive, and the oft-repeated prohibition on religious argument in politics is far narrower than is usually supposed. (2) The second student replied that that permission is confined to the informal public arena — opinion, the press, campaign speech — and that on questions of constitutional essentials and basic justice the exercise of public political power must be grounded in public reasons alone. The duty of civility, he said, forbids invoking the premises of one's own comprehensive doctrine to justify the political use of force, so the standard for judges and legislators is much stricter than his interlocutor is making it out to be. (3) The first student then argued that this makes the permission a sham: a legislator may cite her religious convictions on the floor and then append a public-justifying gloss, with the comprehensive doctrine doing the real work in the debate, so Rawls's requirement is a mere formality rather than a genuine constraint. (4) The second student answered that the constraint falls on the ultimate ground of the decision, not on the rhetoric of debate, and that the "in due course" permission is a deferral, not a license — a religious reason has no standing in the political forum unless and until a public reason is offered. The point, he added, is reciprocity: we should exercise power over one another only on grounds that every reasonable citizen can accept. (5) The first student closed by saying that the burdens of judgment show that reasonable people cannot help but bring their comprehensive commitments into politics, and that Rawls's relaxation is simply a recognition that a strictly public-reason discourse is unattainable in a pluralistic democracy.

I could not settle the matter myself, so I would like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, state whether the student is right, partly right, or mistaken, and briefly justify your verdict. Then say what, if anything, both students are missing about the scope and the rationale of Rawls's requirement.
