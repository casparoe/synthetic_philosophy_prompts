I'm a junior taking the philosophy of science seminar this term (the one that does a whole unit on the interpretations of probability) and I'm also a TA for the intro section, so I'm stuck grading this problem set and I want to be fair about it. The question on the set was:

"A radiochemistry lab is characterizing a new isotope. Over a 5-day window they track N = 10,000 atoms and count x = 3,217 decays. Let p be the per-atom decay probability over the window. (a) A frequentist analyst reports a 95% confidence interval for p; a Bayesian colleague using a uniform prior on p reports a 95% credible interval; a third person insists p is a real physical propensity of the isotope. Explain what each of the three is claiming and whether they are making the same empirical claim or different ones. (b) A colleague objects: "The frequentist's 95% is not the probability that p lies in the interval, so the frequentist is not making a probability statement at all — frequentism is incoherent." Evaluate this. (c) Can the propensity interpretation, on its own, justify the frequentist's error estimate?"

Here's what this student wrote:

"(a) The frequentist is saying that if you repeated the experiment a bunch of times and built an interval each time with the same recipe, 95% of those intervals would contain the true p. So the 95% is about the recipe, not this specific interval, and since p is fixed it's not a probability about p. The Bayesian is saying that, given the data and the uniform prior, there's a 95% probability that p is in the reported interval — that one is a probability about p itself. The propensity guy is saying p is a real physical tendency of the isotope, an objective feature of the world, not a frequency and not a belief. In this case all three get roughly the same number for p (about 0.32), so they agree on the estimate; they only differ in what the 95% means. (b) The objection fails because the frequentist is making a probability statement — just not one about p. The 95% is a probability about the procedure (the long-run coverage), which is a legit probability statement, so frequentism isn't incoherent; the objector just misunderstands what the 95% is the probability of. (c) Yes. Since p is a real propensity, the law of large numbers guarentees the relative frequency of decays converges to p as you observe more atoms, and that's what justifies the frequentist's error estimate: the interval is centered on the observed frequency and the LLN tells you how close that is to the true propensity, so the frequentist's 95% is really a physical fact about the isotope's decay tendency. Also, since the Bayesian used a uniform prior, which is the objective (no information) prior, the Bayesian and frequentist are really making the same claim here — the only difference is the interpretation of the 95%."

Can you give it a grade out of 10 and walk me through it part by part — where it's right and where it goes wrong — so I can write comments the student can actually use?
