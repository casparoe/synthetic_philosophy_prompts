I am writing a novel in which a historian uncovers documentary proof that a famous nineteenth-century treaty was never ratified, and she tells a colleague that she has found a truth that was true all along but no one knew. Two epistemologists at a dinner party argue about whether such a discovery is coherent, and I need the scene to be fair to both sides. Please write a dialogue between them: one holds that Fitch's paradox of knowability is valid and forces the conclusion that unknown truths do not exist, so the historian's claim is incoherent — if there is a truth here, it only comes into existence in the moment of discovery; the other holds that the paradox at most shows that not every truth is knowable, pointing to truths that are intrinsically unknowable, such as whether the number of grains of sand on a particular beach at dawn was even, and further suspects the derivation itself, since it turns on a truth that refers to its own unknowability. Neither speaker should be a strawman, and they should engage each other's actual points rather than talk past one another; the dialogue should end unresolved, with each having pressed the other on the weakest step of their argument.
