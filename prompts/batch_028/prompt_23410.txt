I've been covering AI safety for a few years now, and I keep circling back to this 1955 Fortune essay by John von Neumann called "Can We Survive Technology?", which is a bit odd since he's famous to most people for the eponymous game theory and computing work. In it he basically argues that technological progress has become a self-reinforcing force that expands faster than the political and social environment around it can organize, and that this gap is the real crisis, not any single invention. I was talking to a friend last week who works in AI alignment, and she kept framing the whole question of AGI as an engineering problem: build the right training procedures, get the incentives right, and the system behaves. But von Neumann's framing makes me think that gets the level of the problem wrong, because he was talking about a technology that builds its own successor, in a world where no international arrangement can keep up.

So I'd like your take on the most important implications of von Neumann's framework for the current AI situation. I'm not after the obvious "AI is just another nuclear bomb" take, and I'm not asking you to paraphrase the essay back at me. I want the non-obvious consequences: if his picture of technology as an autonomous expanding force is the right lens, what does that do to the case for slow-downs, to how we should think about alignment as a technical program, and to whether the bottleneck is really the models at all? And is there any sense in which his 1955 logic actually breaks down when the technology in question is software that can improve its own development?
