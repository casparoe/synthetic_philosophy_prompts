I host a small podcast where I interview people working at the edges of machine learning, and I've been trying to teach myself the interpretability literature on my own for the better part of a year, mostly because the phrase "the model represents X" keeps showing up in coverage and I wanted to understand what it's supposed to mean. Next month's guest is a researcher who has been arguing, in a few papers and public talks I've tracked, that sparse autoencoders and other dictionary-learning methods are finally letting us recover the "true" monosemantic features of a language model, and that this — rather than eyeballing individual neurons or tracing circuits by hand — is the genuine path to interpretability. I'm a little worried that if I just ask him what he thinks, I'll get a polished restatement of the view and learn nothing new, so could you draft me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual substance of the position rather than inviting him to restate it?

I'd like at least one question on the basis-dependence problem, i.e. the fact that two different sparse autoencoders trained on the very same model can produce quite different feature sets, and what that does to the claim that there is a fact of the matter about which features are the "real" ones the model is actually using. I'd like at least one on the tension with superposition: if the network genuinely stores its information in overlapping, approximately orthogonal directions, then in what sense are the clean, discrete features the autoencoder hands us the network's own features, as opposed to a basis we've imposed from the outside on a representation that was never organized that way to begin with? And I'd like at least one on the causal side — whether ablating a single autoencoder feature actually removes the corresponding concept from the model's behavior, or whether the effect just diffuses across a large number of features so that no single feature is really doing the work. For each question, please give me a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and close the sequence with a question about what kind of evidence would genuinely count against the view, not just make it more complicated.
