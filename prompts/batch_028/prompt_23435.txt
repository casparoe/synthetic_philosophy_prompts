I am putting together a reading pack for my graduate seminar on the foundations of constructive mathematics, and I keep landing on a particular fault line that I would like a careful essay to settle for me. The issue is the status of Markov's principle within intuitionistic logic: the conditional existence statement, for a decidable predicate P over the natural numbers, that if it is not the case that P fails for every n, then P holds for some n. I want the essay to take seriously the disagreement between the two broad constructive camps rather than flattening it into a single "constructivists reject this" verdict. On one side stand the Russian-school and realizability-oriented constructivists, for whom the principle was adopted early and who can justify it by embedding constructive arithmetic into a classical metatheory and observing that an unbounded search, given a predicate that is not everywhere false, will eventually hit a witness. On the other side stand the Brouwerian and Bishop-style constructivists, who worry that the principle smuggles in a form of double-negation elimination and, in its stronger form, comes into tension with Brouwer's creating-subject arguments; note also that a weaker Markov principle, concerning the positivity of a real number, is much more defensible and actually follows from Brouwer's continuity principles, while the stronger form does not. I would like the essay to weigh these considerations fairly, to bring in the subtlety that Markov's rule — the inference from a proof of not-not-exists-n P(n) to a proof of exists-n P(n), for decidable P — is admissible in Heyting arithmetic even though the corresponding axiom is not provable there, and to say something about what that asymmetry between admissibility and provability actually tells us about the principle's standing.

Please reach and defend a conclusion: should a working constructivist, who wants a philosophy of mathematics that is not merely a proof-theoretic restriction of classical mathematics, accept Markov's principle as part of the constructive framework, reject it, or accept only the weaker form? Justify your verdict by engaging with the strongest version of the opposing position, and say explicitly what kind of constructive mathematics — in terms of its intended interpretation of what it means to possess a witness — your position commits one to. I am less interested in a neutral survey than in a reasoned judgment that a careful reader on either side could find defensible.
