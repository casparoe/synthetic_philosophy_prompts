I need a careful argument on the Pasadena paradox, with the aim of locating the paradox not in expected utility as such but in the assumption that every admissible lottery must be representable by a single real-valued expected utility. Please argue that the standard Pasadena lottery—where the positive outcomes 1, 2, 4, ... occur with probabilities 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, ... and the negative outcomes -1, -2, -4, ... occur with the same probabilities—should be treated as a case in which expected utility is undefined because the positive and negative parts of the expectation both diverge, and hence the payoff variable is not integrable, not as a case in which expected utility theory has been shown false. In doing so, explain why the non-absolute convergence of the underlying series makes it impossible to assign a determinate expected value without importing an arbitrary ordering, grouping, or truncation convention, and why different conventions can yield different limiting values.

Then develop the positive case for a restricted-domain or partial-preference account: expected utility can still guide choice for lotteries with well-defined expectations, while Pasadena-like lotteries can be handled by other coherence conditions, such as stochastic dominance, lottery-wise dominance, or rationality constraints inherited from finite approximations. Finally, respond to the objection that such an account may permit cycles or intransitivities in preferences over infinite lotteries. Argue that a partial order, or a total order only over a suitable class of well-behaved lotteries, is a more principled response than either declaring all preferences in these cases irrational or assigning an arbitrary real expected value to make the paradox disappear.
