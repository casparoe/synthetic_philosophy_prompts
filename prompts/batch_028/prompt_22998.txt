I am a novelist, and I am currently working on a long manuscript about the slow transformation of a religious community's ritual life across several generations — how a practice that began with one meaning is gradually drained of that meaning and re-anchored to another, without the community ever making a single clean break. To get the philosophical underpinning of that process right, I have been reading Maimonides' *Guide of the Perplexed*, and I have been drawn to the chapter in which he accounts for the biblical institution of animal sacrifice (Part III, Chapter 32). I am working from the older English translation by Michael Friedländer, whose diction is a little stiff, and I find myself stumbling over a particular passage that I cannot quite parse. I have reproduced it below.

"It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner."

What I cannot resolve is the precise work that the phrase "in the same manner" is doing, and whether the argument as Maimonides states it contains a genuine tension. On the one hand, he says the old worship was directed at "created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal" — which seems to commit him to the view that the old gods were not merely mistaken objects of veneration but literally non-existent, so that the old rites were, from the start, performed toward nothing. On the other hand, he insists that preserving the very forms of those rites (the temple, the altar, the offering, the bowing) was the means by which the "traces of idolatry were blotted out." If the forms are what made the worship a worship of "imaginary and unreal" things, how does keeping the forms intact help establish the "Existence and Unity of God" rather than perpetuating the confusion he wants to dissolve? And how should I read the hypothetical prophet — the one who would command us to serve God "in thought, and not by any action" — in this context? Is Maimonides gesturing at a final ideal in which ritual is abandoned altogether, with the sacrificial forms serving only as a temporary scaffold that the community is meant to outgrow? I would be grateful for a reading that untangles these threads rather than summarizing the chapter at large.
