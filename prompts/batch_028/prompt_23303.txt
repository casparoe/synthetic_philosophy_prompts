I've been re-reading the anthropics literature — Bostrom's doomsday argument work, the Dieks and Olum rebuttals, and a few of the follow-up blog posts — and I keep tripping over the SSA/SIA distinction, so I'd be grateful if someone could straighten this out for me. My working understanding, which I'm not fully confident in, is that the self-indication assumption is the one that powers the doomsday argument: you treat your own existence as if you were a random sample drawn from the set of all observers who will ever exist, and since you show up relatively early in the birth-rank sequence, that is evidence that the total number of observers is small. The self-sampling assumption, by contrast, is the principle that you should, all else being equal, favor the hypothesis with more observers, and that is the one Bostrom exploits in the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment, where a philosopher uses it to conclude that a cosmological theory predicting a trillion trillion trillion observers is a trillion times more probable than a rival theory predicting a trillion trillion, even though the non-anthropic evidence had the two tied just before a cheap decisive experiment was about to be run.

Here is where I get stuck, and I suspect I have something backwards. If the self-sampling assumption is just "prefer the big world," and the non-anthropic evidence already assigns equal credence to the two theories, then the philosopher's move looks like it might actually be the correct Bayesian update — you have the fact that observers exist, and that fact should push you toward the hypothesis containing more of them. I can see why people find the scenario "presumptuous," but I genuinely do not see why the conclusion is wrong, and I would be curious what the standard response to that charge is. Am I missing something about how the assumption is supposed to interact with the independent evidence?
