I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of economics, and I've been working through the money-pump literature for a seminar on diachronic rationality; a colleague in game theory keeps insisting that the standard pump argument against intransitive preferences is far weaker than it's usually presented as, and I've been trying to figure out whether he's right. As I understand the argument, it runs roughly as follows. Suppose an agent has intransitive preferences over three options, A, B, and C, such that A is preferred to B, B to C, and C back to A. A trader can then offer a sequence of swaps arranged in a cycle so that, at each step, the option offered is one the agent strictly prefers to the one it currently holds; the agent pays a small fee for each swap, and after completing the cycle it is back with the original option but poorer. Since no rational agent should be willing to pay to go in a circle, the conclusion is drawn that the intransitive preference ordering itself is irrational.

Here's where I get stuck. The argument seems to quietly assume that the agent will, at each stage, act on the pairwise preference that is salient at that moment, without factoring in the fact that the "upgrades" will eventually loop back. But a sufficiently sophisticated agent — one who can foresee the whole cycle and commit in advance to a rule like "I will not accept a swap that returns me to an option I have already held" — would simply decline the swap that closes the loop (or, better, decline to engage with the trader at all), and the pump would fail. If that's right, then the pump doesn't show that intransitivity is irrational simpliciter; it shows only that intransitivity combined with a myopic, non-committing disposition is irrational. So I suspect the argument is either circular — assuming the very diachronic irrationality it is meant to establish — or it is really an argument about the costs of being unable to bind one's future self, rather than about intransitivity as such.

Could you help me sort out which of these diagnoses is correct? In particular: (a) on the standard reading, does the money-pump argument purport to condemn intransitive preferences regardless of the agent's ability to commit, or is its force limited to "naive" agents who cannot foresee or resist the cycle? (b) If a committing agent can evade the pump, does that rescue intransitive preferences from the charge of irrationality, or is there a further argument — perhaps a more sophisticated pump, or an appeal to some independence or consistency principle — that re-captures them? And (c) how does the diachronic-rationality literature typically draw the line between a genuine objection to intransitivity and a mere consequence of the agent's inability to commit?
