I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of computation, so I may be asking a naive question. I have not been able to pin down the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis: it is presented as a profound result, yet it looks to me like little more than a definition of what we mean by an effectively calculable procedure. If it is a definition, how can it be true or false, and what could count as evidence against it? Could you give me a concrete example of a proposed device — an analog computer, a machine that performs a supertask, or something similar — that purports to compute a function no Turing machine can, and show me exactly where the argument for its hypercomputation breaks down? I am especially interested in how this bears on the distinction between the mathematical Church–Turing thesis and the physical Church–Turing thesis, and whether the latter is an empirical claim at all.
