I'm in my third year and currently taking a philosophy of perception seminar, and next week we're hosting a visiting scholar who defends disjunctivism about perception — the view that veridical seeing and a matching hallucination are not instances of the same kind of mental state, even when the two are subjectively indistinguishable. I've been asked to moderate the post-lecture Q&A, and I'd like to prepare questions that press on the actual content of the view rather than letting the guest restate it. The guest is quite skilled at deflecting the standard objections; in particular, every time someone raises the point that a hallucination and a veridical perception can be phenomenally identical, they answer with a line to the effect that "the way things seem to the subject isn't the whole story about the kind of state one is in," and then move on. I was discussing with my classmates how that reply keeps getting reused, and I'd like a sequence of questions that makes that particular deflection progressively harder to deploy.

Could you draft something like six to eight questions, each with a short follow-up keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give? I'd especially like at least one question on the "common kind" assumption — whether the disjunctivist is really entitled to deny that there is any mental state common to the veridical and the hallucinatory case, or whether the view merely relocates the commonality into the external object — and one on the epistemic side, i.e., the idea that in genuine perception one can know that the object is present, and whether that knowledge is something the subject could ever recognize from the inside. I'm also curious what the view implies about "safe" versus "unsafe" (brain-in-a-vat-style) cases, and whether disjunctivism is meant to answer perceptual skepticism or quietly concedes the skeptic's point. Please end the sequence with a question about what kind of evidence — empirical or otherwise — could actually count against the view, since the guest tends to treat that as "not the sort of question that decides the debate."
