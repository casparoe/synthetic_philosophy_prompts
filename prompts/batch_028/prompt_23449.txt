I've been arguing with a friend about Kavka's toxin puzzle for most of an evening without converging, so I have written up my position and would value your comments on it.

The setup, as I understand it: a wealthy eccentric offers you one million dollars on the condition that you intend, at midnight tonight, to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you ill for a day but will do no lasting harm. The money will already be in your account by the time the afternoon comes, and you are free not to drink. The puzzle is that it seems you ought to intend to drink, since that secures the money at no cost to you, but you cannot intend to drink, since you rationally will not drink once you have been paid, and one cannot sincerely intend what one knows one will not do.

My friend takes this to be a genuine paradox about the limits of rational intention: the only reason to form the intention is a reason that is defeated by the very formation of the intention, so the intention is self-defeating and ought not to be formed. I think this misdiagnoses the puzzle. My claim is that it conflates two distinct kinds of intention, and that once we separate them, the puzzle dissolves.

The first kind is a closed intention, in the sense Bratman develops in his planning theory of intention: a commitment that screens out further deliberation about the relevant question and serves as a plan to which other plans attach. The second kind is an open intention: one that the agent forms while explicitly retaining the right to re-examine it when circumstances change. The distinction matters here, because the circumstances do change; the money is paid in the morning, before the afternoon action.

On this distinction, the closed intention to drink is indeed irrational, but not for the reason my friend gives. Because a closed intention screens out further deliberation, it would commit the agent to ignore the decisive reason not to drink, namely that drinking is a net loss once the money has been received. The intention thus defeats itself: the very feature that makes it an intention, its closure of deliberation, guarantees that it will not track the agent's reasons. That is a genuine defect, but it is a defect of the closed intention, not of intention as such.

The open intention to drink, by contrast, is rational. The agent forms it at midnight, knowing that she will re-examine it in the afternoon once she has been paid. It secures the money, and it is compatible with her rationally deciding not to drink when the afternoon comes. The only obstacle to this solution is the assumption that the eccentric requires a closed intention. But his own stipulation, that you need not drink, shows that he anticipates the agent might not drink, and so his condition must be satisfiable by an open intention. If he required a closed intention, the offer would be irrational on its face, since no rational agent could accept it.

So my conclusion is that the toxin puzzle is not a paradox about the limits of rational intention. It is a case in which a condition is rational to satisfy in one way, by forming an open intention, and not in another, by forming a closed intention. The apparent paradox arises only from conflating the two.

I would be grateful for comments on three specific points. First, does the open/closed distinction actually do the work I claim, or is it merely relabeling the standard revisability solution? Second, is the diagnosis of the closed intention correct, that it defeats itself by screening out the very consideration that defeats it? And third, am I reading the eccentric's condition too generously in treating it as satisfiable by an open intention, or does the puzzle require that he demand a closed intention in order to be a puzzle at all?
