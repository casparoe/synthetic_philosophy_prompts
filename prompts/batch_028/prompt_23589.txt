I chair the acquisitions and display committee at a mid-sized public art museum in the Midwest, and I've been turning a case over in my mind ever since a long, somewhat heated argument I had last weekend with a friend who is a philosopher of art and who happens to be visiting from a university nearby. We ended up arguing, over coffee, about whether the value of a work of art depends on its being the original or whether a skilled copy can have value in its own right, and I found that I didn't have a clear answer, which is exactly the kind of thing I need to sort out now, because the committee faces a concrete version of that question. The case: our museum holds a small 17th-century painting — a quiet interior scene with a window and a half-eaten loaf of bread — that has been catalogued for decades as a work by a minor Dutch genre painter, and it has hung in our main gallery for years. A private collector has now offered us a second painting of the same composition, identical in subject and nearly identical in size and style, which our own technical department has confirmed to be a copy made in the minor master's workshop during his lifetime, in excellent condition. The catch: our museum's own painting (the one catalogued as the master's work) has suffered serious varnish discoloration and a small tear, and it is currently in storage for treatment that will take at least a year. During that year, the committee must decide what to display in the gallery where the master's painting usually hangs. The options on the table are: (a) display the newly offered copy in the master's usual location, with a label that identifies it as a copy from the master's workshop; (b) leave the space empty or fill it with a work by a different artist; (c) acquire the copy and display it in a secondary location with a label that makes clear it is not by the master; (d) decline the copy and leave the space empty.

I need you to draft the committee's written opinion — a short memo that I will circulate to the full committee before we vote next month. The memo should recommend one of these options (or a clear variant) with a concrete time frame for any follow-up steps, and it should engage the philosophical issue at the heart of the disagreement: whether and why the value of a work of art depends on its being the original (the master's own hand) rather than a skilled copy, and whether that dependence holds when the original is temporarily unavailable. The memo should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it, and it should be written in a formal tone appropriate for a museum's internal governance documents.
