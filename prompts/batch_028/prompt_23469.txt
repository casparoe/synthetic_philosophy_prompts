A few days ago, our student council committee was deciding whether to start an after-school sports league, and two members got into an argument that I was not able to settle. The decision, as we framed it, depends on two findings: first, whether the league would actually increase student exercise, and second, whether the cost fits within our budget. We all agreed that if both findings hold, then we should start the league.

We then voted on each of the three questions separately. The result was that a majority of the committee said the league would increase exercise, a majority said the cost fits the budget, and yet a majority voted against starting the league.

Alex said that this settles it: since the majority endorsed both findings, the conclusion follows, so we should start the league, and anyone who voted against it was simply being inconsistent. Jordan pushed back and said the two findings are only steps toward the real question, which is whether we should start the league, and that the majority clearly answered no, so we should not start it. Alex replied that by Jordan's standard we could reject a well-supported policy merely because people dislike the proposal, when in fact the evidence should decide. Jordan answered that by Alex's standard we could be forced into doing something that every single member voted against, just because two side questions happened to win by narrow margins, and that the actual decision is what matters.

Neither of them would give ground, and I am honestly not sure which one is more right. Could you go through their exchange and say who is right at each step, rather than just who wins overall? And is there something both of them are missing about what actually happened when the committee voted?
