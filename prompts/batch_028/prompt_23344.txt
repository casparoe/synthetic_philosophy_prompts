I'm a retired physicist, and in the last couple of years I've been working my way into the philosophy of mathematics, which has been a humbling experience. The dispute between classical and intuitionistic (constructive) mathematics keeps coming back to me. It first surfaced when I was reading a popular history of the foundations of the subject, where Brouwer's quarrel with Hilbert was sketched in a few pages, and I found myself wanting a more careful, even-handed treatment of the technical substance behind the dispute. As a physicist I have relied on classical real analysis and the intermediate value theorem without a second thought for most of my career, so I find it genuinely strange that intuitionists regard some of these standard moves as illegitimate, and I'd like to understand the dispute on its own technical terms rather than through the personalities involved.

I would like you to write an essay that weighs this controversy fairly. On one side, present the intuitionist/constructivist case: what the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation is supposed to say about what it means to prove an existence statement, why the law of excluded middle and double-negation elimination are rejected, and what the intuitionists take to be wrong with a proof that shows an object must exist without supplying a method for finding it. Please ground this in a concrete example; for instance, contrast the classical intermediate value theorem—the assertion that a continuous function on a closed interval whose endpoint values have opposite signs must attain zero somewhere in between—with what a constructivist can legitimately conclude from the same hypotheses, and explain why the gap between the two arises. On the other side, present the classical defense: why mathematicians, and working scientists, have found the law of excluded middle and non-constructive existence proofs indispensable, and what the standard classical reply is to the charge that such proofs are empty or merely verbal.

Please do not leave the essay hanging. I want you to reach a definite conclusion about which side of the dispute is more defensible and to justify it. You are welcome to take a nuanced position—for example, that the classical and the constructive logics are best understood as suited to different purposes, or that the right answer depends on what one takes mathematics to be for—but you must commit to a verdict and defend it, rather than ending on a non-committal note.
