I spent thirty-odd years in population genetics and systematics, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology with a mixture of amusement and dismay. The concept I have used every day in my own work, the species, turns out to be, apparently, incoherent, and I cannot yet tell whether that is a genuine discovery or a philosopher's sleight of hand. I would like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology, each of whom is competent and charitable toward the other's position.

The first defends species pluralism: there is no single correct species concept; the Biological, Phylogenetic, Morphological, and other concepts are each legitimate within their own domain, and much of the species problem is a pseudo-problem generated by the expectation that there should be one definition that fits everything. The second holds that the species problem is real and substantive, and should press the pluralist on specific pressure points: the transitivity failure in ring species, in which a chain of interbreeding populations connects two end populations that cannot interbreed with each other; the case of asexual and clonal lineages, for which the Biological species concept has no obvious purchase; and the worry that if the boundary of species is as context-dependent as the pluralist allows, then moral arguments that attach significance to species membership — for instance, the claim that being a member of our own species is what confers full moral status — inherit the same instability.

I want real back-and-forth rather than two monologues: the pluralist should respond to each specific case on its own terms, and the problem-taker should engage with the pluralist's best replies — for example, that different species concepts can be mapped onto one another, or that moral status can attach to individual capacities rather than to a species boundary — rather than simply restating the objection. You may end the dialogue without either side conceding, but by the end each should have identified a specific point on which the other's position is most vulnerable.
