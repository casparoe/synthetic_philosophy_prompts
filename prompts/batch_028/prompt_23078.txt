I was covering a decision-theory workshop for a piece on choice under uncertainty, and two participants got into a long exchange over the "absent-minded driver" problem that I couldn't fully follow. Here is what I was able to reconstruct.

The setup, as I understood it: a driver must leave a highway at the second of two consecutive exits, but her memory is so unreliable that when she reaches either exit she has no idea which one she is at or whether she has already passed the first. One researcher, Lena, argued the whole thing is a phantom dilemma. She said the driver should be modeled as making a single decision at the start — choosing a *plan*, a rule that says "continue at the first exit, get off at the second" — and that once you frame it that way the expected-utility calculation is unambiguous and the so-called paradox simply disappears.

The other researcher, Marco, pushed back. He said that framing smuggles in exactly the information the problem is supposed to withhold: a plan that says "continue at the first, get off at the second" only makes sense if the driver can tell, at the moment of deciding, which exit she is actually facing, and the entire point of the setup is that she cannot. So he insisted the driver faces two genuinely separate decisions, each made under the same self-locating uncertainty, and that the correct move is to evaluate "get off now" versus "keep driving" at each exit on its own. He argued that when you do that, the two evaluations point in opposite directions, which is why the problem is a real paradox and not an artifact of sloppy framing.

Lena replied that Marco is confusing the agent's knowledge with the modeler's knowledge. From the outside, the modeler knows there are two exits and can write down a plan that distinguishes them; the driver does not need to, because she is just following a committed rule, the way you follow a recipe without needing to understand the chemistry. The driver's failure to identify the exit is irrelevant, because the plan was chosen before the uncertainty arose.

Marco answered that this is exactly the mistake. A rule you follow "without understanding" still has to be *applicable* at the moment of action, and a rule that says "if first exit, continue; if second exit, get off" is not applicable to someone who cannot tell which exit she is at. So either the plan approach quietly assumes the driver can identify the exit (and thus misstates the problem), or it assumes she can somehow execute a conditional she has no way to evaluate (and is incoherent). Either way, the one-shot evaluation at each exit is the only honest model, and the resulting tension is a genuine feature of decision-making under self-locating uncertainty, not a bug.
