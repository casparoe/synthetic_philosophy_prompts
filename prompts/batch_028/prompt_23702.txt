I've been reading Zhuangzi off and on for a few months now, mostly on my own, and I keep getting stuck on his attitude toward death. The story I keep coming back to is the one where his wife dies and instead of mourning he sits there drumming on a tub and singing, and when his friend Hui Shi calls him out for being inhumane, he basically explains that if you trace things back, before she was born there was no life, then no form, then no qi, and then all of that gradually transformed into life, and now it's transforming back into death, just like teh four seasons cycling, so to sit there wailing would be to not understand the way things go. It reminds me of the butterfly dream thing, where he dreams he's a butterfly and can't tell if he's a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he's a man — same kind of "transformation" logic, but applied to self rather than death. I get the story, I even kind of like it, but I definately don't see the argument.

Here's my specific confusion: even if I grant the whole transformation thing, I don't see why that's supposed to change how I feel when someone I love dies. The transformation is happening whether or not I "see" it or "get the bigger picture." So is Zhuangzi making a metaphysical claim — like, death isn't really an ending, so there's nothing to grieve about — or is he making a psychological/practical claim — like, if you reframe death as a natural transformation, your grief will just go away? And if it's the second one, that feels less like a real philosophy of death and more like a coping technique or a rhetorical move. I'd really like someone to sort out what the actual conceptual move is supposed to be, and whether it's even coherent, because I keep feeling like there's a gap between "life and death are transformations" and "therefore you shouldn't grieve" that Zhuangzi just papers over.
