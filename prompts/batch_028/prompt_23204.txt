I'm in a course on interpretability of neural nets and two of my classmates got into it about whether the features we find with sparse autoencoders are real or not, and I honestly cant tell who's right.

Sam said the features are real: when you train a sparse autoencoder on a model's activations and you get a feature that lights up on, say, mentions of a particular author, that's a genuine representation the model has of that author. The model really does represent that concept, we've just discovered it. So there's a fact of the matter about what the model represents, and our job is to find it.

Alex pushed back and said that's not right — the features are only as real as the decoder. A sparse autoencoder is just one particular decomposition, and if you changed the decoder or the regularization you'd get different features. So there's no fact of the matter about the model's representations, just lots of possible decompositions, and the SAE features are one convenient one. The model doesn't have representations in the way Sam means.

Sam replied that Alex is setting up a false dichotomy: yes different decoders give different decompositions, but that doesn't mean there's no fact of the matter. The model's behavior is fixed, and the right decomposition is the one that actually explains the behavior causally — if you ablate the feature and the model stops doing the thing, that's evidence the feature is real and not just a decoder artifact.

Alex said but causal relevance is itself theory-laden — you have to already have a hypothesis about what the feature is supposed to do before you can ablate it and see if it matters. So you can't use causal patching to discover representations, only to test hypotheses you already have. Which means the realism about representations is more modest than Sam thinks.

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about each specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
