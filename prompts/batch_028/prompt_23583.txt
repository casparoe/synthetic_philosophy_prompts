I spent my career working under the assumption that a complete physical description amounts to specifying the qualitative properties of the things in the world — masses, charges, distances, field values — and that if two things agreed on every such property, there would simply be no further fact of the matter about whether they were one thing or two. It was only recently, having turned to metaphysics in retirement, that I came across Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment, and I find it considerably more unsettling than I had expected. I have since been trying to sort out the two replies to it that seem to me most worth taking seriously, and I would be grateful for a careful comparison.

The first is what I have come to call the haecceity or primitive-thismessness line: even in Black's perfectly symmetric universe, the two spheres differ in some brute, non-qualitative respect — each is, as it were, precisely that sphere and not the other — and it is this difference that allows the identity of indiscernibles to survive. The second is the relational-properties line: the spheres are in fact discernible once we count their relations to other particular objects, and the principle only appears to fail if we artificially restrict ourselves to monadic, intrinsic properties.

Could you compare these two replies in detail? I am especially interested in three points. First, does either of them genuinely preserve the identity of indiscernibles, or does each merely render the principle trivially or vacuously true? Second, in Black's symmetric two-sphere universe specifically, does the relational-properties reply have any real work to do, or does it quietly collapse into the haecceity reply? And third, as someone trained in physics I find myself drawn to the relational reply, so I would particularly value an honest account of where it is weakest.
