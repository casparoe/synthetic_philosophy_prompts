I am a novelist working on a long novel set in a fictional academy for children, and in the current draft the academy's governing body has to settle a dispute over its own rules of discipline, so I need the document to read as something that body could actually vote on. The academy's founding charter is a paraphrase of Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* (1693), and the two factions on the governance committee are each quoting the same book back at the other. The "habit engine" faction insists on Locke's claim that habit is the true foundation of all that follows and must be worked in while the child is still young and pliable, and therefore that the tutor's whole art is the management of rewards, punishments, and above all the child's dread of disgrace, which Locke treats as the strongest lever of all. The "reason curriculum" faction insists on Locke's other claim, that reason is the faculty that separates man from the brute and is the end toward which all education should aim, and therefore that the academy should spend its hours teaching children to deliberate, weigh, and judge for themselves. Both sides are, as far as I can tell, quoting Locke correctly, which is exactly what makes the deadlock so embarrassing for the committee.

I would like you to write the short written opinion I will circulate before the committee's next sitting. It should recommend a single, concrete course of action for the academy's disciplinary policy, with a time frame for putting it in place, and it should take the form of a memo the body could act on rather than a survey of the literature. The recommendation must do three things at once: it must keep Locke's stated end, the cultivation of reason, as the governing aim of the whole programme; it must decide the proper status of shame and of the other punishments, neither abolishing them on principle nor endorsing the habit faction's near-total reliance on them; and it must face up honestly to the committee's own history, since the academy has, in practice, always disciplined by shame and the new policy will be judged against that record. The most important test of the opinion is that it answers the question the two factions are not asking: if reason is the end, why does Locke need shame at all, and does a programme that "simply teaches reason" survive contact with his own account of the young mind, on which reason is a weak engine in the child and cannot yet do the work the reason curriculum faction wants it to do?

In the opinion, please give a fair and serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side of your recommendation, not a straw man of it, and say plainly where your recommendation departs from what Locke himself would have approved, since the charter is his and the committee will notice any drift. I want the voice to be that of a committee member who is genuinely wedded to the text and is trying to make two of its most important claims work together, not a commentator summarizing the book. Keep it to roughly a page and a half, with a clear bottom line in the opening lines and the reasoning underneath.
