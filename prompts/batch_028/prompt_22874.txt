Alan Turing died in 1954, before computational complexity theory had developed, and so he never had to grapple with the distinction it draws between problems that are merely computable and those that are computable efficiently, in polynomial time. A central consequence of this theory is the existence of NP-complete problems, whose solutions can be verified quickly but for which no efficient method of finding solutions is known, giving rise to the still-open question of whether P equals NP. Suppose Turing were alive today to learn all of this. How do you think he would have reacted? In particular, consider the observation that human beings appear able to solve some NP-complete problems quickly, even though no efficient algorithm for them is known. Would Turing have taken this as evidence that the mind relies on some non-computational process, or would he have insisted that it is still computable — perhaps merely not yet captured by an efficient algorithm — and how would this judgment bear on the arguments he advanced in his 1950 paper about whether machines can think?
