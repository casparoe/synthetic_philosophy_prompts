I've been reading about Maimonides' views on animal sacrifice, and I keep running into what I take to be a genuine contradiction in his thought, and I was hoping you could help me sort it out. In his Guide of the Perplexed, he has a well-known discussion in which he explains that God did not originally intend animal sacrifice at all, and that the Torah's sacrificial laws were essentially a strategic concession to the ancient peoples: since they were already accustomed to slaughtering and burning animals in honor of their gods, Moses was commanded to tolerate and redirect that practice, gradually weaning the people away from idolatry. From this I infer that Maimonides privately regards the whole sacrificial system as spiritually empty — a kind of political theater that a philosopher has no real use for. But then I noticed that in his own legal code, the Mishneh Torah, he devotes an entire book to the precise procedure of the sacrifices: which part of the altar the blood is thrown on, what defects disqualify an animal, how the priests must handle the entrails, and so on. I also gathered that he holds that when the Temple is eventually rebuilt, the sacrificial service will be fully restored.

My question is how we are supposed to reconcile these two sides of his writing. If the sacrifices are really just a pedagogical trick for a primitive people, what exactly is the point of codifying the ritual in such minute detail, and why treat it as something that ought to come back in the future rather than something that should quietly disappear? Is there a standard way to resolve this tension, or is Maimonides simply being inconsistent?
