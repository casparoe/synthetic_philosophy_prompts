Two people I know got stuck in a long argument about whether Confucian role ethics is actually better than the usual universalist/Kantian approach, and I honestly couldnt tell who was making the better points, so I wanted a referee.

Alex started by saying role ethics — the Confucian idea that you basically are your relationships (parent, child, friend, colleague) and your duties flow out of those specific roles — is more honest and more humane than universalism, which treats every person as an equal abstract moral agent with the same duties to everyone. Universalism, Alex said, strips away what actually makes us who we are; you can't and shouldn't love your kid and a random stranger the same, and pretending the moral weight is equal is just a distortion. The particularity of relationships is a feature, not a bug.

Jordan pushed back that this is just special pleading with a cultural veneer. If your duties are set by your role, the content of morality depends on what station you happen to be born into, and the classic Confucian role set (ruler/subject, father/son, husband/wife) is explicitly hierarchical and bakes in inequality. So "role ethics" is really "the ethics of your social position," which is a recipe for oppression, not ethics.

Alex said Jordan was strawmanning. Role ethics doesnt say the hierarchy is arbitrary or fixed forever; each role has its own virtue (a ruler should be benevolent, a son filial) and the ties are reciprocal — a ruler who fails to be benevolent loses his mandate of heaven. The point isn't "obey your station," it's "be the person your relationships call you to be." And a father-son bond being different from a friendship isn't a flaw, it's just what it's like to be a person embedded in a web of ties.

Jordan replied that reciprocity doesn't rescue it, because the relationships are asymmetric by definition — a parent-child tie isn't between equals, and Confucianism is explicit that the child owes the parent a different, more deferential obligation than the reverse. So even granting "reciprocity," the theory still ranks some people's claims above others' based on the role they occupy relative to you, which is exactly the partiality universalism was supposed to rule out. And the mandate-of-heaven bit is just a mythological pressure valve; it doesn't give you a principle for what the right hierarchy is.

Alex then said universalism has a problem Jordan is ignoring: it can't actually account for the fact that we do have stronger obligations to some people, and when it tries (impartiality plus special obligations as an exception), it just treats those special obligations as an embarrassing patch on a theory that's really about strangers. Role ethics starts from the actual structure of moral life. And the oppression charge proves too much: by that logic, any theory that says you owe more to your family than to a stranger is oppressive — including Jordan's.

Jordan's last word was that no, because in universalism the default is equal concern for everyone and special obligations are limited, justified exceptions, not the foundation. In role ethics the foundation IS the unequal web, so unequal treatment is the default and equal treatment is the exception. That's the difference, and it matters.
