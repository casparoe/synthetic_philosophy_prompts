I'm on the clinical ethics committee at a large academic medical center, and we've got a case that's kinda stuck the committee because it's not your normal capacity question. A 34-year-old patient, let's call them Sam, was diagnosed with a fast-growing but currently localized tumor. The oncology team's recommendation is standard treatment: surgical resection followed by several months of chemotherapy. It's the high-probability path — something like a 90% five-year survival — but it's brutal: a major operation, then months of chemo with the usual side effects, and a long stretch where Sam will be pretty unwell. There's also an open experimental immunotherapy trial that's less invasive and, if it works, would clear the tumor with minimal scarring, but it's riskier and less proven, with a real chance of serious adverse effects.

Sam is fully competent, well-informed, and has clearly thought this through. They've refused the recommended surgery-plus-chemo and are demanding enrollment in the risky trial. Their stated reasoning is the quantum immortality one — the idea that comes out of Tegmark's quantum suicide setup — namely that under many-worlds there's always a branch where they survive any life-threatening event, so death has no real disvalue for them subjectively and they're effectively immortal. On that basis they argue they have no reason to avoid the riskier option, and that they should just pick the path that gives the best outcome in the best branch, since the branches where they don't make it are the ones they'll never experience. They're not delusional in the clinical sense, they can articulate the argument coherently, and they keep pushing back on "but you could end up in a branch where you're alive but in agony or a vegetable." I've been reading the rationalist stuff for a while so I'm not totally lost on the many-worlds part, but I'm not a physicist and I don't want to pretend to settle the physics here.

I need to circulate a short written opinion to the rest of the committee before we vote next week, and I want it to actually land. Could you draft that memo for me? It should give a clear bottom-line recommendation — do we honor Sam's choice, do we recommend the standard treatment anyway, or do we go some conditional route like a second opinion, a capacity re-evaluation, or a defined window before we proceed — and it should walk through the reasoning so the other commitee members can follow it. In particular I want you to engage Sam's quantum immortality argument on the merits rather than just waving it away as a weird belief: where exactly does that reasoning hold up, and where does it break? I'm especially interested in the move from "I can't die" to "therefore I should take the risky option," and in whether survival-in-some-branch is even the right thing to optimize for. And please give an honest treatment of the strongest case for the side you end up not recommending, because I don't want the memo to look like it's just steamrolling a competent adult's autonomy.
