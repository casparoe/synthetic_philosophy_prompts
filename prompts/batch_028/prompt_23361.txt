Student's answer:

"The Frege-Geach problem is about the fact that moral vocabulary appears in embedded contexts, especially as the antecedent of conditionals, in ordinary moral reasoning. Consider 'If lying is wrong, then lying to save a life is wrong.' A simple attitude-expressionist holds that to assert 'lying is wrong' is just to express an attitude of disapproval toward lying. The problem is that it's unclear what attitude, if any, the whole conditional expresses, so the expressionist can't explain how moral reasoning, which runs through such conditionals, works. Geach's point was that moral terms must be used with the same meaning in embedded and unembedded contexts, or else the argument is invalid.

Blackburn's response is to treat moral sentences as expressing plans: 'If p, then q' expresses a plan to take up an attitude toward q if one has an attitude toward p. It's an elegant move, and I think it works. The main cost is that we have to give up the idea that moral sentences can be true, which is a price a lot of people are willing to pay."
