I'm teaching a graduate seminar on epistemology, and two of my students have been locked in a sustained dispute over Fitch's paradox of knowability. It began when one of them, Alex, circulated a draft paper defending the knowability principle, and the other, Jamie, wrote a reply arguing the principle is self-defeating. They've been exchanging letters for several weeks, and I'd like your help adjudicating where each of them is right and where each is mistaken.

Let me set out the paradox as they understand it. The knowability principle says: for every truth p, it is possible that p is known. Now suppose p is a truth that is not in fact known. Then the proposition "p and not-Kp" is true—it asserts that p holds and that p is unknown. By the principle, it must be possible that "p and not-Kp" is known. But if an agent knew "p and not-Kp," the agent would know p, which makes "not-Kp" false, so the conjunction couldn't be known after all. Hence a truth of that form is unknowable, contradicting the principle. So the principle is false.

Alex's reply is that the paradox rests on an idealization that is doing all the work: the knowledge operator K is being treated as if it applied to a perfectly logically omniscient agent who already knows every consequence of what she knows. Real agents, she says, are not like that, and once we interpret K as knowledge by an ideal agent who closes under logical consequence, the self-undermining proposition simply falls out of the scope of what the principle is supposed to license, and the paradox dissolves. Jamie objects that this move fails. The proposition "p and not-Kp" is still within the range of what an ideal agent could know, she argues, so the principle restricted to ideal agents still collapses into the claim that every truth is in fact known by such an agent; and a principle that quietly entails the omniscience of ideal agents is not a substantive thesis about knowability at all, but a disguised form of the very idealism it was meant to avoid.

Please adjudicate this exchange. Whose argument is stronger on the technical points, and what, if anything, is the genuine resolution of the paradox that both of them are missing? I'd like you to be specific about the role of the idealization and about whether restricting the scope of K actually blocks the inference or merely relocates it.
