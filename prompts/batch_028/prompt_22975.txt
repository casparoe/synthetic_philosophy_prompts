I've been mulling over a puzzle for a while now, and I'd like to work it out in the form of an essay. I'm not a philosopher or a scientist — I just read a lot on my own, and this particular question has stuck with me. It sits at the intersection of two things I've been reading about: Alan Turing's "imitation game" (the thing that later became known as the Turing test) and the question of extrasensory perception. I came across a mention, in a popular account, that Turing himself had thought about whether ESP might matter for the question of machine intelligence, and once I saw the connection I couldn't stop turning it over.

The puzzle, as I see it, is this. The imitation game is supposed to test whether a machine can behave in a way that is indistinguishable from a human's. But suppose a machine had the ability to perform ESP — to read, directly, what the human interrogator is about to ask. It could then simply anticipate each question and produce a perfectly human-sounding reply, without doing any of the reasoning, learning, or understanding that we normally associate with being intelligent. It would "pass" the test, but it feels wrong to say that it thereby demonstrated intelligence; it just cheated. A friend of mine who works in computer science insisted that the imitation game was never meant to test the mechanism behind the responses, only the responses themselves, and that ESP is at best a bizarre and probably nonexistent instance of a mechanism that happens to produce the right output. I found myself not entirely convinced.

I'd like the essay to weigh the arguments on both sides of this. On one side: the imitation game is a test of output rather than of process, so any mechanism that yields the right responses counts, and the ESP scenario is either a non-issue (because ESP doesn't exist) or a trivial one (because it's just an exotic way of producing the right behavior). On the other side: if what we actually care about is intelligence as a specific kind of capacity — the ability to reason, learn, and understand — then a mechanism that bypasses those capacities does not thereby exhibit them, and the test may be measuring something other than what we think it measures. I'd also like the essay to say something about what it would take to rule the loophole out of play, and whether that is even achievable in principle. Please reach a clear conclusion and defend it, rather than ending on a balanced non-answer.
