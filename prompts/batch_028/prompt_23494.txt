Two of my lab-mates got into it at the fairness reading group about the impossibility result — the one where, if the base rate of the positive outcome differs across groups, no nontrivial classifier can be both calibrated (equal positive predictive value) and have equalized odds (equal true and false positive rates) for all groups.

Sam started by saying the result is a real constraint, not a technicality. His point is that when base rates genuinely differ, satisfying calibration literally entails violating equalized odds and vice versa, so it's not a smooth trade-off you can just dial in — it's a sharp either/or, which means we have to explicitly pick which group to be unfair to, and pretending some metric sidesteps that is just dishonest.

Riley pushed back that the theorem is almost trival — it's just the arithmetic of conditional probabilities given that the base rates differ, so it doesn't really tell you anything new. And more importantly, Riley said, it only applies to group-based statistical criteria. The right move, Riley argued, is to abandon statistical fairness and go to individual or counterfactual fairness (treat similar individuals similarly), which the impossibility result simply doesn't reach.

Sam replied that fleeing to counterfactual fairness just relocates the problem. To even define "similar individuals" you have to pick a counterfactual distribution over the features, and that choice itself encodes values (like which features count as invariant under the intervention). On top of that, there's a line of results showing that strong individual/counterfactual fairness — invariance to the protected attribute — is incompatable with accuracy, so the classifier has to be basically constant. So counterfactual fairness doesn't escape the trade-off, it just hides it.

Riley conceded the counterfactual stuff is technically messy but said that doesn't rescue Sam's framing. The theorem, Riley said, is a mathematical fact about the tension between specific criteria, not a moral fact that one group must be treated unfairly. It just forces us to make the value judgment explicit, which is a good thing. And whether the trade-off is "sharp" or "smooth" is an empirical question about the data, not something the theorem settles — in practice you move along a frontier and pick a point.
