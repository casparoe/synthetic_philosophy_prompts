I'm putting together a long-form piece on the philosophy of free will and I've finally gotten around to this thing called Molinism, named after the 16th-century Jesuit Luis de Molina. I think I've got the core idea: God has this this middle knowledge by which he knows what any free person would do if they were placed in any given circumstance, even circumstances that never actually happen. Fine, I can follow that much. What I keep getting stuck on is the word "middle" itself. My working assumption is that "middle" is just about the degree of certainty — like, God is fully certain about what actually happens in the real world, but he only has a "middle," more probabilistic confidence about what free creatures would have done in all those counterfactual situations that never got instantiated. So the whole idea is basically God's best educated guess at free will, right? I can't shake the feeling that if that's the framework, it's not really all that different from ordinary foreknowledge, just with a confidence meter attached.

So before I write anything up, I want to make sure I'm not building the whole piece on a misundestanding. Is "middle" really about certainty, or am I completely off? And if it's not, then what on earth makes this knowledge "middle" — middle between what exactly? I ask because I've seen people describe it as sitting "between" God's knowledge of necessary truths and his knowledge of what actually happens, but that phrasing just makes me more confused, not less.
