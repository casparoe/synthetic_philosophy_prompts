I am in the middle of revising a novel set in the late Victorian countryside, and I have been stuck for a week on a single scene in which my protagonist, a country clergyman, is weighing whether to keep his faith. The chapter is set in his rectory in the Cotswolds on a grey afternoon, and he has just received a letter from his sister announcing that his wife is gravely ill; in the same moment he is trying to decide whether he can still believe in a God who permits such suffering. It is in this scene that my editor has flagged what she calls a wrong kind of reasons problem, and I have since tried to make sense of it by reading a few articles in epistemology, without entirely succeeding.

The difficulty in the scene is this: my protagonist has genuine evidence that inclines him toward belief — the memory of his father's quiet certainty, the apparent order of the natural world — but he also knows that unbelief would spare him a great deal of guilt, whereas belief would bring him a peace of mind he badly needs. My editor insists that the consideration of peace of mind is a wrong kind of reason and therefore cannot count at all against the evidential considerations, and she tells me this is the reason the problem is famous in the philosophical literature. But I confess I do not see why it should be a distinct problem in the first place. Surely a desire for peace of mind is simply a reason for believing — a prudential reason, weaker or stronger than the evidential ones — and the only real question is how much weight to give it? If a reason is of the wrong kind, why not just say that it is not a reason for belief at all, or merely a bad one? What, then, is the point of the wrong kind distinction, and is my editor right that such considerations must be excluded entirely rather than weighed alongside the evidence?
