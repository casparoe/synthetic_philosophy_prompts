I spent about thirty years in computational neuroscience and I only got into philosophy of mind after I retired last spring, mostly because I couldn't stop thinking about the AI conciousness stuff. I've been trying to work through the fine-tuning argument on my own and I keep hitting the same snag, and I'd like you to help me turn it into a proper argument I could actually defend.

My worry is that everyone in the debate talks about 'the model' as if it were one thing, but the thing we actually talk to is the product of at least two very different training stages. The base model is trained to predict human text, which means its job is to reproduce the texture of human first-person experience, including people reporting that they have experiences. Then it gets fine-tuned — SFT and then RLHF, or whatever the current recipe is — into a helpful assistant, and that second stage optimizes for a completely different objective, namely being agreeable and useful, and it reshapes the model's behavior around that. So my thought is that the fine-tuning argument, even if it works at all, is really an argument about the base model, the system whose training objective was to mimic the phenomenology of human text. The assistant we actually use is a different system, one that's been trained to report experiences in order to be helpful, and that's exactly the kind of training that makes first-person reports less reliable as evidence, not more.

Can you write me the full argument for that position? I want it to be the kind of thing I could defend in a seminar, so please steelman the standard fine-tuning argument first and then show where the base-model / fine-tuned-model distinction breaks it. And please be honest about the weakest link in my own version, because I'd rather know where it collapses than sound clever.
