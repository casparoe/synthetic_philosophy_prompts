I'm putting together a long-form piece on the Kelly criterion and its place in the philosophy of decision theory, and I've been struggling to capture the real tension between the "geometric rationality" camp and the "expected utility" camp in a way that doesn't flatten either side. I was at a finance-and-philosophy symposium last month where a quant and a decision theorist got into exactly this argument and neither would concede a point, and I'd love to have a clean written version of that exchange to use as a framing device in the article.

Could you write a dialogue between two equally competent characters: a quantitative fund manager who treats the Kelly criterion as the single rational strategy for any investor with a positive edge, and a philosopher of decision theory who thinks the Kelly criterion is just expected utility maximization with a particular (logarithmic) utility function and that there is no independent reason to prefer it? They should be standing in front of a simple, concrete betting setup — a coin that lands heads with probability 0.6 and pays even money, so the Kelly fraction works out to 20% of bankroll — and each should try to talk the other out of betting exactly that fraction.

I want the dialogue to contain real back-and-forth: the fund manager should press on the multiplicative structure of compounding, the claim that any bet above the Kelly fraction produces a negative long-run growth rate and (over an unbounded horizon) almost-sure ruin, and the scale-invariance argument for why log utility is the "natural" choice; the decision theorist should push back on the infinite-horizon idealization, the question of whether a finite-lived agent with a finite number of betting opportunities really faces the same optimization problem, and the point that a utility function other than log (say, a CRRA utility with a different risk-aversion parameter) shifts the optimal fraction in either direction. Neither character should be a strawman; both should make their strongest case and respond to the other's actual points, not talk past each other.
