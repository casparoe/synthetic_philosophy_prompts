I've just spent an hour and a half arguing with a friend about Aumann's agreement theorem, and we did not resolve it, so I've drafted a short essay to try to settle the point in my own head. I'd be grateful if you could read it and give me comments. Here is the draft:

The familiar slogan that rational agents who share a prior can never agree to disagree is, I think, both more and less accurate than it looks. It is more accurate in the sense that the impossibility of agreeing to disagree is not the full strength of what a common prior buys you: the genuinely tight result is the "no mutually favorable bet" condition — that there is no bet which both agents, given their own information, regard as favorable to themselves — and this condition is necessary and sufficient for the existence of a common prior, whereas the mere impossibility of agreeing to disagree is only a necessary condition. In that sense the common prior is the real engine of the theorem, and the common-knowledge requirement is doing comparatively little work. The slogan is less accurate in the sense that it quietly assumes a single, unambiguous prior: once you allow ambiguity in the sense that the statements agents receive can be interpreted differently by different agents, two agents who share the relevant prior structure can in fact agree to disagree, as Halpern and Kets showed. So the clean slogan is really a theorem about a special case in which beliefs are crisp, and the interesting modern results are the relaxations — common ε-belief rather than full common knowledge, and priors that are merely close rather than identical — which tell you how robust the conclusion is when you loosen the assumptions.

My friend's objection, which I find the hardest to answer, is that I am overstating how much the common prior does. He insists that the common-knowledge condition is itself doing real work, that the theorem's content is the interaction between the two assumptions rather than the prior alone, and that my framing makes the theorem look trivial when it is not. He also objected that I am being too quick about the dialogue process: I suggested that if two agents simply keep announcing their posteriors to one another, they must converge to agreement, and he pushed back that this is a separate result requiring finiteness of the state space, not something you get by iterating the original theorem.

Please comment on three things. First, accuracy: flag any place where I have overclaimed, mischaracterized a result, or conflated a sufficient condition with a necessary one. Second, the interpretive thesis: do you think I am right that the common prior is the real content of the theorem, or is my friend right that the common-knowledge condition is doing as much work? Third, suggest one concrete improvement — for example a cleaner illustrative example, or a sharper statement of the no-favorable-bet condition — that would make the essay more persuasive.
