I'm a third-year PhD student in epidemiology, and I'm trying to untangle something that's been buging me since I sat in on an incident review at the hospital where I do my fieldwork. A patient on a ward was supposed to get a dose of an antibiotic by 2 p.m. The nurse on the unit was supposed to give it but, because the order in the chart was written in a confusing way and nobody had flagged the patient, she just didn't give it that day. The patient's infection got worse and they ended up in the ICU. Now the hospital is trying to figure out who to blame, and I keep hearing two totally different stories. The family and the risk-management people say it's obvious: the nurse failed to do the one thing that would have prevented this, so she's the cause, full stop. But the charge nurse I work with says that's backwards — the real problem was the system: the order was ambiguous, the patient wasn't flagged as high-risk, and the unit was short-staffed, so even a careful nurse would have very likely missed it. She says singling out one person's inaction makes no sense when that inaction was basically expected given the setup.

I dont get how these two are supposed to be reconciled, and honestly I'm not sure which one I should trust. My instinct is that "the nurse didn't do X" is a different kind of thing from "the nurse did X," like the inaction isn't really a cause in the same way an act is, but then I also can't say that her not giving the drug had nothing to do with the outcome. And if it's the system's fault, in what sense is it the system's fault, since the system doesn't literally do anything? Is there a principled way to think about when a failure to act actually counts as a cause of something bad, versus when it's just a failure that happens to line up with a bad outcome? I'd love help sorting this out, because I have to write up my observations for the review and I don't want to take a side I can't defend.
