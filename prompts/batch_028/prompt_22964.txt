I've been thinking a ton lately about what you're supposed to do when you find out that someone you trust just as much as yourself disagrees with you on something, and last night at dinner two of my friends, Owen and Lena, got into a really heated argument about exactly this, and I honestly couldn't tell who where more right, so I wanted to lay it out and get a second opinion. They were using a specific case to argue: they're both trying to decide whether to buy a particular stock, and they're each 80% confident it'll go up, but they find out that a mutual friend they both consider just as sharp as themselves is 80% confident it'll go down.

Owen started by saying you should split the difference and drop your confidence to 50%, because if your peer is as reliable as you, then their belief is just as likely to be correct as yours, so the disagreement tells you that at least one of you is probably wrong and it's a coin flip which one. Lena pushed back and said that's double-counting, because if you already know your peer is as reliable as you, then their opinion is already baked into your 80%, so lowering your confidence again is counting them twice. Owen said no, the fact that they disagree is brand new information you didn't have before, and that new information is exactly "at least one of us is wrong," which should push you toward the middle. Lena then said that even granting all that, it only works if you're actually equally reliable on this specific question, and you know you did way more research on this stock, so you're not really peers on this one. Owen conceded that point but said the general principle still holds in the symmetric case where you are equally reliable. Lena's last move was the one that really stumped me: she said the "split the difference" rule is self-undermining, because if you're supposed to split the difference with every peer you have, then you should also split the difference about whether the "split the difference" rule is itself correct, and once you do that you no longer have enough confidence in the rule to follow it.

I'm not a philosopher or anything, I just find this really interesting and keep coming back to it, and I'd love it if you could go through their exchange and tell me who's right about each specific point they made, and also what, if anything, both of them are missing.
