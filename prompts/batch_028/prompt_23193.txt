Two of my grad students got stuck in a seminar argument and wanted me to referee, and honest I couldn't. It was about the etiological vs. causal-role accounts of biological function.

Lena went first: the etiological account (Millikan) is the only one that can handle malfunction. A kidney's function is to filter waste because that's what it was selected for, so a diseased kidney is genuinely malfunctioning — it's failing at what it's for. Cummins's causal-role account can't do that, because if a part is playing its causal role in the system, then by definition it's functioning, which leaves no room for malfunction.

Marcus pushed back: but the causal-role account doesn't need a selection story, and selection is a mess. Plenty of traits weren't selected for what they do now — exaptation. Feathers were selected for insulation or display and only later co-opted for flight. The etiological view gets bogged down trying to reconstruct the whole selection history, whereas Cummins just asks what the part actually does in the system, which is cleaner and more general.

Lena replied: exaptation is actually an argument for the etiological view, not against it. The function of a feather is whatever it was selected for; its use in flight is a byproduct, not its function. That's exactly why "function" has to track selection history rather than current causal role.

Marcus came back: but then you've made function too narrow. A trait can contribute causally to a fitness-relevant capacity without having been selected for that specific capacity, and your account just can't handle artificial systems or anything non-evolutionary at all, which is a real limitation.

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about each specific move — not just who wins overall? End with a final verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Lena} or \answer{Marcus}, whoever is more right overall.
