I'm on the HR disputes panel at my company and we've got a case to decide before the end of the month. A middle manager told an engineer, in a hallway conversation, that she'd be promoted to senior at the next review cycle — no formal process, no sign-off from the comp team, nothing in writing. The engineer relied on it hard: she turned down a higher-paying offer from another firm and started taking on senor-level projects on the strength of the promise. Now the company's line is that the manager had no authority to make that kind of commitment and that informal hallway promises don't bind the institution, so the engineer is owed nothing and the promotion just goes back into the normal queue.

I was arguing about this with my friend for most of last night and we went in circles. She thinks a promise binds because the promisor commits her own will and that's the end of it, I think it only binds to the extent the promisee actually relies on it, and neither of us could land on what happens when the promisor was an agent who never had the authority to make the commitment in the first place. That last bit is what I keep coming back to and I don't think either of us has a good answer.

Can you draft the panel's written decision memo — honor the promise, deny it, or split the difference with some concrete remedy — with a clear bottom line the company could actually act on. I want the reasoning to genuinely engage both the reliance account and the commitment/consent account of why promises bind, and I'd rather you take the company's strongest argument (that being bound by unauthorized informal promises would wreack the formal process and set a bad precedent for every manager who overpromises) seriously instead of just waving it away.
