I'm working on a long-form piece about the philosophy of science, and I've got tangled up in the concept of verisimilitude in a way that I'd like your help straightening out. My working understanding is that verisimilitude is supposed to be an objective, mind-independent measure of how close a given theory is to the actual truth — and, crucially, that it is not the same thing as the probability that the theory is true, nor is it the same as the proportion of its predictions that happen to come out correct. The whole point, as I read it, is that it lets us say that science is progressing — that our theories are getting closer to the truth over time — even on the assumption that no theory we actually have is fully true. And I'm aware that there's a substantial body of work in the literature showing that various natural attempts to pin down verisimilitude in terms of a theory's true and false consequences run into trouble: the orderings they produce don't always match our intuitions, or they collapse into something trivial.

Here's where I get stuck, and I want to make sure I'm not missing something obvious. If verisimilitude is a real, objective property of a theory, it seems like it should at least be comparable in principle: given two competing theories, shouldn't we be able to say which is more verisimilar, or at least estimate the difference, using the evidence we have on hand? And yet the literature I've read seems to insist that we can never actually compute or estimate a theory's verisimilitude, because doing so would require knowing the actual truth — which is, of course, exactly what we don't have. If that's right, then it seems like verisimilitude is a concept we can never actually put to work, and I'm not sure how that squares with it being a meaningful and useful part of the philosophy of science. So: am I misunderstanding the nature of the concept, or is this a genuine problem that the literature hasn't resolved?
