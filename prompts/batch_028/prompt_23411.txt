I retired from mathematical physics about four years ago, and in the intervening time I've taken to the philosophy of mathematics on my own, mostly working through whatever I could get my hands on. The reason I'm writing is that I'm helping a former postdoc, now a mathematical physicist at a nearby university, respond to a referee report on a paper we were both involved with. The paper needed a standard existence result: that a linear functional, defined and bounded on a subspace of a Banach space, can be extended to the whole space without increasing its norm. As I recall from my own working days, the clean proof runs through Zorn's lemma — you partially order the partial extensions by inclusion, check that every chain has an upper bound, and then invoke the lemma to conclude that a maximal extension exists, which one then verifies is defined everywhere. My postdoc is perfectly comfortable with this, but the referee has objected, in substance, that Zorn's lemma is a non-constructive device and that the proof does not really establish that such an extension exists, only that no contradiction follows from supposing one does. I have to confess the objection has stuck with me, and that I can't quite articulate what is genuinely at stake, as opposed to what is merely terminological grumbling.

So I'd be grateful for help thinking this through. In particular, I'd like a clear account of what a non-constructive existence proof of this kind does and does not establish, and of whether the referee's worry is a legitimate philosophical point or a confusion. I should be upfront that I have no intention of abandoning the proof — the rest of the paper leans on the existence of that maximal extension — so I'm not looking for a way around Zorn's lemma, but rather for an honest assessment of the strongest version of the objection and for a response I could put in the rebuttal letter that takes it seriously without conceding that the proof is defective. If this sits inside a broader, named debate in the philosophy of mathematics, I'd like to know what it is called, because I have a feeling I've been reading the wrong half of the literature, and I'd rather not keep arguing with a ghost.
