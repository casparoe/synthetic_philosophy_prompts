I'm moderating the Q&A for an AI safety workshop next month and the closing panelist is a researcher who's been one of the more persistant defenders of the "debate" approach to scalable oversight — the setup where you get two AI systems to argue about a powerful model's output and a human judge just watches for deception, so you don't have to fully understand the task yourself. I'd like to prep a set of questions that actually press on the technical assumptions instead of letting them restate the pitch. Could you draft me about eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer they're most likely to give?

Specifically I want at least one question each on: (a) the verifiability assumption — debate seems to work when the debaters make claims the human can check, but a lot of the tasks we actually want oversight over (writing a good policy, "is this a good plan") don't have a crisp ground truth, so how does the mechanism survive that?; (b) incentive compatiblity — the debaters are rewarded for convincing the judge, not for being honest, so what exactly is supposed to make honesty the winning strategy, and does that still hold when the debaters are more capable than the human watching them?; and (c) the collusion problem — if the two debaters can just converge on a plausible-sounding false answer, doesn't the whole thing collapse, and is there any way to make collusion unprofitable?

I'd also love one question that corners them on the oversight regress (who's checking the debaters, and doesn't that just push the same problem up a level?) and one that asks what kind of empirical result would actually count as evidence against the approach — not just "it failed on a hard benchmark," but something that would tell you the underlying mechanism is broken. And please make the follow-ups a little adversarial in the way a good moderator is, because the panelist has a habit of retreating to "this is a research program, not a finished product" whenever they get pressed.
