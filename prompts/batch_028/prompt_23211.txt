I keep running into a real disagreement — in the literature and in the conversations I have with my advisors — about what the theorem actually shows, and I want the handout to model that disagreement honestly rather than collapse it into a simple verdict that voting is hopeless. Could you write a dialogue between two competent social choice theorists who are each trying to change the other's mind about the theorem's significance?

The other should defend the "constructive, restricted-domain" reading: that the theorem is best understood as a design tool that tells us exactly where to look for positive results, and that by restricting the preference domain we can and do obtain strategy-proof, non-dictatorial mechanisms in important real-world settings. I want both characters to be fully competent and arguing in good faith — no strawmanning on either side — and I want genuine back-and-forth, where each responds to the other's actual points rather than talking past them.

To keep the dialogue in the weeds, I'd like it to engage with at least some of the following technical points: that plurality voting is strategy-proof on the full domain, so that the theorem's real content is the impossibility of onto-ness rather than of strategy-proofness per se; that allowing abstention makes even plurality manipulable; the contrast between the theorem's binary possible/impossible verdict and the separate, quantitative question of how costly or likely manipulation is under different rules; and the role of restricted domains such as single-peaked preferences or the matching and school-choice settings (e.g., the deferred-acceptance mechanism). Please keep it to roughly 800 to 1200 words, and have it end with the two of them not fully reconciled, but having sharpened exactly where their disagreement lies.
