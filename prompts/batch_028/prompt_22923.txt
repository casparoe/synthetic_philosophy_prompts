The lottery paradox involves a fair lottery with a large number of tickets — say 1000 — of which exactly one wins. For any particular ticket, the chance that it loses is 999/1000, which is very high, so it seems reasonable to believe that each ticket will lose. But the tickets cannot all lose, since one of them must win. So if the agent believes of each ticket that it will lose, her beliefs are jointly inconsistent.

The standard response to the lottery paradox is that high probability by itself does not amount to belief or knowledge. The lottery agent's only ground for thinking a given ticket will lose is that it is highly probable; she has no positive evidence concerning that specific ticket. So we can deny that she really believes each ticket will lose, and once we do, the inconsistency disappears.

The preface paradox has a similar shape. An author has carefully checked every claim in her book, and so she has good reason to believe each individual claim. But she also believes that at least one claim in the book is false, because the book is long and it is certain that at least one error has slipped in. Thus she believes each individual claim, and she also believes that the conjunction of all her claims is false. Indeed, she believes the conjunction of all her claims, and she also believes that this conjunction is false, so her belief set contains a flat contradiction.

The "high probability is not belief" response does not carry over to the preface paradox. In the lottery case the agent's beliefs rest purely on high probability, so we can simply deny that she holds them. In the preface case, by contrast, the author has actually verified each claim, so her beliefs rest on genuine evidence rather than on mere probability. We cannot simply deny that she believes each claim, since that would imply that a person who has carefully checked every one of her claims does not believe them, which is too high a price to pay. So the preface paradox survives the standard response to the lottery, and it threatens the requirement that a rational agent's beliefs be consistent — a requirement the lottery paradox does not put under comparable pressure.
