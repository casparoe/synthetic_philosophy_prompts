I'm taking a course on Bayesian epistemology this term, and we've just finished a unit on the problem of old evidence, which I keep finding more tangled the longer I sit with it. As I understand the setup: on the standard Bayesian account, a piece of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H just in case it raises H's probability, P(H|E) > P(H), which (given Bayes' theorem and the usual non-degeneracy assumptions) is equivalent to the Bayes factor, or likelihood ratio, P(E|H)/P(E|~H), being greater than one. The trouble is that if E is "old" — already in the agent's evidence base, so the agent already assigns it probability one — then conditionalizing on E leaves the agent's credence in H exactly where it was, and the Bayes factor collapses to one, so on the face of it E seems to confirm nothing, even in the paradigm cases like the anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury, which was already established long before general relativity was the hypothesis that explained it. I've been mulling over the various proposed fixes, and I've landed on the "Bayesian" solution: to assess whether old evidence E confirms H, compute the confirmation with the agent's prior "Bayesian" — the "Bayesian" the agent would have had before learning E — rather than with the agent's current "Bayesian", in which E is already certain.

Could you please write a tight argument for exactly that position? I'd like it to (a) state the problem of old evidence precisely, including why both the naive test P(H|E) > P(H) and the Bayes-factor test go wrong when the agent's current "Bayesian" assigns P(E) = 1; (b) explain the "Bayesian" solution and why using the prior "Bayesian" is legitimate rather than a sleight of hand; (c) take on head-on the strongest objection, namely that the agent actually knows E and so can never genuinely "Bayesian" on it, which seems to make the whole confirmation vacuous or incoherent — and say concretely why the objection fails (I'm thinking the confirmation is a "Bayesian" relation between E and H rather than a "Bayesian" event in the agent's belief history, but I want that developed properly); and (d) be honest about what this view gives up or costs, so it reads as a real philosophical position rather than a survey that happens to land on a conclusion. Please keep it at the level of a strong undergraduate essay, roughly 600 to 900 words, and don't pad it by recapping every proposed solution in the literature.
