Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) Bell's locality condition — local causality — says that once the hidden variable λ is specified, the outcomes at the two wings are independent of each other and of the distant setting, so the joint probability factorizes: P(A,B|a,b,λ) = P(A|a,λ)P(B|b,λ). The other assumptions are measurement independence, P(λ|a,b) = P(λ), i.e. the settings a and b are statistically independent of λ, and completeness, the idea that λ is a complete specification of the relevant physical state. From these one derives the CHSH inequality |S| ≤ 2, where S = E(a,b) + E(a,b') + E(a',b) − E(a',b'). One caveat: Bell's original 1964 derivation actually assumed that λ determines the outcomes with certainty, so strictly speaking the 1964 argument rules out only deterministic local hidden-variable theories; a stochastic local hidden-variable theory, in which λ merely fixes probabilities, could in principle evade it.

(b) Quantum mechanics does not permit faster-than-light signaling. The marginal probability of Alice's outcome is independent of Bob's setting, P(A|a,b) = P(A|a), so Alice cannot use an entangled pair to send a message to Bob. In that operational sense QM is local. The Bell violation is therefore not about signaling: it is about the fact that the joint probabilities do not factorize, P(A,B|a,b) ≠ P(A|a)P(B|b), a genuine 'outcome dependence' between spacelike-separated events that no common cause in the shared past can explain. QM predicts |S| = 2√2 (the Tsirelson bound), and experiments, from Aspect's in the early 1980s through the recent loophole-free tests, confirm the violation. One should keep in mind that the no-signaling principle is really just Bell's locality condition expressed in operational form, the same requirement without reference to λ; so the fact that QM respects no-signaling is consistent with, and in a sense the operational shadow of, its violation of local causality.

(c) If one wants to preserve locality, the assumption most commonly targeted is measurement independence. Denying it yields superdeterminism: the settings are not free but correlated with λ from the start, so the 'random' choices were fixed by the same initial conditions that fix the hidden variables. The cost is severe — one gives up the methodological assumption that experimenters can choose settings independently of the system under study, which many regard as a threat to the scientific method, and the theory becomes hard to falsify, since any observed correlation can be explained by a conspiratorial common cause."
