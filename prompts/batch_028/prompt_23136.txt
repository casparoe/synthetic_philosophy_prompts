I am a novelist, and I also, somewhat improbably, sit on the grants committee of the Aldergate Foundation, a small literary arts foundation that funds first novels and mid-career fiction projects. This spring we received 214 applications and have authority to fund at most 40. The committee's bylaws require two kinds of statement in the annual report: for each funded project, a line to the effect that the committee judges it more probable than not that the project will achieve its stated objectives; and a single aggregate statement to the effect that the committee judges it more probable than not that all funded projects will achieve their stated objectives. The committee also sends a standard rejection letter in which it states, for each unfunded project, that the committee judges it more probable than not that the project as submitted will not achieve its objectives.

The difficulty, which I have raised for two years without success, is that these requirements are jointly unsatisfiable in any year in which the committee is honest. Each of the 40 individual judgments is, on our scoring, warranted — each funded project is assessed at roughly a 90 percent chance of success — but the chance that all 40 succeed is then on the order of 1 or 2 percent, so the committee knows in advance that the aggregate statement is false even as it signs each of the individual ones. The same structure recurs, in the other direction, in the rejection letters: each of the 174 unfunded projects is individually a strong candidate for failure, yet the conjunction of all 174 such claims is plainly false, since many of those projects will succeed elsewhere. I recognize the first case from my own work: it is, I take it, the preface paradox — the case of an author who vouches in a book's preface for the accuracy of every statement in the book while knowing the book contains errors — and I suspect the second is the lottery paradox, in which one is entitled to say of each ticket in a fair lottery that it will not win. I would like the committee's policy to be fixed before next year's cycle, and I have been asked to circulate a memo.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee before its June meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — what the bylaws should say about the individual and aggregate statements, whether any threshold of confidence should replace the "more probable than not" standard, and what the rejection letters should say — and it should explain, for a committee of writers and editors rather than philosophers, which of the two paradoxes is at work on each side of the ledger, why the inconsistency does or does not matter for a body whose minutes are a public document, and what the strongest case is for leaving the language as it is. The recommendation should be something the committee could actually vote on, and it should not resolve the difficulty by pretending the arithmetic is on our side.
