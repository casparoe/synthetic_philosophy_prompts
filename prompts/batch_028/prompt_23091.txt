The question I set was: "The knowledge norm of assertion (KNA) holds that one should assert that p only if one knows that p. Defenders of this view — Williamson, Stanley, and others — typically argue that the norm is *constitutive* of assertion itself, not merely a piece of good advice: an utterance that fails to meet it simply isn't a genuine assertion. But consider a doctor who, at 3 a.m., tells a family 'your mother will be fine,' based on strong but non-conclusive evidence, when the stakes of being wrong are enormous. Intuition says we'd blame the doctor more for being wrong in this high-stakes case than in a low-stakes one. Do such cases support a pragmatic-encroachment view — that practical stakes raise the standard for knowledge, and thereby for proper assertion — or do they show that the knowledge norm is too strict and that we actually operate by a lower, belief-based norm? Defend one position and address the other."

The student's answer is: "I think the 3 a.m. doctor case supports pragmatic encroachment, not a belief-based norm. Williamson and Stanley argue the knowledge norm is constitutive of assertion, so if the doctor's evidence falls short of knowledge in the high-stakes setting, her utterance isn't really an assertion at all — it's more like a hopeful guess. But that can't be right: we clearly do blame her, and blaming someone presupposes she made a claim that could be wrong. So the better move is to keep the constitutive claim but let the *threshold* for knowledge vary with stakes. This is exactly DeRose's point in his bank cases: the same evidence can support knowledge in a low-stakes context but not in a high-stakes one, because what counts as a 'relevant alternative' you must rule out depends on what you stand to lose. So in the 3 a.m. case, the doctor has to rule out more bad outcomes before she can truly know, and she hasn't, so she shouldn't have asserted. I don't think this requires the knowledge norm to be constitutive, though — even if assertion is just a speech act governed by a lower belief norm, we can still say the doctor's belief wasn't strong enough given the stakes."
