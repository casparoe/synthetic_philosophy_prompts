I spent my professional life as a physical chemist, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of objects. I understand the rough idea that a whole is made of parts, but I am stuck on why philosophers treat the special composition question in mereology as a distinct and difficult question, rather than simply asking whether a collection of parts forms a recognizable object. Could you explain what is meant by asking when some things compose exactly one thing, and how that differs from asking what kinds of objects there are in the first place?

I would especially like to understand why answers such as unrestricted composition, mereological nihilism, and constraints based on connectedness, functional unity, or kind-relative maximality are proposed as answers to this particular question, and why some philosophers think that appealing to those constraints may make the answer depend on our classificatory interests rather than on a fact about the parts. I would also be grateful if you could clarify the role of mereological addition. I see that a principle saying that any two distinct objects have a fusion seems to settle the matter, but I do not see why it is not either a trivial formal assumption or simply an answer that already assumes unrestricted composition. Please explain how the addition principle relates to the special composition question, and why it is compatible with, or in tension with, the other positions you mention.

A concrete example involving a swarm of birds, a composite material, or a phase boundary in a solid would help me see the issue from a scientist's perspective. I am not asking for a survey of every position, but for a clear conceptual map that shows where the genuine disagreement lies and where the terminology is doing most of the work.
