I'm a staff engineer on the discipline committee at my company (a mid-size SaaS shop), and I have no formal philosophy background, so I'd rather think this through carefully than cite anyone I can't actually defend in front of the committee. We have to decide, by Friday, how to sanction an engineer whose change caused a 47-minute checkout outage last Tuesday. As far as the post-incident review could establish: the engineer added a new webhook-retry endpoint that appends to the billing ledger without an idempotency key; the same third-party provider had a partial failure that night and retried aggressively; the retry storm hit the shared write path and deadlocked the ledger. Six weeks earlier, a colleague on the same team had shipped a structurally identical change — a sibling endpoint, also missing the idempotency key, also past the same automated checks and the same human reviewer, also following the deploy checklist. That same provider had a similar partial failure that week, but the retries happened to hit a cached path, so nothing happened; the defect was only caught in a post-hoc audit. So the two actions are, as far as we can tell, identical up to what happened afterwards, and the only difference in how we're treating them is the outcome. I think this is an instance of what philosophers call moral luck, but I don't know the literature, and I've been going back and forth on it with a teammate since the retro without converging.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's committee meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — what sanction (if any) for the engineer whose change caused the outage, what happens to the colleague's change, and what process changes we make — and it should engage the luck issue head-on: whether it's coherent, or at least defensible, to sanction someone more heavily for an outcome that was, on the information available at the time, outside their control. My honest guess is that the strongest case for letting outcomes matter is that the bad outcome is evidence the risk was higher than either the author or the reviewer estimated, and that an institution which ignores outcomes has a perverse incentive structure; I'd rather the memo take that case seriously and then explain why the recommendation still holds, or concede it and change the recommendation, than wave it away in a line.
