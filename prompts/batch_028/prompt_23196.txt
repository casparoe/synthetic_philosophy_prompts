I've been reading, on my own and with no training in philosophy, Popper's discussion of Darwinism — specifically the part where he argues that the theory of evolution by natural selection, taken as a whole, is not directly testable or falsifiable in the way a strict scientific theory ought to be, and that it instead works more like a metaphysical research program: a general framework that is heuristically valuable because it generates specific, testable hypotheses about particular species and particular environments. I find this a genuinely interesting claim, and I've been wondering how it would bear on some developments in evolutionary biology that came after Popper's day.

In particular I'm curious about two things. First, the neutral theory of molecular evolution (the one associated with Motoo Kimura), the idea that a great deal of genetic change at the molecular level — especially in parts of the genome that don't code for proteins — is driven by random genetic drift rather than by natural selection. Second, the widespread discovery of horizontal gene transfer, in which organisms (bacteria above all) exchange genes with one another in ways that don't fit the neat branching tree-of-life picture, so that the history of life looks more like a web or a network than a single tree.

Given Popper's framework — that natural selection as a whole is a metaphysical research program, while the specific evolutionary hypotheses it spawns are the testable scientific content — what do you think he would have made of these two developments? Does the neutral theory undercut his point, or does it fit neatly inside it? And does horizontal gene transfer change anything about the status he assigned to the theory of evolution by natural selection? I'm not after a definitive verdict, just a careful reconstruction of what his position would most plausibly have been, and the spots where it might have come under pressure.
