A friend of mine, Lena, and another friend, Marcus, got into it at a reading group over Quine's indeterminacy of translation. Lena opened by saying that the real upshot of the gavagai example is that meaning itself is indeterminate: there is no fact of the matter about what a word means, so when she uses a word like "cat," there's no fact of the matter about what she means by it. Marcus pushed back on two fronts. First, he said Quine's thesis is about the indeterminacy of translation across languages, not about meaning within a language, and conflating the two overstates the result. Second, he said it's really an underdetermination result — the behavioral evidence fails to pick out a unique translation manual, but there is still a fact of the matter about what the speaker means, we just can't get at it from the outside — and that Quine's behaviorism is doing all the heavy lifting, which is a premise we're entitled to reject. Lena replied that Quine is explicit that this is indeterminacy, not mere underdetermination, and that the behaviorist restriction isn't an arbitrary extra assumption but is built into the radical translation setup, where the linguist has nothing to work with but observed behavior. If you allow the native speaker's intentions and mental states as evidence, she said, you've already imported the very notion of meaning you're trying to explain, so you've begged the question. Marcus said he isn't begging the question; he's just noting that Quine's argument rests on a hidden premise — that behavior is the only admissible evidence — and that this premise is a methodological choice, not a necessity. And the claim, he said, that there is no fact of the matter is strictly stronger than the underdetermination result: to get there you need an additional metaphysical commitment, not a logical consequence of the evidence. Lena answered that the metaphysical commitment is precisely what makes the thesis interesting, and that it follows from the idea that meaning is a theoretical construct that has to be cashed out in behavioral terms — if meaning can't be cashed out behaviorally, then there is no fact of the matter. Marcus closed by saying that's circular: you're assuming the behaviorist criterion of meaning in order to prove that meaning is behaviorally indeterminate. A non-behaviorist, he said, can consistently hold that there is a fact of the matter about meaning that simply isn't accessible from behavioral evidence alone.
