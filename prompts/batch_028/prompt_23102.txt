The question is whether arthāpatti (postulation, or presumption) is a genuine independent pramāṇa, or whether it just collapses into anumāna (inference).

Here's the crux as I see it, and I want the essay to engage with exactly this instead of the general picture. The Nyāya definition of anumāna is pretty strict: a valid inference needs a hetu (a reason) whose link to the conclusion rests on a vyāpti, a universal concomitance you already know independently before you reason, the way you know that smoke goes with fire before you look at the hill and conclude there's a fire up there. But in the textbook arthāpatti case, where someone who is thin through the day turns out to be fat at night and we conclude he must have eaten after dark, there's no such pre-established general rule. We're just building the best explanation that fits the facts, on the spot. So the Nyāya's argument is that this can't be anumāna, it has to be its own source of knowledge. The reductionist counter is that the concomitance in the eating case is definately just a weak or singular generalization (food makes you gain weight), and that the Nyāya's demand for a fully established vyāpti is arbitrary; once you relax that requirement, postulation is just a kind of abductive, best-explanation inference.

I keep coming down on the reducibility side myself, but I'm not sure my reason for it is the right one, and I have a feeling I'm underestimating the Nyāya's defense. So please weigh both sides honestly, steelman the irreducibility claim first and then push back, and actually reach a verdict and defend it, don't end on it's complicated.
