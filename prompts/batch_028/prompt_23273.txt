I was at a dinner party last month where two friends got into a long argument about the replication crisis in psychology, and I honestly couldn't tell who was more right. I'd been following the p-hacking and replication literature for a while — I'd read the Open Science Collaboration's 2015 reproducibility study and a handful of the Bayesian-versus-frequentist threads on LessWrong — so I thought I had a handle on the issues, but their argument took me into some territory I hadn't thought about carefully.

One of them, who's a statistician, started by saying the crisis is fundamentally a frequentist problem. His argument was that p-values are systematically misinterpreted: people treat p < 0.05 as "there's a 95% chance the null hypothesis is false," when it actually means "if the null were true, there's a 5% chance of getting a result this extreme." This misinterpretation, he said, is what drives the flood of false positives, and the fix is to switch to Bayes factors, which give you the actual probability of the hypothesis given the data.

The other friend, a philosopher of science, pushed back. She said the replication crisis isn't a frequentist problem — it's a problem of incentives. Researchers publish positive results, not null results, so the literature is skewed toward false positives regardless of whether you use p-values or Bayes factors. The real fix, she argued, is to pre-register studies and publish null results, not to switch statistical methods. The statistician countered that publication bias is a symptom, not a cause: the cause is that p-values are too easy to obtain, and researchers can p-hack by peeking at the data and stopping when they get a "significant" result. She agreed that p-hacking is a problem but said it's not specific to frequentist methods — you can p-hack with Bayes factors too, by peeking and stopping when the Bayes factor crosses some threshold of your choosing.

This is where the argument got genuinely interesting. The statistician said that optional stopping is a *frequentist* problem, not a Bayesian one. In frequentist statistics, the p-value depends on the sampling distribution, which depends on the stopping rule, so if you peek, the p-value is no longer valid. But in Bayesian statistics, the posterior depends only on the likelihood and the prior, not on the stopping rule, so optional stopping is not a problem for Bayesian methods. She responded by invoking the likelihood principle: the likelihood of the observed data given the hypothesis is the same whether you pre-registered a fixed sample size or stopped early, so the evidential content hasn't changed. She said he was conflating "how much evidence do I have in hand" with "how reliable is this procedure over the long run" — those are different questions, and the likelihood principle says the stopping rule is irrelevant to the first. He pushed back by saying the likelihood principle is wrong precisely because it ignores long-run error rates, and that the p-value is a long-run frequency that is genuinely sensitive to the sampling distribution, which is exactly why peeking corrupts it.

I couldn't referee this. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? I'm particularly interested in whether the statistician's claim that optional stopping is a purely frequentist problem is correct, and whether the likelihood principle actually saves the Bayesian position here, or whether it's doing less work than she thinks.
