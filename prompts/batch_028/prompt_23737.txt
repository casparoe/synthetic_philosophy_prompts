I'm a retired chemist who only got into philosophy a couple of years ago, so forgive me if I'm getting something basic wrong. I've been reading about moral worth and this 'acting for the right reason' requirement, and I keep hitting a wall I just can't get past.

As I understand it, an action only has moral worth if the agent acts for the right reason — the moral reason, not some self-interested or accidential one. Fine. But then: suppose someone helps a stranger because they sincerely believe it's the morally right thing to do, when in fact they're working from a false moral belief. In that case the reason they're really acting on isn't a genuine reason at all, since it's built on a false belief — so they can't be acting for the 'right' reason, because the right reason has to be an actual reason. Which means moral worth seems to depend on whether the agents moral belief happens to be true, i.e. on moral luck. But I thought the whole point of the 'right reasons' literature was to get moral worth off the hook of luck and tie it to the agent's motivation or character instead. So how does the literature actually avoid this? Or am I just misunderstanding what 'the right reason' is supposed to be?
