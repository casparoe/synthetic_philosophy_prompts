I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of action, and I keep coming back to a puzzle I can't quite close, so I'd be grateful for a careful, worked-through answer rather than a survey of the literature. Here's the concrete case I'm wrestling with. I have a clear plan for working on my dissertation: I intend to tackle the hardest chapter in the morning, when I'm fresh, and leave the more mechanical writing for the afternoon. In practice, I almost always reverse this — I do the mechanical work first and keep deferring the hard chapter — and afterward I feel a distinct sense of having failed myself. A behavioral economist friend explained this to me as textbook hyperbolic discounting, or "present bias": the cost of starting the hard chapter looms larger the closer it is in time, so my preferences are time-inconsistent, and at each moment I'm simply doing what I most want at that moment, which is locally rational. On her account, there's no genuine weakness of will here; the classical puzzle just dissolves, because I never actually act against my strongest current preference.

My difficulty is that this explanation feels to me like it re-describes the phenomenon rather than explaining it, and I can't fully articulate why, which is what I'd like your help with. If at every moment I'm choosing the option I currently prefer, where exactly is the akratic failure? And if the answer is "the failure is that my earlier, more deliberative preferences get overridden by my later, more myopic ones," then the real question seems to be whether the earlier self's preferences should bind the later self at all — a question about the identity and authority of the self across time, not about a failure of will. So can you help me sort out whether the hyperbolic-discounting account genuinely dissolves the traditional akrasia puzzle (the Socratic versus Aristotelian contrast), or whether it merely relocates it? I'm especially interested in the strongest version of each side, and in whether there's any coherent sense in which the "stronger" future self's preferences can count as the agent's "real" preferences — and whether granting that point quietly concedes the very thing the hyperbolic-discounting account wants to deny.
