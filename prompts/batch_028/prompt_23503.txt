For a seminar in the history of moral philosophy, I want a model essay on the following question: does the core of Rousseau's argument against the theater in his 1758 letter to d'Alembert — that the spectator, feeling on the character's behalf, substitutes the experience of moral sentiments for their actual exercise, thereby weakening the very disposition of virtue the spectacle purports to cultivate — extend to modern narrative media such as film, television, and the novel? The essay must give a genuine exposition of the argument (not merely the familiar claim that theater corrupts morals) and must confront the strongest internal objection: Rousseau's own qualified endorsement of reading in the Émile (Robinson Crusoe, later history and Plutarch) and the implied contrast between actively imagining oneself in a role and passively receiving a spectacle. Weigh the arguments and reach a justified conclusion on whether the theater-specific argument generalizes, is an artifact of eighteenth-century Geneva, or has been superseded by Rousseau's later thought.
