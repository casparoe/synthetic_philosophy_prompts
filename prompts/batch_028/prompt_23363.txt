I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and I'm drafting a paper that argues a causal-role account of biological function can do the work that etiological accounts are usually credited with — above all, accommodating malfunction — without appealing to a trait's evolutionary history. Below is the core section. I would be grateful for comments on three things: (1) whether I am representing the etiological and causal-role positions, and Davies's objection, accurately; (2) whether my proposed solution actually escapes the history that the causal-role theorist wants to avoid, or whether it quietly reintroduces it; and (3) how I should best handle a trait, such as the heart, that plays several causal roles at once.

The standard contrast is between etiological accounts, on which a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected (Millikan's selected-effects account) or, in Neander's etiological version, the effect that, in a causal-historical sense, was responsible for the trait's being favored by selection, and causal-role accounts, on which a trait's function is simply the role it plays in the current causal organization of the organism that contains it. Etiological accounts are favored in part because they seem to explain malfunction: a trait malfunctions when it fails to perform the function it was selected for, and the failure is a genuine norm-violation relative to a historical standard. Davies has argued, however, that historical malfunction is impossible — if a trait was selected for effect E, then a member of the species that lacks E is not a genuine instance of the selected trait, so the case of malfunction collapses into a case of a different trait or of extinction. This, he concludes, is an internal failing of etiological and selected-effects theories.

I want to claim that the causal-role theorist is not threatened by Davies, because the causal-role theorist never needed a historical standard in the first place. On the view I am developing, the relevant norm is internal to the organism's own regulatory architecture: a trait malfunctions when it departs from the performance standard that the organism's homeostatic systems treat as the set point. A heart malfunctions not because it fails to perform some historically selected function, but because it fails to maintain the blood pressure and flow that the kidneys, baroreceptors, and other control loops of the same organism take as the target. The normativity of function, on this picture, is a current, internal, causal fact about a self-regulating system, and it is available to the causal-role theorist without any appeal to the trait's past.

I concede two difficulties. First, the set points themselves — the blood pressure a healthy mammalian cardiovascular system regulates toward — are themselves the product of the same evolutionary history the causal-role account is supposed to dispense with, so it is unclear that the account is not etiological in disguise. Second, the heart does not have a single causal role: it drives systemic circulation, venous return, thermoregulation, and the transport of hormones, so it is not obvious which of these roles, or which combination of them, constitutes the standard against which the heart is to be judged.
