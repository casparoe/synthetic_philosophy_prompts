I've been trying to work through the Judy Benjamin problem on my own this week, because I keep seeing it described as the classic counterexample to the idea that every rational change of belief can be handled by Bayes's rule or its probabilistic generalization. Here is the version of the setup as I understand it: Judy Benjamin is a soldier who has just been dropped into a region that is divided, on one axis, into a Red part (R) and a Blue part (not-R), and, on a second, independent axis, into a Second-Company part (S) and a Headquarters part (not-S). Before she learns anything more, she has no reason to favor any of the four quadrants, so she assigns each of them probability 1/4. Then she is told the conditional "If the region is Red, then it is Second Company," which she takes to mean P(S | R) = 1. The part that keeps confusing me is what this is supposed to do to her probability that the region is Red. I can see why the answer is not simply "recompute with the usual rules," because the new information seems to force the Red-and-not-Second-Company quadrant down to probability zero, but I do not see why that should pin down the total probability of Red in any particular way, or even why it should fail to.

Could you please describe the most important implications of this problem for the question of whether probabilistic updating is a complete model of rational belief change? I would especially like to understand what the problem shows about the difference between learning a plain fact and learning an indicative conditional, why the update on the antecedent turns out to be underdetermined rather than merely unknown to me, and what that underdetermination means for the claim that rationality always dictates a unique probability to assign. I am not asking for a step-by-step solution to my version of the problem; I want the conceptual stakes, so that I can see why philosophers treat a setup that looks like a simple conditional-probability exercise as genuinely significant.
