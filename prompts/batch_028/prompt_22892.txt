I'm preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of vagueness, and I've been going back and forth with a colleague over how to present the two leading accounts of vague predicates: epistemicism (the view associated with Williamson) and supervaluationism (the view associated with Field and Keene). I want to make sure the seminar isn't just a rehearsal of the standard textbook contrast, so I'd like a careful, technical comparison rather than a survey of the literature.

I'd like you to compare the two theories on three points where I think the real disagreement lives. First, the metaphysics of borderline cases: on the epistemicist picture every vague predicate has a sharp, determinate extension, so for any borderline case there is a fact of the matter about whether it falls in — we simply cannot know which cases lie on the boundary — whereas on the supervaluationist picture a borderline case is genuinely indeterminate, true under some precisifications of the predicate and false under others, with no fact of the matter. Second, the logic each theory is committed to: whether vagueness forces us into a many-valued or otherwise non-classical logic, or whether classical bivalence can be preserved. Third, and this is the point I find most underappreciated, how each theory handles credence and higher-order vagueness: on the epistemicist picture a rational agent can attach a probability to a borderline case's falling in the extension, but the supervaluationist's natural move is to let the credence be the fraction of precisifications on which the case is in the extension, which presupposes a probability measure over precisifications — and it is far from clear that any natural such measure exists, particularly given that the collection of precisifications itself looks vague.

Weigh these considerations and reach a conclusion: which of the two accounts is more defensible, or at least more internally coherent, and why? I'm not asking for the easy hedge that epistemicism is elegant but counterintuitive while supervaluationism is intuitive but plagued by a regress; I want you to pick a side and defend it on the technical points above, and to say where the losing account is genuinely vulnerable.
