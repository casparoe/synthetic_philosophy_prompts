I spent about thirty years as a field ecologist, mostly tracking predator-prey dynamics out in the grasslands, so I've had a front row seat to a fair amout of wild animal suffering — the slow starvation of fawns in a bad winter, the parasitized half-dead birds, the prey that get chewed alive. I've only recently started getting into philosophy (I'm doing an online ethics course and I'm a real beginner, so bear with me), and all of this has been eating at me. I want to write an essay on whether we actually have a moral obligation to intervene to reduce suffering in wild animal populations, and I'd like you to write it for me, or at least give me a strong draft I can work from.

The thing is, I don't want the obvious answer that we should help animals just because they can suffer — I think that's the easy part and I already believe it. What I want you to grapple with are the two objections that actually keep me up at night. First, the population problem: if you reduce death in a wild population, the population tends to rebound, which means more total individuals and posibly more total suffering, so does reducing suffering even make sense as a goal here, or do you just shift the suffering around? Second, the "wildness" objection: a lot of people (including some of my old colleagues) feel that there's something intrinsically valuable about a natural, wild world, and that tinkering with it is wrong in itself, regardless of the suffering involved. I want you to weigh these two objections seriously against the case for intervention, and then actually reach a conclusion and defend it — don't leave it as a balance of considerations. I'm happy for you to conclude that we shouldn't intervene, or that we should but only in very limited ways, as long as you justify it.

I'm not fussed about length, but I'd like it to be a proper essay, not a list of bullet points. And if you can, anchor it in a concrete example or two from the wild — a specific predator-prey system, or a specific conservation intervention — rather than just abstract principles, because that's how I think and I find the purely abstract versions a little hard to follow.
