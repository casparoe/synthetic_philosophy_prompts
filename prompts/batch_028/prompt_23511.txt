I'm a retired marine geologist — 38 years out on drilling ships and squinting at cores, now mostly gardening and reading — and a friend dragged me into a philosophy reading group last autumn. We're working through Wittgensteins On Certainty (I know, the posthumous one, the late notes), and I have to confess I'm stuck on one of the early entries and the group kind of moved on past it. It's note 4:

"I know that I am a human being." In order to see how unclear the sense of this proposition is, consider its negation. At most it might be taken to mean "I know I have the organs of a human". (E.g. a brain which, after all, no one has ever yet seen.) But what about such a proposition as "I know I have a brain"? Can I doubt it? Grounds for doubt are lacking! Everything speaks in its favour, nothing against it. Nevertheless it is imaginable that my skull should turn out empty when it was operated on.

Here's my problem with it. As a scientist, "it's imaginable" is basically what a hypothesis is — the empty-skull scenario has some tiny non-zero probability, and that's exactly the sort of thing we'd take seriously as a ground for doubt, however improbable. So why does he say "grounds for doubt are lacking" and then immediately hand me a scenario that looks like a ground for doubt? What's the difference he's drawing between something being concievable and something being doubtful, and is that "nevertheless" sentence meant as a concession, or as a trap for the reader? And how does all this fit with the hinge idea he develops later — the notion that some of our certainties aren't beliefs at all but the fixed framework that makes doubting possible in the first place? I keep feeling like he's making a purely linguistic point, but I want to know whether there's a substantive epistemic claim in there that I could actually argue with, the way I'd want to argue with a scientific claim. A concrete example or two would really help.
