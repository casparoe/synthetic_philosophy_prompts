I'm working on a short essay for my philosophy seminar on the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, and I'd like some feedback before I turn it in. Here is the core of my argument:

My working claim is that anekāntavāda is best understood as a sophisticated form of perspectival relativism. As I understand it, the doctrine holds that any object or substance has an infinite number of properties or aspects, and that no single statement or standpoint can capture more than one of them. For that reason, the Jain tradition develops the practice of qualifying every assertion with a condition — the "syāt" ("in some respect") prefix — and it culminates in the famous sevenfold formula, according to which, from different standpoints, a thing can be affirmed, denied, both affirmed and denied, or placed beyond description. The upshot I want to argue is that anekāntavāda is, at bottom, a doctrine of intellectual tolerance: since every one-sided claim (what the Jains call ekāntavāda) is only partially true, the rational attitude is to hold one's own view loosely and to take others' views seriously.

Two questions. First, on accuracy: is my characterization of the doctrine fair, or am I distorting it in some important way? Second, on the argument itself: even if the doctrine is exactly as I have described it, does it really follow that the rational upshot is tolerance, or is that a non sequitur I am smuggling in? I would appreciate being told specifically where I am wrong, rather than just being told to "consider other perspectives."
