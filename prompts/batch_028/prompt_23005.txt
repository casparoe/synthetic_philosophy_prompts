I'm working on a long-form piece about how different intellectual traditions handle genuinely conflicting truths, and I've been digging into the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda — the "many-sidedness" idea. My editor wants me to steelman the Jain side rather than just dismiss it as "everything goes," so I've been trying to get the details right. The standard illustration is the house: one person calls it a house, another calls it a pile of bricks, and Jain logic (the seven-fold predication, saptabhaṅgī, where each claim is prefixed with "syāt," i.e. "in some respect") says they're both right.

Here's where I get stuck, and I'd love a sanity check. The popular accounts I keep reading present anekāntavāda as basically the claim that every viewpoint is equally true from its own perspetive — that the "syāt" qualifier is a way of leveling all perspectives onto one plane. If that's really what it amounts to, then how can Jains defend ahimsa as the objectively correct ethical stance rather than just one more perspective among many? It seems to me the doctrine either colapses into relativism (in which case the ethical claim is empty) or it's not actually the "all perspectives are equally valid" thing the popular accounts make it out to be. Am I misreading it, or is there a real tension here that the standard presentations just paper over?
