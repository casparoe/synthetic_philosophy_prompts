I've been reading on my own about Kripke's arguments on proper names and possible worlds — mostly secondary sources and a couple of long popular essays — and I have the broad shape of the picture: names are supposed to rigidly designate their referents across all possible worlds, which is why the claim that the evening star is the morning star is supposed to be a necessary truth that we can only know a posteriori. But there's a detail I keep getting stuck on, and I can't tell whether I'm missing something obvious or whether the position is genuinely in trouble here.

If the identity is true in every possible world, it seems it should be true even in a world where no one has ever seen the morning star — a world where the planet we call Venus exists but no one there has ever observed it as the morning star, and no one has ever used the name. In such a world, what makes the statement true? There is no morning-star usage there for the name to hook onto, so it's not as if the name picks out the planet by anything happening in that world. Does this mean the necessity claim is really just a claim about our world and our language, or does the framework somehow allow a name to refer in a world where the name is not used at all — and if so, how do we make sense of that? I'm also stuck on the extreme case: in a world where the planet itself does not exist, is the statement false there, or simply not true? If it is not true there, then it cannot be true in every world, which is exactly what "necessary" was supposed to mean.

What I'm after is a clear explanation of how, on this view, the truth of such an identity statement is supposed to work in worlds where the names are not used and where the referent may not even exist — and whether that explanation is as solid as it looks, or whether the whole necessary-a-posteriori story quietly depends on stipulating the problem away.
