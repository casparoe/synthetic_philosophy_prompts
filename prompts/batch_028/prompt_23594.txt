I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or the foundations of probability, and I've run into a puzzle I can't seem to resolve on my own. It's the one usually called the Judy Benjamin problem, from a 1981 paper by Bas van Fraassen. The setup, as I understand it: you are dropped into a region that is partitioned along two independent axes — Red versus Blue territory, and Second Company versus Headquarters — and you assign each of the four resulting quadrants equal probability, 1/4, so your prior probability of being in Red territory is 1/2. You then learn a single piece of information: conditional on being in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 in favor of being in the Headquarters section, i.e. the new conditional probability is P(Headquarters | Red) = 3/4. The question the paper poses is what your new probability of being in Red territory should be.

My confusion is about why this is a problem at all. My first instinct is to just apply Bayes's rule, but I can't see how to do that, because the thing you learn isn't an event in the sample space — it's a statement about a conditional probability. I can see why ordinary conditioning doesn't go through, and I suspect Jeffrey's rule doesn't just apply either, but I'd like someone to explain both of those failures clearly. Then I'd like you to walk through the minimum-relative-entropy (Infomin) way of resolving it: set up the optimization, show that it pins down a unique posterior, and give the resulting probability for Red territory (I've seen the number 0.467 quoted, but I don't understand where it comes from). I'm especially interested in the counterintuitive part, which I think is that this update actually lowers your probability of being in Red territory below the prior 1/2, even though the new information is, in some sense, "about" Red territory.

Finally, I'd like you to be honest about whether the Infomin answer is really the right one or just one defensible option among several. I've gotten the impression that different distance functions between the prior and posterior probability assignments give different answers, and that some people think the problem doesn't have a unique solution at all. I'd appreciate an explanation of what is actually in dispute here, what the competing resolutions are, and what a careful probabilist would say is the upshot.
