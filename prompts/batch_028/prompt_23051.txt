Two people in my study group got stuck in an argument. We'd just read about Frankfurt cases in a free will textbook — the one where a neuroscientist named Black is about to make a guy named Jones do something, but only if Jones isn't going to do it on his own. One of them said, "That kills the principle of alternative possibilities — Jones couldn't have done otherwise, so he can't be responsible." The other pushed back: "He could have — he just had to want to. Black only steps in if he doesn't. That's a flicker of freedom, and that's enough." The first person then said, "A flicker is a real alternative, not a fake one. You can't call it an alternative and then say it doesn't count." The second conceded that but said, "Responsibility isn't about having options anyway. It's about the decision being yours — coming out of your own reasons and commitments. Jones's decision was like that, so he's responsible. The flicker only matters because it shows the decision was his, not because it was an option he could have taken." The first person replied, "So if the flicker is just evidence that the decision was his, then remove the flicker entirely — make it physically impossible for Jones to do otherwise — and he's still responsible? That's absurd. And if the flicker isn't just evidence, then you're back to saying it's a real alternative, so you've conceded the point." They're stuck. Can you go through the exchange and say who's right about what, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
