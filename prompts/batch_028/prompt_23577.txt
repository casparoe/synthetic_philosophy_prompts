I am preparing the reading list for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of vagueness, and in a recent discussion with my colleagues I noticed a persistent and, I think, instructive confusion between two items that the literature on the sorites paradox usually presents together: the epistemicist thesis itself, and what is often called the margin-for-error argument. I would be grateful if you could write a careful comparison of the two, treating them as distinct objects of assessment rather than as a single package.

To set the terms: on epistemicism, a vague predicate such as "heap" or "tall" has a sharp extension, and the paradox is dissolved by denying the tolerance premise, since there is some pair of neighboring members of a sorites series at which the predicate switches. The margin-for-error argument, on the standard presentation, begins from the plausible idea that if one cannot know whether a borderline case falls within a vague predicate, then one cannot know whether a case only slightly further inside the predicate's range falls within it either, and concludes by iterating along the series that no one can know where the boundary of a vague predicate lies. What I would like your comparison to address is the following. First, does the margin-for-error argument actually support the positive epistemicist claim that sharp boundaries exist, or does it at most show that we cannot know where the boundaries are — and does that distinction matter, given that the existence of a sharp boundary is what the argument is supposed to explain? Second, what is the status of the tolerance premise on each view: is it false, or merely unknown, and are these the same thing? Third, how does each item fare against the objection that the argument is itself vulnerable to a sorites, because the notion of a "small" or "negligible" difference is itself vague.

Please keep the focus on these two items; I am not looking for a survey of supervaluationist or fuzzy-logic alternatives, and I would prefer that you not attribute views to particular authors more strongly than the literature supports. If there is a point where the two items are commonly conflated in the literature, flag it explicitly; that is the main thing I want to use in the seminar.
