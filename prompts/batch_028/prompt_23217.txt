I've been trying to work out how I should treat my own judgment when I find myself in genuine disagreement with a knowledgeable friend about an empirical question, and I keep coming back to Aumann's 1976 result, "Agreeing to Disagree," which I understand as saying that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot, in equilibrium, have their differing posterior beliefs become common knowledge — if the posteriors do become common knowledge, they must coincide. I've also read that the no-trade theorems in financial economics (Milgrom and Stokey's 1982 "Information, trade and common knowledge") rest on this same common-prior / common-knowledge structure, so the result feels to me like it has real bite beyond pure theory.

From this I've been reasoning that the everyday implication is fairly strong: when I genuinely disagree with a well-informed friend, either one of us is being irrational, or the disagreement is unstable and the "right" move is to converge — for instance by averaging our views or, in the limit, deferring to the person I judge better informed. My question is whether the theorem actually supports that reading. If it does, what is the correct decision procedure for me when I find myself at odds with a knowledgeable friend — should I always defer to them, always split the difference, or is there some principled middle ground the result points toward? If it doesn't, I'd like to understand precisely which assumption is doing the work, and why the theorem, if anything, fails to license the kind of updating rule I've been assuming.
