I am taking an upper-level course on the philosophy of mind this term, and we have been reading on the nature of belief, which led two of my study-group mates into a fairly heated argument about Moore's paradox that I was unable to referee. For context, the paradox is the oddity of a sentence that combines a claim with a flat denial of one's own belief in that claim; the standard example is a statement that it is raining while the speaker says that he does not believe it. Such a sentence seems absurd to assert, yet its two parts do not appear to be logically incompatible.

Maya began by insisting that the sentence is not a genuine contradiction. The proposition it expresses is perfectly consistent, she said, because it is entirely possible for it to be raining while I fail to believe so, as when I have not looked out the window. The absurdity, she argued, is a feature of assertion rather than of content: in asserting the whole conjunction I present myself as believing that it is raining, which collides with the second conjunct. The paradox is therefore, on her view, a pragmatic one, a clash between the speech act and the proposition, not a logical one. Jonah pushed back by appealing to silent thought. If I merely entertain the thought to myself, without uttering a word, he said, it still strikes me as absurd. If the paradox were purely a feature of the external speech act of assertion, the unspoken thought should be unremarkable; since it is not, he concluded, the paradox must concern belief itself, or at least the logical structure of the thought, rather than the way the content is communicated. Maya replied that thinking the thought is itself a kind of assertion, an inner assertion in which I present the content to myself, so that the absurdity is still a matter of assertoric force, merely internalized, and that there is no such thing as entertaining a proposition without some assertoric commitment. Jonah answered that this only relocates the paradox rather than dissolving it: if the absurdity requires assertoric force and every thought possesses it, then the paradox is a fact about the nature of thought and belief, not about communication, and a purely pragmatic account cannot explain it away.

I honestly could not tell which of them was more defensible, and I would like someone to go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please end your reply with a final verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Maya} or \answer{Jonah}, naming whoever you judge to be more right overall.
