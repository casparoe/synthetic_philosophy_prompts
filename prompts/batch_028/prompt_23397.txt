I've been working on a novel in which one of the characters is a pragmatist philosopher wrestling with whether her own belief in free will is justified, and I've been reading about the "wrong kind of reasons" problem to calibrate how to write her internal conflict. She is drawn to the idea that a belief is justified insofar as it pays off in practice — that its practical consequences are what make it rational to hold — but she keeps circling back to a worry that this position is circular: if the belief is justified because it has good practical consequences, and the practical consequences include the very fact that she holds the belief, then the justification seems to depend on what it is supposed to explain. I'm a bit confused, though, about the distinction between "right kind" and "wrong kind" reasons as it's usually drawn in the literature. It strikes me that the distinction is just between good reasons and bad reasons: a "right kind" reason is a good reason to believe something, and a "wrong kind" reason is simply a bad reason to believe something. If that's all the distinction amounts to, then the "wrong kind of reasons" problem is just the trivial observation that bad reasons are bad, and I'm not sure why it is treated as a significant philosophical problem rather than a piece of common sense. Could you help me understand what the non-trivial content of the distinction is, and why it matters for the kind of character I am trying to write?
