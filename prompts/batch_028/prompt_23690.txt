I've been thinking about Wigner's "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics," and I keep getting stuck on one specific case rather than the general claim, so I'd like a dialogue that drills into it. The case I mean is Wigner's own classification of elementary particles by the irreducible unitary representations of the Poincaré group, in which mass and spin (or, for massless particles, helicity) label the particle types. To my eye this is the cleanest instance of the whole phenomenon: a piece of pure group theory, motivated by the symmetries of spacetime, turns out to line up exactly with the observed spectrum of particles.

I want the dialogue to be between two people who are both genuinely strong on this material and who actually engage each other's points. One should defend a structuralist or mathematical-realist reading: that the non-accidental fit between the representation theory and the particle content of nature is real evidence that mathematical structures are discovered rather than invented, and that the predictive byproducts of the framework — the spin-statistics connection, the prediction of antiparticles — are what make it more than a bookkeeping device. The other should press the strongest version of the "it's partly a tautology" objection: that in Wigner's scheme an elementary particle is, roughly, defined to be an irreducible representation of the Poincaré group, so the classification is built in, and the real question is just why we should expect nature to be organized by the symmetry group of special relativity in the first place. I'd like the second person to try to dissolve the mystery rather than to concede realism, and I'd like the first person to respond to the definition point specifically rather than to wave it away.

Please make it a real back-and-forth of perhaps six to ten exchanges, with each speaker reacting to the specific thing the other just said, and have them end still disagreeing but having sharpened exactly where the disagreement lies.
