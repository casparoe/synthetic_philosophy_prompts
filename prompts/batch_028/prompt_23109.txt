I have been reading Frederick Douglass's speeches from the 1850s and 1860s on my own, and I am trying to reconstruct the strongest version of his later argument that the original Constitution was, on its face, an anti-slavery document, even though he had earlier insisted that it was a pro-slavery one. The move I find hardest to defend is his reading of the fugitive slave clause in Article IV: he treats it as applying only to indentured servants and apprentices who owe service under contract, and not to slaves at all, and I want to argue that reading seriously. Could you help me build that case, including responses to the obvious objections — that the clause seems to recognize the master's property right in the person who escapes, that the three-fifths clause looks like a reward to slave states for extra representation, and that the 1808 limit on the slave trade seems to legitimize the trade for two decades? I would also like to know how much weight I can honestly place on his observation that the word "slave" never appears in the text. Please make the case that the 1787 Constitution, read on its own terms and without the later amendments, is best understood as an instrument tilted against slavery, in the way Douglass came to understand it.
