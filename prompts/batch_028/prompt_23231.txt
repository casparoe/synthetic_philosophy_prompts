Two friends of mine, who both read a bit of ethics but neither is a philosopher, got into a long argument about whether doing the right thing for the right reason is necessary for an act to have moral worth. I don't read enough of the literature to referee it, so I'd like a careful adjudication. Here's how it went.

Alex said moral worth requires the agent to be moved by the moral consideration itself, not merely by a desire that happens to point the right way. His example: someone who donates a large sum to a charity because they genuinely care about the people it helps, versus someone who donates the same sum because they enjoy the feeling of being seen as generous. The first donation has moral worth; the second doesn't, even though the outcome is identical.

Jordan said that's too strict, and that it makes moral worth depend on a kind of mental purity no one actually has. Most people who do good things have a mix of motives — they care about others, but they also feel good about themselves or want recognition. If the moral motive has to be the only motive, then almost no one ever has moral worth, which seems wrong.

Alex replied that the point isn't that the moral motive must be the only motive, but that it must be a genuine reason for the act, not an accidental byproduct. In the "feeling good" case, the person isn't really motivated by the good of others; they're motivated by their own feelings, and the good of others is just a means to those feelings.

Jordan said that's a hard line to draw. In most real cases the motives are entangled — you can't cleanly separate "caring about others" from "feeling good about caring about others." So the "right reason" requirement is really just a way of saying that moral worth depends on how the act looks to the judge, not on anything objective.

Alex conceded the line is fuzzy, but said vagueness doesn't make a concept useless, and that the obvious alternative — judging moral worth purely by outcomes — is clearly worse, since it lets off the hook people who do harm with good intentions.

Jordan agreed the outcome-only view is bad, but said the "right reason" view has its own problem: it makes moral worth a matter of intention alone, which is too subjective. And she wasn't sure what to do with cases where someone tries hard to help but accidentally does harm — does their moral worth come from the intention, or is it destroyed by the bad outcome?

They couldn't resolve it. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And point out anything both of them are missing.
