I just spent about two hours in a bar arguing with my friend Daniel about whether he is being irrational in the way he has been trading collectible cards over the past few months, and I would like a second opinion from someone who thinks about rational decision-making more carefully than either of us. We are both collectors, and Daniel currently holds three cards from the same set — call them A, B, and C — and he has stated his preferences among them with great confidence: he would rather have A than B, B than C, and C than A. I have always assumed that this kind of looping preference is simply a fact of human psychology; people's tastes shift with mood and context, and I could not see what was wrong with it. Daniel disagrees, and he has an argument that he says is decisive.

His argument is that if his preferences are truly what he says, then I — as a fellow collector who happens to own the same three cards and not care much which one is in whose hand — can offer him a series of trades that he cannot rationally refuse. "Pay me twenty dollars and I will swap your A for B," I would say; he prefers B, and the sum is trivial, so he accepts. Then "Pay twenty dollars and I will swap your B for C"; he accepts. Then "Pay twenty dollars and I will swap your C for A"; he accepts. At the end of the sequence he is holding exactly the card he started with, and I am sixty dollars richer. Daniel says this proves that a looping preference set is incoherent and that a rational agent is not permitted to have one; the point of the argument, he says, is to characterize what an ideal agent must satisfy, not to predict what an actual person will do in a bar. I replied that the argument does no real work, because the real Daniel would see the pattern after the first trade and simply refuse the second, so the "proof" only applies to a hypothetical version of himself who simply does not notice what is being done to him. He said that reply concedes nothing, because each individual trade is still one he would prefer to make, so even a forewarned version of him with those fixed preferences would complete the cycle.

I am not asking for advice about trading cards, and I do not need a verdict on who argued better. What I want to know is whether his argument is a genuine constraint on rationality — something that shows a looping preference set to be a defect that a rational agent must avoid — or whether it is a cleverly dressed-up threat that only works against an agent who would accept offers it should not. In particular, I would like to know whether my "he would just refuse the second trade" reply defeats the argument, and if not, exactly what the argument does show and what it does not.
