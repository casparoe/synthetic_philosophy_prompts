I've been circling Williamson's anti-luminosity argument — the one from *Knowledge and Its Limits* that runs against the idea of knowledge as a "luminous" state, and which keeps resurfacing on the forum whenever someone claims you can't fail to know what you know — and I keep getting stuck on a specific joint in the reasoning rather than the headline conclusion, so I'd rather work through the structure carefully than get a summary. My current reconstruction is this: the luminosity or transparency thesis says that if *p*, then you are in a position to know that *p*; Williamson argues this fails by pointing to a "buffer zone" of borderline cases, where *p* is true but you're not in a position to know *p* because *p* sits right at the margin, and symmetrically where not-*p* is true but you're not in a position to know not-*p*. The intended punchline is that luminosity is false; and since the KK principle (if you know *p*, you know that you know *p*) is generally taken to *imply* luminosity of knowledge — roughly, knowing that you know *p* requires you to be in a position to know *p* — the argument is meant to drag KK down with luminosity. What I'd like you to do is lay out the actual logical steps in a way that cleanly separates (i) the move connecting KK to luminosity, (ii) the role of the buffer zone, and (iii) the assumption that the relevant epistemic predicate — "being in a position to know *p*" — itself has margins or borderline cases.

Then I want you to adjudicate a specific worry I have, rather than give a neutral survey: the argument seems to establish, at most, that you are not always *in a position to know* that you are in a position to know *p*, which is a claim about the ability to know, whereas KK is a claim about *actual* knowledge. So does the anti-luminosity argument genuinely refute KK, or does it only refute the stronger transparency/luminosity thesis, leaving KK intact as a constraint on the cases where you actually do know? And is there a reading of "in a position to know" — say, a safety-based one, on which you're in a position to know *p* only if your belief in *p* couldn't easily have been false — on which the buffer-zone premise simply fails?
