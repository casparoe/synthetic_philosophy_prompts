Dana started with Aumann's 1976 "agreeing to disagree" result, and said it basically proves you can't have two rational people with the same prior who both know each other's probabilities and still end up disagreeing — so in a real market, if we're both rational and we both know each other's number, one of us has to be wrong, or we don't actually share a prior, or the disagreement isn't common knowledge. Which means, he said, nobody's trade is ever purely about private information, because the moment you'd trade you'd have to know the other guy's estimate and then you'd be forced to converge — that's the whole no-trade theorem, and it's why markets are just evidence that someone's always violating the assumptions. Marcus pushed back and said Dana was smuggling in an assumption: the theorem doesn't just require each person to know the other's number, it requires the disagreement itself to be common knowledge, like each knows the other knows, and so on, forever, and markets never get there. You see a price move and you infer something, but you never get the other guy's exact posterior as common knowledge, so the theorem doesn't say no trade, it says no trade under full common knowledge, which is a situation that basically never happens. The trading, he said, is exactly the process by which information gets revealed and common knowledge gets partially built up. Dana fired back that this is just giving up — if the theorem only applies in a situation that never occurs, then it's not a constraint on markets, it's a tautology, and you can't claim a no-trade theorem and then admit the real world never meets the conditions. Marcus said it constrains in the way all idealized theorems do, as a limiting case: the more common-knowledge-like the information gets, the more trade on private information dries up, and that's a real claim about the direction of market efficiency, and it also diagnoses what's actually doing the work in real markets, which is the failure of common knowledge, not irrationality. Dana said then the whole thing is a misnomer, it's really a "no trade under common knowledge" theorem, which is basically the obvious statement that if you both know everything about each other you don't trade. And Marcus closed by saying the common prior assumption is doing even more work than the common knowledge one, because people in markets have genuinely different priors, different models of the world, and the theorem is silent on that — or rather, it says if priors differ then disagreement is fine and trade is fine, so the theorem is really about a very narrow slice, same prior and full common knowledge, and the rest of trading is outside its scope entirely.
