I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I also sit on the advisory board of a nonprofit fact-checking initiative that rates official and political claims. Last fall, at a press conference ahead of a state emergency-management hearing, a regional official said that the September storm "was one of the most powerful to hit the state in recorded history." Our desk pulled the data. By peak sustained wind speed — the meteorological standard for a storm's intensity — the storm ranks eleventh among the state's named storms; by minimum central pressure, nineteenth; by economic damage, fourth. The board is now split on how to rate the claim, and I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

One editor wants to rate it false. Her argument, which a philosopher on the board has labeled supervaluationism, is that a vague claim is true only if it comes out true under every reasonable way of making it precise: "one of the most powerful" has no sharp cutoff, and since a reasonable reading — say, the top five, or the top ten by wind speed — excludes the storm, the claim is not true. The other editor objects that this misdescribes the semantics. He calls his position epistemicism, in the sense associated with Timothy Williamson: there is a fact of the matter about whether an eleventh-ranked storm counts as "one of the most powerful"; the boundary is simply beyond our cognitive reach; and a fact-checker's job is to report the best available evidence about which side of the boundary we're on, not to apply a truth rule that would condemn most of the vague claims people actually make. I lean toward the second view, but I'm not sure it survives contact with the practical constraint that our ratings come from a fixed menu — true, mostly true, misleading, false, unverifiable — and I'd rather have the opinion argue the case than assume it.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete rating for the storm claim and a standing rule for how the desk should treat vague claims generally — claims built on predicates like "one of the most," "largely," or "most" that lack a sharp cutoff. I want it to engage both positions seriously, including the strongest version of whichever one it recommends against, and to say plainly what a reader of the fact-check should take away from the storm claim either way. If the memo concludes that our rating menu itself is part of the problem, it should say so and propose what to do about it.
