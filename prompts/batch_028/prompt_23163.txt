I have been trying to work out for myself, without any formal training in philosophy or psychiatry, what Ian Hacking means by the "looping effects" of human kinds, and I have settled on a rough picture: a psychiatric category does not merely describe a fixed set of people who happen to fit it, but once the category exists it reaches back and changes the people who find themselves inside it, who then behave in ways that partly keep the category in place or alter it, so that the classification and the classified are locked in a feedback loop rather than in a simple describing-and-described relationship. What I want from you is not a restatement of this idea but the most important practical and conceptual implications it carries, and I would like you to work through them in the context of a fairly specific situation rather than in the abstract. Suppose a person I know is considering whether to accept a new, still fairly contested diagnostic label for a pattern of experience that has recently been carved out of a broader, older diagnosis, and they are worried that accepting the label will do something to the way they understand and manage themselves over the coming years. Given the looping-effects picture, what are the most important implications for how that person should think about whether, when, and under what conditions to accept the label? In particular, I would like you to address the tension between the label being genuinely useful for access to treatment and for giving the person a vocabulary for their own experience, on one hand, and the risk that the loop will start to shape the person's self-concept, their sense of what is "normal" for them, and the way clinicians and others expect them to be, on the other. I would also like you to say something about whether this looping logic applies in the same way to a stable, long-established diagnosis as it does to a newly minted one, and about whether there is any sense in which a person can "opt out" of the loop or moderate its strength. Please keep the answer concrete to the situation I have described, and do not spend much time re-explaining the basic idea, which I already understand.
