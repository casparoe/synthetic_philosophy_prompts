I am a teaching assistant grading homework for a graduate seminar on decision theory, and one of the assigned questions concerned Kavka's toxin puzzle. The question and a student's answer are below. Please assign a letter grade and a score out of 10, and justify the grade with point-by-point comments that identify which claims in the answer are correct and which contain errors.

Question: An eccentric billionaire offers you one million dollars tomorrow morning on the following condition: that at midnight tonight you intend to drink a certain toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin is harmless in the long run but will make you severely ill for a day. You have no independent reason to drink it. (a) Explain why the puzzle seems to show that a rational agent can form an intention that is irrational. (b) Present Kavka's response to the puzzle, and say whether it dissolves the puzzle or leaves something unresolved.

Student's answer:

The puzzle exploits a timing asymmetry. At the decision point, intending to drink is instrumentally rational, since the million dollars depends on the intention, not on the drinking. But when afternoon arrives, the payment is already locked in, and the only remaining consequence of drinking is a day of suffering, so at that point drinking is plainly irrational. Kavka's puzzle is thus a reductio: it shows that a fully rational agent can end up intending something that is itself irrational, because the intention that was rational to form turns out to be irrational to act on. Kavka's response is that the puzzle dissolves once we notice that intending to drink rationally requires believing that you will drink. Since you know that at the moment of drinking you will have every reason to abstain, you cannot rationally believe you will drink, and so you cannot rationally intend it. The puzzle therefore collapses into the question of whether you can rationally believe that you will do something you know you will have decisive reasons not to do.

I think that question is genuinely unresolved, because you can in some circumstances rationally believe that you will act against your own future reasons, as when you form a firm commitment. But Kavka has at least shown that the original puzzle is not as simple as it first appears.
