I'm a second-year PhD student in the philosophy of probability, and last week I sat in on a graduate reading group that was working through Kyburg's paper "The Reference Class" (1983) alongside some secondary literature on direct inference. The discussion centered on the reference class problem: roughly, the fact that any individual belongs to indefinitely many reference classes with different relative frequencies of the property in question, so it is not obvious which class's frequency should be taken as the individual's probability. The group had been operating with the standard diagnosis that the problem is handled by a "most specific reference class" principle, on the model of Reichenbach's discussion: when the individual belongs to several relevant classes with known frequencies, one should use the frequency of the most specific such class, where "most specific" is cashed out as the narrowest class for which a warranted and reasonably precise frequency judgment is available. Two students in the group, Elena and Marcus, got into a fairly pointed disagreement about whether that principle actually does the work it is credited with, and I found myself unable to decide who was more right.

Elena started by defending the principle: it is not just a slogan, it tracks a real methodological practice. She gave the example of a thirty-five-year-old non-smoker with no family history of heart disease who wants to know his probability of a cardiac event in the next decade. The frequency among "adults" is one thing, among "non-smokers" another, and among the full conjunction of his relevant properties a third, and the principle tells you to use the third, which is exactly what an actuary or a physician would do. Marcus replied that the example is rigged, because it quietly assumes that the properties in the conjunction are the relevant ones; nothing in the principle itself says why "never bitten by a wasp" or "born in a leap year" do not count as additional specific properties, and once you allow arbitrary properties you can construct a reference class with whatever frequency you like, including one that contradicts the actuary's number, so the principle as stated has no built-in defense against gerrymandering. Elena conceded that the principle needs a relevance constraint—you cannot just add any property—but she insisted that this concession does not defeat it: the relevance question is a separate, prior matter, and once you have settled which properties are relevant, "most specific" does real work, telling you how to combine them and explaining why, when two reference classes with known frequencies genuinely conflict, the narrower one wins. Marcus accepted that the principle is useful in practice but pushed back on the claim that it solves anything. He said that the moment you add the relevance constraint, the real work is being done by the relevance account, not by the specificity principle; "most specific" is at best a tie-breaking heuristic among classes that have already been judged relevant, and calling it "the" solution to the reference class problem overstates what it is doing. Elena's final move was that this is a misreading of the principle's role: it was never meant to be a standalone solution, it is meant to be applied in tandem with a background account of which properties matter, and Marcus's objection only works if you treat it as a complete account on its own. Marcus's final move was that even charitably construed the principle is doing less work than the literature usually credits, and that the real solution to the reference class problem is the relevance account, with "most specific" as a secondary criterion.

Could you adjudicate this exchange for me? I would like you to go through it move by move and say, for each of Elena's and Marcus's claims, whether it is right, wrong, or only partially right, and why. I am particularly interested in whether the gerrymandering objection is a genuine refutation of the most-specific-reference-class principle or merely shows that it is incomplete, and in whether the relevance constraint is a separate prior question or is entangled with the specificity principle in a way the debate does not acknowledge. Please also say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Work from your own knowledge of the reference class literature; I do not need you to cite specific papers, but I do want you to engage with the actual philosophical issues rather than just summarizing the debate.
