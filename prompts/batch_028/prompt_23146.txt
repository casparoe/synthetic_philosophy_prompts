I've been reading about a thought experiment Max Black ran with two identical spheres, and I keep going back and forth on whether it actually defeats the principle it's aimed at, so I ended up in a long argument about it with two friends of mine last weekend, and I genuinely couldn't tell which of them was more on track. I'll set it up the way it went, and I'd like you to go through it and tell me who's right about which specific point, plus anything both of them are missing.

The principle in question is the identity of indiscernibles—roughly, that two things can't be exactly alike in every respect and still be two different things. Black's example is a universe containing only two perfect spheres of the same size and the same uniform material, sitting a mile apart, with nothing else in it.

My friend Dana started by saying the example is a clean counterexample: the spheres match on every intrinsic property—size, shape, material, color, all of it—and since there's nothing else in the universe for them to be related to, there's no property that one has and the other lacks, so they're two distinct indiscernible things and the principle is just false. Marcus pushed back that she was only counting intrinsic properties; the spheres are in different places, one at one location and one at another, and "being at this location" is a property the first has and the second doesn't, so they're discernible and the principle is safe.

Dana replied that he was smuggling in spatial location as a property, but the whole point of the setup is a universe where space is just the relation between the two spheres, so "being at a location" isn't a real property, it's just a way of describing the one-mile-apart relation—and that relation is symmetric, since each sphere is exactly a mile from the other, so even the relational properties come out identical. Marcus conceded that only if you already assume space is relational, and said that's a separate metaphysical commitment: if space is a real arena with real, distinct points, then the spheres genuinely occupy different points, and that's a real difference independent of any relation to other bodies, so the counterexample only works against someone who has already decided space is relational.

Then Dana said that even granting all that, it just shows the principle is doing less work than people think—if it survives only by leaning on the existence of absolute space, then it's not the deep principle Leibniz is supposed to stand for, because the moment you have to add "and they differ in position in an absolute space" to save it, you've already conceded that being qualitatively identical doesn't guarantee being the same thing. Marcus answered that she was conflating "the principle needs some specification of which properties count" with "the principle is false"; every principle needs its terms pinned down, and spatial position is a perfectly legitimate property, so the principle, properly understood, is true and the spheres are discernible.

Dana's last point was that even the absolute-space version has a hole: if the spheres are the only things in the universe, then "position" has to be defined relative to something, and if it's defined relative to the other sphere you're back to the symmetric relation, but if it's defined relative to absolute space you need those locations to be genuinely different—and in an empty space there's nothing to tell the locations apart, so you've just pushed the problem one level up instead of solving it. Marcus sort of conceded that was a real point and said the honest answer was probably that the two-spheres case doesn't cleanly refute the principle, but it does expose a real ambiguity in it; he'd grant it's not the simple deep principle people imagine, but he still didn't think it's a counterexample.

So where that leaves me is that I think both of them have real points and I can't tell where each of them overreaches. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say, for each major claim, whether it's right, wrong, or only conditionally right, and then tell me what, if anything, the two of them are both missing, and give me your overall sense of who was more on track?
