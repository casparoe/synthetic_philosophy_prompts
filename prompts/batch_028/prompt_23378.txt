I'm a science journalist, and I'm preparing a long-form piece on the foundations of quantum mechanics for a magazine. I've been reading accounts of Bell's theorem, and I keep hitting what I suspect is my own confusion, but I can't locate it. The standard narrative is that Bell's theorem shows quantum mechanics is nonlocal: the correlations between measurements on entangled particles cannot be reproduced by any theory in which the outcome at each detector depends only on the local measurement setting and on information shared from the particles' common past, and the usual conclusion is that nature itself is nonlocal, in the sense of the action-at-a-distance picture Einstein objected to in the EPR argument. But then there is the no-signaling result, sometimes called the no-communication theorem, which says that the statistics recorded at one measurement station cannot depend on which measurement setting the distant station chooses, so that the entangled correlations can never be exploited to transmit a message faster than light. The experiments agree with this: no signal ever crosses the gap between the detectors.

So here is my question, and I'd be grateful for a precise answer. If "locality" is supposed to mean that no physical influence or signal propagates faster than light, and what Bell's experiments show is only that no such influence is ever sent, then what exactly has Bell's theorem ruled out? To me, the phrase "quantum nonlocality" looks like it's doing all the work without any content: if nothing is transmitted across space, in what sense is anything nonlocal? And why does the no-signaling result deepen the puzzle rather than dissolve it — it seems to confirm that, operationally, everything behaves locally? I want to understand what the nonlocality in Bell's theorem is supposed to be, and where my reasoning goes wrong if it does.
