I'm a philosophy PhD student working on non-classical logics, and I've been trying to make sense of the Jain saptabhaṅgī (the seven-fold predication) as part of my reading on anekāntavāda and its possible relationship to many-valued logics. I can recite the seven predicates without trouble: for any proposition, one can say that in some ways it exists, that in some ways it does not exist, that in some ways it both exists and does not exist, that in some ways it is inexpressible, and so on, up through the seventh, which combines all three modes at once. What I can't get a grip on is the logical architecture sitting underneath that list.

Two things are bugging me. First, the seven predicates look exactly like the non-empty subsets of a three-element set of modes — existence, non-existence, and inexpressibility — and that combinatorial regularity tempts me to formalize the scheme as a genuine multi-valued logic in the Łukasiewicz sense, with truth functions over a seven-element value set. But that formalization seems to run straight into a problem: two of the three modes, existence and non-existence, are classically contradictory, and yet the scheme freely combines them into the legitimate middle predicate "exists and does not exist." If I try to read that as a conjunction inside a truth-functional system, it looks like I've just baked a contradiction into the value set, which would collapse the logic. So I'm no longer sure the "seven-valued logic" framing is even the right one.

Second, and more fundamentally, I've started to suspect the saptabhaṅgī may not be a logic at all in the truth-functional sense, but rather a taxonomy of standpoints — where the "syād" (in some ways) qualifier is doing the real work by indexing a single proposition relative to different aspects or perspectives of the object, so that "exists" and "does not exist" are never asserted of the same aspect at the same time and so never genuinely collide. If that's the right reading, then the scheme is a device for managing partial perspectives rather than a many-valued calculus, and the question of whether it violates the law of non-contradiction or the law of excluded middle dissolves in a quite different way than it does under the truth-functional reading. Could you walk me through the conceptual distinction between these two interpretations — as a many-valued logic versus as a standpoint-indexed taxonomy — and explain what actually decides which one is correct, what the status of the law of non-contradiction and of excluded middle is under each, and why that distinction matters for whether the doctrine is doing real logical work or merely rhetorical synthesis?
