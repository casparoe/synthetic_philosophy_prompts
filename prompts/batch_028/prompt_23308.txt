I am a junior at a public high school, and I was sitting between two of my friends at lunch when this argument came up, so I heard the whole thing. Over the fall, my friends Sam and Riley had decided to start a zine about our school's history. They picked the name together, split the first issue's work evenly, and put up a flyer together. In January, Riley stopped showing up to the meetings and told Sam they were "done with it," but did not tell the other two contributors or take down the flyer. Sam is furious and says Riley broke a promise. Riley says they never promised anything binding; it was just a shared interest, and they were free to walk away.

Here is, as near as I can reconstruct, what was said. Sam said that Riley could not unilaterally dissolve something the two of them had jointly intended: when they had announced the zine to the school as a "we," that had created a commitment that only the two of them together could end, and Riley had needed Sam's consent to exit. Riley replied that no such thing had ever existed, because a real "we"-intention — and Riley said they had read that this is what philosophers call a joint intention — requires each person to actually expect the other to be in it, and Riley had privately been afraid the whole time that Sam would lose interest, so what Riley had was only their own intention plus a hope. Riley added that, in any case, nothing of the sort could lock a person in forever; quitting is always available, one simply pays the cost. Sam answered that the cost is precisely the point: by vanishing without a word, Riley had burned the trust of the other two contributors, who had scheduled their own work around Riley's participation, and the fact that quitting was expensive was evidence that something shared had been broken, not merely that some practical inconvenience had been caused. Riley closed by saying the opposite: the reason quitting is costly is simply that people rely on one another's continued participation in ordinary cooperations, and Sam was smuggling in a metaphysical category — a shared object called a "joint intention" — to explain what is really just two ordinary facts, Riley's decision to stop and the others' disappointment.

I take AP government, not philosophy, so I could not referee this, and I am still not sure which of them was more right. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point — not just who wins overall — and say what, if anything, both of them are missing? Please end your reply with a final verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Sam} or \answer{Riley}, whoever is more right overall.
