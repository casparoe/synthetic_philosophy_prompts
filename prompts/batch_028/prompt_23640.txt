I've been trying to make sense of the dollar auction on my own, and I keep getting stuck on a point that I think most of the standard explanations gloss over, so I'd really appreciate a careful answer. For the setup, in case it's useful: a one-dollar bill is auctioned off, bids go up in one-dollar increments, the highest bidder pays their bid and keeps the bill, and the second-highest bidder also has to pay their bid but gets nothing in return. The auction is usually presented as a neat demonstration of the sunk cost fallacy and escalation of commitment — people keep bidding well past the point where the deal is clearly bad, as if they're trying to recoup what they've already spent. I accept the broad point, but I'm not convinced that "irrational loss aversion" is the real engine of the thing, and I suspect there's a more structural explanation.

Here's the specific snag. If I try to steelman the game-theoretic account — the idea that this is a trap even fully rational players can fall into, not just a cognitive bias — I keep hitting a problem. Imagine I'm the losing bidder, sitting one dollar behind the current high bid. My options look like this: either I stop and forfeit everything I've already put in, or I raise my bid by one dollar. But if I raise and the other person immediately stands down, I end up paying two dollars for a one-dollar bill, which is strictly worse than just stopping and losing my original bid. So at each individual step, "raise in order to win the dollar right now" looks like a dominated choice. If that's right, then the escalation can't be driven by anyone rationally raising in order to win the dollar immediately. So what actually keeps the bidding going past the dollar? My working hypothesis is that each player is really trying to avoid being the one who ultimately absorbs the largest loss, and is hoping the other person will be the one to break first — which would make this more like a war of attrition or a chicken-style standoff than a sunk-cost problem. If that's on the right track, then I'd like to understand at what point, if any, a perfectly rational player should stop, and why the bidding doesn't simply halt the moment it crosses the value of the bill. And finally, I'd like to know whether the "sunk cost" explanation and the "structural trap" explanation are genuinely two different accounts of the same phenomenon, or just two different descriptions of one and the same mechanism.
