I'm a third-year PhD student in bioethics, and I've been asked to give the eulogy for my father at next Saturday's service. He suffered a massive stroke in late March; his whole brain was dead within a few days, and the team confirmed it on two separate examinations, after which he remained on the ventilator at my mother's request until we asked them to withdraw it in early June, when his heart stopped within the hour. The complication is that my family is genuinely divided about what happened, and I don't think I can give an eulogy that pretends otherwise. My aunt, who has worked in an ICU for twenty years, is certain he died in March, the moment the brain death was confirmed, and she has been grieving since then. My uncle, who has never accepted that a warm body with a beating heart can be a corpse, insists he died in June, when his heart finally stopped, and he will hear "he's been dead for three months" as an obscenity. My mother seems to be living with both dates at once, and I suspect she is right, but I can't articulate why to the room. I've spent a semester of my coursework on exactly this question — which of the two criteria, whole-brain or circulatory, is the right one — and I've learned that it is not a question with a settled answer, which is not the most comforting thing to carry into a eulogy.

I need a eulogy of about ten minutes for a mixed room: my mother and my two siblings, my uncle and aunt, a few of his old colleagues from the plant floor, and two of the ICU nurses who were very kind to us. I don't want a lecture on the criteria of death — I'm the one in the room with the training, and the last thing my mother needs from me is a definition — but I do want the distinction between the two deaths to do the real structural work, because I think it's the only honest shape available. My hunch is that the eulogy should treat him as having died twice, in two different senses that both matter, and that the months between the two deaths were not a stretch of "not him" but a passage that each of us has to grieve at a different pace. If you think that's the wrong shape, say so briefly and then write the eulogy in the shape you think is right. One last constraint, from him: he was a stubborn man who despised sentiment that wasn't earned, so nothing in the eulogy may be false or merely consoling; every claim about him has to be one I could stand behind under questioning, and if the philosophical point is going to be in there at all, it has to arrive as something he would have recognized as true about his own life, not as a point I am making about him.
