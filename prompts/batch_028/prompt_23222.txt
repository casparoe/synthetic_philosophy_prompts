In a reading group I attend as a first-year philosophy of science PhD student, two colleagues recently reached an impasse over the pessimistic meta-induction. One argued that it is the strongest argument against scientific realism: the history of science is a sequence of theories that were once taken to be successful — Aristotelian physics, phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous aether — and that are now known to be false, so by induction our current best theories are probably false as well. The other replied that the list is rigged: several of those candidates were never empirically successful in the first place, and a meta-induction built on theories that were outcompeted on the evidence is no threat to realism. The first answered that this concedes the point, because the no-miracles argument — the claim that the success of our theories is a miracle unless they are true — requires that we count success, and by any honest standard the aether theory counts, since it was part of Maxwell's electrodynamics. The other said the no-miracles argument is not an argument at all but a restatement of the realist's commitment, whereas the meta-induction is a genuine argument precisely because it does not assume what it sets out to prove. Neither would budge. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — who is right about which specific claim — and say what, if anything, both sides are missing.
