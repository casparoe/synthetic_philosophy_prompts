I sit on the standards committee for a consumer-products testing lab that certifies products for consumer labeling (think of it as the body that decides whether a product can be labeled "shatterproof" or "waterproof" on the package). We've been asked to rule on whether a new phone case meets our "shatterproof" standard, which is defined as: the product must not shatter when dropped from a height of 1.5 meters onto a concrete surface. The case in question has a novel "self-reinforcing" polymer lining that is soft under normal conditions but hardens rapidly when it detects an imminent impact, via a proximity sensor and an accelerometer. In our standard drop test, the case did not shatter — the polymer hardened and absorbed the impact. However, in normal use the polymer is soft, and the case is, in a meaningful sense, breakable: if the sensor were to fail, the case would shatter when dropped. This raises a question I've been turning over in my mind: does the case *have* the property of being shatterproof, or is it *merely* the case that, when dropped, it does not shatter (because of the polymer)? In other words, is shatterproofness a property the case possesses in its normal state, or a property it acquires only in the moment the test is being conducted?

I was discussing this with our lab's lead engineer last week, and she argued that the case is *not* shatterproof because, in its normal state, it is breakable, and the polymer is merely an external factor that changes the case's behavior only when the test is being conducted — so the label would be misleading to a consumer who buys the case and later drops it in a situation where the sensor doesn't trigger. The manufacturer, on the other hand, argues that the case is shatterproof because it does not shatter when dropped, and that the standard's definition is satisfied to the letter. I've been thinking about this on my own since that conversation and I'm not sure which side is right; I suspect the answer depends on some general point about what it means for an object to "have" a property that shows up only under certain conditions, but I can't quite put my finger on it. Please draft a short written opinion for the committee that recommends a concrete decision (certify or not certify) with clear reasoning, and that engages the lead engineer's position seriously rather than just restating the standard's definition.
