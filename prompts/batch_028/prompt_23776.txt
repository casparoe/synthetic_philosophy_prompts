Question: Explain Harsanyi's utilitarian theorem. State the assumptions it relies on and what it concludes, and situate it relative to the veil-of-ignorance / impartial-observer argument.

Student's answer:
"Harsanyi's utilitarian theorem is a result in social choice theory that tries to pin down what the correct social welfare function should look like, given some rationality assumptions. The basic idea is that we have a group of individuals and we want to aggregate their preferences into a single social preference ordering.

The theorem has three main assumptions. First, each individual is a rational expected-utility maximizer, so each person has a well-defined von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function. Second, the group as a whole is also rational and maximizes expected social welfare. Third, the Pareto principle holds: if every individual in the group prefers one probability distribution over social states to another, then the group as a whole prefers it too.

Under these assumptions, Harsanyi proves that the social welfare function must be a weighted sum of the individuals' utility functions — that is, total social welfare is the sum, over each person, of that person's utility times a weight. And in fact the theorem shows that all of these weights have to be equal, so the conclusion is just plain utilitarianism: social welfare is the unweighted sum of everyone's utilities.

This is what connects the theorem to the veil of ignorance: behind the veil, an impartial observer doesn't know which person they'll turn out to be, so the only fair way to weigh the individuals is to weigh them all equally, which is exactly what the theorem delivers."
