I'm putting together a piece for a science magazine on whether we should just program an AI to want what we want, and I've gotten stuck on something that keeps bugging me. On one side there's Nick Bostrom's coherent extrapolated volition from Superintelligence, the idea that we should build a system that does what humanity would agree to want if it had much longer to think about it, but in a cohernt way. On the other side I keep thinking about Parfit's distinction in Reasons and Persons between what he calls true rationality and actual rationality, where the actually rational thing is what your current beliefs and preferences support, and the truly rational thing is what a fully informed, coherent version of you would endorse.

My question is: are these really the same idea, or does CEV do something more than just apply Parfit's distinction to a whole species? I'm especailly curious about where the analogy breaks down. For instance, what does "coherence" even mean when you're extrapolating a population of preferences that flatly contradict each other, and does CEV actually need the idealized human to be more rational, or just more informed? I also have a nagging worry that CEV quietly assumes there's a single consistent endpoint to the extrapolation, which is exactly the thing Parfit's framework doesn't require of the idealized self. Can you compare the two approaches in detail, spell out where the comparison holds up and where it doesn't, and then tell me which of the two you think is the more defensible way to talk about idealized preferences, and why?
