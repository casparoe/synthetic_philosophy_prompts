Next week's guest on my podcast is a philosopher who has written a book defending the view that the epistemic problem with conspiracy theories is structural rather than about their content: they posit a hidden causal structure — a coordinated group of actors acting to conceal — that is in principle hard to rule out, and that this is what generates a genuine epistemic asymmetry relative to non-conspiratorial explanations. I've been covering the anti-vax movement for a year, and this is the answer I actually want to test, because the reporting never lets me tell whether the problem is the reasoning or the believers. She deflects the obvious objection, "but Watergate and MKUltra were real, so you can't dismiss the genre," by saying the existence of some true conspiracy theories is precisely what makes the genre epistemically interesting rather than trivially dismissible. Write me eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual content of her structural account: at least one each on (a) whether her account is a "conspiracy theory" (a specific hypothesis) or a "theory of conspiracy" (a general epistemology); (b) the cover-up problem, i.e., how her framework handles the fact that conspiracy theories often claim the contrary evidence is being suppressed, which threatens to make them unfalsifiable; (c) whether there is a genuine epistemic asymmetry — a principled reason to think that, all else equal, a conspiracy explanation is worse than a non-conspiratorial one — or whether the apparent asymmetry is an artifact of how the evidence is structured; and (d) what her account predicts about the line between legitimate cases (Tuskegee) and illegitimate ones (anti-vax), and whether it can draw that line in principle rather than only in practice. For each, give a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and order the questions so that the deflection above gets progressively harder to use, ending with a question about what evidence could count against the structural account.
