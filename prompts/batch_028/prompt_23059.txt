I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and this term I've been teaching a small seminar on epistemology as a bit of a change of pace. One of my students turned in the response below to a homework question, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on it before I post grades. The question was: "In a short essay, compare three proposed norms of assertion — the truth norm (assert p only if p), the knowledge norm (assert p only if you know p), and the belief norm (assert p only if you believe p). State the standard objection to each, and say which norm you find most defensible and why."

The student wrote:

"Assertion is a speech act, and like other speech acts it is governed by a normative constraint on when it is appropriate to perform it. Three candidates are usually discussed.

The truth norm says that you may assert p only if p is true. Its standard objection is that it is too weak: it would permit asserting a proposition that is true but that you do not know, such as a lucky guess that happens to land on the truth. Because the norm ignores the assertor's epistemic position, it fails to capture what makes an assertion appropriate.

The knowledge norm says that you may assert p only if you know p. Its standard objection is that it is too strong: it forbids asserting propositions that are true and that you are fully justified in believing, even though in ordinary life we routinely make such assertions without strictly knowing what we say.

The belief norm says that you may assert p only if you believe p. Its standard objection is that it is too weak, for the same reason the truth norm is too weak: it permits asserting propositions that are true but that the assertor does not know.

On balance I find the truth norm the most defensible. The knowledge norm is unrealistically demanding, and the belief norm collapses into the truth norm once we notice that one cannot coherently assert what one does not believe. If we supplement the truth norm with a requirement that the assertor believe p, we get a norm that is simple and never too strong, and that avoids the knowledge norm's excessive demands."
