Question: Explain Hume's argument in Section X of the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding that no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle. In your answer, state the definition of a miracle on which the argument depends, identify the two kinds of evidence that Hume sets in opposition, and state the condition under which Hume holds that testimony would, after all, be sufficient.

Student's answer: Hume defines a miracle as a violation of the laws of nature. A law of nature is, on his account, a regularity grounded in uniform experience: an event of the relevant kind has simply never been observed, so our experience that the law holds is as complete as it can be. The only evidence that could favor an alleged miracle is the testimony of witnesses, and the credibility of that testimony itself rests on our experience that human testimony is usually reliable. We therefore have two bodies of evidence in opposition. On one side stands the uniform experience that the law of nature held; on the other, the testimony that it was violated in this case. Since we should proportion our belief to the strength of the evidence, and since our experience of the fallibility of testimony is never as strong as our experience that the law in question held, the testimony cannot outweigh the contrary experience. Hume's own rule is that no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a nature that its truth would be more miraculous than the fact which it endeavors to establish. Because the untruth of human witnesses is never more miraculous than any alleged miracle, no miracle can ever be rationally established on the strength of testimony. The upshot, then, is that Hume has shown miracles to be impossible: they cannot, as a matter of fact, occur.
