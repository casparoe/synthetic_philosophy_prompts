I've just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether deep neural networks genuinely have representations, and we've made essentially no progress. The dispute began from a modest observation — that interpretability work increasingly reports finding 'features' inside networks, things that look as though the network has isolated a concept, such as 'a cat' or 'a particular visual style' — but it quickly hardened into a standoff over what that observation is supposed to establish. My friend insists that isolating such a feature is a discovery of something real in the network, on a par with identifying a genuine property; I keep replying that the network's input–output behavior is invariant under a change of basis in its activation space, so the 'features' we report are an artifact of the particular coordinate system we happen to have chosen, and that the whole enterprise is closer to selecting a convenient description than to uncovering a fact of the matter.

I think our disagreement runs deeper than either of us fully articulated, and I suspect it turns on a few specific points that we kept circling. One is whether the superposition of features — the idea that networks pack more features than they have dimensions, so that features overlap and no clean separation is available — genuinely undermines the realist claim, or whether it still leaves a determinate set of real features lying underneath that we are merely failing to resolve. Another is whether the fact that different interpretability methods yield different, equally valid decompositions of the very same network is a reductio of realism, or just a symptom of a domain in which the 'right' level of description is underdetermined. And a third is whether the causal success of ablation studies — zeroing out a candidate feature and watching the behavior change accordingly — settles the ontological question, or merely shows that the feature is a useful causal lever without showing that it is a real constituent of the network.

Could you write a dialogue between two researchers — one a committed interpretability realist and the other a committed basis-invariance skeptic — working through exactly these points? I want both of them to be genuinely strong: the realist should not be a strawman who simply asserts that features are 'obviously' real, and the skeptic should not merely dismiss interpretability as meaningless or incoherent. I'd like the dialogue to contain real back-and-forth, in which each of them responds to the specific point the other has just made rather than talking past one another, and in which they at least partially converge on some point (perhaps agreeing that the question of which level of description is 'right' is harder than either initially thought) while still ending in genuine, unresolved disagreement about what the interpretability results ultimately establish.
