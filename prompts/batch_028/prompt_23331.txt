I retired from experimental physics about four years ago and recently fell down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly decision theory and the philosophy of probablity. Last month I was at a dinner with two friends -- one's a quant who used to work on algo trading, the other's a philosopher of science we met through a reading group -- and they got into it about the Kelly criterion, and I sat there going "oh no, here we go."

The quant started by saying Kelly betting is the rational strategy, full stop. It's the unique fraction of your bankroll that maximizes your long-run growth rate, and in the the limit any other fixed fraction gets dominated -- your wealth relative to the Kelly bettor's goes to zero almost surely. So if you're going to commit to one fraction, Kelly is the only defensible choice; anything less is just leaving money on the table forever. The philosopher pushed back and said that "dominance" is doing a lot of quiet work. Kelly's wealth beats yours in the limit, sure, but yours also goes to infinity. The only thing Kelly actually wins is the growth rate, and caring about the growth rate as such is exactly the same as assuming log utility. So "Kelly is rational" is really just "log utility is rational," which is a claim nobody's justified yet. The quant replied that log utility is the only utility function that doesn't depend on an arbitrary horizon. If you use a finite horizon, why 30 years instead of 40? The infinite horizon is the only non-arbitrary limit, and it picks log utility, hence Kelly, so the arbitrariness charge just moves the goalposts. The philosopher said the infinite horizon is its own arbitrary extreme -- it assumes you live forever, never need to cash out, and the market never closes. A 30-year horizon with a real probability of ruin is just as principled, and it picks fractional Kelly. And "leaving money on the table" is misleading: a half-Kelly bettor who ends up at a thousandth of Kelly's wealth is still fabulously rich, so the "loss" is only in the growth rate, not in any outcome you'd actually live through. The quant conceded that if you have a real chance of needing the money or dying, fractional Kelly makes sense, but said that's just a constraint, not a refutation -- strip away the practical constraints and the pure answer is still Kelly, and the practical deviations are just engineering. The philosopher ended by saying the idealized problem is the one you only get by assuming away everything that makes it a problem about a person -- infinite life, no liquidity, no ruin -- so the "pure answer" is the answer to a question no one is actually asking, and that "rational" is doing a lot of work the setup is quietly doing.

They went back and forth a bit more after that, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better argument. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about each specific point -- not just who wins overall? And if there's something both of them are missing, I'd really like to hear that too.
