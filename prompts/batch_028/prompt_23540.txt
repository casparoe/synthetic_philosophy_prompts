I'm in a course on the philosophy of economics and I keep getting stuck on this argument two of my classmates had a few weeks ago. I wasn't taking sides, but I never did figure out who was actually right, and I'd like a second opinon. One of them, let's call her Sam, said that "pure" time preference — the idea that, holding everything else constant, you rationally prefer a benefit now to the exact same benefit later — is just incoherent. Her argument was that if you discount the future at any steady positive rate, then no matter how big a future payoff is, there's a long enough wait such that you'd prefer a trivial payoff now to it. Like you'd rationally prefer a dollar today to a million dollars a hundred years from now. That's absurd, so the underlying preference has to be irrational.

The other one, Alex, pushed back and said that's not what discounting even is. Discounting the future just tracks the fact that the future is uncertain — you might not be alive, the money might not exist, whatever. So it's not a "pure" preference for the present, it's an "impure" preference that's just responding to real risk, and responding to real risk is rational, not irrational. Sam answered that fine, the risk part is rational, but that's not the thing she was objecting to. Even after you've factored in the probability that you'll actually be around to enjoy the payoff, there's still a leftover preference for the earlier payoff, holding survival and everything else fixed. And that leftover is what's irrational — if you're certain you'll be alive in a hundred years and certain you'll get the million, the discounting view still has you preferring the dollar today, which is just arbitrary.

Alex's last reply was that the "leftover" is doing all the work in her argument, and it's not actually arbitrary at all: it's just the fact that your current self cares more about your current welfare than about your future self's welfare, and you're not obligated to care about your future self as much as your present one. So it's not irrational, it's just a fact about what you value. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Can you walk through their exchange and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall?
