I'm working on a piece about the intersection of market design and epistemology, and at the conference I was covering, two of the attendees — a financial economist and a philosopher of economics — got into a dispute over how to read the no-trade theorem. I'm neither an economist nor a philosopher, and I couldn't resolve it myself, so I'd like your help adjudicating their exchange.

The financial economist said: "The no-trade theorem is a result about strategic interaction, not about epistemology. Milgrom and Stokey show that once the information structure is common knowledge, any bid or offer reveals the bidder's private information, and the counterparty anticipates that and adjusts before the trade goes through, so no one can profit. It's a revelation problem in market design — the same family as Myerson and Satterthwaite's result on bilateral trade. The epistemic content is trivial."

The philosopher of economics replied: "That reads the epistemology out of the result. The deep claim is that once the information structure is common knowledge, there is no residual private information left to trade on: what one agent can infer from the other's willingness to trade is already fixed by the common-knowledge structure, so the private signal adds nothing the counterparty hasn't already priced in. The theorem is about the structure of what is and isn't common knowledge, not about incentives per se."

The economist answered: "But the epistemic claim is doing the work in the opposite direction. The theorem shows that the only way a trade can be mutually beneficial is for the parties to lack common knowledge of the information structure — or for one of the other assumptions to fail. So empirically, any observed trade is evidence of some epistemic or strategic gap. That's why I read it as a theorem about markets, not about belief spaces."

The philosopher said: "Careful — that's a non-sequitur. The theorem is a statement about equilibrium, not about actual behavior. The fact that people actually trade doesn't falsify it; people trade because of noise, differing risk preferences, or a lack of common knowledge of the structure. None of that tells us how often which assumption fails in the real world, and the theorem is silent on that."

The economist conceded that, but added: "I still think the epistemic framing is misleading. The real content is that in a properly specified competitive equilibrium, prices are informationally efficient in the sense that no rational agent can systematically profit from private information. That's a theorem about market prices as a summary statistic of dispersed information, not about belief spaces."

The philosopher objected: "No — I think you're conflating two different results. Milgrom and Stokey show that under rational expectations and common knowledge of the information structure, no trade occurs at all; they do not show that prices summarize information. The claim that prices aggregate dispersed information is a separate, positive claim, and it's precisely what Grossman and Stiglitz's paradox undermines: if prices fully reflected private information, no one would pay the cost of acquiring it, and the price system would stop working. The no-trade theorem and the efficiency paradox are distinct results with distinct assumptions, and neither implies the other. And the assumption that the signal structure is common knowledge is a substantive epistemic assumption, not a notational choice — if agents don't share that common knowledge, the theorem's conclusion need not hold."

Please adjudicate this exchange turn by turn: for each of the six statements, say whether the speaker is right, wrong, or only partly right, and briefly explain why. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Evaluate the arguments on their merits; I'd rather not have you defer to either speaker's credentials.
