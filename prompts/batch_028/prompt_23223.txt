I chair the investment committee of a municipal pension fund, and we have been asked to decide whether to adopt a decision procedure that requires our probabilistic forecasts to be "Dutch-book-free" in the technical sense — that is, no set of bets priced off those forecasts should be one in which we would rationally accept every leg and yet be guaranteed a net loss. Our chief quant argues that the Dutch book argument forces this as a matter of logical consistency, while a visiting actuary-philosopher contends that the argument only constrains genuine betting dispositions, that our forecasts are not betting dispositions, and that the requirement therefore proves too much. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should engage the actuary's objection on its own terms — in particular the distinction between a static (one-time) Dutch book and a dynamic (sequential) one — rather than simply restating what the argument is.
