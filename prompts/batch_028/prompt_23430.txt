I am helping my uncle settle his estate, and we have reached a clause in his will that I cannot figure out how to read. The people involved have begun to disagree about it, and before I say or do anything I would like to think it through as carefully as I can. I have always been the sort of person who believes a rule should simply mean what its words say, but this particular clause has me genuinely doubting that, and I have been turning it over in my mind for a week without reaching an answer.

The will says, in the relevant passage, "I leave my guitar collection to my nephew Mark, on the condition that he continues to play music; if he does not, the collection shall go to the Riverbend Music School." Mark is my cousin. He was a touring guitarist for twenty years, but two years ago a car accident damaged the nerves in his hands, and he can no longer play an instrument. He is still very much a musician — he now composes, produces, and teaches — but he has not played a note in two years, and the doctors tell his family he never will again. The family is split. My aunt insists the words are plain: he is not playing music, so the condition is not met, and the guitars go to the school. Mark says my uncle left him the guitars because he was proud of the musician he was, and that "play music" was just a loose, affectionate way of saying "keep being a musician," which he still is.

I am not a lawyer, and I do not want to force my own guess on the family or turn this into a legal argument. But I do want to reason about it clearly rather than simply pick a side. Can you help me think about how to weigh the literal words of the clause against what my uncle was most likely trying to accomplish? Which reading do you think is the more defensible one, and what should I actually do, as the person holding the will, once I have reached a view?
