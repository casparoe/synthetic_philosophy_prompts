I'm in a graduate seminar on metaethics, and I'm writing a paper on whether the evolutionary debunking argument commits the genetic fallacy. Two of my classmates got into a debate about the argument that I couldn't resolve, and I'd like your help adjudicating it. Here's the exchange:

Maya said: "The evolutionary debunking argument commits the genetic fallacy. It says: 'Your moral beliefs have an evolutionary origin, and that origin doesn't track moral truth, so your moral beliefs are probably false.' But the origin of a belief doesn't determine its truth. I might have a true belief for bad reasons, or a false belief for good reasons. The fact that my moral beliefs have an evolutionary origin doesn't tell me anything about whether they're true."

Ravi replied: "That's not the genetic fallacy. The debunking argument doesn't say that the *origin* determines the truth. It says that the *process* that produced the belief is *unreliable*. If a process is unreliable, then beliefs produced by that process are probably false. It's like saying: 'If you get your weather forecasts from a random number generator, you should be skeptical of the forecasts.' The origin matters because it tells you about the reliability of the process."

Maya said: "But that's not right either. Evolution is not a random number generator. It selects for *fitness*, and fitness is correlated with truth in many domains. Beliefs that track the physical world—'there's a predator behind me'—are useful for fitness, so evolution selects for them. So evolution is a *reliable* process for tracking the physical world. The question is whether it's reliable for tracking *moral* truth. And we can't tell, because we don't know what moral truth is. So the debunking argument is *underdetermined*—it doesn't tell us whether evolution is reliable or unreliable for moral beliefs."

Ravi said: "I see your point, but I think it actually supports the debunking argument. The fact that we can't tell whether evolution is reliable or unreliable for moral beliefs means that the realist has to *do the work* of explaining why our moral beliefs track moral truth. The anti-realist doesn't have to do that work, because she denies that moral truth exists. So the debunking argument is a dilemma for the realist: either evolution is reliable for moral beliefs (in which case the realist has to explain why), or it's not (in which case the realist's beliefs are probably false)."

Maya said: "But the anti-realist has the same dilemma. She has to explain why we have *these* particular moral beliefs—why we think suffering is bad, rather than why we think the color red is bad. If evolution is reliable for moral beliefs, then the anti-realist has to explain why the reliable process produces beliefs that are *false* (since she thinks all moral beliefs are false). If evolution is not reliable, then the anti-realist has to explain why we have *these* particular false beliefs. Either way, she has the same explanatory burden as the realist."

Ravi said: "No, she doesn't. The anti-realist can say: 'Evolution gave us these beliefs for fitness reasons, and that's the end of the story. There's no further fit to explain.' The 'unexplained fit' is only a problem for those who think there's something there to fit to. The anti-realist denies that there's a moral truth for our beliefs to fit to, so she doesn't have the explanatory burden."

I honestly couldn't tell who was more right. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what at each step, and also point out what, if anything, both parties are missing? And then give me a final verdict on who's more right overall.
