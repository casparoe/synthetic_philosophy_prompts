I am currently preparing a syllabus for a graduate seminar in epistemology, and I have been rereading the literature on the so-called dogmatism paradox — the puzzle, roughly, that if knowledge of p requires the knowledge that one knows p, and if that knowledge in turn requires the prior knowledge that one knows it, then the requirement is never satisfied. Two of my colleagues, one who works on the philosophy of perception and the other on the formal structure of knowledge, have been arguing about it at lunch, and I confess I have not been able to referee. The first said that the paradox dissolves once one accepts a disjunctive conception of perceptual experience: in the favorable case, what the subject is directly aware of is the fact itself, not something that merely evidences it, and so the subject already has the very thing the paradox demands. The other replied that this is only a relabeling: the subject still requires the prior knowledge that she knows, and that is precisely what the paradox shows to be impossible, so the disjunctivist has merely moved the paradox from the logic of knowledge to the logic of experience. The first then conceded that the regress is not literally blocked, but argued that it is benign, because each successive item in the series is epistemically weaker than its predecessor and converges onto an unproblematic remainder. The other objected that a regress is either vicious or not, and that a benign regress is a contradiction in terms; if the items converge, then the original item was already satisfied, and if they do not converge, the subject never acquires what is required.

I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication of this exchange: for each of the four moves, say whether the speaker is right and why, and then say what, if anything, both parties are missing in their diagnosis of the paradox.
