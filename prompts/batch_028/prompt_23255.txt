I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been working on a piece about the therapy/enhancement distinction in bioethics, where I keep getting stuck on a particular version of the normal-function criterion: the view that therapy consists of restoring a function to its species-typical range, while enhancement consists of pushing a function beyond that range. I'm less interested in the standard objections to this criterion — that species-typicality is vague, that it's insensitive to individual variation, that it struggles with novel or absent functions — than in a quieter problem that I think is more fundamental: the baseline itself drifts. If a capacity, say memory or the ability to sustain attention for long stretches, is treated as an enhancement today, then as the relevant intervention becomes widespread, the species-typical range shifts to incorporate it, and the very same intervention ends up classified as therapy tomorrow. That makes the distinction not merely hard to draw but unstable over time, so that the classification of an intervention appears to depend on the date on which we ask the question. My editor is pressing me to take a side rather than end on a non-committal note, so I'd like you to write an essay that takes this concern seriously and reaches a defended verdict on it.

Weigh the baseline-drift problem against the best replies available to the normal-function criterion. Consider, for instance, the claim that what matters is not the species-typical range as it happens to be at any moment but the range at some fixed reference point, such as the capacities of pre-enhancement humans, which would anchor the line in place; or the claim that the drift is a feature rather than a bug, because the distinction was never meant to be a permanent moral boundary between two kinds of intervention but a practical instrument for triage, insurance, and regulation, and instruments are allowed to track the state of the world. Then conclude: does the baseline-shifting problem, on its own, show that the normal-function criterion cannot ground the therapy/enhancement distinction in the way its proponents want? If you conclude that the criterion can be salvaged, say precisely what it has to be modified into for the drift to cease being a threat, and check that the modification hasn't quietly abandoned the criterion's core idea of an objective, non-arbitrary line. If you conclude that it cannot be salvaged, identify which of the motivations behind the distinction — if any — survive the failure, and whether the surviving motivations point to a different criterion altogether.
