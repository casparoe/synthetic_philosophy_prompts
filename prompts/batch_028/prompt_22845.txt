I'm a retired neuroscientist — decision making was my whole career — and I only recently got into philosophy, so I may be using the terms wrong. I sit on my hospital's AI governance commitee, and we vote next month on a system that infers each patient's treatment preferences from behavior: past choices, how answers shift when the same option is rephrased, even passive cues like how long someone hovors over a choice. It's meant to personalize recommendations and to fill in advance directives when a patient can no longer speak for themselves. I can't shake three worries, and I'd like a short memo I can circulate before the vote that takes them seriously. (1) My own field shows that choices flip with framing, so is there a stable "preference" for the system to infer at all, or is it just fitting the noise of how the question was asked? (2) For a patient in chronic pain or early dementia, current behavior may reflect what they've adapted to rather than what the person they used to be would want — whose preference should count? (3) When a patient can still state what they want, do we trust the words or the behavior, and what do we do when the two disagree? Give us a clear recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with the reasoning, and be honest about the strongest case for the side you're not picking. Keep it followable by non-technical members.
