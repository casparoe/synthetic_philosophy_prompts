The original question was:

"Explain the discursive dilemma (doctrinal paradox) using the standard contract case. Then describe the List–Pettit impossibility result for judgment aggregation, and discuss how the premise-based and conclusion-based rules each 'escape' the result, including the trade-off between the two."

The student's answer:

"The discursive dilemma, also known as the doctrinal paradox, was introduced by Kornhauser and Sager in their work on jury decision-making. Consider a three-judge court deciding a breach of contract case. The defendant is liable (r) if and only if there was a contractual obligation not to perform some action (p) and the defendant performed that action (q). The judges vote as follows: Judge 1 accepts p, q, and r; Judge 2 accepts p and rejects q and r; Judge 3 rejects p and accepts q and r. By majority rule, the collective verdict accepts p and q but rejects r, which is inconsistent given that all judges accept the conditional r iff p and q. The List–Pettit theorem shows that no aggregation rule can satisfy unanimity, systematicity, anonymity, and consistency for all agendas. The theorem's proof is a generalization of the discursive dilemma to arbitrary sets of logically connected propositions. The escape routes are the premise-based rule, which votes on the premises and then derives the conclusion, and the conclusion-based rule, which votes on the conclusion directly. The premise-based rule satisfies the other three conditions but violates independence, while the conclusion-based rule satisfies independence but is vulnerable to collective inconsistency. So the trade-off is essentially a matter of which condition you're willing to drop, and the impossibility theorem shows that you can't have all of them at once."
