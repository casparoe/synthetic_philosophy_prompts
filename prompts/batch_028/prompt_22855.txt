I've just finished a rather long and, frankly, exasperating argument with a friend of mine — who works in the same area as I do — about how to structure the verification layer of a system we're both involved with. The system is simple in the way that matters here: a "generator" model drafts answers to a set of technical questions, and a separate "judge" model scores those answers for correctness, and we want the judge's verdicts to be as reliable as we can make them without putting a human reviewer in front of every single one.

The argument kept circling back to the same point, which is that the moment you ask "but what justifies the judge's own verdicts?" you seem to be right back where you started, and I've come to believe that the classical epistemological regress problem is precisely the right framework for thinking about what we are allowed to assume in a setup like this. I'd be grateful if you could lay out, with some care, the most important implications of the three standard responses to the regress — foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism — for how we should actually design this verification pipeline.

Concretely, I want to understand what each response implies about whether we need a small set of human-checked verdicts that we simply take on faith as the foundation of the whole enterprise, whether a web of mutually cross-checking judge models that is internally consistent but never anchored to anything external is defensible, and whether there is any coherent sense in which we ought to keep refining and re-justifying the judges' reasoning without ever reaching a terminus. I'm most interested in where the three responses genuinely diverge on the practical question of how much residual circularity or arbitrariness we are left holding, and what that means for how much confidence we are rationally entitled to place in the final verdicts we actually ship.
