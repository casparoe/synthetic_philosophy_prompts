I am a PhD student in epistemology, and I am preparing a seminar on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism. I was discussing the issue with a friend who works on formal epistemology, and he suggested that the problem disappears if we treat knowledge ascriptions as speech acts rather than as reports of cognitive states, but I am not sure that this is enough. Suppose a subject S has the same evidence, the same reliability, and the same risk of false belief in two situations, except that in one situation S has a strong practical reason to act on p, while in the other S does not. If practical stakes affect whether S knows p, then it seems that S may know p in the low-stakes situation but not know p in the high-stakes situation, even though S's epistemic position has not changed. Could you explain this conceptual issue in some detail? I want to understand whether pragmatic encroachment is best understood as making knowledge itself stake-sensitive, or as making only the appropriateness of ascribing knowledge stake-sensitive, and how this distinction bears on the problem of self-knowledge.

More specifically, I would like you to clarify why a change in the subject's practical situation might seem to undermine the subject's ability to know that S knows p, and what a defender of pragmatic encroachment might say in response without making knowledge depend on irrelevant features of the speaker. I am especially interested in the difference between a view on which the truth of the ascription that S knows p varies with the stakes relevant to the speaker, a view on which the truth of the ascription varies with the stakes relevant to the subject, and a view on which the subject's cognitive achievement itself varies with stakes. Please keep the explanation at the level of the conceptual issue rather than the history of the debate, and use a simple case if that helps.
