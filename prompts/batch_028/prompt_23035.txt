I'm a first-year teacher in a public high school, and because my department just asked me to help redesign how we introduce a new unit, I've been reading Whitehead's The Aims of Education (1929) more carefully than I used to. Two things keep coming back to me: his claim that a student's engagement with a subject runs through a rhythm of romance, then precision, then generalization, and his warning that the deepest failure of schooling is to produce inert ideas — knowledge that has been received but never used, tested, or joined to anything else — in the service of an aim he frames not as skill acquisition but as what he calls the art of life. The trouble is that I've been talking with a colleague who thinks that whole scheme is a description of how a lucky, motivated student happens to feel rather than a prescription for how to build a unit, and that if I hand a group of sixteen-year-olds over to romance with no criteria, I'm just giving them a dignified way to avoid the hard work. I can't quite tell whether we're disagreeing about a fact or about a value, so I'd like you to write a dialogue between a Whiteheadian teacher who defends organizing the unit around the three-stage rhythm and guarding against inert ideas, and a pragmatic colleague who thinks the rhythm is underdetermined and that what students actually need is durable, transferable skill and the discipline to push through routine.

Make both of them genuinely good at their case — neither should be a strawman — and have them respond to each other's actual points rather than talk past each other: for instance, have the Whiteheadian meet the charge that romance is just unfocused play, and have the pragmatist meet the charge that skills-first teaching is precisely how inert ideas get produced. It would help if the dialogue ended without a tidy resolution, because I honestly don't know who's right.
