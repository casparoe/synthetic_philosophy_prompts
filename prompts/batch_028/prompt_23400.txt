(b) Explain how epistemic contextualism, and in particular stakes contextualism, is supposed to explain the bank case. (c) Explain one response an invariantist might give, using a principle like safety. (10 marks)"

The students answer:

"(a) The pragmatic encroachment thesis is the view that whether a subject knows a proposition is not determined solely by the epistemic facts — the evidence, the truth, the justification — but also by practical considerations, like what is at stake for the subject if they are wrong. So a person with the same evidence can know in a low-stakes context and not know in a high-stakes context.

(b) Epistemic contextualism is the view that the truth conditions of a knowledge attribution 'S knows that p' vary with the context of the person making the attribution. Stakes contextualism is a specific version of this: the standard of evidence that S must meet to know that p is raised or lowered by the practical stakes in the attributor's context. DeRose's bank case is the standard motivation. In the low-stakes version, Hank and Sally are just chatting, and it's right to say that Hank knows the bank is open on Saturday, because he's been there many times and it's usually open. In the high-stakes version, Hank has to deposit a check on Saturday, and if the bank is closed he can't pay the rent, and now it seems right to say that he doesn't know. Since the stakes are higher for Hank, the standard goes up, and so he no longer knows. This shows that the bank case supports stakes contextualism, and that it's a problem for ordinary contextualism (DeRose, Lewis), because on ordinary contextualism the standard is set by which alternatives are salient in the conversation, not by the stakes.

(c) An invariantist can respond by appealing to a principle like safety: S knows that p only if, in nearby possible worlds where S believes p on the same basis, p is true. Hank's evidence and the safety of his belief haven't changed between the two cases, so whether he knows hasn't changed either. The invariantist says the shift in our intuitions is a pragmatic phenomenon about what is appropriate to say, not a shift in the truth of the knowledge attribution."
