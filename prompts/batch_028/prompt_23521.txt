I was discussing this with a friend of mine over dinner last week, and we ended up going in circles, so I have been working through the puzzle on my own since, and I would be grateful for a clear explanation. I have no formal background in philosophy, but the question has been nagging at me: can our mental states — our desires, beliefs, and intentions — genuinely cause anything to happen? The argument that keeps coming back to me runs roughly like this: the physical world is causally closed, in the sense that every physical event that occurs already has a complete physical cause. So when I raise my arm because I want to stretch, the raising of my arm is fully accounted for by the physical state of my body and brain. If that is right, my want seems to be doing nothing; it is merely along for the ride.

What I cannot quite pin down is a distinction that I suspect matters enormously. Does this argument show that my mental states cause nothing at all, or does it show something weaker, namely that they do cause things but are redundant — that is, they are genuinely there and seem to make a difference, yet the effect would have happened anyway because of the physical cause? And if the argument establishes only this weaker conclusion, that the mental is redundant rather than nonexistent, why should that be a threat to someone who believes mental states are genuinely real? I would like you to explain, as intuitively as you can, what the argument actually establishes, where the line falls between the mental being excluded from causation and the mental being merely redundant, and why drawing that line is so consequential.
