I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I'm serving as a TA for an advanced cosmology course. I need to grade a student's answer to a ten-point exam question, and I'd like a careful, point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression.

The exam question was:

"Explain the 'Boltzmann brain' objection as it is applied to a cosmological model in which the universe is approximately de Sitter space with a positive cosmological constant. Then assess whether the objection is a decisive refutation of such models, or whether there are responses that defuse it."

The student's answer is as follows:

In a de Sitter universe, the vacuum energy gives the space a finite horizon entropy, and over a sufficiently long time thermal fluctuations will produce self-aware observers that simply fluctuate into existence out of the thermal state. These are the "Boltzmann brains." They come into being with memories and an apparent environment that are consistent with a low-entropy past, even though no such past actually occurred for them. Ordinary observers, by contrast, are the product of a genuinely low-entropy initial condition, namely the early universe.

The objection is a typicality claim. Because the de Sitter phase lasts for an effectively infinite time, the total number of Boltzmann brains that fluctuate into existence is enormously larger than the number of ordinary observers produced by the early-universe process. So if we pick an observer at random from all observers in the history of the universe, the probability that the observer is a Boltzmann brain is overwhelming. But a Boltzmann brain, by construction, has the same first-person experiences as an ordinary observer would have, including the memory of a smooth, low-entropy early universe. So the theory predicts that we should be Boltzmann brains, whereas on the theory's own account we are a rare ordinary observer. That is the tension: the model makes our being an ordinary observer an atypical case, which undermines the model's ability to predict what we should observe.

Boltzmann himself raised exactly this objection in his work on the recurrence of equilibrium states, so the problem is not new.

A response that defuses the objection is the following. A genuine Boltzmann brain is a random fluctuation, so its memories are not in fact coherent records of a real past; they are just noise that happens to look like a memory. Since we do have coherent, mutually consistent memories, we can rule out being Boltzmann brains, and the typicality argument loses its force. On this view the objection is not decisive.
