I'm taking a graduate seminar on infinite ethics this term, and I keep getting stuck on what I think is the core technical difficulty for aggregative consequentialism, namely the divergent series problem. If the universe is infinite in space or in time and contains both positive and negative value, then the total sum of value is a series of positive and negative terms that generally does not converge, and the Riemann rearrangement theorem tells us that a conditionally convergent series can be shuffled to sum to essentially any value we like. So the theory seems to either collapse into moral indifference (no action changes the total) or to hand us arbitrary, order-dependent verdicts that depend on which beings we happen to count first. I want to write an essay that takes this problem seriously and tries to resolve it rather than just describe it.

The essay should weigh the main strategies that have been proposed — temporal or spatial discounting, restricting the domain of moral concern to a finite or "relevant" region, abandoning total consequentialism in favor of a non-aggregative or local-comparison framework, and the various clever summation methods (Cesàro, hyperreal-valued sums, and the like) — and then reach and defend a clear conclusion about which strategy, if any, actually solves the problem rather than merely postponing it. In particular, I'd like the essay to say something substantive about the trade-off between keeping aggregation (which captures the intuition that the total amount of welfare genuinely matters) and keeping the assumption that the universe might be infinite, since I think most of the proposed fixes quietly give one of those two up without admitting it. Please don't end on a non-committal "it's complicated" note; pick a side and justify it, even if the side you pick has costs you have to own.
