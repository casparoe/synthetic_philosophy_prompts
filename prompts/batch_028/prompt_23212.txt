I'm TAing a grad seminar on formal epistemology this term and I'm in the midle of grading the problem set on Bayesian updating, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on one submisson before I lock in my grade. The question was:

"Explain Jeffrey conditionalization. (a) State the probability kinematics formula. (b) Apply it: you're looking at a glass across the room and you're sure it contains either red wine or green juice, so your evidence partition is {red, green}, with prior P(red) = P(green) = 0.5. You also know P(it's a gift | red) = 0.9 and P(it's a gift | green) = 0.1. You look closely and it looks reddish, so you now assign P'(red) = 0.9 and P'(green) = 0.1. What is your new credence that the glass is a gift? Show your work."

Here's the student's answer:

"Jeffrey conditionalization is how you update when the evidence doesn't pin down a single proposition but instead shifts your credences across a partition. The formula (probability kinematics) is P'(H) = Σ_i P'(E_i) P(H | E_i), where the E_i are the elements of the partition, P'(E_i) are your new credences in them, and P(H | E_i) are your old conditional credences.

For the example, let H be 'the glass is a gift.' The partition is {red, green}. Your old conditionals are P(gift | red) = 0.9 and P(gift | green) = 0.1, and your new credences are P'(red) = 0.9 and P'(green) = 0.1. Plugging in: P'(gift) = P'(red)·P(gift | red) + P'(green)·P(gift | green) = 0.9·0.9 + 0.1·0.1 = 0.81 + 0.01 = 0.82.

The key point is that this is really just ordinary Bayesian conditioning in disguise. Since the evidence is 'the glass is red or green,' you can simply condition on that disjunction, and the weighted average is just Bayes' theorem applied to the disjunction. So Jeffrey's rule is only a special case of Bayes' rule, and it's notably more responsive to the evidence than plain conditioning, which is why it's the right tool for uncertain evidence."
