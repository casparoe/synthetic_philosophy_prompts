I've been going down a rabbit hole about the dream argument lately, mostly from a popular philosophy book and a couple of long-form essays, and I keep running into this thing called bootstrapping and I just cant get a handle on it. The basic idea as I understand it: you can't prove you're not dreaming, because the only reason your current experience is evidence that you're not dreaming is the assumption that you're not already dreaming. So the argument is circular, you're trying to pull yourself up by your own bootstraps. Fine, I can see that. But then I read about this barn example where apparently the exact same move works: you're driving through the countryside, you see a red barn shaped thing, and you just know it's a barn. Except the only reason your eyes are trustworthy here is that you're in a region where barn shaped things are usually real barns, which you didn't independently check—you just looked at the thing. So you're relying on a background fact you never verified to get your knowledge, which sounds exactly like bootstrapping, but nobody calls it circular. I think I've seen the label "easy knowledge" used for this, meaning knowledge you get by a process that kind of presupposes the thing you're trying to know, but that doesn't help me see the difference.

So what's the principled difference between these two cases? Is there a real line between "evidence whose reliability depends on an unverified background fact" that's acceptable (the barn) and one that's not (the dream), or are we just being inconsistent because the dream case feels scarier? And if it turns out you have to pick—either you actually do know you're not dreaming after all, or you don't actually know it's a barn—which way does it usually land, and why? I'd like a gut-level explanation, not a formal one, and not a who-said-what survey. Just the shape of the argument and where it breaks.
