I work on the experimentation platform at my company — the service that runs A/B tests for product launches and decides when a test has "enough data." Last week two colleagues of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into a long argument over whether the platform's default should be to collect the full pre-registered sample before any launch decision, or to allow early stopping. The exchange went roughly as follows, and I'd like a referee. I have no formal background in decision theory or philosophy; I know the applied statistics (peeking, sequential testing) but not the underlying theory, so I honestly couldn't tell where the argument went wrong.

(1) Dana started from a result in decision theory, Good's theorem on the value of information, which she stated as: the expected value of information is never negative. Our test data is cheap to collect — we're just logging events that are already flowing — so the platform should never stop a test early: always collect the full pre-registered sample before the launch decision, since stopping early throws away information that, by the theorem, has non-negative value. (2) Marcus replied that she was conflating ex ante and ex post: the theorem says the expected value of a signal, evaluated before you receive it, is non-negative, but a particular realization can be bad news that lowers your expected utility — learning that the variant is terrible is a negative-value realization — so "information is never harmful" is a category error, and the theorem is an expectation statement, not a pointwise one. (3) Dana conceded the distinction but said it doesn't change the policy: if the ex ante value is non-negative and collection is free, you collect and then ignore it if it's useless — which is exactly what the theorem's proof relies on — and the bad-news realizations are the point, because without that data you'd have shipped the bad variant. (4) Marcus said she was smuggling in "free": each extra day of the test exposes more users to the losing variant, and delaying the launch decision has an opportunity cost, so the theorem's non-negativity, which is about the value of the information itself, says nothing about the value of the information minus its cost; and the deeper point is that the value of additional data depends on the current posterior — once the data has already pushed the posterior past the decision threshold, the next batch of data has essentially zero value, because it won't change the action, so "always collect" means paying a positive cost for zero-value information. (5) Dana answered that early stopping has its own costs: peeking at the data repeatedly and stopping when it looks significant inflates the false-positive rate, and a sequential test that allows early stopping has different error properties from a fixed-horizon test, so you'd have to re-derive the error budget; the theorem-clean policy is to fix the sample size in advance and wait. (6) Marcus closed by saying that's a statistical-validity point, not a value-of-information point, and it's solvable with sequential methods — alpha spending, always-valid inference — without giving up early stopping; the theorem's non-negativity is an option-value argument that works precisely because the agent can ignore the information, and once the information is costly, the decision is a trade-off that the theorem doesn't resolve — you have to compare the expected value of each additional batch of data against its marginal cost, which depends on the current posterior.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall?
