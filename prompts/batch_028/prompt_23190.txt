I'm in a decision theory seminar right now and we've been going in circles on the two envelope paradox for like two weeks, and I keep geting the sense that the people in the room are using "the paradox" to mean different things without realizing it. I'd love a dialogue between two students in the seminar — both genuinely sharp, neither a strawman — who disagree about whether the paradox is a real problem for expected utility theory or just a fallacy that disolves once you do the math carefully.

One of them, let's say Priya, is the "dissolver." She thinks the naive expected-value calculation (the one that says the expected value of the other envelope is 1.25 times the amount you see) is just wrong because it conflates two different random variables: the "n" in "the other envelope has 2n" is the smaller amount, while the "n" in "the other envelope has n/2" is the larger amount, so you can't plug the same observed n into both branches. She should bring in the Bayes calculation (the probability that you're holding the smaller envelope, given that you see n, depends on the prior over the smaller amount, not on a flat 1/2), the pre-opening symmetry point (before you open anything, the expected value of the other envelope is just equal to the expected value of yours, by linearity), and the improper-prior point (the 1.25 factor only comes out of a scale-invariant prior over (0,∞), which isn't a real probability distribution, so the expected value is undefined rather than 1.25n).

The other, let's say Marcus, is the "survivor." He grants all of Priya's math but thinks the paradox still shows something substantive. His moves: (1) the "just specify a prior" response is question-begging, because the actual player at the table has no information about how the host constructed the game, so the "dissolution" really just says "EU maximization has no verdict here," which is a genuine limit on expected utility theory, not a trivial slip; (2) the cycling argument — if you buy the naive "always switch" rule, then after switching you're in the same epistemic position and should switch back, and so on forever, so the rule is self-undermining; and (3) the bounded-support point — if the host's distribution over the smaller amount is bounded above by some M, then seeing more than M tells you you're holding the larger envelope, so the "always switch" recommendation is just wrong in that case. The dialogue should have real back-and-forth: when Priya lays out the Bayes calculation, Marcus should push on where the prior is supposed to come from for the actual player; when Marcus gives the cycling argument, Priya should say it's just a symptom of the same conflation error, not an independent problem, and Marcus should have to say whether that's adequate; and at some point they should try to articulate whether their disagreement is about the math, about the role of priors in decision theory, or about what counts as "the paradox" in the first place. They don't have to end up agreeing, but they should end up having pinned down exactly where they diverge.
