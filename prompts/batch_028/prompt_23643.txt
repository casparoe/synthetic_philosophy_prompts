I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been asked to give the toast at my sister's wedding next month. My sister is a graduate student in philosophy, and for the past year she has been preoccupied with the problem of transformative experience: the view that some experiences, such as becoming a parent, are such that no amount of prior testimony or imagination lets you know what they will be like, and that they may change who you are in ways the pre-experience self cannot anticipate. She and her partner are planning to have a child soon, and she is anxious about it, and at dinner she keeps reminding me that none of us can honestly tell her what it will be like. Please write me a two- or three-minute toast that addresses that anxiety head-on: it must not promise her how the experience will be, since she will rightly reject that as epistemically dishonest, and it must not retreat into dry epistemology, since a few philosophers from her program will be in the room and will notice any sloppiness, while the rest of the room is family who have never read a philosophy paper and need the speech to land as affection, not as a lecture. The thought that she cannot know what it will be like should do real structural work in the speech — it should shape what I can and cannot say — and the comfort it offers should come from somewhere that epistemic point itself does not rule out.
