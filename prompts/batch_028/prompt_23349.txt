I work on distributed systems, and I've been thinking about coordination among a very large number of autonomous agents. I'd like you to argue a specific position. Consider a one-shot coordination dilemma played simultaneously by N agents, where each agent's action is weakly correlated with every other agent's because they all share a common-cause prior over an unknown population disposition (loosely, a "cooperativeness" parameter). The evidential-cooperation argument says that an agent should cooperate, because its own cooperation is evidence — via that shared prior — that the other agents will cooperate, so the expected payoff of cooperating includes a term from the others' cooperation. Please argue that this evidential-cooperation argument cannot ground a rational basis for cooperation when N is very large, even if we grant the correlation. In particular, do the scaling work: as N grows, how does the evidential value of one's own action — the shift in the posterior over the disposition, and hence the expected payoff from the other N−1 agents — behave, and what does that imply about whether the cost of cooperating can ever be justified? Give a clean, self-contained argument rather than a survey of the decision-theoretic literature, and state which assumptions it depends on.
