Two of my colleagues argued for an hour over coffee about whether "pure time preference" is rational — that is, whether it's reasonable to prefer a pleasure now to an identical pleasure later, for no reason other than that the earlier one comes first.

Nora, the philosopher, said pure time preference is incoherent. If the two pleasures are genuinely identical and nothing about you changes, the only difference between them is their temporal location, and the bare fact that something is earlier can't be a reason to prefer it. Her illustration: suppose you're held captive for a year and then given a day of freedom. The captivity is bad, but the day of freedom isn't made worse by arriving a year late — what makes the situation bad is the captivity, not the delay. So if you find yourself discounting the future purely on the basis of delay, you're reacting to a difference that isn't there.

David, the economist, pushed back. "Pure time preference" is just what people actually want, he said, and calling it irrational is a trick of the question. When you really have to choose, you do prefer the present, and that's a genuine preference, no different in kind from preferring coffee to tea. Nora's captive case, he said, answers a different question — whether the delay is bad once you're already in it — not whether, from where you stand now, you're entitled to weight the present more heavily. And a theory that labels the actual preferences of every living person "irrational" has a problem.

Nora conceded that people discount for plenty of good reasons — you might die, your tastes might change, you might need the money now, the future is uncertain. But those reasons have something to do with the situation, not with time as such. Strip all of them away and you're left with "I like earlier better," and she didn't see how that makes the earlier pleasure better than the later one.

David granted that mortality and uncertainty are separate reasons, but said Nora was still assuming what she needed to prove. Why can't "earlier" be a real feature that some people value, the way some people value being close to home when they buy a house? And from the inside, he added, the present is not just one moment among others — it's where you are, and it's reasonable for "where you are" to matter.

They asked me to referee, and I couldn't. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
