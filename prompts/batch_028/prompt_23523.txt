In a seminar on imprecise probability I want to work through a concrete case of dilation, and I would like help both checking the mathematics and thinking through its normative significance.

Set up as follows. A coin has an unknown bias θ, and the modeler is completely agnostic about it, so the prior is the whole set of probability distributions over θ ∈ [0,1]. The event of interest is that the next toss lands heads. Because the modeler's prior is the entire set of priors, the prior probability of heads on the next toss is every value in [0,1]. Now suppose the modeler observes that the current toss lands heads and updates by the usual Bayesian rule within the set of priors.

First, please verify, with the relevant calculations, that the posterior probability of heads on the next toss is again every value in [0,1], so that the observation has not narrowed the modeler's credence at all: the posterior credence interval contains the prior one. This is the phenomenon called dilation.

Second, discuss whether dilation should be treated as a genuine defect of imprecise probability models — a sign that they are too permissive to be useful — or as a feature that precise probability is uniquely positioned to obscure, in that it forces a single number that pretends to more information than the agent actually has. Weigh both readings and defend a verdict rather than ending on a balanced, non-committal note.
