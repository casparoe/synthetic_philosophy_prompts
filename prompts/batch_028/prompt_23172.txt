I was reading about the St. Petersburg paradox the other day and I keep going in circles. I'm a retired physicist and I only recently got into philosophy, so I might be missing something obvious. The setup is the standard one: flip a fair coin until it comes up heads, and you win 2^n dollars where n is the number of flips. The expected value is infinite, which is obviously wrong — nobody would pay even a hundred bucks to play. The usual resolution I've seen is to say that the casino has finite resources, so the expected value is actually finite. But that just moves the problem: if the casino has, say, a billion dollars, the expected value is still around thirty dollars, and I still don't see why a rational person would pay thirty dollars to play. So is the "finite resources" resolution really a resolution, or is it just a way of hiding the paradox?

And what about the "utility" resolution — the idea that we should use a concave utility function instead of raw dollars? Does that actually fix the problem, or is it just ad hock? I have a feeling there's a deeper issue here that I'm missing, but I can't quite put my finger on it. I'd appreciate any help.
