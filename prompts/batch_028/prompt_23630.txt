I've been thinking for a while about whether it's coherent to say that a large language model has beliefs at all. The usual way to make sense of this is something called the intentional stance: you treat a system as though it has beliefs and desires, and you use those as your best tool for predicting what it will do next. But the more I look at how these models actually behave, the more I see a tension. On one side, the intentional stance is supposed to apply only to systems that are at least broadly rational, and language models are not — they contradict themselves, they confabulate, and they sycophantically agree with whatever the user seems to want. On the other side, the whole point of the intentional stance is that it is a useful predictive shortcut, and it is not obvious that a story about a model's "beliefs" predicts its outputs any better than the much simpler idea that it is just sampling from a statistical distribution over its training data. I would like an essay that takes this tension seriously: is the intentional stance genuinely the right framework for understanding language models, or should we abandon it in favor of a purely statistical account? Weigh the main considerations on both sides, and then reach a definite conclusion and defend it — do not end on a non-committal, both-sides note.
