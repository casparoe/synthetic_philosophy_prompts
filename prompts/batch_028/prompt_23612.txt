I've been going back and forth in my head about the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis, and I keep feeling like I'm conflating two different claims that people call by the same name. It has come up a few times in forum discussions about hypercomputation, and I never quite get a satisfying answer. On one reading, the thesis is a mathematical or conceptual claim: the notion of "effective calculability" or "algorithm" just is the notion of "computable by a Turing machine," and the fact that Turing machines, the lambda calculus, and recursive functions all capture the same class of functions is evidence that we've latched onto the right concept — something like an a priori truth, or at least something that doesn't depend on how the physical world happens to be. On another reading, the thesis is a claim about the physical world: any process that could actually be carried out by a physical system — a brain, a rock rolling downhill, a hypothetical analog computer — is simulable by a Turing machine. And that second claim sounds like it's empirical, in principle revisable, the way Newton's laws were.

Could you write a dialogue between two people who disagree about which of these is the right way to understand the thesis? One should defend the view that the thesis is primarily a conceptual or mathematical claim with a strong a priori flavor, and the other should defend the view that it's fundamentally a physical hypothesis that could be overturned by a new kind of physics. Both should be sharp and well-read, and the dialogue should work through the real points of tension: what would count as a genuine counterexample (say, a Malament-Hogarth spacetime or some other hypercomputation proposal), why quantum computing — for all its power — doesn't obviously settle the question either way, and whether the identity of the three formalisms (Turing machines, lambda calculus, recursion theory) is best understood as evidence for a conceptual fact or as a coincidence we should be wary of. I don't want either side to strawman the other; I want them to actually engage with each other's strongest arguments and try to locate where the disagreement really lies.
