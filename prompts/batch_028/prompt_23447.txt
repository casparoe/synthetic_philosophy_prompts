I retired from experimental physics a few years ago and have lately been reading my way into philosophy, and I confess that social choice theory has been the most rewarding of my detours. I have, somewhat to my own surprise, been asked to sit on the steering committee of the research institute where I worked, and the committee is about to vote on where to install a new shared spectrometer. There are five candidate sites, all lying along a single corridor of the campus, and each of the seven of us works at a different point along that corridor; to a good approximation, every member prefers the site nearest to their own office, and the further a site is from one's office, the less attractive it is. One colleague, who has clearly read more of this literature than I have, has argued that because there are more than two alternatives and no one of us is a dictator, the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows that whatever rule we adopt will be manipulable, and that we should therefore stop pretending otherwise and simply take a first-past-the-post majority over the five sites. I am not convinced that the theorem actually binds our situation, but I am not confident enough to say so plainly, and I would rather not be the one to get it wrong in front of the committee.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate to the committee before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete rule and a concrete course of action, and it should engage my colleague's appeal to the theorem seriously rather than simply dismissing it. In particular, I would like the opinion to say plainly whether, in a case like ours in which each member has a single most-preferred site and their preferences fall away on either side of it, the impossibility result still applies; if it does not, to explain why, and to identify whether there is a rule that is safe from strategic misreporting in exactly this kind of setting, and if so, say which one and why it is safe. I would also like an honest account of the strongest reason my colleague might still be right — for instance, if any of us has preferences that do not in fact fall away cleanly on either side of a peak, or if the rule we adopt throws away how strongly we feel about the sites — so that the recommendation does not look like it is simply hiding from the theorem.
