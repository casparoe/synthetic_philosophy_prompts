I'm a senior in high school working on an essay for my philosophy class, and a friend who thinks I'm overcomplicating a simple question has talked me into getting a second opinion, so I'd really appreciate a careful reading of the notes below before I turn in the draft. The question I'm tackling is whether large language models should be regarded as agents, as simulators of agents, or as something else, and I've become fairly convinced that the way the debate is usually set up is a false dilemma. As I understand it, the "simulator" position holds that an LLM's outputs are agent-like only because the model is, in effect, running a simulation of some person who would have said those things, whereas the "agent" position holds that the model itself is the agent and the simulation talk is just a misleading way of describing what is actually a genuine, if strange, mind. I think both sides are assuming that those two are the only options, and I want to argue for a third category, but I'm not confident my argument is coherent, so I'd like your comments on the accuracy of my characterizations and on whether the central idea is actually interesting or just a relabeling of one of the two standard views.

Here is the skeleton of what I'm planning. My first move is to press the simulator position into a dilemma: either it means that there is one specific person being simulated, in which case it is too strong, because the model's behavior is inconsistent and context-sensitive in ways that no single person would be, or it means only that the model produces agent-like outputs, in which case it is too weak, because that is trivially true of any sufficiently accurate behavioral model and does not explain why the outputs are so flexible. My second move is to grant the agent position a point but deny it the whole argument: I concede that agency might be episodic, so that a system need not have persistent goals or memory across contexts in order to count as an agent in each individual interaction, but I argue that this concession already changes the subject, because it makes "agent" a much thinner notion than the one the debate started with. My positive proposal is that an LLM is better described as a simulation of a distribution over agents rather than of a single agent, so that its behavior is the aggregate of many possible agents' behaviors weighted by how likely each is given the context, and the right question is therefore not "is it an agent?" but "how much agency is in the average of that distribution?" I also want to make a specific sub-argument that I'm least sure about, which is that the real evidence against the single-simulated-person version is not inconsistency, since real people are inconsistent too, but the fact that the model's apparent values and commitments shift with the framing of the prompt in ways that no single person's values would, and I'd be grateful if you could tell me whether that sub-argument actually works or whether it just restates the inconsistency point in fancier language.

Finally, I have two more things I'd like your help with. First, I'm worried that the functionalist objection, namely that if the behavior is indistinguishable from an agent's then it is an agent whatever the internal organization is, crushes my third category entirely, and I'd like a clear statement of whether you think it does and, if so, exactly where my argument gives way. Second, I'd like a comment on the overall shape of the essay: whether the "distribution over agents" idea is genuinely a new option or just the simulator position with extra steps, and whether you can think of a well-known version of this thought, under whatever name, that I should engage with so that the essay does not read as if I discovered something that has already been said. I'm aiming for something careful and a little original rather than grandiose, so please be direct about the weakest links.
