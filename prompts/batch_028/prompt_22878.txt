I am writing a novel in which a character, a former actuary, insists that after a coin toss and a partial memory loss, her rational credence in the coin having landed heads is not a single number but an interval. I want the scene to be technically defensible, but I don't want it to collapse into a generic monologue about subjective uncertainty. Could you explain, at a fairly fine-grained level, the conceptual difference between representing uncertainty with a single probability distribution and representing it with a set of probability distributions? In particular, I would like to understand why the lower and upper probabilities of an event are not usually obtained by simply taking the minimum and maximum of a single parameterized family of distributions, why conditioning on new evidence under such a representation is more subtle than applying ordinary Bayes's rule to the endpoints, and what exactly is being bounded in the natural extension of a set of probabilities.

Please avoid a full measure-theoretic treatment. I need the philosophical and intuitive structure that would let a character argue convincingly: what is the underlying object being represented, what kinds of constraints are being imposed, and why the correct update may be wider or narrower than a naive interval update would suggest?
