Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) Both frameworks partition the change in frequency of a social allele into a within-group component and a between-group component. Hamilton's rule, rB > C, gives the condition for the allele to increase in terms of relatedness, benefit, and cost, while the Price equation gives an exact decomposition of the change in a mean quantity into a covariance term (association/selection) and an expectation term (transmission bias). The equivalence result is that the two frameworks yield the same condition for the allele to increase in a population structured into groups, so they are, as some put it, two bookkeeping systems for the same underlying process.

(b) Those who think the debate is settled point out that since the two equations are mathematically equivalent, choosing a gene-centered or a group-centered level is just a matter of which bookkeeping is more convenient, so the question 'what is the unit of selection' has no factual content beyond modeling convenience. This is a strong point, and I agree with it, and moreover the Price equation can be applied at any level of organization, which shows that selection acts at all levels simultaneously. Those who resist say the equivalence is only formal: the two approaches attribute different causal processes to the same population dynamics. In particular, in the Price equation decomposition, groups themselves function as replicators whose mean fitness differs, so group selection is a real process in its own right, not just a shadow of kin selection.

(c) Even if formally equivalent, the frameworks can differ in which phenomena are easy to model: kin selection makes inclusive-fitness calculations natural for interactions among relatives, while the Price equation makes it easier to handle interactions among non-relatives and structured populations. Also, the two can diverge in dynamic or spatially structured populations where the assumptions behind the equivalence break down."
