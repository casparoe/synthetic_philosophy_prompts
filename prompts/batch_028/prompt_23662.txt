I've been turning over a puzzle for a while now, and I'd like an essay that actually settles it for me rather than just laying out the options. There's a familiar example I keep coming back to: you have a bank account you haven't checked in a while. On an ordinary Friday when you just want to stop at the ATM, it feels perfectly true to say "I know I have enough in the account." But if you're about to write a big check and an empty account would be a disaster, it feels like you don't really know — even though nothing about your evidence has changed. I've seen this called the idea that practical stakes can affect what you know, or pragmatic encroachment.

There's a second idea I keep running into, usually called contextualism: whether it's true to say someone knows something depends on the context of the person making the claim, so a casual "I know the bank won't bounce my check" is true in everyday talk but false in a careful, high-standard discussion. Notice that contextualism locates the variation in the speaker's or listener's context, not in the knower's own stakes.

Here's the tension I can't resolve. In the bank case, it looks like the knower's stakes are doing the work: when the check is on the line, the standard for knowledge rises. But contextualism says the standard is set by the attributor's context. So consider a case where the person with the account has very high stakes, but the person talking about them is in a completely casual, low-stakes context. Encroachment seems to say they don't know; contextualism seems to say they do. I want an essay that weighs whether these two views can genuinely be held together, and if so, whose situation — the knower's or the attributor's — is really setting the standard. Don't end on a non-committal note; pick a side and defend it.
