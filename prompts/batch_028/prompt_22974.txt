I'm moderating the Q&A for next month's epistemology colloquium, and the guest is a philosopher who has recently defended the explanatory reply to the problem of old evidence — the view that a fact already known (say, mercury's being liquid at room temperature) can still confirm a hypothesis (say, the electronic structure of the mercury atom) insofar as the hypothesis explains the fact, even though the standard argument holds that the fact and the hypothesis are independent, since the fact was known before the hypothesis was ever formulated, and so it cannot raise the hypothesis's probability. Draft six to eight questions that press where the literature presses rather than having the guest restate the reply: at least one on why the idea that a hypothesis fits the evidence is not merely a restatement, in other words, of the idea that the evidence confirms the hypothesis; one on whether explanation and confirmation can come apart, with things explaining without confirming or confirming without explaining; and one on whether the fact confirms the hypothesis only when it explains the fact better than its rivals. For each, give a follow-up keyed to the answer they're most likely to give. Close with a question about what empirical fact could count against the view, and one that forces the guest to confront the rival that reframes the question diachronically, asking whether an agent who already knew the fact should credit the hypothesis more than an agent who did not.
