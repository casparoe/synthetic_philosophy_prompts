I have been preparing a seminar for the spring term on the epistemology of collective decision-making, and I have been presenting the Condorcet jury theorem to my students as one of the cleanest mathematical justifications for the epistemic reliability of majority rule. As I understand the theorem, if each of n voters independently answers a binary question correctly with probability p, where p is greater than one-half, then the probability that the majority verdict is correct increases with n and converges to one as the electorate grows without bound. On this basis, I have been arguing to my students that the theorem provides a genuine epistemic — rather than merely procedural — vindication of majority rule, and indeed that it establishes, in a precise mathematical sense, the popular "wisdom of crowds" claim that large assemblies are more likely to get the facts right than smaller ones or than any individual member.

I say this with some confidence, but I have recently been nagged by a doubt that I have not been able to fully articulate, and I would value your help in resolving it. Specifically, I am uncertain whether the theorem's conclusion is as robust as I have been presenting it. In particular, I would like to understand: to what extent does the result actually depend on the independence assumption, and what specifically happens to the probability of a correct majority when voters' errors are correlated, as they presumably are in real electorates that share common media environments, social pressures, and cognitive biases? And more broadly, is there something about the structure of the theorem — perhaps the nature of the questions to which it applies, or the way it treats the voters' competence — that limits its epistemic force well below the level I have been suggesting in my lectures? I would prefer an answer that is careful about the exact assumptions and scope of the theorem rather than one that simply reiterates the standard slogan.
