Exam question:
Explain the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants (MEC). Critics argue that MEC is untenable because it seems to imply that a soldier in a manifestly unjust war (for example, a soldier in an invading army) is morally on par with a soldier defending against that invasion. Distinguish, if you can, the moral claim MEC is meant to make from the related legal doctrine of combatant immunity, and say whether the critics' objection succeeds.

Student's answer:
MEC is the view that combatants on both sides of a war are morally equal, in the sense that they have an equal right to use lethal force against one another and an equal obligation to follow the laws of war, regardless of whether their war is just or unjust. The rationale is that the justice of a war is a matter for the state or its political leadership, not for the individual soldier, who is generally not in a position to judge the war or to refuse to fight. So a soldier who fights in a war that turns out to be unjust is not personally responsible for that injustice, and is therefore not morally inferior to the soldier on the other side.

The critics' objection is that this seems counterintuitive in cases of manifest aggression, such as an invading army. It looks wrong to say that an invader's soldier and a defender's soldier are morally on par, since the invader is clearly doing something wrong.

I think the objection fails, or at least is misdirected, because it confuses two different things. MEC is a moral claim about the symmetry of the right to use force in combat, whereas combatant immunity is a legal doctrine in international humanitarian law that protects combatants from prosecution for lawful acts of war. These are related but distinct: a soldier can be legally immune from prosecution for killing in battle even if, on a moral view, the war was unjust. So the fact that the law treats combatants symmetrically does not by itself settle the moral question, and vice versa.

Therefore, the critics are really objecting to the legal doctrine of combatant immunity, not to the moral doctrine of MEC. Once we separate the two, MEC can be maintained on the ground that individual soldiers are not responsible for the justice of the war, and the counterintuitive intuition in cases of manifest aggression is a reaction to the legal doctrine, not to the moral one.
