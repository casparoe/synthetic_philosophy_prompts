I am preparing a graduate seminar in which I want to set aside the standard conflict narrative of the Galileo affair and instead compare two theses that, to my students, look like two sides of the same coin but are structurally different. The first is the thesis behind the 1277 Paris condemnation: the Bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, prohibited the teaching of 219 propositions, among them the eternity of the world and the denial of free will, on the ground that they restricted the absolute power of God and converted contingent features of the world into demonstrative necessities. The second is the position Galileo developed in his 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina and defended in the face of the 1616 censure, in which the proposition that the Sun is stationary at the center of the world was judged to be absurd in philosophy and formally heretical, and the Earth's motion at least erroneous in faith. Galileo's argument, roughly, is that Scripture and nature are both utterances of the same author and so cannot genuinely contradict one another; the book of nature speaks in the language of mathematics and geometry, while Scripture, which is concerned with salvation and not with how heaven goes, must therefore be interpreted in a way that does not conflict with what is demonstrated in natural philosophy.

What I would like from the seminar is a careful comparison of these two theses as strategies for managing the relation between theological authority and natural philosophy. In particular: first, the direction of the constraint differs, since the 1277 position asserts that theology limits what philosophy may claim to demonstrate, whereas Galileo asserts that demonstrated truths limit how Scripture may be interpreted in the natural-philosophical domain; please explain why each direction of the constraint is, for the opposing side, intolerable, and whether either can be defended on its own terms rather than by appeal to historical outcome. Second, does the 1277-style move, the insistence that God could have made the world otherwise and that the laws of nature are contingent rather than necessary, actually create a safe space for new natural philosophy, and what is its cost, if any, on the theological side? Third, and this is the question I find most interesting: is Galileo's position, in effect, a mirror image of the Averroist double-truth doctrine that 1277 condemned, the doctrine that a proposition can be demonstrably true in philosophy and false in faith? In other words, does Galileo quietly re-instate a division of authority between the domains of reason and of revelation, merely reversing which domain gets the final word, and if so, is that a fatal weakness in his position, or does his argument from the two books dissolve the division in a way the Averroists' did not?

I would like the comparison to be carried out along three axes, the conception of divine omnipotence, the epistemic status of demonstration, and the hermeneutic status of Scripture, and to conclude with a judgment about which of the two theses is more defensible, with reasons, rather than a summary of what happened next.
