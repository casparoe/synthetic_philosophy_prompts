"Define cognitive penetration of perception, and explain why the fact that expectations influence what we perceive is not by itself enough to settle the debate. Then evaluate whether colour constancy is genuine evidence for penetration, and say which side of the debate you find more defensible."

Here's what one student wrote:

Cognitive penetration of perception is the thesis that non-perceptual mental states -- beliefs, expectations, emotions, inferences -- can affect the content or the phenomenal character of perceptual experience, rather than merely affecting what we later judge or infer about what we see. I think it's worth separating this from a much weaker and uncontroversial claim, which is that expectations influence perception in the trivial sense: if you expect a shape to be a cup, you'll be faster to notice it and more likely to report it. Everyone agrees about that. The real debate is about the stronger claim, namely whether these higher-level states can change what it is like to see something, or what the experience represents, not just what we conclude afterwards.

The best evidence for penetration, I think, comes from colour constancy. In colour constancy, we perceive an object's surface colour quite stably even when the illumination changes a lot: a white barn looks white whether it's in neutral daylight or in warm indoor light, rather than looking yellowish in the warm light. What's going on here is that our higher-level knowledge about the lighting conditions is 'correcting' the raw sensory input, so the experience we end up with is shaped by cognition. That's exactly what penetration is supposed to be.

I also think the impenetrability view is hard to defend. If perception were really insulated from cognition the way the impenetrability theorist claims, it would have to be a purely passive, bottom-up process with no top-down contribution at all. But we know from vision science that perception is full of top-down processing, so impenetrability can't be right. For these reasons I find the penetration view more defensible.
