I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm putting together a long-form piece on what emotions actually are. I've drafted a short essay for it, and I'd like you to stress-test the argument before I sign off on the framing, particularly on accuracy and on whether the central move holds. The working draft goes roughly like this: the question is usually posed as a trichotomy — are emotions judgments (the line associated with Robert Solomon and Martha Nussbaum, on which to feel fear just is to judge that something is a threat to something you care about), perceptions (the perceptual-theory line, on which emotions are a kind of evaluative perception of the world), or feelings (the William James line, on which emotions are perceptions of our own bodily changes, so we tremble because we are afraid rather than the reverse)? My draft argues that this trichotomy is a false question, and that the judgment and perception camps are actually closer than they look: the perceptual theorist's "evaluative perception" is hard to specify without smuggling a judgment in as its input, so the two positions converge on the claim that emotions are world-directed and content-bearing, and the only thing genuinely at stake is whether that content is best described as a judgment or a perception. The draft then claims the real live question is epistemic: if emotions are perceptions, they can be veridical or misleading in a way that gives them genuine, if fallible, authority as evidence about the world, whereas if they are just judgments, they inherit the epistemic status of the judgment and add nothing on top.

I'm least confident about two points, and those are where I'd most like your comments. First, whether my characterization of the judgment and perceptual positions is accurate, or whether I've flattened a real distinction — in particular, whether a perceptual theorist could plausibly insist that the perception is of the world itself, rather than of a prior judgment, and whether my convergence claim survives that objection. Second, whether the argument survives the standard dissociation cases — a person who correctly judges a roller coaster to be unsafe but feels no fear, or one who feels fear while insisting the ride is perfectly safe — since those look like they let the content and the emotion come apart in a way that both the judgment and the perception accounts have to explain, and I'm not sure my epistemic reframe actually resolves them. Could you tell me where the draft is most vulnerable, and whether the epistemic reframe is doing the work I think it is, or whether it quietly assumes its conclusion?
