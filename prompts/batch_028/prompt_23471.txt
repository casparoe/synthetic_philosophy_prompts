I am a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on conditionals and probability, and I am grading the midterm this week. I would appreciate a careful, point-by-point assessment of the following answer, including a letter grade (the question is worth 10 points: 6 for the proof, 4 for the final statement) and specific comments explaining where credit is earned and where it is lost.

Exam question:
"Let P be a regular probability function over a Boolean algebra of propositions, and let P(A→B) denote P's value for the indicative conditional 'if A, then B'. Assume the Ramsey equation: for all A, B with P(A) > 0, P(A→B) = P(B | A).

(a) Prove that, for any A and B, P(A→B) = P(B) ∧ A ∨ P(B | ¬A) ∧ ¬A.
(b) State precisely what this result does, and does not, show about the Ramsey equation."

Student's answer:
"Let P be regular and suppose the Ramsey equation holds. Since A and ¬A partition the space, the law of total probability gives P(A→B) = P(A→B | A) ∧ A + P(A→B | ¬A) ∧ ¬A.

For the first term: by the Ramsey equation, P(A→B | A) = P(B | A ∧ (A→B)); because the conditional A→B is true in every outcome where A is, and P is regular, this equals 1, so P(A→B | A) = P(B | A) = P(B).

For the second term: on ¬A the conditional A→B is vacuously true, so P(A→B | ¬A) = 1. The argument is symmetric to the first case, and hence P(A→B | ¬A) = P(B | ¬A).

Substituting both terms yields P(A→B) = P(B) ∧ A + P(B | ¬A) ∧ ¬A, as required.

This result, Lewis's triviality theorem, shows that the Ramsey equation is impossible: no probability function can assign the Ramsey probability to any conditional, so the equation must be rejected outright."
