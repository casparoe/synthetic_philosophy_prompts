I have spent the last forty years as an experimental physicist, and only in retirement have I begun to read philosophy seriously; I am currently working through Hobbes's views on geometry, which I had never suspected he held any views on at all. What strikes me is how far his position was from the received image of the geometry of his age. He insisted that geometry is a science of the consequences of definitions, that it should be reduced to a small number of definitions and postulates, and that the certainty of mathematics comes not from any intuitive grasp of figures but from calculation — indeed, he held that knowledge, in its most certain form, is a species of reckoning. This program, of course, was attacked by John Wallis in the mid-1650s, and the quarrel between them turned on questions such as whether the quadrature of the circle had really been solved, and on whether Euclid's Elements could be reconstructed on Hobbes's own foundations. I was discussing all this with friends last week, and the conversation came around to a question I have no idea how to answer.

The question is this: how would Hobbes have reacted to the computational turn in mathematics — the mechanization of proof, the formalization of Euclid in type-theoretic proof assistants such as Lean, and the machine verification of long human proofs like the Feit–Thompson odd-order theorem? On the one hand, one might expect him to be delighted: if mathematical certainty is computation, then a proof checked by machine is a proof in the strongest sense, and his own dispute with Wallis over the quadrature of the circle would have been settled by having both men encode their constructions in a formal system. On the other hand, he was also a theorist of human knowledge and language, and he might have objected that a proof no human being can read is not knowledge at all, or that the formalism has smuggled in precisely the kind of empty, signifying-nothing terms he condemned in the scholastics.

I would like a considered reconstruction of his probable position rather than a list of generalities. Please engage with specific elements of his views — his insistence on definitions and construction, his hostility to terms that signify nothing, his reading of Euclid, and the particular issues in the Wallis controversy — and say which of the two reactions above is better supported by the text, together with the strongest argument for the other side. I would also be grateful for your thoughts on whether he would have regarded the formalization of Euclid as a vindication of his reconstruction project or as a betrayal of it.
