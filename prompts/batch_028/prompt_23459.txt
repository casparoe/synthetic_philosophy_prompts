I'm a novelist who has, over the past year, been reading enough philosophy of language to get drawn into arguments I can't fully referee, and this one has been lodged in my head for weeks. At a dinner a few weeks back, two of my friends — one a working logician and the other a philosopher of science — got into a long, civil, and entirely unresolvable exchange over whether the identity "water is H2O" is a necessary truth or a contingent one, and I left the table not knowing which of them I should have been siding with.

The logician began from Kripke's standard story: "water" is a rigid designator, fixed by an initial act of ostension and then carried along a causal-historical chain, so the sentence "water is H2O," once we've learned it through chemistry, is necessarily true — a genuine necessary a posteriori, with no mystery about it. The philosopher of science pushed back: a proper name is fixed by a baptism and then dragged along a chain, but "water" was never baptized; we latched onto water by pointing at lakes and rain and saying "that's water," and pointing is underdetermined, since it could fix the reference to "the clear, thirst-quenching liquid," or to "H2O," or to "the wet stuff" — so something descriptive has to be doing the disambiguating, and a term whose reference is fixed by a description is not rigid. The logician answered that the underdetermination is resolved by the community: the experts, the environment, the causal chain running through the science — the point Putnam pressed in the Twin Earth discussion — pin the term down, and once it is pinned to the substance, the substance is essentialized by its microstructure, so the term rigidly tracks H2O and nothing else. The philosopher of science then replied that this just kicks the can down the road, because the experts fix the reference by pointing to the microstructure, which is itself a contingent, a posteriori fact; so in a world where the clear liquid in the lakes is XYZ, "water" would refer to XYZ, and "water is H2O" would be false there — hence contingent, not necessary. The logician's answer was the Twin Earth case itself: in such a world the liquid is not water, it is a different natural kind that merely fills the same functional niche, and that is exactly why the identity is necessary rather than contingent. To which the philosopher of science said she was being asked to stipulate the conclusion — "whatever is H2O is water, whatever is water is H2O" — and then to call the resulting necessity a discovery about the world, when it looks more like an artifact of how the reference was set up.

I'd like you to go through their exchange and say who is right at each step, not just who wins overall — and I'd be grateful if you'd also flag what, if anything, the two of them are both missing.
