Here's what one student wrote: "The common prior assumption says that two agents must start with the same probability measure over the set of states of the world. Aumann's theorem shows that if two agents share this common prior and it's common knowledge that they are both rational Bayesians, then they cannot 'agree to disagree' on any event — their posteriors must be the same. This is why the result is sometimes described as showing that agreeing to disagree is impossible under the right conditions. As an example, consider two agents who both believe that a coin is fair (prior 0.5 on heads), but one agent has seen the coin land heads 10 times in a row while the other hasn't. The first agent's posterior on heads is higher than the second agent's, so they have different priors on the proposition 'the next flip will be heads.' This doesn't violate the common prior assumption because both agents started with the same prior (0.5 on heads) and just updated differently based on different evidence."
