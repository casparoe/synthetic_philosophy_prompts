I'm a retired physicist who has only recently taken up philosophy, and I overheard a disagreement between two friends of mine — one who works in decision theory, the other in probability — that I could not follow to a resolution. They were discussing the standard money pump: an agent with intransitive preferences (preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A) who, starting with A, is offered a run of trades — first A for C, then C for B, then B for A — each at a small cost. Because the agent prefers each trade to holding on, it accepts all three, ends back at A but poorer, and the trader can run the cycle again and again.

The decision theorist said the pump only shows that the agent has intransitive preferences, not that they are irrational; the preferences may be perfectly defensible on their own terms, and a rational agent can simply recognize the pattern and decline to be pumped, which is a legitimate higher-order preference that overrides the first-order ones. The probabilist replied that "just decline" does not work, because the trader can restart the cycle from the beginning; the agent's intransitive preferences still make it prefer each fresh trade, so the only escape is to override one's own preferences — which means the intransitive preferences are not really the agent's governing preferences, and the incoherence is merely relocated to a higher order. The decision theorist answered that this higher-order preference is not a relocation but a genuine part of a sophisticated agent's preferences, and that the dynamic money pump is in any case a weaker argument against intransitive preferences than the static Dutch book is against incoherent credences, because in the dynamic case the agent has time to reflect and form higher-order preferences, whereas in the static case there is no such escape. The probabilist closed by saying that even granting the higher-order preference, the intransitive first-order preferences remain incoherent on their own terms, and the higher-order preference is only a patch that does not repair the original structure.

Could you adjudicate this move by move, saying who is right about each specific point and why, and noting what, if anything, both of them are missing?
