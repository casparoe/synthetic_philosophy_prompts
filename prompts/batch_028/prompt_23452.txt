I'm a novelist working on a courtroom novel in which the prosecution's case rests on a single DNA match, and I have invented two forensic statisticians — one a strict frequentist, the other a strict Bayesian — each of whom is asked to explain the evidence to the jury. The lab reports that the defendant's profile would be expected in one person in ten million, and the city in question has about ten million residents. Please write a dialogue between the two in which they argue over what the jury should take from that number. I want the frequentist to defend the likelihood ratio and to explain why he will not state a "probability of guilt," and the Bayesian to defend a posterior probability of guilt computed from an explicit prior, and to say where he gets that prior. They must genuinely engage the prosecutor's fallacy — the slide from "a one-in-ten-million chance of a random match" to "a one-in-ten-million chance he is innocent" — and the base-rate problem that the city is the size of the reference class. I need real back-and-forth: at least one exchange in which each concedes a point the other has made, and neither speaker should be a strawman.
