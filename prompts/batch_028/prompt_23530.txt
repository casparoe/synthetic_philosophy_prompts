I've spent most of my career as a condensed-matter physicist, and I only began reading philosophy in the last couple of years, so please forgive me if I frame things a little idiosyncratically. I've been following the interpretability literature on large language models with a mixture of excitement and suspicion, and a conversation I had recently with a former colleague who works in machine learning has left me more puzzled than when we started. He kept insisting that the "features" these researchers isolate are really there in the model, in the same sense that an energy level is really there in an atom. I found this both reassuring and completely unconvincing, because in my field we are very careful to separate the invariant content of a theory—the quantities that do not depend on our choice of coordinates, gauge, or basis—from the representational scaffolding we use to describe it. A choice of basis in a Hilbert space is, for us, a gauge freedom: the physics lives in the invariants, not in the particular vectors we happen to pick. When an interpretability paper reports that it has found a direction in activation space that "encodes" the property of being a capital city, I read that the way I'd read a claim about a particular, non-invariant coordinate axis, and I'm not sure the analogy to a genuine physical level is warranted.

So I'd like you to make the case, as rigorously as you can, against representational realism about neural networks—that is, argue that the statement "this network has a representation of X" is not a fact about the network in isolation, but a fact about the network together with an interpretive scheme that we bring to it. Please ground the argument in the non-uniqueness of feature decompositions: because any invertible linear re-basing of the activation space is equally admissible, and because the semantic labels we attach to the resulting directions are fixed by our observations of the model's input–output behavior rather than by anything the internal geometry itself singles out, the "features" we recover are, on this view, artifacts of our decoder rather than discoveries of intrinsic structure. I'd be especially grateful if you could also address the strongest counterexample I can think of, namely the empirical fact that causally editing a putative feature in a controlled way tends to shift the model's downstream behavior in exactly the direction the feature's label predicts, and say whether that fact is, in your telling, genuinely threatening to the anti-realist position or merely compatible with it.
