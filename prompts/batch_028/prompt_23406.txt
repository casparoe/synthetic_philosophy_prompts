I'm a student in a philosophy elective, and this semester we've been reading about the School of Names (the mingjia, sometimes called the Logicians) of ancient China. I've been thinking a lot about Gongsun Long (c. 320–250 BCE) and his famous "White Horse" essay, in which he argues that a white horse is not a horse. As I understand it, he has two main lines of reasoning. The first is about naming: "white" names color, and "horse" names form, and since color is not form, the compound "white horse" names something different from "horse." The second is about demanding: if someone says they want a horse, a yellow or black horse will suffice, but if they say they want a white horse, a yellow or black horse is rejected on account of its color; since what is demanded in the two cases is different, he concludes that a white horse is not a horse. I find the second argument clever, but I suspect it mostly exploits the fact that the word "want" is sensitive to context, so I've been trying to think of a way to test whether the argument has any real bite.

That's when I had the following thought, and I want to put it to Gongsun Long himself, as it were. Imagine that he were suddenly given access to a modern search engine, and that he were also shown how the engine works under the hood. He would notice two things. First, when you type "horse" into the engine, the results include horses of all colors, but when you type "white horse," the results are only white horses; the machine distinguishes the two demands in exactly the way his demanding argument describes, so it looks like a machine can tell the difference between "horse" and "white horse" in the way a person does. Second, and this is the part that worries me, in the database behind the engine a white horse is simply a horse that has the color property white; that is, the machine represents white horses as a subset of horses, which is precisely what his conclusion denies.

So my question is this: given these two facts, what would Gongsun Long say? Does the machine's differential response to the two queries vindicate his argument, or does the subset representation refute it? Or does he dissolve the tension by appealing to some distinction he already draws in the "White Horse" essay — for example, between a name as it is used in a demand and the thing itself, or between the role of "white" as naming color and the role of color as a property of a horse? I'd like a reconstruction of his view that stays faithful to the reasoning of the "White Horse" essay and doesn't just have him concede the point, and I'd also like a short assessment of which of the two modern facts is the more serious threat to his position.
