I am a philosopher of economics, and I am giving the opening keynote at a two-day workshop on the epistemology of markets. The room is roughly half economists, half epistemologists, and the talk should run about twenty minutes. Please write it as a speech, organized around the following argument: first, present the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem (1982) without measure-theoretic notation, and draw out the philosophical point that the absence of trade is itself evidence — that, under rational expectations, silence must be common knowledge that no agent's signal has disturbed the marginal conditions; second, turn to the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox (1980) and ask what it does to the idea that price can summarize the market's information, given that full informativeness would destroy the incentive to pay for information acquisition in the first place. Anticipate the economists' objection that the no-trade result is confined to a narrow class of models — risk-averse traders, complete markets, Pareto-optimal initial endowments — and handle it gracefully, showing why the epistemologists in the room should still care. It should land as an invitation to a shared research program, not as a survey.
