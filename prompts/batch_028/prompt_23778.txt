I retired some years ago from a long career in radio astronomy, where most of my working life was spent in the unglamorous business of choosing, among several competing explanations for a noisy signal, the one worth taking seriously. Only recently have I begun to read philosophy for its own sake, and I find myself circling a question that sits, I think, right at the boundary between decision theory and something rather more uncomfortable.

I know Pascal's Wager in its familiar form: because the stakes of belief in God are unbounded, one ought to wager on belief even when one's honest estimate of its probability is small. What has struck me is how closely the thought experiment known as Roko's basilisk — a proposal that circulated around 2010, in which a future superintelligent AI is said to punish anyone who failed to help bring it into existence, and who therefore ought to help — reproduces that wager in a darker key. Both tell you, in effect, that when the payoff is effectively infinite, the rational move is to align yourself with the entity that might be watching, whatever your private estimate of its existence.

There is, however, a detail I cannot shake. Pascal held that God deliberately conceals himself, and that this very concealment is to be read as a sign, a test addressed to the soul. The basilisk, by contrast, is a hidden agent whose concealment is pure menace, and who punishes the very failure to align. So I would like to ask you to take Pascal's place and answer as he would, in his own concepts: confronted with the basilisk, would he recognize it as a legitimate extension of his own wager, or would he reject it? And if he would reject it, which of his own resources — the hidden God, the primacy of the heart over mere calculation, his suspicion of geometric reasoning when it is pressed into the service of the soul — would he reach for, and why? I would be most grateful if you could be precise about the point at which his framework, turned upon itself, fails to carry the argument.
