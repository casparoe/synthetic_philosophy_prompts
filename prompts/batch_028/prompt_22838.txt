So I retired from field ecology about three years ago (thirty years of counting birds and wading in mud, of all things) and I've been trying to fill the void with philosophy, mostly epistemology. A while back I got stuck on something that happened with my friend Dave, who's a retired statistician, so we're both the kind of people who like to put probabilities on things and revise them when we get new info. Last summer we found an old coin in my garden and we got into a friendly bet about whether it's actually a 19th-century piece or a modern fake. We each did our own little investigation first — I weighed it and compared it to reference tables, Dave looked at the wear patterns under a maginifier — and then we sat down at a pub and tried to reconcile. I said I'd put the chance it's genuine at 0.7. He said 0.55. And then it got weird: he said, well, if you think it's 0.7, I'll move to 0.6, and I said, okay, given that, I'll say 0.65, and we kept going like that, each of us nudging our number a bit toward the other's, for like ten rounds, and we ended up stuck at 0.64 and 0.61. A stalemate. We both felt we were being perfectly honest and careful the whole time.

What bothered me was that we never actually converged, we just sort of asymptotically approached each other and stalled out. I have a vague memory of reading somewhere that there's some result about two rational people who share the same background assumptions not being able to keep disagreeing like this, but I can't remember the details. So my question is: is it even possible for two careful people to end up stuck like this, or does the stalling tell us something went wrong? If it's the latter, what? My first guess would be the priors, since we we clearly had different hunches about how many fakes float around in the antiques trade, but I'm not sure that fully explains why we stalled instead of just meeting in the middle somewhere.
