The exam question was as follows:

"Consider Max Black's two-sphere case: a universe containing exactly two perfectly homogeneous iron spheres, each one mile in diameter, each the same temperature and color, etc., with their centers a mile apart. (a) State the principle of the identity of indiscernibles (II) precisely, and explain why Black's case is intended as a counterexample to it. (b) A defender of II has two main replies to Black's case: (i) that the two spheres have distinct relational properties, and (ii) that the two spheres have distinct pure properties (their spatial positions). Evaluate these replies. In your answer, be careful to distinguish different kinds of relational properties, and to say what, if anything, the success of reply (ii) depends on."

Here is the student's answer:

"(a) II states that if two things have all their properties in common, they are identical — or equivalently, no two distinct things are exactly alike. Black's case is intended to show that II is false: a universe containing exactly two perfectly homogeneous iron spheres, each one mile in diameter, each the same temperature and color, etc., with their centers a mile apart, contains two distinct objects that share all their properties. So II, if true, is at best contingent, not a necessary truth.

(b) A defender of II might reply that the two spheres have distinct relational properties. But this reply fails: the only relation between the two spheres is distance, and distance is symmetric, so the two spheres share the property of being a mile from each other. Even allowing relational properties, the two spheres remain indiscernible. A better reply for the defender of II is to say that the two spheres have distinct pure properties — namely, occupying distinct spatial regions. This reply is the most promising, because it does not depend on relational properties, and it works regardless of one's view of space: even on a relational view of space, the two spheres occupy distinct regions, so they have distinct pure properties. Therefore, the pure-position reply saves II, and Black's case is not a genuine counterexample."
