I'm the TA for a graduate decision theory course and I'm grading a take-home problem set on the Allais paradox. I'd like a second opinion on a student's response before I finalize the grade. Here is the question we asked, followed by the student's answer.

Exam question:
Consider the two questions below. In each, choose the gamble you prefer.
Question 1: Gamble A — you receive $1 million for certain. Gamble B — $1 million with probability 0.89, $5 million with probability 0.10, and $0 with probability 0.01.
Question 2: Gamble A — $1 million with probability 0.11 and $0 with probability 0.89. Gamble B — $5 million with probability 0.10 and $0 with probability 0.90.
(a) State the independence axiom of expected utility theory.
(b) Prove that an expected utility maximizer cannot prefer A in Question 1 and B in Question 2.
(c) Explain why the pattern "A in Question 1, B in Question 2" is problematic for expected utility theory, and note which pattern experimental subjects actually tend to display.

Student's answer:
(a) The independence axiom says that if two lotteries share a common outcome, the choice between them should not depend on that common consequence; the common part cancels out of the comparison.

(b) Let u be the von Neumann–Morgenstern utility, measured in millions.
Question 1: EU(A) = u(1). EU(B) = 0.89u(1) + 0.10u(5) + 0.01u(0) = 0.89u(1) + 0.10u(5). Preferring A to B requires u(1) > 0.89u(1) + 0.10u(5), i.e. 0.11u(1) > 0.10u(5), i.e. u(1) > (10/11)u(5).
Question 2: EU(A) = 0.11u(1). EU(B) = 0.10u(5). Preferring B to A requires 0.10u(5) > 0.11u(1), i.e. u(1) < (10/11)u(5).
The two requirements, u(1) > (10/11)u(5) and u(1) < (10/11)u(5), cannot both hold, so no utility function makes the pattern A-then-B an expected utility maximization.

(c) The pattern is problematic because it violates the independence axiom: in each question the two gambles share an 89% common consequence ($1 million in Question 1, $0 in Question 2), and that common consequence should not affect the choice, yet it does. Experimentally, most subjects choose A in Question 1 (the sure million) and B in Question 2 (the riskier gamble with the higher prize), which is exactly the inconsistent pattern.
