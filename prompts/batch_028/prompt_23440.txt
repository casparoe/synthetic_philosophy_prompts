I retired from a career in applied mathematics a few years now, and in the intervening time I have found myself pulled, rather against my inclination, into political philosophy, much to the amusement of my former colleagues. I have been working my way through the literature on social choice theory, and I keep returning to the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, which I find at once elegant and, in a certain sense, underwhelming. As I understand the result, it establishes that no social choice function defined over the full domain of preference profiles can be simultaneously strategy-proof, non-imposed, and non-dictatorial, provided there are at least three alternatives. The other evening I was discussing this with a former student of mine who now works in operations research, and he made a remark that has not let me go: he suggested that the theorem's bite depends essentially on the assumption that the social choice function is defined over the entire space of possible preferences, and that once one restricts the domain — for instance to single-peaked preferences — non-dictatorial mechanisms that are strategy-proof, such as the median rule, become available.

I would like you to make a careful and sustained argument for the following position: the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem should be read not as a blanket prohibition on strategy-proof voting but as a domain-sensitivity result, and its practical import for the design of real electoral systems is considerably weaker than it is often represented to be. In making this case, I would be grateful if you could explain precisely what work the full-domain assumption is doing in the theorem, why a restriction of the domain (say, to single-peaked or otherwise structured preference spaces) can yield non-dictatorial mechanisms that are nonetheless strategy-proof, and what the upshot is for how we should think about electoral design in practice. I should confess that I am still not entirely comfortable with the precise sense in which the median rule is strategy-proof under single-peaked preferences, so please be exact about the conditions under which its strategy-proofness holds and about the circumstances in which it can fail.

Finally, I would like you to anticipate and answer the most natural objection to this reading, namely the charge that domain restriction is a kind of sleight of hand — that by stipulating a structured preference space one has simply assumed away the very phenomenon the theorem was meant to rule out. I am curious whether you think this objection is ultimately successful, and if so, how far the domain-sensitivity reading can be pushed before it loses its force.
