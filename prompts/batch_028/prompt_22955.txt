I'm a novelist, and I'm currently deep in the revision of a book about a protagonist who receives an infallible prophecy. My editor keeps telling me that the central prophecy scene reads as a mere plot device, and that I need to make it land as a genuine epistemic crisis; to work through this, I'd like you to write me a short story that dramatizes a specific, subtle point about the classic "Death in Damascus" paradox — not the surface drama of a man fleeing a pursuer, but the strangeness at the core of the puzzle. Concretely, I want the story to render the proposition "you will die in Damascus on Friday" as true in the actual course of events (the character does, in the end, die there) yet as something he cannot know in advance: the moment he could come to know it, his rational response — fleeing — would make it false, so he is structurally barred from ever holding it as knowledge, and can at best hold it as a belief he is compelled to doubt. I'd like this to come across not as a dry logical point but as a felt, almost uncanny experience, the horror of a truth that the subject is forbidden from knowing.

For the story itself, please aim for roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, and feel free to choose the period and setting you find most effective, though I'd prefer the prophecy be delivered by a single, specific figure — an oracle, a letter, a dying stranger — rather than by some vague, impersonal fate. I'd also like the ending to make it clear to the reader that the character does, in the end, die in Damascus, so that the proposition is genuinely true, while the body of the story dwells on the fact that he was unable to know this in advance; please avoid resolving the tension with a simple moral lesson about the character's hubris, since the point is the epistemic structure rather than a character flaw.
