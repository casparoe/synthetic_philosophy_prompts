I just had a rather heated discussion with a friend about Hannah Arendt's essay "Truth and Politics," and I'm still not sure I've got the argument straight. My friend kept emphasizing that Arendt's central claim is that political lies are fundamentally different from private lies because their purpose is not deception. In private life, she said, a lie is a tool you use to achieve some end — you lie to your mother about breaking her vase, you lie to a friend to get out of a dinner invitation. But in politics, the lie is not a tool for a specific end; it is the end itself. The goal is not to make people believe one false fact, but to make them inhabit a fabricated reality.

This is where I get confused, because my friend then went on to say that this means political lies are "not really lies at all" in the traditional sense. If a lie is defined by its purpose — to deceive — and the purpose of a political lie is not to deceive but to impose a world, then isn't it a category error to call it a lie? My friend seemed to think Arendt was arguing that political lies are more like a form of world-building or reality-creation, and that the word "lie" is actually misleading when applied to them. I pushed back and said that a false statement is a false statement regardless of the speaker's purpose, but I couldn't quite articulate why, and now I'm not sure I was right.

So here's my question: does Arendt actually argue that political lies are not lies, or is my friend misreading her? And if Arendt is not making that claim, what is the precise sense in which she thinks political lies differ from private lies, and why does that difference matter for her broader argument about the vulnerability of factual truth to political power?
