I've been re-reading Harsanyi's 1955 "Cardinal Welfare, Individualistic Ethics, and Interpersonal Comparisons of Utility" for a post I'm slowly drafting, and I keep going in circles on which of its assumptions is actually doing the ethical work. The theorem, in the form I care about: given a finite set of states, individuals with von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, and an "impersonal observer" whose preferences over (lotteries over) utility profiles satisfy a Pareto condition, the observer's social welfare function must be an affine weighted sum of the individual utilities, with weights that don't vary by state. Friends of mine keep telling me the Pareto condition is the only load-bearing assumption and everything else is bookkeeping, which feels wrong, because the observer's preferences over lotteries are also doing a lot of invisible work.

So: please rank the following assumptions by how load-bearing they are for the conclusion — i.e., if you were forced to drop or weaken exactly one, which drop would break the weighted-sum conclusion (or its ethical punch) the most, and so on. I'm not asking for a philosophical judgment about which assumptions are *desirable*, just how much the derivation and the conclusion actually depend on each one. For each, include one sentence on what specifically the theorem loses when that assumption goes.

Assumptions to rank:
1. The Pareto condition (if everyone's utility is at least as high and someone's is higher, the observer strictly prefers the profile)
2. Individuals' preferences admit a VNM (expected-utility) representation
3. The observer's preferences over lotteries over utility profiles admit a VNM representation
4. State-independence of the welfare weights (the same weights apply in every state)
5. The symmetry condition (permuting the individuals' utilities permutes the weights), used to get equal weights
