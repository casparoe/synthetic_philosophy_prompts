I've been reading about decision theory on a few philosophy blogs, and one puzzle has been stuck in my head for weeks—the one usually called the absent-minded driver. As I understand the setup, a driver has to turn at the first of two intersections that look exactly alike, and the problem is constructed so that the driver will have no reliable memory of having passed the first one, so that when they pull up to a fork there is, from the driver's own point of view, no way to tell whether they are at the first intersection or the second. What keeps pulling me back to it is not the driving but the fact that the plan which looks best when you sit down and think the whole trip through in advance can look like a mistake the moment you are actually sitting at the fork, and I have been trying to decide for myself whether that is a genuine paradox or just a case where the self that does the planning and the self that does the acting are being asked to do different jobs.

I would like to turn this into a short story rather than another essay, because I think the tension comes through better as something a character actually lives through than as a list of arguments. Could you write a short story, roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words, about a character who faces exactly this kind of situation: a run of decision points that are subjectively indistinguishable, a single rule they have to commit to before the run begins, and then a moment at one of the points where following the committed rule feels wrong even though they cannot quite say why? I would like it to stay faithful to the structure of the puzzle. The character should genuinely be unable to tell which instance of the decision they are in, not merely be careless or inattentive about it, and the ending should not resolve the tension by having them suddenly remember or by revealing a distinguishing feature they had overlooked. I care less about a tidy moral and more about the felt difference between deciding in advance and deciding in the moment coming through on the page.
