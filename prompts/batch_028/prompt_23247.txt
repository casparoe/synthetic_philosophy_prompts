I retired from experimental physics a few years ago and have, to the surprise of colleagues who knew me for decades, fallen rather deeply into philosophy of mind, reading Chalmers and the surrounding literature with the particular intensity of a person who has only lately realized how much his own discipline left unexplained. A former graduate student of mine, now running a small philosophy-of-mind seminar for undergraduates, has asked me to help grade a set of short essays, and I would be grateful for a careful second pair of eyes on one of them, since I am still far from confident that I can tell a genuine mistake from a mere difference of emphasis in this corner of the field. The assigned question and the student's answer are reproduced below. Could you give the answer a grade out of twenty, together with a point-by-point commentary identifying which claims are correct, which are mistaken or fail to follow, and why? I would in particular like your attention to the treatment of the conceivability distinction and of the explanatory gap, but please do not confine yourself to those; I would rather have a fair, itemized assessment than a general impression, and I have not formed a settled view of the work myself.

The question: (a) Reconstruct the zombie, or conceivability, argument against physicalism in its standard form, making explicit the role of the premise that links conceivability to metaphysical possibility. (b) Explain the distinction Chalmers draws between negative and positive conceivability, and state which of the two the zombie argument actually requires. (c) Explain why a physicalist might accept that there is an explanatory gap, in the sense Joseph Levine introduced, while still denying that this gap entails the falsity of physicalism.

The student's answer: The zombie argument can be put in four steps. First, it is conceivable that there is a being who is physically and behaviorally identical to a conscious human being but who lacks phenomenal consciousness altogether; such a being is a zombie. Second, conceivability is a guide to metaphysical possibility, so a zombie world is metaphysically possible. Third, if a zombie world is possible, then it is possible for the physical facts to obtain without the phenomenal facts, which means the phenomenal do not supervene on the physical. Fourth, since physicalism requires the phenomenal to supervene on the physical, physicalism is false.

On the conceivability question, Chalmers distinguishes negative conceivability, which is just the absence of a contradiction, from positive conceivability, in which one can actually form a vivid mental picture of the scenario. He insists that the zombie argument depends on the stronger notion, positive conceivability, because a mere failure to find a contradiction is too weak to ground a metaphysical conclusion. But we plainly cannot form a positive image of a being that is physically and behaviorally identical to us yet has no inner experience at all; the concept resists visualization. So the argument, on its own terms, is blocked at the very first premise.

As for the explanatory gap, Levine's point is that even a complete physical description of the brain would leave unexplained why there is something it is like to be that brain. I think this actually strengthens the case against physicalism in a way that is independent of the conceivability move. For if the gap is genuinely unbridgeable, then it cannot be merely epistemic; it must track a real metaphysical distinction between the physical and the phenomenal. A gap in our explanations, after all, must correspond to a gap in nature. So the explanatory gap, on its own, delivers the same conclusion as the zombie argument: the phenomenal do not supervene on the physical.
