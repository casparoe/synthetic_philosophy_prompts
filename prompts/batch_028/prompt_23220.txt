I work in formal verification. Specifically, I verify the control logic of implantable medical devices in a proof assistant, and I am about to start a project in which we move the base theory from a deliberately small fragment to something much closer to full ZFC, because the new device's software is too complex for the fragment to handle. At a recent lunch I raised the worry I had been sitting with: what if the axioms we are building on turn out to be inconsistent? My friend, who works in mathematical logic, waved it away with the argument that if ZFC is inconsistent, the first contradiction anyone would actually be able to exhibit would be incomprehensibly deep and remote from anything we do, so the practical risk is effectively zero. I have been mulling over whether that reassurance is actually sound, and I keep feeling that it quietly assumes something it has not justified.

Could you compare two positions on this question: the "harmless inconsistency" position, that an inconsistency in the base axioms, if it exists, is not a practical problem for mathematics or for applied work like mine, and the "foundational catastrophe" position, that such an inconsistency would undermine the trustworthiness of formal proofs in a way that matters in practice. I would like the comparison to be specific rather than a survey. What independent assumptions does each position rest on — about the nature and location of the contradiction, about how proofs are checked and certified, and about the relationship between the theory and the applications built on it — and in which domains of applied or formal work do those assumptions look weakest? And finally, given that I cannot actually verify the consistency of ZFC, what would it be responsible for me to conclude about the status of the proofs I ship?
