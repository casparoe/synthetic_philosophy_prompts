For a piece I'm writing on moral heroism, I spoke with two ethicists who got into a pointed disagreement about supererogation — the idea that some acts are genuinely optional yet genuinely praiseworthy, as when someone donates a kidney to a stranger. The first of them insisted the concept is coherent and that the standard puzzle against it, namely why a non-obligatory act should be praiseworthy, rests on a false premise. Praise, she argued, is grounded in the production of good, not in obligation; the donation is praiseworthy because it saves a life, and whether the donor was required to do it is irrelevant to that praiseworthiness — it merely marks the act as supererogatory rather than obligatory.

The second of them pushed back. If praise really just tracks value, then optionality drops out and the word "supererogatory" does no work: any act that saves a life is praiseworthy, optional or not. But that isn't our actual moral practice, she said. We plainly think the donor is *more* praiseworthy than someone who was required to donate, so optionality has to be doing some explanatory work, and the only way she could see it doing that is if the act was, in some sense, obligatory after all. The category "optional but praiseworthy," on this view, is unstable: if the act is praiseworthy because it was optional, it was required; if it's genuinely optional, the praise is a mistake.

The first replied that the "more praiseworthy" intuition is explained by sacrifice, not obligation. The donor gives up a kidney, a real cost, and we praise the sacrifice; that is what makes the act admirable, and it has nothing to do with any hidden duty. Even a small, costless kindness can be praiseworthy, she added, and the point is the same in both cases — the praiseworthiness comes from the good produced and the effort involved, not from requirement.

The second conceded that cost plays some role but said it wasn't the whole story. If cost were the explanation, she argued, then a costly act that produces no good should be praiseworthy, and it isn't. And the costless-kindness case just shows the first is smuggling value back in, which is the very point she'd made at the start. So the extra credit for optionality is still unexplained: it can't come from value, because then optionality is irrelevant, and it can't come from cost, because cost without good isn't praiseworthy.

They weren't close to resolving it, and I'd like a referee. Please go through their exchange and say who is right at each step, not just who wins overall, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
