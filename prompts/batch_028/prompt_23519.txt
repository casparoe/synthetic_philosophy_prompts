Im in an epistemology seminar rn and I keep going in circles on the old evidence problem in Bayesianism. I wrote up a short essay and I'd really appreciate comments, esp. on whether my proposed way out actually works or if I'm just moving the goalposts.

Draft:

The old evidence problem. Let E be "old evidence," something already known before a hypothesis H is even proposed. The textbook case is Mercury's perihelion precession as support for general relativity: the anomaly was well documented long before Einstein. Suppose the agent knows E with certainty, so P(E) = 1. Bayes gives P(H|E) = P(E|H)·P(H)/P(E) = P(E|H)·P(H). But E is a fact about the world fixed independently of whether H is true, so P(E|H) = P(E|~H) = 1. Hence P(H|E) = P(H): the old evidence raises credence in H by exactly zero. Yet its obvious E supports H, so standard conditionalization seems to fail.

My proposed fix. I think the bug is treating E as the evidence at all. Once E is certain, conditioning on it is degenerate — it can't discriminate H from ~H. The real new information when H gets proposed isn't E (already in the ledger) but the fact that E is old, i.e. that it was established independently of H. So the agent should condition on that fact, not on E. A cleaner version of the same move: stop modeling E as certain. If P(E) < 1 and H makes E more probable than ~H does, then E can do evidential work, and the zero-update result is just an artifact of the P(E)=1 stipulation.

My question: does relaxing the certainty actually dissolve the problem, or does it just hide it? If E is genuinely known, isn't P(E)=1 the honest model, and doesn't setting P(E)<1 smuggle in the very evidential support we're trying to explain?
