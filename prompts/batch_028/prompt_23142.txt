I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether the numbers count — Taurek's position, the one he develops in *Should the Numbers Count?*, that the mere fact that more people would die is not itself a moral reason to act, that each person holds an equal claim and that the five have no claim whatsoever on the one's life, and that the total value of the lives at stake and the strength of anyone's claim on them can come cleanly apart. We went in circles for hours over whether that is a defensible ethic or a convenient evasion, and I came away more convinced than before that the entire question turns on a distinction we kept blurring: whether the numbers are being used as a reason to sacrifice a particular person, or as an expression of impartiality toward everyone. Which is precisely why I am uneasy about the eulogy I have to deliver on Saturday for Dr. Alan Reyes, my mentor in emergency medicine for fifteen years, who died last week at sixty-eight.

Alan ran trauma triage at the county hospital for thirty years, and the department had a saying that he "let the board decide" — the triage board on which the sickest patients were ranked by prognosis and need, and on which he would, without hesitation, withhold the ventilator from a younger, more charming patient in favor of an older one when the arithmetic pointed the other way. His family, and his wife in particular, have asked that the eulogy not make him sound like a machine, not reduce him to the job, and not flatter him into a hero who single-handedly saved the most people. But the room on Saturday will be roughly half physicians and nurses who worked beside him and who know exactly what he did at that board, and half family and friends who simply want to hear about the man. I need to honor the fact that he treated every patient as a person of equal worth, and I also need to be honest that he spent his career letting the numbers decide who lived, without making those two commitments look like a contradiction he was ashamed of, and without making him sound cold.

Please write the eulogy. It should run about four to five minutes when spoken aloud, it should be addressed to this mixed audience, and it should not name any philosopher or reach for terms like "utilitarian" or "the triage ethic" as jargon, because the family will not follow it — and yet the reasoning has to be doing the real structural work inside the speech. It should land the idea that his equal regard for each patient and his willingness to defer to the arithmetic of need were not two separate things but a single commitment, that he let the numbers speak precisely because he refused to let his own face, his own familiarity, or his own charm decide who lived, and that this was the most respectful thing he could do for each person as an equal. It has to comfort the family without saying anything Alan would have considered false, and it has to be something the physicians in the room can sit through and nod at without wincing.
