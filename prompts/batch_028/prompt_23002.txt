I teach an epistemology seminar and two of my grad students, Dana and Owen, got properly stuck in an argument about the regress problem during our reading group last week, and I honestly couldnt referee it, so I'm bringing it to you for a second opinion before I take it up in class. They were going around in circles (which felt apt) and neither would budge. Here's the exchange as I remember it:

(1) Dana: "The regress problem is a genuine trilemma. Any belief's justification is either grounded in a basic belief that doesn't need further support, or it rests on an infinite chain, or it's circular. The infinite chain can't do the work, because a finite agent can never actually deploy infinitely many reasons, so no belief in the chain is ever actually justified. And circular justification is just self-support, which is no support at all. So foundationalism is the only live option: some beliefs, like perceptual and introspective ones, are justified by their relation to the world rather than by other beliefs."

(2) Owen: "You've got the infinite-chain rejection wrong, but I don't need to win that point, because coherentism sidesteps the whole trilemma. The trilemma assumes justification is a linear, belief-to-belief chain that has to terminate somewhere. It isn't. On coherentism, a belief is justified by its coherence with the agent's whole web of beliefs, and that's a global, non-linear relation — there's no chain to terminate and no first link to ground, so the regress simply doesn't get off the ground. Your basic beliefs, by contrast, have a real problem: what makes a belief basic? If it's just that it's perceptual or introspective, you've pushed the justificatory work into the stopping condition, and the same regress question now applies to the stopping condition itself."

(3) Dana: "The stopping condition is exactly what's supposed to stop the regress — that's its job, and asking 'what justifies it?' is a category mistake. Basic beliefs are justified by their relation to the world (perception, introspection), not by other beliefs, so there's no further belief to regress into. Your coherentism has a much worse version of this problem: a web of beliefs can be perfectly coherent and yet entirely false. Coherence has no necessary connection to truth, so on your view a person could be fully justified in a completely false belief. That's the fatal flaw."

(4) Owen: "You're smuggling in the assumption that justification is supposed to track truth, and that's just the foundationalist's own premise. Coherentism is a theory of justification, not of truth; it says coherence is what makes a belief justified for the agent, and it doesn't deny that truth matters, it just denies that justification is a truth-tracking relation to the world. And your 'category mistake' reply is too quick: the stopping condition is itself a claim — that perception is a legitimate source of justification — and claims are the kind of thing that can be asked for justification of. So the regress doesn't stop at basic beliefs, it just hides."

(5) Dana: "Fine, grant that the stopping condition is a claim. But then what justifies your coherence principle? If your beliefs are justified only by cohering, what justifies the claim that coherence is what justifies? If you answer 'it coheres with the rest of the web,' you're begging the question — you're using the principle to justify itself. And if you answer 'it's just a basic, unargued principle,' you've quietly conceded foundationalism. Either way, coherentism can't escape the regress; it just relocates it."

(6) Owen: "The begging-the-question charge only works if justification has to be a linear chain where each link is justified by an earlier one. But on coherentism, the coherence principle isn't a belief in the web that needs a separate justification; it's a thesis about the structure of the web, and it's supported by the same web it describes. That's not vicious circularity, because the web isn't trying to justify the principle by a separate route — the principle is part of what it is for the web to be a web of justifications. And you keep demanding that justification be something the agent can perform step by step, but that's an internalist assumption you haven't argued for."
