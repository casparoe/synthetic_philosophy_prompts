I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and I've been trying to pin down exactly what Arrow's impossibility theorem says and doesn't say, because I keep seeing it invoked loosely in seminars. Last week two of my colleagues, Maya and Jordan, got into a fairly heated debate about it over coffee, and I've been turning it over ever since.

Maya started by arguing that Arrow's theorem basically settles that majority rule is unfair, since it's the rule that actually produces the Condorcet cycle, so we should stop treating majority rule as a fair default. Jordan pushed back, saying that's a misreading: the theorem is a result about social welfare functions — mappings from profiles of individual orderings to a single complete, transitive social ordering — not about any particular voting rule, and it doesn't single out majority rule. It just says no such function can satisfy all four of Arrow's conditions at once. Maya conceded that the theorem is stated for welfare functions, but pressed that in practice every real voting system ultimately fails on transitivity, which is exactly the failure majority rule exhibits, so the theorem is really about majority rule after all. Jordan then brought up Borda count as a counterexample to that: Borda always produces a complete, transitive ordering by summing points, so it dodges the cycle problem, and it satisfies Pareto, unrestricted domain, and non-dictatorship — which means the only condition Borda violates is independence of irrelevant alternatives. To make it concrete, he laid out a profile with three candidates A, B, and C and seven voters: four rank A over B over C, and three rank B over C over A. Running Borda with all three candidates (2, 1, 0 points) gives B ten points, A eight, and C three, so Borda ranks B above A. But if you drop C and re-run Borda on just A and B (1, 0 points), the four voters who rank A first give A four points and the three who rank B first give B three, so now A beats B — even though not a single voter changed their mind about A versus B. Maya's response was that this only shows IIA is a technicality: transitivity is obviously required of any sensible ordering, but IIA is an obscure, contestable condition, and if we're willing to give up something as reasonable as IIA, the theorem starts to look like it's policing a requirement nobody actually cares about.

Can you work through this exchange and tell me, point by point, who is right about what? I'd especially like you to (a) check whether Jordan's Borda example actually does what he claims, (b) say whether Maya is correct that the theorem reduces to a statement about majority rule, and (c) weigh Maya's challenge to IIA against Jordan's defense — is IIA really a technicality, or is the spoiler problem it captures a substantive cost? And if there's something both of them are missing about what the theorem is actually doing, I'd like to hear that too.
