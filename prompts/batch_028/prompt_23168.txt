I'm a 2nd year PhD student in epistemology (dissertation on moral testimony), and I sit on my university's research ethics board as the student rep. We just got a case I'm stuck on and I'd love a second pair of eyes before I circulate a memo.

A CS team wants to deploy an AI triage system at a partner hospital and has come to us for approval. The lead PI — a senior professor, totally legit as an ML expert but not a moral philosopher — submitted a memo arguing deployment is not just permissible but morally required, because the expected benefit is large and the residual risk is negligible. The problem: the board is mostly non-experts in ML, so we genuinly can't independently check his risk numbers. Half the board wants to take his moral conclusion as authoritive testimony (he's the domain expert, and the moral claim is tightly bound up with the empirical stuff we can't verify). The other half insists we form our own moral judgment and treat his input as only about the facts, not the moral bottom line.

I think the crux is whether a moral judgment like this decomposes into an empirical part (where he has real authority) plus a moral-principle part (where we should apply our own judgment), or whether it's holistic enough that his overall conclusion carries real epistemic weight even for us. Could you draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete recommendation and the reasoning, engaging the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than just waving it away? I'd like the memo to name the moral-expertise / moral-testimony issue explicitly so the researchers can actually respond to it.
