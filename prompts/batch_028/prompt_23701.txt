I've been teaching myself decision theory from a patchwork of blog posts and a couple of secondhand textbooks, and I keep running into a result I've started calling "Good's theorem": the claim that the expected value of information is never negative, on the grounds that a rational agent can always simply ignore what they learn and behave as if they'd never received it, so that being offered a chance to observe something extra cannot, on average, make things worse. That argument feels airtight to me. Yet in the same literature I also keep meeting the opposite-sounding claim that information can be a curse — that it is sometimes rational to pay to *avoid* learning something, and that in certain settings a piece of information has genuinely negative value. I can't reconcile these two statements, and I suspect I'm conflating two distinct ideas that both get casually labeled "the value of information."

Could you carefully compare (1) Good's non-negativity result with (2) the broader claim that information can be worth less than zero? I'd like you to lay out exactly which assumptions the first result leans on — risk neutrality, the freedom to ignore the information, the absence of any cost to receiving it, and the like — and then, for at least two of those assumptions, show with a concrete worked example what changes when you drop it and the information's value actually turns negative. I'm especially interested in the ex ante / ex post distinction: I think Good's theorem is a statement about the value of the *option* to observe, made before you learn anything, whereas "information is a curse" may be a true statement about your situation *after* you've actually received a particular piece of news. If that's the right way to separate them, please say so and give an example where the option to learn is worth a positive amount yet the particular thing you end up learning leaves you worse off than if you'd never looked; and if the two claims are in fact genuinely in conflict rather than just operating at different times, I'd like you to point to the precise step in the non-negativity argument that fails.
