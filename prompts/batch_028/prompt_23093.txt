I'm taking a seminar course on the philosophy of causation this term, and we've been working through the contrast between David Lewis's counterfactual theory and James Woodward's interventionist theory. The part I keep getting stuck on is how the two accounts treat cases of redundant or preempted causation — situations where a putative cause is, as it were, backed up by another cause that would have produced the same effect on its own, so that the effect no longer counterfactually depends on the first cause. I understand the standard preemption examples we've done in class, but I'd like to see the issue pressed against a more applied setting, and I was talking this through with a friend in the epidemiology program who thinks the abstract examples are hiding the real difficulty.

Concretely, imagine a city that, in the same year, both begins chlorinating its drinking water and completes a new sewage-treatment plant, and either of these two measures on its own would have been sufficient to bring down the incidence of a waterborne illness that had been running high. The illness does in fact decline sharply over the following months. I'm trying to figure out how I should attribute causal credit here — that is, whether the chlorination caused the decline, whether the sewage plant caused the decline, whether both did, or whether the honest answer is that neither is a cause in the relevant sense.

Could you walk me through the most important implications of the Lewis-versus-Woodward contrast for exactly this kind of situation? In particular, I'd like you to (i) say how each theory classifies the chlorination and the sewage plant as causes or non-causes of the decline, (ii) explain where the two theories disagree and what the underlying technical reason for the disagreement is — for instance, the role of the surgical-intervention and holding-fixed conditions in the interventionist account, versus the counterfactual-dependence and causal-influence conditions in Lewis's account — and (iii) draw out what this disagreement means for how a public-health researcher like me should report and interpret the causal effect of each intervention in a real setting where the two measures were deployed together.
