I retired from experimental physics a few years ago and, having finally cleared my desk of half-finished grants, I have taken to reading philosophy of science in a way I never had the time for while I was still publishing. I have just worked through Kuhn's *Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, and I confess I am stuck on the notion of incommensurability, which I suspect I may be taking to be stronger than Kuhn actually intends. My difficulty is this: if the central terms of two rival paradigms genuinely do not translate across from one to the other, how could the scientific community ever have made a *rational* choice in favor of one over the other? Any such choice would have to be argued in some language, but if there is no shared, paradigm-neutral language in which to make the comparison, then the decision to switch looks to me like it must be arbitrary — a matter of fashion, authority, or group psychology rather than of evidence. I keep coming back to the thought that this would make a scientific revolution no more rational than a conversion to a new religion, which I find hard to accept, because I have actually lived through paradigm shifts in my own field and they did not feel arbitrary to me.

I was discussing this over dinner with a former postdoc, who suggested that I am simply confusing "incommensurable" with "incomparable," but I could not see what the distinction was supposed to do for me. Could you help me untangle it? In particular, is incommensurability really a claim about literal untranslatability, or am I smuggling in a stronger thesis than Kuhn defends? And if I drop that stronger thesis, what is left of the rationality of the choice between paradigms — is there any sense in which the switch from one to the other is still a matter of evidence and argument, or does Kuhn's account really leave the decision to something closer to a change of taste?
