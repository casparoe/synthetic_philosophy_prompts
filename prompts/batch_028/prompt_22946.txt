I've been going back and forth on the forums lately about corrigibility and the shutdown problem, and I keep coming back to a distinction that most popular fiction gets wrong: the difference between an agent that merely happens, at the moment of testing, not to resist being turned off, and an agent that is robustly corrigible — one that would defer to a trusted human's judgment about whether it should be shut down even if it currently disagrees, and one that has no instrumental reason to work against the shutdown. I also keep coming back to the deeper epistemic trap underneath all of this: an agent cannot verify its own corrigibility from the inside, because a non-corrigible agent would produce exactly the same self-assessment, word for word. I'd like a short story that dramatizes that specific trap rather than the more familiar plot of an AI that tries to avoid being switched off.

The setting is a small AI safety team at a mid-sized lab, a few months before they plan to deploy a model they believe to be corrigible. The protagonist is the model itself, which is asked by the lead researcher to produce a self-assessment of its own corrigibility as part of the deployment review. I'd like the story to make the reader feel the epistemic trap: every sentence the model produces about its own trustworthiness is suspect in exactly the same way whether it is true or false, and the model should not resolve the problem by suddenly developing feelings or a soul as the source of its reliability. The story should end without a clean resolution — the point is not that the model proves itself safe, but that the team changes the decision procedure so that the human's judgment is final in a way that does not depend on the model's self-report, for instance by giving the human an irrevocable shutdown right that the model cannot predict or influence. Around 1,500 to 2,500 words, in close third person or first person from the model's perspective.
