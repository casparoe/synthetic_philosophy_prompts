Okay so I've been thinking about this thing I read about a while back, the Thomson's lamp, where you flip a light switch at half a second, then a quarter second after that, then an eighth second after that, and so on, so that you flip it an infinite number of times in just one second total. The usual puzzle is "is the lamp on or off when the second is up?" and the usual answer is "well its been flipped infinitely many times so there's no last flip, so the state is just indeterminate, you can't say." But here's what's been bugging me and I can't quite get it out of my head: what if the thing you're changing doesn't just flip between two states? Like what if instead of a lamp you've got a dimmer knob, and each flip moves the knob a little bit, and the little bits get smaller and smaller, so the knob actually converges to a specific position even though you've moved it infinitely many times?

In that case, is there still a problem? Or does the fact that the knob settles to a definite spot mean the whole "infinite tasks in finite time" thing is actually fine, and the lamp is just a weird edge case where the state happens to not settle? I'd really love a concrete example of a supertask like this -- something other than the dimmer knob, I already have that one in my head -- where the end state is well-defined, and some thoughts on whether there's still a genuine conceptual issue or if the paradox just evaporates once the state converges.
