I'm teaching a small seminar on philosophy of biology and two of my students got into a long argument in office hours that I still cant fully untangle, so I'd love a second opinion. They were going over whether a trait's function is fixed by its evolutionary history or by the causal role it plays now, and they kept coming back to the same example, which is the human craving for salt.

One of them, let's call her Priya, said the salt craving's function is to keep sodium levels in the right range, because that's what it was selected for in an environment where salt was scarce, and the fact that it now drives people to over-consume is a textbook case of malfunction — the trait is doing what it was 'for' but the environment changed out from under it. She insisted this is exactly why you need the etiological account (she waved at Millikan a lot); a purely causal-role account can't even articulate the malfunction, because ont that view the function just is whatever causal role the trait happens to play right now, so if it's playing the role of making people drink too much salt, that's its function and there's nothing for it to be 'failing' at. The other, Tom, pushed back hard. He said Priya's 'proper function' was doing normative work she hadn't earned — you can ground it in selection all you want, but selection isn't goal-directed, so it's not clear how 'what it was selected for' gives you a 'proper' function in the normative sense you need for malfunction, and the whole 'malfunction' language is just normative residue. On his view the only naturalistic option is to say the craving's function is its current causal contribution to fitness, and if that contribution is negative (hypertension, heart disease) then the trait isn't malfunctioning, it's just a maladaptive mismatch, and 'mismatch' is a perfectly naturalistic concept that doesn't need 'proper function' at all. Priya said that collapses the concept of malfunction entirely, since then nothing ever malfunctions, it just does whatever it does. Tom said that's a feature, not a bug, because the malfunction concept is doing all the philosophical work in her account and it's doing it with a premise she hasn't earned.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about each specific claim — the malfunction point, the 'proper function is unearned' point, the 'mismatch is naturalistic' move — not just who wins overall? And say what, if anything, both of them are missing, because I have a feeling they're partly talking past each other about what 'function' even means.
