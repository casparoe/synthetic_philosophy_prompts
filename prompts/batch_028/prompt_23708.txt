I'm drafting a section for my dissertation on Frederick Douglass's shifting view of the Constitution, and I'd love some comments before I finilize it. Here's the draft:

Douglass's view of the Constitution is usually told as a story of two documents. In his Garrisonian phase — most explicitly in the 1849 North Star editorial 'The Constitution and Slavery' — he treated the Constitution as a pro-slavery instrument — a compact with the slaveholders, one that a free Black man could not honestly swear allegiance to. By 1860, in his speech "The Constitution: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", he had flipped: the word slave appears nowhere in the constitution, and its due process clause protects liberty for all persons, so the document is, on its face, anti-slavery, and the Fugitive Slave Laws are the real constitutional betrayal.

My argument is that this isn't just a tactical change. The shift tracks a change in Douglass's hermeneutics — from an appeal to the original intent of the framers to a literalist reading influenced by Spooner — and the Declaration of Independence stops being a weapon aimed at the Constitution and becomes the Constitution's own interpretive standard. The 1849 Douglass needs the Constitution to be bad in order to indict the nation; the 1860 Douglass needs it to be good so that abolition can be accomplished from within the law, not by tearing it down.

Specific things I'd want feedback on: (1) is the "two documents" framing fair, or does it flatten the Declaration-as-standard point? (2) is attributing the shift to Spooner's literalism overreaching? (3) any factual errors? (4) is the ending too weak?
