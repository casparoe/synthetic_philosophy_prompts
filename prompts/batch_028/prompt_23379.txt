I was covering a panel at a philosophy-of-physics conference where two speakers got into a real argument over the thermodynamic arrow of time, and I could not follow all of it. One is a philosopher of statistical mechanics, the other a cosmologist. Here is roughly the exchange.

The philosopher said the arrow of time is explained by statistical mechanics plus a low-entropy initial condition, the "Past Hypothesis." The underlying dynamics are time-symmetric, so they alone cannot pick out a direction of time; the arrow comes from the boundary condition that the early universe was in a very special, low-entropy state. Given that condition, the entropy increases toward the future as a matter of statistical typicality. That, he said, is the explanation of the Second Law and of the arrow.

The cosmologist pushed back: that is not an explanation but a restatement. You have merely moved the asymmetry from "entropy increases toward the future" into the Past Hypothesis itself, which singles out the past and asserts that entropy was low there. The Past Hypothesis has a temporal direction built in, so you have assumed the very asymmetry you are trying to explain.

The philosopher replied that the Past Hypothesis is a boundary condition, not an assumption of the arrow. The dynamics are time-symmetric; the asymmetry comes entirely from combining a time-symmetric law with a low-entropy boundary in one time direction. That is a genuine explanation, not a restatement.

The cosmologist then raised what he called the Boltzmann-brain problem. If the universe is in, or tends toward, thermal equilibrium, the overwhelmingly typical observers are not beings in a low-entropy early universe who evolve for billions of years, but rare spontaneous fluctuations, Boltzmann brains, that briefly pop into existence, carry false memories of a normal past, and then dissolve. Since we are observers, we should expect to be such a fluctuation. But we have actual evidence of a vast, orderly, low-entropy past—the cosmic microwave background, the fossil record, the expansion of the universe—which a Boltzmann fluctuation would not provide; it would provide only false memories of such a past. So equilibrium plus typicality is in serious trouble.

The philosopher answered that the Boltzmann-brain problem is a problem for eternal-equilibrium or de Sitter cosmologies, not for the Past Hypothesis account. The Past Hypothesis is precisely the claim that the universe began in a low-entropy state and has been evolving out of it, not sitting in equilibrium. So the Boltzmann-brain argument actually supports the Past Hypothesis: it shows the equilibrium account fails, which is why we need the low-entropy initial condition.
