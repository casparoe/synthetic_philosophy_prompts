I've read a lot of presentations of the Dutch book argument, and I understand the basic idea: if your credences violate the probability axioms, a bookmaker can construct a set of bets such that, whatever the state of the world, you suffer a net loss, and the argument is that no rational agent should be willing to accept such a set. I also understand that the argument relies on the assumption that you're willing to accept bets at your own credences, and that the bookmaker can offer you the relevant bets. What I'm less clear on is the step from credence-coherence to expected utility maximization over acts. The standard presentation seems to just extend the same Dutch book reasoning to acts, but I don't think that extension is as straightforward as the credence case, because the bookmaker can't simply offer you a bet on the consequences of an act unless the act is itself a bet or is under the bookmaker's control. Can you explain exactly where the argument for act-rationality diverges from the argument for credence-coherence, and what additional assumptions are needed to make the Dutch book argument work for acts rather than just for bets? In particular, I'm interested in whether the sure-thing principle plays a load-bearing role in this step, and whether there are known limitations to the extension.
