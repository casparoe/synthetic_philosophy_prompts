I've been thinking about Frege's political diary (the spring-1924 one) for a seminar I'm teaching on the history of logic, and I've written up a short essay on whether it actually bears on his philosophy. I'd love a second pair of eyes before I hand it out to the students. Heres the rough draft: Frege's diary, kept over a few weeks in the spring of 1924, a little over a year before his death, is a mess of anti-Semitic, nationalist, and monarchical opinions, and it's hard to read without wincing. The obvious question is whether any of this bleeds into his philosophy, or whether his logicism and his philosophy of language are just... there, independent of the politics. I want to argue that there's a real connection, but a subtle one.

The connection I have in mind is his view of "thoughts" (Gedanken) as objective, timeless entities that we "grasp" rather than create. This is a weird idea even by the standards of his time, and I think its not a coincidence that it lines up with his view of "German" reason as a kind of objective, racial essence. The idea that truth is "out there" waiting to be grasped by the right kind of mind has a nationalist flavor to it, even if Frege never says so explicitly. Now, I know the genetic fallacy is a real concern here: even if his politics influenced his philosophy, that doesn't make the philosophy wrong. But I think the genetic fallacy cuts both ways — we shouldn't dismiss his philosophy just because of his politics, but we also shouldn't pretend the politics is irrelevant to understanding the philosophy.

So my questions are: (1) Is my argument about the connection between his view of objectivity and his politics actually supported, or am I just projecting a nationalist reading onto a neutral philosophical position? (2) Is the genetic fallacy point a red herring, or is it a genuine complication? (3) What am I missing? I'd especially like comments on whether the "German reason as racial essence" move is too strong, and whether I should be more careful about the distinction between Frege's explicit philosophical claims and the broader cultural context he was writing in.
