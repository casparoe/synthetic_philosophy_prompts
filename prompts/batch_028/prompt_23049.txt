I'm on the math advisory board for a TV game show, and we're debating whether to add a "switch" option to our two-envelope segment, where one envelope has twice the amount of the other and the contestant doesn't know which is which. The basic setup is that the producer picks an amount X, puts X in one envelope and 2X in the other, shuffles, and the contestant picks one; the question is whether we let them peek at their amount and then switch.

Half the board is convinced a savvy contestant will just always switch, because of the two-envelope reasoning — "no matter what's in my envelope, the other one has expected value 1.25× mine, so switching is +EV" — and the show will bleed money. The other half (including our in-house statistician) thinks that's a fallacy, an impropper prior thing, and that a real contestant won't actually reason this way, so we should just let them switch and it'll be fine. I keep going back and forth, and I was re-reading the old LessWrong threads on the two-envelope problem trying to settle it and I'm not sure I've got it.

Could you draft a short decision memo for the board that recommends whether we should allow the switch, and if so, how to structure the envelope amounts — a bounded prior, a specific distribution, whatever — so the show doesn't lose money? I want it to engage the two-envelope reasoning seriously rather than just hand-wave the fallacy, give a concrete recommendation with a specific rule or distribution change, and honestly treat the strongest argument on the losing side.
