I recently hosted a small dinner where I deliberately structured the drinking — a fixed number of rounds, a designated host keeping the pace — on the theory that a little wine loosens the conversation and stops it from going flat. It worked, but afterward I kept thinking about Plato, because I can't reconcile the Symposium with the Laws on this point. In the Symposium, moderated drinking (the agreement that each should drink only as much as he pleases, with no compulsion, and the dismissal of the flute-girl) seems to be a precondition for good philosophical talk, whereas in the Laws the Athenian Stranger legislates against drunkenness, especially for those in positions of responsibility, treating the intoxicated official as someone who no longer knows where he is. Yet the Laws also appears to license a state-orchestrated, bounded festival of Dionysus in which the older citizens are deliberately given over to a controlled drunkenness under an appointed master of the feast. So my question is: what is the actual conceptual difference, in Plato, between the private, spontaneous intoxication the Laws forbids and the public, regulated, collective intoxication it authorizes — and does the Symposium's model of wine as an aid to philosophical discourse map onto that licensed festival, or is it something the Laws would not recognize? I'm after the structural reasoning, not just "moderation is a virtue."
