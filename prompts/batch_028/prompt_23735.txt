At a meetup last week, two people got into it about whether what you believe can change what you actually see, or just what you think you see. Dana said it clearly changes what you see: she brought up the Müller-Lyer illusion, where two equal lines look different because of the arrowheads, and said that once you're told the trick, the illusion weakens or goes away for some people. So your knowledge changed the perceptual experience, not just the judgment. Marcus pushed back: the illusion doesn't go away, you just know the lines are equal, so your judgment changes but your experience doesn't. You still "see" the difference, you just don't believe it. Dana said that's just moving the goalposts — if "seeing" includes everything from the retina to the final judgment, then of course cognition is in the loop, but she meant the raw experience, and the raw experience changes. Marcus said the raw experience is defined by the bottom-up sensory processing, so by definition cognition can't get in there — the question is kind of a catagory error. Dana said that's question-begging: you're defining "perception" so narrowly that the answer is trivially no, but the interesting question is whether higher-level states can causally contribute to the character of experience. Marcus conceded the question is interesting but said the evidence is weak — the Müller-Lyer effect is probably just response bias or attention, not a change in experience. Dana said the Sherif autokinetic effect is a better case: people in a dark room watching a stationary point of light report it as moving, and when you put them in a group, their reports converge, so social context changes perception. Marcus said that's a change in judgment, not perception — they still see the light as stationary, they just report it as moving because of social pressure. Dana said that's the same move again: you're just defining "perception" to exclude any effect of higher-level states, which makes the debate unresolvable by construction. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall?
