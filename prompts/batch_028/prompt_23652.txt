I'm a second yr grad student in epistemology and I'm writing my qual paper on the epistemology of conspiracy theories, and I've been going in cirles on one specific issue for like two weeks now. It's the "conspiracy theory of everything" objection. The basic move is: if you grant that a particular conspiracy theory is well-justified — say, that the CIA had a hand in the JFK assassination — then by the same epistemic standards you should be willing to grant any conspiracy theory, like the moon landing being faked, because the underlying epistemic structure is identical. So either you swallow the whole lot (which is absurd) or you reject the epistemic structure of conspiracy theories as inherently suspect, full stop.

I keep feeling like the objection is doing too much work, but I can't quite articulate why. I've been toying with a few angles that I think might break the symmetry. One is what I'm calling the "lucky coincidence" problem: to explain why a conspiracy stays hidden, the theorist has to posit that a large group of actors has all independently failed to leak it, which is itself a striking coincidence that the theory doesn't explain away. Another is the base-rate issue: the prior probability that any given set of anomalies is the product of a coordinated cover-up is just low, so the evidential bar should be higher than for a mundane explanation. And then there's the specific-vs-general distinction — I think specific, falsifiable claims (the CIA killed JFK) are in a genuinely different epistemic category from sweeping general ones (the government is hiding the truth about 9/11), and the CTE objection blurs that.

Could you write me an essay that weighs the arguments for and against the claim that the epistemic structure of conspiracy theories is inherently suspect? I want it to actually grapple with the CTE objection, the lucky-coincidence problem, the base-rate point, and the specific/general distinction, not just name-drop them. And I need it to land on a clear verdict and defend that verdict — I don't want a "it's complicated, both sides have points" ending, because my advisor already thinks I'm being way too soft on the conspiracy theorist and I need to be able to point to a defensible bottom line. That said, I also don't want to come across as just reflexively dismissive, so whatever conclusion you reach, I'd like the strongest consideration on the other side handled honestly rather than waved away.
