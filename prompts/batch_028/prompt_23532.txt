I host a small podcast about science, and next week's guest is an evolutionary biologist who defends group selection — the view that natural selection can act on whole populations of organisms, not just on genes or individuals. I've been thinking about this debate on my own for a while, and I want a sequence of questions that go beyond having him restate the view. I'd like at least one question pressing the point that kin selection and multilevel selection are mathematically equivalent ways of decomposing the same evolutionary process, and what that does to the claim that group selection is a real causal force rather than a bookkeeping choice; at least one on why a gene-centered theorist would distinguish the unit of selection from the unit of adaptation, and whether that distinction is doing real work or just rescuing the gene view from an unfortunate name; and at least one on whether the gene-centered view's talk of selfish genes is a literal claim or a metaphor, and what explanatory work that metaphor is supposed to do. For each, please include a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, and close with a question about what evidence or prediction could actually count against group selection as opposed to the gene-centered view.
