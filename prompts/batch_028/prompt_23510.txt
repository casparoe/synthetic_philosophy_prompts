I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of language, and the chapter I'm currently drafting is on Davidson's theory of radical interpretation. I've been going back and forth with a colleague about a point I think is underappreciated in the secondary literature: how exactly to cash out Davidson's notion of triangulation. On the reading my colleague favors, triangulation is a constitutive condition on thought and meaning — there simply is no genuine thought, and no linguistic meaning, for a being who has never been triangulated with at least one other agent over a shared world. On the reading I'm leaning toward, triangulation is at most a methodological or empirical generalization about the conditions under which interpretation reliably succeeds, and it leaves open the logical possibility of solitary or pre-social representational states. I'd like to pressure-test this by having you write a dialogue between a defender of the constitutive reading and a defender of the weaker, methodological reading.

Set the dialogue around a concrete case that forces the issue: a person who has lived in total isolation from birth — a hermit, or a feral child — and have each speaker try to explain what, if anything, that person can genuinely think, and why. Both characters should be equally competent and genuinely sympathetic to Davidson's broader project, so neither should come off as a strawman; I want real back-and-forth in which each responds to the other's actual points (for example, the constitutive defender pressing on the incoherence of proto-mental states, and the methodological defender pressing on the problem of the first thinker, or on the regress a constitutive account seems to generate) rather than a sequence of pre-prepared talking points. Please keep the dialogue anchored in the actual technical vocabulary of radical interpretation — the principle of charity, meaning postulates, the disquotational schema, the T-schema — and keep it to a handful of exchanges rather than a long, monologue-heavy piece.
