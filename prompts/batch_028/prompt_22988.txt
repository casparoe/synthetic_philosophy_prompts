I'm preparing a seminar on the philosophy of economics, and a graduate student has been pressing me on the status of revealed preference theory, to the point where the question has genuinely gotten under my skin. In the standard textbook presentation I keep repeating to them, Samuelson's revealed preference approach is a triumph of behaviorism: we can recover the ordering that a utility function is supposed to represent purely from observed choices, without any appeal to introspection or to utility as a mental state. On that picture the utility function is, at best, a convenient mathematical representation of the revealed preference relation, and at worst a fiction we could dispense with entirely, since all we ever need is what the person actually chose.

The student then asked me a question I could not answer satisfactorily. If the whole method is to read preferences straight off behavior — ranking what the person chose above what they rejected — then the resulting ordering fits the data by construction, and there is nothing left to test it against. So how can revealed preference ever tell us that a person is being irrational or inconsistent? To call a choice "irrational" surely requires some independent standard of rationality to judge it by, and that standard looks suspiciously like the very notion of utility we were supposed to have eliminated. I find myself suspecting that the "behaviorist" story is either more modest or more compromised than I have been telling my students, but I cannot pin down exactly where the descriptive and the evaluative are supposed to come from.

Could you help me sort this out? I want to know whether revealed preference is genuinely a purely descriptive reading of preferences off of behavior, or whether it already presupposes a notion of rationality — and hence of utility — that it cannot honestly claim to have derived from the data.
