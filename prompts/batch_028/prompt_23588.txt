I'm putting together a short talk for a grad seminar on the history of arguments for the immortality of the soul, and I keep running into the same problem with how Gödel gets presented. Most of the secondary literature I'm reading — and honestly a lot of the philosophy-of-mind stuff too — just says "Gödel held that the soul is a simple substance and therefore can't be destroyed, so it's immortal," and then moves on. I was going over this with a student last week and he kept conflating the two arguments, and I don't think thats what Gödel was actually doing. I want to build a section of the talk around arguing that point properly, and I'd like you to write it up.

Could you make the case that Gödel's argument for immortality — the one he worked out in those letters to his mother in 1961 — is really a teleological argument of the Kantian / soul-making family, and not the simple-substance / indivisibility argument that people usually pin on him? The thing I want the argument to nail down is the gap in the simple-substance argument: even if you grant that the soul is a partless simple substance and so can't be dissolved or destroyed, that at most gets you an indestructible substrate. It says nothing about what that substrate goes on to do — whether it develops, whether it's in a "life" at all, whether its capacities ever get fulfilled. So indestructibility just doesn't deliver the conclusion Gödel actually wants, which is that the soul goes on to a further life in which its enormous range of potential is actually realized. I want you to argue that the real work in his case is done by two different premises — the intelligibility-of-nature premise (the world is rationally organized and hence meaningful) plus the under-actualization premise (we actualize only a vanishingly small fraction of what we're built for) — and that it's the combination of those, not the simplicity premise, that licenses the move from "the soul survives death" to "the soul goes on to fulfill its potentials." If you can, close by saying why the conflation with the simple-substance argument is a substantive mistake rather than just a sloppy one, and why it matters for evaluating the argument: the teleological version has to answer the objection that a rationally organized world isn't *required* to do this, whereas the simple-substance version has to answer the objection that "simple" might still be compatible with ceasing to be a mind.
