I'm preparing a feature for my ideas column on the current state of non-classical logic, and a skeptical reader has pushed back on my sympathetic treatment of Graham Priest's dialetheism. His objection, in brief, is the standard triviality argument: if even one contradiction is genuinely true, then by the principle of explosion every sentence is entailed, so the view collapses into the assertion that everything is true and is thereby self-refuting. I'd like to push back on this, but I don't want to do it at the level of the slogan.

Specifically, I'd like you to argue that Priest's preferred logic, the Logic of Paradox (LP), is considerably more conservative than this caricature suggests. Ground the argument in LP's semantics: a sentence can take the value "both true and false," and a formula counts as a theorem precisely when it is designated, i.e., at least true under every valuation. Using that setup, show that LP in fact validates the law of non-contradiction (¬(A ∧ ¬A) is designated for every valuation) and even the law of excluded middle (A ∨ ¬A is designated for every valuation), while it rejects the principle of explosion (A ∧ ¬A does not entail an arbitrary B) and rejects the rule of reductio. The point is to make the reader see that what dialetheism gives up is a pair of inference principles, not the entire architecture of classical truth-functional reasoning.

Then close by drawing out the philosophical significance. What does it mean for a dialetheist to retain non-contradiction and excluded middle as logical truths while giving up explosion and reductio? How does this bear on the self-refutation objection, and how much of ordinary mathematical and everyday reasoning survives intact under LP? I'm after a precise, well-organized argument rather than a survey, and I'd be grateful if you flag any step where the conclusion depends on the particular choice of paraconsistent logic rather than following from the dialetheist commitment itself.
