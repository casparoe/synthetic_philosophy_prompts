I'm a third-year philosophy student, and this semester I'm taking a philosophy of art seminar that has turned out to be more practically useful to me than I expected, because I also sit on my university's small art museum's acquisitions committee as the student representative. We have a live case in front of us, and I'd like your help drafting the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's committee meeting.

The museum has been offered, as a gift, a watercolor landscape attributed to Walter Ashby, a nineteenth-century regional landscape painter (active roughly 1840–1868) of whom the museum holds a small but much-loved group. The donor is a long-time patron whose family has quietly funded two of our galleries, and the work would anchor a new "regional landscape" room we are trying to open by spring; the donor has asked for our answer by the end of the month. The trouble is that the attribution is genuinely contested. Our conservator's technical analysis found a synthetic organic pigment in the shadows that, as far as she can determine, was not commercially available until a generation after Ashby died, and the paper stock also appears later than the artist's lifetime. Against that, the provenance is strong and documented back to the artist's own studio, passing through his son and a succession of known collectors (though there is a roughly thirty-year gap in the twentieth century when the ownership is unrecorded), and a specialist art historian we consulted believes the style and handling are entirely consistent with Ashby's secure works and thinks the physical evidence can be explained away. About half the committee wants to accept the gift on the strength of the provenance and the donor relationship; the other half thinks we should decline anything we cannot technically verify, full stop.

Could you draft a short written opinion for the committee that recommends a concrete decision — accept, decline, or accept with specific conditions such as a particular label or a round of further testing — together with a realistic time frame for acting on it? Please make the reasoning something the committee can actually act on, and make sure you give an honest treatment of the strongest version of the case against your recommendation rather than simply restating our policy. I've been turning over, since a seminar discussion on the status of copies and forgeries, what if anything an "original" is supposed to be and whether that should matter for a museum whose mission is educational rather than investment-grade; I'd like the memo to take that question seriously rather than sidestep it.
