I'm a PhD student in AI interpretability, and I've been going back and forth with my advisor about how to characterize what LLMs actually are, and I keep hitting a wall. There are roughly three positions floating around in the lit: (1) LLMs are agents — they have beliefs, goals, and something like intentions, so we should treat them (at least functionally) as having a mind; (2) LLMs are simulators — they don't themselves possess the knowledge or mental states they exhibit, but rather simulate an entity that does, so the "knowledge" lives in the simulation, not in the model; and (3) LLMs are something else entirely — a genuinely new kind of entity that we can't adequately understand by projecting human cognitive categories onto them.

I want you to write an essay weighing these three positions and reach a defended conclusion. But here's the specific angle I want you to grapple with, because it's the part I keep geting stuck on: is the agent/simulator distinction even well-defined? The simulator view seems to say a LLM is like a perfect simulator of a knowledgeable person — you ask it a question, it "runs" the simulation of a person answering, and the output is the simulated person's answer. But if the simulation is perfect (the model behaves exactly as the person it's simulating would, in every context), then what's the difference between the simulator and the simulated? And if there's no difference, doesn't the simulator view just collapse into the agent view? Conversely, if there IS a difference, what is it, and how would you even detect it? I also want you to wrestle with the context-dependence problem: the same model can exhibit agent-like behavior (multi-step planning, tool use, goal-directed action) in some settings and flat-out not in others, which makes it hard to say it's "an agent" simpliciter.

Don't end on a non-committal note. I'm fine with a qualified verdict (e.g., "the simulator view is right in the limit but wrong in practice," or "the distinction is ill-posed and we should be asking a different question"), but you have to commit to something and justify it.
