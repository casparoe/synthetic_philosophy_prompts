So I was sitting in on a seminar for my epistemology grad students last week and two of them got into a real argument about Goodman's grue, and I honestly still can't tell who had the better side of it. One, lets call her Sam, opened by saying that grue is basically fatal to induction: all the evidence we have for 'all emeralds are green' is exactly the same as the evidence for 'all emeralds are grue,' and there's no logical or probabilistic principle that can prefer one over the other, so there's just no fact of the matter about which prediction is right, which is why induction is fundamentally unjustified. The other, Riley, pushed back and said that's a non sequitur — grue isn't a natural kind, and the reason we project 'green' is that green tracks some real, stable feature of the world while grue is this artificial temporaly indexed mess, so sure the evidence underdetermines, but the fact of the matter is fixed by which properties are natural kinds, and that's a substantive metaphysical question, not a gap.

Sam came back and said that just kicks the can, because 'natural kind' is itself a projectible notion you can't define without already assuming the inductive principles you're trying to justify, and besides you can do the grue trick to any predicate by defining 'bleen' and shifting the discontinuity, so by Riley's own standard nothing is projectible. Riley said no, the grue/bleen decomposition only works because green and blue are the basic natural properties, so the decomposition presupposes the naturalness of its components and that's what breaks the symmetry. Sam then said OK but now you're committed to a whole substantive metaphysics of natural kinds and you haven't told me how to tell which kinds are actually natural, so if that's up to us you've just relocated the arbitrariness from the predicate to the metaphysics. Riley's last line was that natural kinds aren't up to us at all — they're objective features of the world that science discovers, so the arbitrariness is gone.
