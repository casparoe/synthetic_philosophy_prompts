I sit on the investment commitee for my university's endowment, and we've got a live offer on the table that I need to write a recommendation on before next Tuesday's meeting. A hedge fund has offered to buy our entire position in a green infrastructure bond — it's been in the portfolio for about six years and hasn't actually traded in months, so the last quoted price is pretty stale — at roughly a 4% premium to that last quote. Their pitch is that they have proprietary valuation data showing the bond is worth more than the market thinks, and they'd like it. The committee is split: half of us think we should just take the money, and the other half keep saying that if a sophisticated fund wants our side of the trade, something is off, because either our valuation is wrong or theirs is, and we don't know which one.

This is literally the thing I argued with my friend about for like two hours last night. He's a quant and he kept insisting there are these "no-trade" results in the econ literature that basically say a mutually agreeable trade can only clear if the two sides disagree about something, so the fact that they want the bond is itself evidence that we're underpricing it, or that they're making a mistake we're about to profit off of. I was telling him trade can also just be risk transfer — they might have a mandate to hold green bonds, or a different time horizon, or a different tax situation — and nobody has to be wrong. I couldn't resolve it, and now I have to.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? I want it to recommend a concrete course of action — take the offer, decline, or counter with specific conditions — with a rough timeline, and I'd like it to actually grapple with the epistemic question of what it means that a sophisticated counterparty wants our side of the trade, including the strongest version of the argument against whatever you recommend. It should be something the committee could actually vote on, not a survey of both sides.
