So I run a small podcast where I interview people who actually write papers on metaphysics (mostly to keep myself honest), and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends the Identity of Indiscernibles -- the version that says no two objects can share all their properties -- and who argues that Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment doesn't refute it. I've been going back and forth on this since the old rationalist forum threads on haecceitism, and I want questions that pull on the actual load-bearing parts of the view rather than have him restate the slogan. Could you draft six to eight questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give?

I want at least one question on the triviality problem: if the quantifier over properties ranges over identity properties like 'being identical to sphere A', PII is trivially true, so the defender has to restrict the vocabulary -- press on what the restriction is and why it's not just stipulated to dodge Black, and have a follow-up for the 'we only quantify over qualitative, non-singular properties' answer that asks whether relational properties count as qualitative, given that in Black's universe each sphere stands in exactly the same relations to everything that exists (including the other sphere), so the 'they differ in their relations' reply seems to come up empty in the original setup. I also want one on quantum mechanics: are identical particles -- two electrons in the same state, or a two-particle system in a symmetric state -- genuine counterexamples, and if he says QM particles aren't really objects in the first place because of permutation invariance, follow up on whether that deflates PII into a claim about classical objects only. And one on the connection to haecceitism: im pretty sure PII with the necessary operator implies non-haecceitism, but I'm not convinced the converse holds, since a world with two indiscernible spheres isn't a haecceitistic variant of another world, it's just a world -- have him say out loud whether he thinks the two are equivalent or whether PII is strictly stronger, with a follow-up for either answer. End with one question about what evidence, from physics or elsewhere, could count against the view, since if PII is just a priori it's hard to see why anyone should care.
