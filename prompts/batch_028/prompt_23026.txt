I am a novelist whose current book is set in a genetics laboratory, and in revising I have been trying to get straight in my own prose the difference between a trait that genuinely malfunctions and a trait that is merely neutral or incidental, because my editor keeps flagging passages where I treat the two as the same. I was told the cleanest philosophical answer runs through Millikan's etiological, or selected-effects, account of biological function, and that its chief rivals are the causal-role and contributionist accounts associated with Neander. My next guest on my small philosophy podcast is a philosopher who defends the etiological account, and I would like ten to twelve interview questions that go well beyond having her restate the view. I want at least one question each on: how the account is supposed to handle a trait that has only very recently been selected, and why that case is harder than it first looks; what the account owes to the fact that we routinely attribute malfunctions to traits whose selected effect is obscure, absent, or indistinguishable from a merely incidental effect; and how a selected-effects theorist should answer a contributionist who says a function just is a causal contribution to a system-level effect that researchers have picked out as interesting. For each question, please also give me a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, so that I can press the point without having to improvise. If you can, end the sequence with a question about what kind of empirical finding in evolutionary biology would count against her account rather than merely against its current formulation.
