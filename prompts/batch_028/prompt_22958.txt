Assigned question: In the epistemology of testimony, explain the reductionist and anti-reductionist positions. What is the role of the analogy with perceptual knowledge in the debate? And what is the main objection to reductionism that runs through the regress line, and how should the reductionist respond?

Student's answer: The reductionist about testimony holds that a hearer can know p on the basis of a speaker's testimony only if the hearer has independent, non-testimonial reasons to believe the speaker is reliable — for instance, evidence of the speaker's track record, or that the speaker's claim coheres with what the hearer already knows. The anti-reductionist denies that such independent reasons are required: on their view, the speaker's assertion itself can be a source of justification, so the hearer can be justified in believing p on the strength of the testimony alone, without independent corroboration, and testimony is thereby a basic, irreducible source of knowledge.

The debate is usually modelled on the analogous debate about perceptual knowledge: just as there is a question whether perceptual justification requires independent grounds that one's visual faculties are reliable, there is a question whether testimonial justification requires independent grounds that the speaker is trustworthy.

The main problem for reductionism is a regress. Any independent evidence that the speaker is reliable — her track record, her consistency, her expertise — must itself be established through further testimony, and that further testimony would in turn require independent evidence, ad infinitum. So the reductionist is driven into scepticism: she cannot coherently require independent grounds for testimonial trust without thereby making testimonial knowledge impossible. The anti-reductionist avoids this by allowing testimony to be a basic source of justification. And the analogy with perception actually favours anti-reductionism: we plainly do not need independent, non-perceptual grounds to trust our eyes in ordinary cases, so by parity of reasoning we need no independent grounds to trust testimony either.
