**Question:**
Bradley's regress of relations is often read as a problem about the unity of the proposition: the proposition "aRb" unites a, R, and b into a single fact, and Bradley's regress threatens to show that no such unification is possible.
(a) State the regress.
(b) Explain why it is a problem for the unity of the proposition, not merely for relations in general.
(c) Evaluate Russell's solution in terms of relational complexes. Does it succeed?

**Student's answer:**

(a) The regress: if a is related to b by R, then R must itself be related to a and b. Since R is a universal, it can't already contain a and b, so we need a further relation R2 relating R to a and b, and R2 needs a further relation, and so on ad infinitum. Bradley concludes that relations are impossible and that only the Absolute — a single concrete whole — is real.

(b) The problem for the unity of the proposition is that the proposition "aRb" must be a single fact, not a heap of a, R, and b. If the unification of a, R, and b requires a further unifier, and that unifier requires a further unifier, and so on, then the proposition is never actually unified — it's just an infinite regress of unifications. So the regress shows that the proposition has no unity.

(c) Russell's solution is to treat the relational complex "aRb" as itself a particular fact. R is a universal, but it's "in" the complex without being related to a and b by any further relation. The complex is a new entity, so there's no need for a further relater. This defuses the regress because it blocks the step where Bradley assumes every relation must itself be a term in a further relation.

I think Russell's solution succeeds, because the regress only arises if we assume relations are external to their terms. If relations are internal, then the relation is just part of the term and no further relater is needed.
