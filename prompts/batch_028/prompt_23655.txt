Here's a draft of a section on the taxonomy of optional actions and the moral residue account of supererogation. Im looking for comments on whether the tension I identify between the moral residue account and the marginal principle is real, and whether I'm missing a standard objection or response.

The taxonomy of optional actions distinguishes between actions that are supererogatory and actions that are merely permissible. The moral residue account holds that an action is supererogatory if and only if it is optional and generates a moral residue — that is, a reason to be grateful for it being performed, a reason for regret if not performed, or a reason for blame if not performed. The residue is what distinguishes the supererogatory from the merely optional.

The marginal principle holds that whether an action is supererogatory is not an intrinsic property of the action but depends on the alternatives available to the agent: the same action can be supererogatory in one context and obligatory in another, depending on what the agent could have done instead.

The tension: the moral residue account grounds supererogation in a moral property (the residue), which is a reason to be grateful, a reason for regret, or a reason for blame. But if the action generates a moral reason to be grateful for it being performed, then it seems the action is morally good. And if it is morally good, why isnt it obligatory? The marginal principle, on the other hand, grounds supererogation in a non-moral property (the availability of alternatives), which avoids the problem of moral goodness but leaves the residue unexplained.

So the moral residue account and the marginal principle are in tension: the former grounds supererogation in a moral property, the latter in a non-moral property. The question is whether this tension is real, or whether there is a way to reconcile the two.
