For my population-ethics seminar I want to argue that the dust-specks paradox (one person tortured, or an astronomically large number of people each getting a speck of dust in the eye) is generated by an expected-value move we should reject: treating a tiny probability of torture as equivalent to a small sure harm. That move is what lets us slide from "N people each get a dust speck" to "N people each face a tiny risk of torture" and thence to "the expected number of tortures exceeds one." My position is that we should draw a firm line between aggregating sure harms and aggregating risks: a large number of sure dust specks may be worse than one torture, but no number of people each facing a sufficiently tiny risk of torture is worse than one person being tortured for sure. Please lay out this argument rigorously and say precisely where the expected-value identification is doing the paradoxical work.
