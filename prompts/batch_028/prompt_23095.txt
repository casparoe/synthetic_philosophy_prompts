I retired from materials science about three years ago and have since been reading philosophy with a fair amount of enthusiasm, though I'll admit I'm still a beginner. One topic that has been particularly gripping for me is the debate about dispositions — properties like fragility, solubility, and conductivity, which I spent most of my career taking for granted in the lab. At a study group last month, two friends of mine got into a fairly heated argument about the so-called "fink" and "mask" cases, and I found myself unable to tell who was right. I would appreciate your help adjudicating.

The background, briefly: the straightforward way to analyze a disposition is to say that a thing has the disposition if and only if it would manifest it when triggered — a glass is fragile iff it would shatter if struck. But "fink" cases (in which some agent removes the disposition just before it would manifest) and "mask" cases (in which the disposition is present but its manifestation is blocked by an independent factor, as with bubble wrap around a fragile glass) are the classic counterexamples. The standard fix is the "safe conditions" account: a thing has the disposition iff it would manifest it when triggered, in safe conditions, i.e., absent finks and masks.

My friend Priya argued that the safe conditions account is the right approach, and that the fink/mask distinction is crucial to making it work: in a fink case, she said, the disposition is genuinely absent at the moment of triggering (the fink has removed it), whereas in a mask case, the disposition is genuinely present (the mask merely blocks its manifestation), and it is this distinction that lets the safe conditions account correctly identify dispositions. My other friend, Tom, argued that the safe conditions account is circular, because to specify "safe conditions" we must first identify what counts as a fink and what counts as a mask, and to do that we must already know what the disposition is — to know that the bubble wrap is a mask, we must know that it is blocking the fragility in particular, and to know that, we must already know the glass is fragile. Priya replied that finks and masks can be identified by their causal structure independently of the disposition concept: a fink is an agent that removes a property, and a mask is a property that blocks a manifestation, and neither identification requires prior knowledge of the disposition. Tom insisted that this does not avoid the circularity, because to know the bubble wrap is a mask one must know it is blocking the shattering, and knowing that requires knowing the glass is fragile. Priya then said we can observe that the bubble wrap prevents the shattering without knowing the glass is fragile, but Tom countered that this is just the simple conditional analysis in disguise: "the glass is fragile iff it would shatter if struck, absent things that block the shattering" is trivially true and tells us nothing. I could not referee the exchange, and I would be grateful if you could go through it and say who is right at each step. In particular, I am interested in whether the safe conditions account is genuinely circular, and whether the fink/mask distinction is a real distinction or a pseudo-distinction.
