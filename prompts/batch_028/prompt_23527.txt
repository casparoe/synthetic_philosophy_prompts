I am a third-year PhD student in the history of analytic philosophy, currently drafting a chapter on Frege's logicism, and I keep returning to a question I cannot settle. The material in question is the diary (Tagebuch) that Frege kept from March to May 1924, in the last year of his life, which was published in 1994. Beyond a scattering of entries that bear on the genesis of his philosophical work, it contains a sustained record of virulent antisemitic and ethnonationalist convictions, and entries in which he expresses sympathy for Ludendorff and, in May 1924, approval of an article praising Hitler. A friend in the history department insists that all of this is a matter for the historian, not the philosopher: the arguments of the Foundations, the Basic Laws, and the later logical writings stand or fall on their own, and the diary is a private psychological document that belongs in a separate drawer. I am not sure he is right, but I do not know how to say what the diary does, if anything, to the philosophy.

What I would like from you is an essay on this controversy: does Frege's political diary bear on the assessment of his philosophy, and if so, in what sense? Please weigh the main considerations on both sides. On the side that says it bears on the philosophy, you might consider that Frege's account of the objectivity of thoughts depends on an idealization of a public court of opinion, that thoughts are public and timeless rather than private mental items, and that the so-called third realm of Der Gedanke (1918), a realm of objective thoughts belonging neither to the external world nor to the mind, is precisely a picture of rationality as a shared public good; a man who imagined the rational community in ethnonational terms may have built that idealization into the architecture of the philosophy in a way the arguments themselves do not make visible. On the other side, you might consider that the diary is a private document of a different genre from a published argument, that the separation of the argument from the arguer is a methodological commitment analytic philosophy itself makes, and that the diary puts pressure on the ideal of a context-free, impersonal argument only if one already assumes that the ideal is not an ideal. You are welcome to bring in further considerations, for instance about the history of the discipline and about how the Frege-Russell-Wittgenstein lineage was canonized.

Whatever conclusion you reach, please defend it explicitly and do not end on a non-committal note. A qualified verdict is acceptable: for example, that the diary is relevant only to the history of the discipline and not to the arguments, or that it is relevant in a limited sense to the interpretation of the ideal of objectivity, or that it genuinely undermines a specific claim Frege makes. But you must say which of these positions you endorse and why, and you should engage the strongest version of the opposing view rather than a weakened one.
