I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been reading on the side about intuitionistic logic and constructive mathematics because it looks relevant to the formal-verification work I do. Two colleagues of mine — one a mathematician, the other a computer scientist who works on proof assistants — got into a long argument at lunch, and I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of who was right about what.

I'd mentioned that the classical intermediate value theorem doesn't hold as stated in constructive mathematics. The mathematician (I'll call her Elena) said:

(1) "The constructive intermediate value theorem is provable, but only in a weak form: it says that for every positive rational ε there is some point where the function is within ε of zero, but it does not hand you an actual root. The gap is not a technicality. Constructively, the root may not depend continuously on the function — an arbitrarily small perturbation of the function can make a root appear or disappear — so no uniform procedure can output a root from the function alone. The classical proof you're thinking of is a bisection argument, and it fails at a precise step: at each stage it has to decide which half-interval carries the sign change, which requires deciding the sign of the function at the midpoint, and the sign of an arbitrary real is not decidable constructively. That decision is exactly where the law of excluded middle is doing work."

The computer scientist (I'll call him Marcus) pushed back:

(2) "The bisection argument is fine constructively if you're computing with real numbers. You evaluate the function at the midpoint m: if f(m) is exactly zero you're done; if it's positive you go left; if it's negative you go right. Every real number is exactly one of those three, so that's just a three-way case split, and the nested intervals converge to the root. The 'weak' theorem is just a weaker statement of the same result, not a different theorem."

(3) "And here's a cleaner example that shows the real issue, one that has nothing to do with continuity or approximation. Take an infinite binary sequence that is either eventually all zeros or has infinitely many ones. Classically, one of those two alternatives holds, by the law of excluded middle. Constructively, you cannot prove it: to decide which alternative holds you would have to be able to read the sequence and tell which case it is, and that's exactly what excluded middle would give you. So the real diagnosis is that excluded middle fails, and the intermediate value theorem is just one casualty of that. Your 'roots don't depend continuously on the function' story is the wrong diagnosis."

Elena replied:

(4) "Your binary sequence is a good example of excluded middle failing, and I grant it's a purely logical phenomenon. But it's the wrong diagnosis of the intermediate value theorem, because the weak intermediate value theorem is provable — what fails is only the classical formulation that asks for an actual root. There's a precise sense in which the root really does not exist as a computable function of the data: you can present a function with a sign change on an interval, but no procedure, given the function as an oracle, can output a root. And double-negation existence is not the same as existence. Markov's principle — accepted by the Russian constructivists and rejected by Brouwer — is exactly a bridge principle that converts some double-negation existence statements into genuine existence, which is why the Russian and intuitionist schools disagree about which statements count."
