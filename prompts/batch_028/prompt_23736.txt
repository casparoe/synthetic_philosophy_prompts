Clifford and William James over whether we are ever permitted to believe on less than sufficient evidence, I would like you to referee an exchange I overheard between two of my colleagues at a panel I was covering. They were arguing over whether James's "The Will to Believe" genuinely answers Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" or merely sidesteps it.

The Cliffordian colleague began: (1) Clifford's real claim is epistemic, not moral. The shipowner is wrong because the evidence for the ship's soundness is insufficient; the talk of wronging the crew is just emphasis on the consequences. So the principle is simply never to believe on insufficient evidence, and the believer who decides on passional grounds is still believing on insufficient evidence, merely relabeling the move.

The Jamesian colleague replied: (2) You are assuming that suspending judgment is always a genuine option, and that is exactly what James denies. In a forced option — whether to believe the world is real, whether to trust a person, whether to believe in God — you cannot in fact just wait for more evidence; you must live as if one answer is true, and suspending in practice means living as if the negative answer is true, which is itself a commitment with consequences. Clifford is smuggling in the false premise that there is always a neutral do-nothing option.

The Cliffordian colleague answered: (3) Even granting that one cannot always suspend, that does not confer a right to believe on insufficient evidence. It only shows that one must make a practical decision under uncertainty, which is ordinary and undisputed. One can make a practical decision without thereby being epistemically justified in believing the thing in the strong sense. James conflates being practically committed with being justified in believing. The shipowner, too, had to decide whether to send the ship; making that practical decision did not make his belief that the ship was sound responsible.

The Jamesian colleague came back: (4) Now you are drawing the very distinction James is attacking. You separate practical commitment from belief and say that only the latter requires evidence. But in the cases James cares about, the practical commitment is the belief: if you live your whole life as if God exists, you believe in God, in the only sense that matters. And the commitment is licensed precisely because the option is forced and the stakes of getting it wrong are asymmetric. So the right to believe is really a right to commit, in the direction with the better expected consequences, given that one cannot avoid committing. That is a clarification, not a cop-out.
