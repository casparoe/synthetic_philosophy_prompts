I am preparing a graduate seminar on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I want to be precise about what the result excludes. The theorem is usually presented as showing that, under the usual regularity assumptions, once individual preferences are represented by von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities and the social welfare function satisfies the Pareto condition together with an independence condition, the social welfare function must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. I am puzzled, though, by the continued treatment of prioritarianism as a distinct alternative to utilitarianism. If the theorem permits different positive weights for different individuals, why not simply make those weights welfare-sensitive, so that a unit of utility counts more when it goes to someone who is worse off? That seems to capture the prioritarian concern about the distribution of well-being while remaining within a weighted-sum social welfare function.

My question is whether I am missing a technical point about the scope of Harsanyi's result. In particular, does the theorem's conclusion allow only fixed weights attached to individuals, thereby excluding the kind of state-dependent or concave weighting that prioritarianism requires, or is prioritarianism really just a utilitarian aggregation rule with a more sophisticated choice of weights? I would like to understand why the distinction matters, not merely as a matter of terminology, but as a matter of which aggregation functions are actually licensed by the theorem.
