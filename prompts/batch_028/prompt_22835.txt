im working on a little essay connecting the dollar auction to stuff that happens at my job, and since im not a game theory person i want a second pair of eyes before i post it. heres the draft:

Last quarter our team and a neighboring team were both trying to lock in reserved GPU capacity for training runs. The provider had a fixed pool and we ended up in a bidding war. I kept telling the PM "if we back out now the last 3 months are wasted" and he kept saying "or we overpay and lose the whole project" and we just kept going. We paid more than the capacity was worth to us, and the other team also lost money. I've been reading about the dollar auction — the one where the auctioneer sells a $1 bill but the second-highest bidder has to pay too — and i think what happened to us was exactly that, not just a generic sunk cost story. The difference: in a normal auction, once you're out you walk away with no loss, so stopping is easy. In the dollar auction, once you're the second-highest bidder, every extra cent you bid is locally "rational" because you're trying to shrink your loss, not grow your gain, and there's no clean point where it becomes irrational to bid one more. The trap is that both sides can end up paying more than the prize is worth. In our case the "prize" was the GPU capacity and both teams were paying for it. On top of that there was escalation of commitment: the more we'd already thrown at it (time, internal politics, the PM publicly staking his credibility), the harder it was to stop, even though all of that was sunk. My takeaway is that the dollar auction structure (second place also pays) is what makes the escalation self-reinforcing, and most "sunk cost" stories are missing that structure.

Questions: (1) is my characterization of the dollar auction actually accurate? (2) does the GPU example really have the dollar auction structure, or am i just dressing up a generic sunk cost story? (3) anything im getting wrong about escalation of commitment?
