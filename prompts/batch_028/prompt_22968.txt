I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm working on a long-form essay for a magazine about Leibniz's moral philosophy — specifically, the distinction he draws between metaphysical necessity and what he calls moral necessity. I've been mulling this over for a few weeks, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on the central argument before I commit it to print.

The argument goes like this. Leibniz insists that God is not compelled to create the best of all possible worlds by any metaphysical or logical necessity; to think otherwise, he argues, would be to reduce the divine to something like a machine that simply executes its program. Instead, God is bound by a moral necessity that flows from God's own perfection: because God is perfectly good and perfectly wise, God would always choose the best compossible set of individuals. This moral necessity is supposed to be a genuinely different kind of constraint from metaphysical necessity — it preserves divine freedom in a way that logical compulsion does not. My claim is that this distinction collapses under scrutiny. If God is a necessary being whose nature holds in all possible worlds, and if that nature includes perfect goodness and perfect wisdom, then the fact that God always chooses the best is not a contingent feature of God's choices but a necessary feature of God's nature. In that case, the moral necessity is just metaphysical necessity wearing a different label: there is no possible world in which God exists and chooses a suboptimal outcome, because God's necessary nature rules that out. The moral necessity, on this reading, does no independent work.

What I'd like from you is a critical assessment of whether this objection is actually fair, or whether there's a charitable reading of Leibniz that preserves a genuine gap between the two kinds of necessity — perhaps by appealing to the idea that moral necessity leaves open a "could have" that metaphysical necessity does not, even if that "could have" is never actualized. I'm also interested in whether this specific objection has been made in the secondary literature, and if so, how Leibniz scholars have typically responded. I'm not looking for a summary of Leibniz's theodicy; I want the fine-grained metaphysics of the necessity distinction.
