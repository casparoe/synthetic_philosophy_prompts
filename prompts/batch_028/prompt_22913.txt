I'm preparing a short teaching note for my graduate seminar on two-dimensional semantics, and I'd like you to do the heavy lifting for the section that defends the framework against what I take to be its most persistent internal objection. I've been working through the standard treatment — primary intensions as functions from worlds considered as centers of evaluation, secondary intensions as functions from worlds with the actual world held fixed as the center — and the way the framework handles the classic cases, where "water is H2O" comes out with a contingent primary intension but a necessary secondary intension. The objection I want answered is the one that goes roughly like this: the necessary secondary intension credited to "water" is not really a semantic property of the word "water" but of a rigidified surrogate constructed from it, so that the framework does not explain the necessity of the identity sentence but rather replaces it with a different, already-necessary sentence and thereby begs the question.

Please write a sustained argument defending the legitimacy of the rigidified-term analysis as a genuine semantic resource rather than a question-begging trick. I want the argument to do three specific things. First, it should explain precisely what is shared between the primary intension of the original term and the primary intension of its rigidified surrogate, and why that shared primary intension is what preserves the a posteriori character of "water is H2O" even though the secondary intension is necessary. Second, it should confront the worry that rigidification is an external, speaker-independent operation that the framework simply stipulates rather than derives from anything in the meaning of the expression. Third, it should say what the defender owes to the critic who insists that, on this account, "water is H2O" and "water is the watery stuff of the actual world" are not the same sentence at all, and that the necessity has been imported by a change of subject. I'd like the argument to stay philosophically precise about the two dimensions and not to retreat into the claim that the distinction between epistemic and metaphysical two-dimensionalism dissolves the problem.
