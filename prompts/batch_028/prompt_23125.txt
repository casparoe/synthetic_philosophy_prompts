I sit on my university's IACUC (the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, for the uninitiated — though on this commitee everyone knows what it is, and it is as tedious as you'd imagine). We have a protocol in front of us this week that would house a species of wild-caught mouse, one that in the wild digs extensive burrow systems, in a standard cage with paper bedding. The PI's welfare justification is that the animals show no overt signs of distress and are gainning weight just fine, so the digging behavior is, in her words, "not needed" in captivity. A behavioral ecologist on the committee objects, saying the digging was selected for (shelter, predator avoidance, thermoregulation) and that suppressing it is a welfare deficit even if the animal is physically healthy. The committee is split, and the deeper disagreement is about what "function" means: the PI's side thinks a behavior's function is what it currently contributes to the animal's welfare in its actual environment (the causal-role sort of account), while the ecologist thinks function is historical, what the trait was selected for (the etiological account).

This came up in my philosophy of biology seminar last week and I've been stuck on one case since: the appendix, or a trait like it, that was clearly selected for one thing but now causes harm. I can't figure out which account handles that better, and I'm worried that whichever standard we pick, there's a version of this case that breaks it. I don't want to just paper over that in the memo.

Please draft the committee's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a specific course of action and a time frame, and say which account of biological function we should codify as our standard for assessing "normal" behavior going forward. I want the reasoning to engage the appendix-type case explicitly, and to give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses, not a restatement of the winning side's data.
