I got into a massive argument with my friend last night about Russell's theory of descriptions and I honestly still don't feel like I've got the point. Here's the passage from On Denoting (1905) that I keep coming back to:

"Suppose now we wish to interpret the proposition, "I met a man." If this is true, I met some definite man; but that is not what I affirm. What I affirm is, according to the theory I advocate: " 'I met x, and x is human' is not always false." Generally, defining the class of men as the class of objects having the predicate human, we say that: "C (a man)" means " 'C (x) and x is human' is not always false." This leaves "a man," by itself, wholly destitute of meaning, but gives a meaning to every proposition in whose verbal expression "a man" occurs."

His whole objection is that this is just self-contradictory: if "a man" is "wholly destitute of meaning" on its own, how can any sentence containing it have a meaning at all? He keep insisting Russell is just smugling a referent back in through the back door. Can you walk me through what Russell is actually doing here, and specifically how the variable x is supposed to carry the meaning when the phrase itself supposedly doesn't have any? I want to be able to answer his objection properly instead of just hand-waving.
