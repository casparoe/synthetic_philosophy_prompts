I cover science and ideas for a regional paper, and I keep coming back to a puzzle in experimental philosophy that I think would make for genuinely strange fiction. The basic phenomenon is the so-called Knobe effect: ask people whether an executive who knowingly and indifferently brought on a harmful side effect acted intentionally, and most say yes; change a word so that the identical foreseen side effect is beneficial, and most now say no. The asymmetry is robust, and the proposed explanations range from the mundane (people are just letting their moral verdicts leak into their description of the action) to the more exotic. What I find most arresting, though, is a lesser-known twist on the same setup: the effect is not only about the agent's own action but also about what a bystander *knows* another person will bring about. When an observer is told a colleague's plan will bring about a bad outcome, people are more likely to judge that the observer *knew* the outcome would follow than when the colleague's foreseen outcome is good. In other words, the moral valence of a foreseen event seems to contaminate whether we even credit the foresight in the first place.

I'd like you to write a short story (roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words) that dramatizes this second, bystander-side phenomenon rather than the standard version. I have a setting in mind: a near-future actuarial or risk-assessment office where a junior analyst is trained to forecast the outcomes of corporate decisions, and the institution grades her partly on how "accurate" her predictions are. The story should turn on the moment when a prediction that everyone privately regards as obviously true is, for purely moral reasons, quietly refused credit by the people who are supposed to be scoring her — and on the moment when she notices that the same refusal is being applied to her own predictions in a way that is not actually about accuracy. I want the piece to make the reader feel the asymmetry from the inside (the way it *feels* to have a correct, unwanted forecast disbelieved) while staying faithful to the structure of the effect: the contamination should run specifically from the badness of the predicted outcome into the willingness to count the prediction as real, not from any genuine difference in how confident or careful the analyst was.

A few constraints that matter to me. Please do not have any character lecture the reader about the Knobe effect by name or recite the experimental vignette; the mechanism should be legible only through what happens to the characters. Keep the cast small, and let the institutional incentives (promotion, the scoring rubric, the office's stated value of "intellectual honesty") do the work of pressure. I'd like an ending that is unsettling rather than resolved — the analyst should understand, by the last page, that the problem is not that her forecasts are wrong but that the category of "prediction" is being used as a tool, and she should be left deciding what to do with that understanding without the story telling her the decision is the right one.
