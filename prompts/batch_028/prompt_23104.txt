I chair my university's research ethics board, and we've just received a proposal from a legal-psychology lab. The lab wants to survey employees of two companies: one whose CEO, in announcing a merger, said he was "deeply concerned" about the layoffs it would cause, and one whose CEO, in announcing a restructuring, said he was "proud" of the new scholarship fund it would create. Respondents would be shown each CEO's statement and asked whether the CEO "intended" the layoffs, or "intended" the scholarship. The lab says the results will help a state legislature draft a statute distinguishing intentional from merely foreseen harms in corporate liability, and a senator is waiting on their findings. Half the board thinks this is a straightforward survey of ordinary language. The other half, including me, worries that the very question — "did she intend this bad thing?" — pushes respondents toward "yes" in ways that "did he intend this good thing?" does not, so that the answers track the valence of the outcome rather than anything in the CEO's mind, and that the statute would be built on a measurement artifact. Draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a clear bottom line, reasoning the researchers could actually respond to, and a fair account of the strongest reason to run the study as proposed.
