I've been re-reading Hume's Of Miracles (Enquiry, section X) and I keep hitting a wall on the weighing part. Here's the bit: "All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

The thing that's bugging me is the "deduct the smaller number from the greater" rule. He's basically saying you count instances on each side and just subtract? But that's not how probability actually works, right — you're supposed to be comparing and multiplying likelihood ratios, not adding and subtracting raw counts of instances. So is this balancing rule actually a legit way to get the "exact force of the superior evidence," or is it just a heuristic that happens to work for his toy examples (100 vs 50, 100 vs 1) but would break down for the actual miracles case, where the "instances" on the two sides aren't realy comparable counts of the same kind of event? I'd like help thinking through whether the counting move is doing real work in his argument or quietly smuggling in the conclusion.
