I spent about thirty years as an experimental psychologist running vision studies, most of it on how a subject's expectations and the way you word your instructions end up shaping what they report seeing, and it's only since retiring that I've started poking around in the philosophy of perception. So when I ran into this whole debate over "cognitive penetration" I had to be honest with myself and say it reads, to me, like philosophers have finally attached a fancy label to the exact thing I spent my whole career trying to controll for — plain expectation effects, or what the methodologists call confirmation bias, where the participant's beliefs about what they ought to see leak into their report. That's a real nuisance, sure, but it's a more or less solved methodological problem, which is why I'm a bit baffled that anyone is treating cognitive penetration as a live philosophical controversy rather than a lab-hygiene issue.

So here's where I'm stuck. If cognitive penetration is basically just confirmation bias operating inside the perceptual system, then the remedy ought to be the same one we always used: tight double-blind controls, randomised stimulus ordering, a neutral experimenter, that sort of thing. What's the best way to design a study that definitly rules out cognitive penetration, and more to the point, why hasn't this just been done already and the book closed?
