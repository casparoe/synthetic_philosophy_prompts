I've been thinking about the handicap principle — the idea that animals use costly displays, like a peacock's tail, to honestly signal their quality to potential mates. The basic logic makes sense to me: if the display is expensive to produce, only a high-quality animal can afford it, so the display becomes a trustworthy signal of quality. But I'm stuck on a detail. Why does the cost of the display have to differ depending on the animal's quality? Couldn't you just have a display that costs the same amount of energy for every male? If growing a big tail is uniformly expensive, wouldn't that still be a good signal, since only a healthy male could pull it off? Or is there something about a uniform cost that breaks the signaling, and if so, what exactly is doing the work in making the cost an honest indicator?
