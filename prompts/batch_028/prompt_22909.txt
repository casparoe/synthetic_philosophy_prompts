I'm teaching a seminar on Rousseau's political aesthetics and I keep getting stuck on one thing. His case against theater, in the "Social Contract" and in his letter to Marmontel, seems to lean a lot on teh theater turning citizens into spectators — the audience role as corrosive to the general will, because it cultivates a detached, comparative, self-displaying mentality. But a lot of contemporary participatory theater traditions (the kind where the audience is explicitly invited to break the fourth wall, intervene in the scene, and collectively steer the action) are built on dissolving exactly that spectator/performer distinction. So how would Rousseau have reacted to theater like that? Does his anti-theatricalism depend on the audience being passive, or does he have a deeper objection to theatrical representation itself — say, to the very idea of people acting out roles they don't actually believe in — that participatory formats dont dissolve? I'd like you to steelman both readings and give me a verdict on which is more defensible from his arguments.
