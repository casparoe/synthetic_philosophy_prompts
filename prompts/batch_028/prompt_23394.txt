I've been reading Kierkegaard on my own lately, mostly the bits where he goes off on the press and "the public," and it's the part I keep getting stuck on, so I brought it up at dinner and two of my friends ended up going at each other. I only asked the dumb question, basically "is his whole point just that newspapers are bad," and that's when it blew up. One of them, let's call her Mara, said his argument is self-defeating: he keeps calling the public a phantom, "an all-embracing something which is nothing," an abstraction that isn't really a thing, and then he acts like it's some real agent out there leveling everyone down, gossiping, setting the press loose on anyone who stands out. She said you can't hold a nonexistent thing responsible for anything, so if the public isn't a real person it can't be the one doing the leveling, and the whole critique is aimed at air. The other one, Dev, said that's the opposite of what Kierkegaard means. He said Kierkegaard never treats the public as a real person; the point is that it's a fiction people mistake for a real person, and the sickness is in the individuals who stop being themselves and turn into third parties who act as if they're the public. So the target isn't a ghost, it's the shared self-deception, and a shared illusion is still real in the way that matters.

Then Mara came back and said, fine, even if it's a shared illusion, it's just a bunch of beliefs held by individuals, so the real unit is the individual, and all his talk of "the public" leveling or barking at people is a fancy metaphor for individuals immitating each other. She said you could delete the word public out of the whole essay and just describe a crowd of people copying each other and you'd lose nothing. Dev said no, you'd lose the one thing that's actually distinctive, which is that it's distributed — no single person is doing the leveling, which is why its so hard to pin on anyone and so hard to stop. He pointed to where Kierkegaard says the public is "a host, more numerous than all the peoples together," and said that's not a metaphor, that's a real feature: the whole is doing something none of the parts is doing, and you can't reduce a thing that's bigger than its parts back to the parts.

I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, and I'm not even sure the question is "which of them is right" or "is the whole framing wrong." Can you walk through their exchange and tell me who's right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I don't want you to just pick a side, I want the actual reasoning for each of their moves.
