I'm a PhD student in philosophy of art, and I keep tripping over the boundary between aesthetic testimony and what I'd call aesthetic persuasion. I want to write a section arguing that some aesthetic testimony is not just predictive testimony ("you will like this") nor a mere report of the testifier's feelings ("I find this beautiful"), but a kind of perceptual-interpretive transmission that can give the hearer a first-personally actionable reason to attend to the work in a certain way. The case I have in mind is when a knowledgeable person says, roughly, that the dissonance in the final bars isn't a mistake but is what makes the reprise feel like a failed recovery, and the listener, even before re-listening, can use that to organize their perception.

I want the argument to say that this is more like testimony about stable, response-dependent features of the object than like moral testimony, because the relevant competence is partly constitutive of the aesthetic practice rather than a background normative commitment that the hearer may have to endorse. But please don't let it slide into objective beauty, and do address the worry that if I haven't had the aesthetic experience myself, I'm only getting a tip, not knowledge. Keep it argumentative and focused, and assume I already know the basic literature on testimony.
