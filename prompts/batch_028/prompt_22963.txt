I've been going back and forth with a friend about the causal exclusion argument, and we keep conflating two worries that I think are actually distinct, so I'd like a careful comparison. On the one hand there is the standard exclusion argument: given the causal closure (or near-closure) of the physical domain, the causal completeness of the physical, and the assumption that a given mental property is not numerically identical to the physical property that realizes it, any causal role attributed to the mental property is screened off by the sufficiency of the physical cause; the mental property is therefore either epiphenomenal or must be reduced to the physical. As I understand the argument, this version leans crucially on a no-overdetermination or no-redundant-causation premise to block the option that the mental and the physical are simply two distinct sufficient causes of the same effect. On the other hand there is a related but, I think, logically different worry, often labelled the causal-redundancy or overdetermination argument: even granting that the mental property is not strictly excluded by closure, it seems causally superfluous, because the physical cause is already sufficient to produce the effect, so the mental cause, if it does any work at all, is doing work that was already guaranteed.

Could you compare these two arguments in detail? In particular: (1) do they really rest on the same closure premise, or does the redundancy argument have a different logical structure — for instance, does it depend on a premise about the sufficiency of the physical cause rather than on the closure of the physical domain, and does it need the no-overdetermination premise at all? (2) Is the redundancy argument a genuine, independent argument against non-reductive physicalism, or is it just the exclusion argument repackaged in different language? (3) If a non-reductive physicalist concedes the redundancy but denies the exclusion — for example by appealing to a constitutive or causal-role account on which the mental property does not efficiently cause the effect in the same way the physical property does, but rather constitutes or realizes the conditions under which the physical cause operates — does that actually defuse the redundancy worry, or does it merely shift it to a different level of description? I would appreciate a structured answer that keeps the logical form of each argument separate from the metaphysical commitments it carries, and that says something about which of the two arguments a non-reductive physicalist has the harder time answering.
