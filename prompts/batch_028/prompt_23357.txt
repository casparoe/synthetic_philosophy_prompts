I am preparing a short paper for a conference session on Indian epistemology, and I would be grateful for critical comments on a draft of its central section. The paper takes as its subject the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, the many-sidedness of reality, and the related practice of syādvāda, predication with conditions, as these are developed in the canonical Jain texts and in the sevenfold scheme of predication, the saptabhaṅgī. My students in my comparative epistemology seminar tend to flatten the doctrine into a bland pluralism, so the paper's aim is to argue that anekāntavāda is best read as a species of realist pluralism rather than as a form of relativism: the many sides are objective features of the object, not merely projections of the perceiver, and the doctrine's purpose is to correct partiality, not to license it.

Here is the draft section in question:

The objection most often pressed against anekāntavāda is that it is self-undermining. If every affirmation is true only in some respect, then the doctrine's own claim that reality is many-sided is true only in some respect, and so it cannot be invoked as a universal interpretive rule. I want to argue that this objection dissolves once one notes that the doctrine is itself offered as a conditioned statement: it is not that reality is many-sided simpliciter, but that, in the respect in which it is an object of finite cognition, it admits of multiple genuine predications. The doctrine thereby exempts itself from the charge without circularity, since the condition that applies to it is the very condition it describes. A second objection is that the doctrine is incoherent: how can a single substance, say the soul, be both eternal and non-eternal? I answer that the predicates are not conjoined simpliciter but are indexed to distinct aspects of the object, and the saptabhaṅgī is the formal apparatus for keeping the indexation explicit. On this reading, the doctrine is a robust realism about aspects, and its apparent tolerance is the price of refusing to privilege any one aspect as the whole.

I would welcome comments on three points in particular. First, on accuracy: is my characterization of the self-refutation objection and its dissolution faithful to how the Jain tradition itself handles this difficulty, or am I smuggling in a contemporary move that the traditional texts do not make? Second, on the realism-versus-relativism distinction: I suspect that my claim that anekāntavāda is realist may be doing more work than I have shown, and I would be interested in whether there is a sharper diagnostic I could deploy, or a version of the relativist reading that I have not fairly represented. Third, on the aspect-indexation answer to the coexistence of contrary predicates: I am not fully satisfied that it explains what it is for a property to hold of an object only in a given aspect, and I would like suggestions for how to develop that idea or for whether it is better to treat the sevenfold scheme as a logic of truth values rather than as a metaphysics of aspects. More generally, please flag any passage where the argument is weaker than it appears, and tell me whether the section as written would persuade a reader hostile to the doctrine.
