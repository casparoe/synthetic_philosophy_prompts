I'm a novelist currently assembling a short-story cycle in which each piece dramatizes a single, fairly technical idea from decision theory and the philosophy of mind, and the one I keep failing to land is the piece on superrationality. A friend who works in formal epistemology has been badgering me to stop treating it as a cute thought experiment about "you versus a copy of you" and to actually make the reader experience the shift in reasoning, so I'd like to try again with a fresh scenario.

Could you write me a short story of roughly fifteen to twenty-five hundred words in which the protagonist, a mid-level actuary aboard a near-future deep-space freighter, is asked to co-sign a joint wager with a passenger she has never met, and only near the end does the reader learn that the passenger is a verified cognitive copy of the protagonist, instantiated minutes earlier from her own deliberation architecture? I want the piece to let the reader feel the difference between the ordinary game-theoretic instinct ("I can't trust her, so I should defect") and the superrational move, in which the protagonist recognizes that because the other agent is running the same reasoning process, her single choice effectively determines both outcomes. Please keep the technical exposition to a minimum, carry the argument through the character's interiority and the pressure of the scene rather than through explanation, and close on a concrete image instead of a stated moral. I'm after a taut, close-third-person register, in the manner of science fiction that takes its premise seriously rather than as a parlor puzzle.
