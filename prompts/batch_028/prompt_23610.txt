Quick background on me: I spent thirty years as an epidemiologist, so my whole professional life was arguing with people about what the data actually show, and I figured that made me a decent person to sit on the board of our family foundation, which decides where to send a few million dollars a year into global health research. I'm retired now and have recently, to my grandchildren's great amusement, gotten into philosophy, and one idea I keep turning over is this thing I read about where you stop voting on what to do and instead let a prediction market tell you which option will actually turn out best, then just fund that one. It sounds crazy until you think about it, and our new program director has now proposed we do a scaled-down version of exactly this for next year's grant cycle.

Here's the concrete proposal. Instead of the board deliberating and voting on which of the three or four candidate grants to fund, we'd run a little internal market where our grant officers and a handful of outside forcasters bet points on which grant will avert the most disease over the next five years, and we fund whatever comes out on top, full stop. I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's board meeting and I'm genuinly stuck. On the one hand the logic is seductive: we keep pretending our deliberations are more informed than they are, nine retired people arguing in a conference room is not obviously smarter than a market of people who have skin in the game, and the director makes a good point that we'd at least be forcing everyone to put a number on their uncertainty. On the other hand something is wrong and I can't pin it down. I keep thinking that predicting which grant will work is a completely different and much harder problem than choosing which one we ought to fund, and I'm not even sure "averts the most disease in five years" is the thing we actually care about, or that the people running the market are neutral when two of them wrote the grant proposals they're betting on.

Can you draft the memo I'll send out? I need a clear bottom line at the top — adopt, reject, or adopt with specific conditions — followed by the reasoning, and I'd like it to take the strongest version of the director's case seriously rather than just dunking on prediction markets, because a couple of my boardmates actually like the idea and I don't want to lose them. If there's a version of this that keeps what's genuinely good about the proposal (I think there is) while fixing what's broken, I'd rather you recommend that than a flat no.
