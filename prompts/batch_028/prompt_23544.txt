Exam question:
Harry Frankfurt's 1969 paper "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" is usually treated as a counterexample to the Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP). (a) State PAP and explain how Frankfurt's case is supposed to refute it. (b) What is one major objection to Frankfurt's case, and how does it go? (c) How did Frankfurt respond to the objection by revising PAP?

Student's answer:
PAP holds that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise. Frankfurt's case is a counterexample to this principle. Black wants Jones to perform a certain action A. Black is prepared to intervene, if necessary, to make sure that Jones does A, but he waits to see whether Jones will do A on his own. In the actual course of events, Jones does A of his own accord, for his own reasons, and Black never has to intervene. Frankfurt's claim is that Jones is fully morally responsible for doing A, since he did it for his own reasons; yet Jones could not have done otherwise, because had he been about to do anything other than A, Black would have intervened to prevent it. So Jones is responsible for an action that he could not have avoided, which is exactly what PAP says cannot happen. Frankfurt's case is therefore a direct argument for compatibilism, since it shows that moral responsibility is compatible with causal determinism.

A major objection to the case is the two-horned dilemma. On the deterministic horn, if determinism is true, then Jones's decision to do A is fully determined by prior causes, and Black's device plays no causal role at all; on this view, Jones is not responsible for A after all, since his decision was never really up to him. On the indeterministic horn, if the process by which Jones decides is indeterministic, then Jones genuinely could have done otherwise, and so PAP is vindicated in the case. Either way, Frankfurt's case fails to refute PAP.

Frankfurt responds by revising PAP. His revised principle is that a person is not morally responsible for what she did if she could not have done otherwise. This revised principle preserves the intuition about Jones, since Jones did A for his own reasons, and it also explains why a coerced agent is not responsible, since the coerced agent acts in virtue of her inability to do otherwise.
