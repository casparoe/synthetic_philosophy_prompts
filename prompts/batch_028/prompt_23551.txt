I'm on the curatorial comittee of a mid-size museum, and we've got a tricky one on the table for next month's meeting. We have a 17th-century painting that's been in our collection for about forty years, long displayed and catalogued as an original by a major Dutch master. Last spring a conservator running routine condition checks found a pigment in the underdrawing that wasn't available until the early 1900s, and a follow-up provenance dig turned up a 1920s auction listing showing the canvas had passed through a known forger's studio. So the working hypothesis now is that it's a period-style forgery made in the 1920s, not a 17th-century original.

The committee is split: the director wants to keep it on view with a discreet "attribution under review" label, the registrar is pushing to return it to the estate we bought it from on fraud grounds, and a couple of us think it's still a genuinely good painting that deserves to be shown as what it actually is. I was talking this through with a colleague who does aesthetics, and she keeps coming back to a distinction I find hard to pin down — the difference between a forgery and an honest copy, and whether that difference actually matters for what the museum owes the public. I'd like the memo to work through that.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (labeling, display, and whether to return or keep) with a time frame, and it should actually engage the hard conceptual questions — like whether a forgery can count as a work of art at all versus just being a "fake," what (if any) of the original's value survives in a good forgery, and why the forgery/copy distinction matters here — rather than just restating the legal or policy options. Please treat the director's position seriously even if you end up disagreeing with it.
