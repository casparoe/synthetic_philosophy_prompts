I am a novelist, and I have been turning a problem over in my mind for several weeks because it keeps intruding on the book I am writing, about a woman who spends her retirement consuming ever more elaborate entertainments and finding that none of them satisfy her. The problem is this. In her *Discourse on Happiness*, Émilie du Châtelet holds that pleasure is the good, but she treats the pleasures of the senses and the heart as dependent on health and fortune, while calling the love of study an inexhaustible source of pleasure and a sure resource against misfortune; and she goes on to insist that susceptibility to illusion is itself one of the great engines of happiness, so that we owe most of our pleasures to illusion, even though she also maintains that we ought always to seek truth and obey reason. I would like you to put yourself in her position and predict, as specifically as you can, how she would react to two developments that postdate her by centuries.

The first is the modern abundance of effortless, deliberately crafted, immersive pleasure — the endless streams of fiction, spectacle, and simulation that a person can now consume with almost no effort and without ever having to leave the room. The second is the now well-documented finding that people adapt to such pleasures and return to a baseline of contentment, so that the acquisition of ever more of them does not, on the whole, make them happier. I want you to work through, in her own terms, whether she would regard this modern condition as a genuine advance in human happiness or as a peculiar and perhaps dangerous form of impoverishment, and I am especially interested in the tension inside her own view: how does her insistence that illusion is indispensable to happiness sit with her insistence on truth and reason, and does the soul/body distinction give her a principled way to say which of our modern pleasures are the kind that wear out and which are the kind that deepen? Please reason from the actual commitments of her theory rather than from a general picture of what an eighteenth-century moralist might be expected to say, and flag wherever her own account seems to strain or to leave you without a clear answer.
