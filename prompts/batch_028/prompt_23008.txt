Two friends of mine, both of whom read a lot of philosophy of biology, got into a long argument about what determines a biological trait's function. I wasn't involved, but I was asked to referee and I couldn't. Here's the gist of their exchange:

(1) Anna said the proper function of a trait is what it was selected for. The heart's function is to pump blood because hearts were selected for pumping blood. That's what makes malfunction intelligible: a heart "malfunctions" when it fails to pump blood, even if it's doing something else at the time.

(2) Ben replied that this gets the direction of explanation backwards. A trait's function is what it currently does—its causal role in the organism right now. The heart's function is to pump blood because that's what it actually does. Selection history is a fact about how the trait got here, not a fact about what the trait is for.

(3) Anna pushed back with vestigial traits. The human appendix is the standard case: it's widely called vestigial, yet on Ben's account it has whatever trivial current function it happens to have, with no "used to be for" left to point to. Her account captures the point: it was selected for some job, it no longer does that job, so it's a vestige. Ben's "what it does now" can't explain why we call it vestigial at all.

(4) Ben said his account handles that fine. The appendix's current causal role is whatever it happens to be—maybe a minor role in the gut microbiome, maybe nothing. We call it vestigial because the selection pressure on it is weak, not because of some design history. "Vestigial" is a statement about current selection, not about a blueprint.

(5) Anna then brought up exaptation. Feathers are generally thought to have evolved first for insulation, and only later came to be used for flight. On her account, the function of feathers is insulation, and flight is an exaptation—a repurposing. That's the explanatory point: flight evolved because feathers happened to already be there. On Ben's account, the function of feathers is flight, because that's what they currently do, and the insulation origin is just a fact about evolutionary history, not a "function" fact.

(6) Ben conceded the tool analogy cuts both ways, and said a trait's function is what it's used for now, not what it was originally made for—just like a hammer that was originally a paperweight. He added that Anna's "design" is really just a metaphor for selection, and selection is an ongoing process, so the "blueprint" is constantly being updated by what the trait currently does. The difference between them is thinner than it looks.

(7) Anna replied that selection can lag. A trait can keep doing its old job long after selection has stopped acting on it, and we still say it has that function. Ben's "continuously updated blueprint" would say it loses its function as soon as selection stops, but we'd still say it has a function.
