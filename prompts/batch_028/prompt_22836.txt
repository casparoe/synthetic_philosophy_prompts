I retired from condensed matter physics about two years ago and I've been filling the time with philosophy of science, which is turning out to be a lot more confusing than I expected. I keep running into this thing called structural realism and I think I finally get it, but then I suspect I'm wrong about the important part. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that when one theory replaces another — like when the old story about the ether and light waves gave way to quantum electrodynamics — the specific entities and the story about what the stuff is keep changing, but the mathematical structure, the equations and the relations, seem to carry over. So the structural realist says: don't be too comitted to the entities, just commit to the structure, because that's the part with the track record of surviving. That lines up with the argument I saw that our past successful theories were full of unobservable entities that turned out to be false, so we have no reason to trust the entities in our current theories either.

But here's where I get stuck, and I think I might be making a mistake: if you're only willing to commit to the structure and not to what the world is actually like, isn't that basically instrumentalism by another name? You're just saying the math is a useful map and refusing to say it's true of reality, which is exactly what the instrumentalist says. So what's the point of the distinction? Is structural realism just anti-realism for people who don't want to admit it, or is there some real commitment to the world being like the structure that I'm missing? I was at a dinner with a few old grad students from the physics department and one of them, who'd gone into philosophy, kept bringing it up and I couldn't answer him, so I'd really like someone to straghten this out for me. Right now the two views feel identical and I can't tell which part I'm supposed to be taking seriously.
