I sit on the taxonomic review panel that maintains our natural-history museum's species checklist, and we've been asked to adjudicate a proposal to split one catalogued songbird into two species. Here are the facts as I understand them. The bird occupies a continuous range across a broad, unbroken ecoregion — there is no mountain range, river, or other barrier separating the populations. In the middle of the range there is a narrow hybrid zone where two distinct forms meet and interbreed at low frequency. The two forms differ in plumage coloration (a conspicuous difference that appears to be under directional selection) and at a small number of genetic loci that are fixed for different alleles between the forms. Hybrid offspring are viable and fertile, so the two forms are not reproductively isolated; there is gene flow across the zone, though it is reduced. A recent phylogenomic analysis recovers the two forms as sister clades, but the internal branch is short and the statistical support for their monophyly is shallow, so the phylogenetic signal is weak. The proposing taxonomist wants the split, arguing that the two forms are each diagnosable and each corresponds to a monophyletic clade. Our long-time curator objects, arguing that because the populations interbreed and form a single continuous gene pool they are one species, and that splitting on the basis of a plumage difference and a handful of fixed loci is the kind of "splitomania" that will fragment our checklist into an unmanageable proliferation of names.

I need you to draft the panel's written decision memo: recognize the split, reject it, or recognize it with conditions (for example, provisional recognition pending further phylogenomic sampling). Whatever you recommend, give reasons the losing side could actually respond to, and weigh the strongest consideration on that side honestly rather than dismissing it. I'd also like the memo to take seriously the asymmetry in the costs of error — what we lose if we split a single real species versus what we lose if we lump two real species into one — because I suspect that cost structure matters as much as the taxonomy itself, and I want it treated as a first-class input to the decision rather than a footnote.

The reason I'm asking for more than a plumage-and-loci summary is that I've been reading the philosophy-of-biology literature on the species problem, and I keep coming back to the thought that most of this disagreement is really about what a "species" is — whether it is best understood as a kind, as a clade, as an individual (a concrete particular with a career), or as a node in a phylogeny — and that whichever of those you pick seems to do most of the work in the decision. I'd like the memo to make that connection explicit: which concept of species is doing the work in the recommendation, why that concept is the right one to apply to a museum checklist in particular, and where the other concepts would have led to a different answer.
