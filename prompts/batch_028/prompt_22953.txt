I'm a junior in high school, and I help our teacher grade short responses for a small philosophy seminar we run after school. This week we read Harry Frankfurt's 1969 paper "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility," and I've been asked to give a second opinion on one student's answer before the teacher finalizes the grade. I'd like you to act as a careful grader: assign the response a numeric grade out of 10, and then work through it claim by claim, noting which claims are accurate, which are imprecise, and which are wrong, with a brief justification for each. I especially want you to be strict about inferential steps that don't follow and about conflations of distinct theses, not just about flat factual errors, and I'd like your final verdict to be a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. Below is the question I posed and the student's response, reproduced verbatim.

Question: In the standard version of the case from Frankfurt's 1969 paper, an agent (Jones) decides to vote for one of two candidates, and an intervener (Black) who would ensure that Jones votes a certain way, but only if necessary, and who in fact never intervenes. State the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP), and explain why Frankfurt thinks this case is a counterexample to it.

Student's response: PAP is the principle that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if he could have done otherwise. In Frankfurt's case, Jones decides on his own to vote for candidate A, while Black, who would have intervened had Jones shown any sign of voting for B, does nothing. Frankfurt's point is that Jones is still fully responsible for his vote, even though he could not have done otherwise, because Black's presence would have prevented any deviation. So the case shows that responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise, and PAP, as a necessary condition on responsibility, is false. One objection to this conclusion is that Jones might still have had trivial alternative possibilities—for instance, he could have formed a different intention—and so the case does not genuinely eliminate all alternatives. But because Frankfurt's case shows that Jones is responsible even though he could not have done otherwise, PAP is false, and therefore compatibilism is established, since the case shows that responsibility is compatible with the absence of alternative possibilities. Frankfurt's argument depends on determinism's being true, because if the world were not deterministic, Jones would have been able to do otherwise, and the case would not show what Frankfurt wants it to show; the case is therefore really an argument from determinism.
