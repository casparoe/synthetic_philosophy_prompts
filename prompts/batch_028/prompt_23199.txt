The question I'd put on the exam was a Pascal's mugging setup: a stranger on the street holds up a card and says, "Give me five dollars and with probability one in a million I'll transfer you ten million — I have the means, I just need the seed money." Student 1 said the expected value is clearly positive, ten million times one in a million is ten, which beats the five dollar cost, so you pay; the math doesn't care that the whole thing sounds insane. Student 2 pushed back and said the whole thing falls apart on the probability, because that one-in-a-million is the mugger's number, not yours, and your actual credence that some random stranger can actually wire you ten million dollars is basically zero, so the real expected value is negative and you're just handing over cash. Student 1 replied that you can always assign some tiny credence to a wild claim, you can't just round it to zero, and if that tiny credence times the payoff still beats the cost then you should pay on exactly the same logic, so the only way to get the "don't pay" answer is to invent an arbitrary threshold below which you refuse to act no matter how big the prize. Student 2 said that's not arbitrary at all, it's the only thing standing between you and getting drained, because if you'll shell out five dollars for any claim with a big enough payoff, a clever person can just keep making you offers until your broke, so there has to be a point where a probability is too small to act on regardless of the stakes.

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall?
