I spent most of my career as a chemist, and I only got into reading philosophy a couple of years ago, so forgive me if I'm a bit green on this. It struck me how odd it is that in every field I've ever worked in, deferring to a competent expert is just how you get knowledge — I never measured a half-life myself, I trusted the literature. So I was thrown when I ran into the whole 'moral testimony' debate, where people seriously argue that you can't legitimately pick up a moral fact the way you pick up any other fact.

Two friends of mine got into it at a barbecue over whether it's OK to just take a bioethicist's word on a hard case. One said no, you can't, because to trust a moral expert you'd first have to know they're morally reliable, and judging that already requires you to have done the independent moral reasoning you were trying to avoid — so it's circular, or it just just lets you off the hook. The other said that's way too strong, that you can check an expert's reliability with plain non-moral evidence — their track record, whether they're careful, well-informed, and free of the obvious biases — exactly like you'd vet a structural engineer without being one yourself. The first one replied that the engineer case is a false analogy, because 'the engineer is reliable' is a factual claim you can verify with measurements, whereas 'the ethicist is morally reliable' is itself a moral claim, so you can never step outside morality to check it. The second said you can step outside it in practice, by looking at empirical facts about the person, and that demanding an independent moral verdict before you're allowed to trust anyone would make all expert deference impossible, not just moral.

I honeslty couldn't tell who was making the better case. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall — and also flag anything both of them are missing?
