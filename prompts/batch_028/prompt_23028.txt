I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last week I was at a dinner after decision-theory panel where two of the attendees got into a bit of a row about Ulysses contracts — the Odysseus thing, where you bind yourself in advance to a decision so your weaker future self can't talk you out of it. A grad student was defending precommitment pretty hard: her line was that your earlier self, who had a clearer view of your long-term goals, rationally precommitted to overcome a known future weakness, so the commitment is binding and your future self should just follow it, even if she now wants to break it — otherwise, she said, you could never overcome any weakness, because you'd always cave to the momentary temptation. A more senior guy pushed back and said that assumes your future self's preference is a "mistake," but in most real cases (dieting, saving, not checking your phone) it's a genuine preference, maybe based on new information, so by precommitting your earlier self is just dictating to a future self she cant fully predict — a kind of "tyranny of the past self." The grad student conceded that new information matters, but pointed out that the classic Ulysses case is precisely about a known, predictable weakness (Odysseus knows he'll want to listen to the sirens and that it would get him killed), so in that case the precommitment is clearly rational. The senior guy said that's a special, extreme case, and in the ordinary case the future self's preference isn't a mistake, so the Ulysses contract is much more debatable. They didn't end up agreeing. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And if there's something both of them are missing, let me know.
