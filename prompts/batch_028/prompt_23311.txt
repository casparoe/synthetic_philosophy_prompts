I spent thirty-eight years as a field biologist, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy, which has lately led me, rather unexpectedly, into the literature on fairness in machine learning. I have been trying to make sense of the debate over "fairness" in risk scores, and I find myself stuck on a particular family of impossibility results that I believe lies at the center of the controversy. The setup, as I understand it, is this: a predictive score, say for re-offending, is applied to two demographic groups whose base rates of the outcome differ. One family of criteria, usually called calibration or predictive parity, requires that within each group the score correctly predicts the average outcome, so that people who receive a score of 0.7 should, on average, have a 70 percent probability of the outcome, in both groups. Another family, usually called equalized odds, or in its weaker form equal opportunity, requires that the false-positive and false-negative rates be the same across the two groups. A line of results by Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, and by Chouldechova, shows that when the base rates differ, these two sets of requirements cannot both be satisfied by any non-trivial score; the only scores that satisfy both are degenerate ones, such as a constant score or one that merely reads off group membership. Chouldechova's analysis of the COMPAS recidivism instrument was, as I understand it, an empirical instantiation of exactly this tension, and it is the point at which the ProPublica and vendor disputes, to my eye, became a dispute about definitions rather than about data.

What I would like you to do is write an essay on the following controversy: do these impossibility results show that algorithmic fairness is unattainable in some real and important sense, or do they show only that the word "fair" had been used to mean two incompatible things, and that once the incompatibility is made explicit the controversy largely dissolves into a choice of which criterion an institution should adopt? Please weigh the main arguments on both sides, including the objection that the impossibility is merely a definitional tautology, no more alarming than the fact that a number cannot be both even and odd, and the reply that the tautology is doing real work by pinning down the concrete cost of each choice, such as which group bears the false positives and which bears the false negatives. Do not assume I have read the underlying papers; explain the technical steps in plain terms, and be explicit about the assumptions under which each side's argument holds. Finally, do not end on a diplomatic "it depends." Reach a definite verdict on which side of the debate is stronger, justify it, and say what follows for an institution that must actually pick one criterion and live with the choice.
