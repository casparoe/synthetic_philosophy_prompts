I am a retired materials scientist who worked on ceramics, and I have only recently begun to read philosophy. At a museum meeting last month, a conservator and a sculptor had a long argument about a large unfired clay sculpture that the museum was considering acquiring. The conservator said the piece was too fragile to move and should be impregnated with a clear consolidant before installation; the clay would remain in place, but the pores would be filled, the surface would harden, the treatment would not be easily reversible, and the object would become a clay-resin composite. The sculptor objected that the work had been made from clay and presented as a clay work, and that once the consolidant was introduced the old work would effectively be replaced by a new object. The conservator replied that conservation always changes the material state of an object—cleaning, varnishing, mounting, and humidity control all do so—and that if every such change created a new object, museums could never preserve anything. The sculptor said that these examples were not equivalent, because consolidation penetrates the body of the work and alters its mechanical and moisture-related behavior, not merely its surface; a condition report, she argued, could note the change but could not make the old object continue to exist. The conservator then suggested that one could distinguish the material substrate from the artwork object: the substrate changes, but the titled, exhibited, historically continuous work persists. The sculptor answered that this distinction may be convenient for cataloguing, but it does not settle what the artist is responsible for; if the medium specified in the sale agreement is clay, and the object on the pedestal is no longer purely clay, then the museum has acquired something different, whatever its continuity of form.

They turned to me, because I had a materials background, and I could not tell whether the conservator was being pragmatically wise or conceptually sloppy, or whether the sculptor was being appropriately exacting or unrealistically rigid. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, saying which claims are defensible, which overstate the case, and what distinction or set of distinctions would allow the museum to be honest about both the continuity and the alteration involved. Conclude with a single line in the form \answer{conservator}, \answer{sculptor}, or \answer{neither}, indicating which side, if either, is more right overall.
