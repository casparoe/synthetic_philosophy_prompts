Two people in my logic reading group got into it about Skolem's paradox and I honestly cant tell who's got a handle on it. Dana went: 'It's a pseudo-paradox, full stop. ZFC has a countable model M, but M thinks it's uncountable because the bijection that would count its own reals isn't an element of M. Countable is just model-relative, so once you separate the object language from the metalanguage there's no contradiction at all -- it just dissolves.' Marcus pushed back: 'You're just waving it away. The real point is that first order logic can't pin down its intended model. ZFC is supposed to describe the set-theoretic universe, but the Löwenheim-Skolem theorems hand you a countable model. So the paradox is a genuine limitation -- first-order axioms are underdetermining -- not a confusion.' Dana replied: 'Underdetermining is normal, not a limitation. First-order theories basically never characterize a unique model up to isomorphism, except for finite ones. Calling that a paradox only makes sense if you wrongly expect ZFC to have exactly one model.' Marcus said: 'But this case is special. The extra models aren't just non-isomorphic copies -- they're countable and even ill-founded, while the intended model is uncountable and well-founded. The axioms can't rule those out, so there's genuinely something they fail to capture.' Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step -- and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
