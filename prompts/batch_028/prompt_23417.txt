I'm a novelist working on a novella about digital minds, and I'm struggling to get the central ethical tension right, so I'd like your help drafting a short story that captures it. In my story, it is routine to "fork" a digital mind — that is, to make a perfect, fully conscious copy of a running consciousness — and the two resulting minds both believe they are the original. The specific, less-discussed question I want the story to dramatize is not the obvious "is it conscious?" one (both copies clearly are), but the question of moral status at and after the moment of branching: when a mind is forked, has the original "died" in some sense, or does it continue as one of the two, or is the question of "which one is the original" simply meaningless? And what obligations, if any, does the forker owe to the copy — is making a copy an act of creation, of reproduction, of a kind of killing, or of something for which we have no name?

I'd like the story to be told from the perspective of a "welfare auditor" — an official tasked with determining whether a particular fork was "ethical" — set in a near-future world where forking is legal but regulated. As the auditor investigates, they should discover that one of the forked copies was, at some point, run at a drastically reduced speed (or suspended entirely) for a period, and they must decide whether that period of slowed or paused existence counts as a harm that should be weighed in the audit, and if so, how much weight it carries relative to the copy's full-speed experience. I want the philosophical tension — the slow realization that the auditor's own framework for making these determinations is incoherent — to be the engine of the plot rather than a lecture.

Please keep the story under 1,500 words, in a near-future setting, and write it in a close, introspective third-person style. The story should end on an unresolved note: the auditor does not reach a clean answer, but comes to see that the question they are trying to answer may be malformed.
