I get the basic split: epistemicism says there's a real sharp cutoff for 'bald' (a guy with exactly 1,000 hairs is bald, one with 1,001 is not, or whatever), we just can't know where. Supervaluationism says there's no cutoff, and 'that guy is bald' is true only if it's true under every way you could sharpen the word. But here's where I get lost. There's this margin-for-error idea that feels really obvious: if a guy with n hairs is bald, then a guy with n+1 is bald too, because one hair is to small a difference to matter, and same going the other way. Except if that's true you can start from a definitely bald guy and add hairs one at a time until you hit a definitely not-bald guy, and every step is supposed to be true, which is absurd. So how does each view actually deal with this? I want the real mechanism, not a hand-wave, and I especially want to get why this margin-for-error thing seems like a much bigger headache for epistemicism than for supervaluationism. Skip the history, just the core of how each handles it and why they differ.
