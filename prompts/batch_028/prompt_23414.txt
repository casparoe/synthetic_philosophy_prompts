I've been following the AI safety debate for a while now, mostly through blog posts and podcasts rather than anything formal, and I keep coming back to Goodhart's law — the idea that a measure stops being a good measure once it becomes a target. I came across a paper that sorts Goodhart's law into four kinds, which I'll call regression, extremal, causal, and adversarial, and I've been turning over in my head whether that taxonomy is really capturing four distinct phenomena, or whether it's just four different descriptions of the same underlying thing wearing different costumes.

I'd like you to argue for the position that the four kinds are not genuinely distinct but are all instances of a single mechanism — the divergence between a proxy and a target under selection pressure — and that the "kind" label is just a shorthand for which causal structure is doing the diverging. In doing so, please address three things specifically. First, what that single underlying mechanism actually is, and why each of the four kinds can be read as an instance of it rather than as a separate law. Second, what the unified view implies for how we should think about specification gaming in AI: in particular, whether the standard framing that "stronger optimizers produce more specification gaming" is really about optimization strength at all, or about something more structural in the proxy-target relationship. Third, give a concrete worked example — maybe one from a real or plausible deployment scenario, like a reward function in a warehouse robot or a metric used in a hospital — where telling the four kinds apart still matters in practice, even if they are all the same phenomenon underneath. I want the argument to be honest about where the unification breaks down, not a one-sided sell.
