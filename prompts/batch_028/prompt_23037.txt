I'm in a high school honors philosophy class, and for this week's review our teacher had us peer-grade a classmate's short answer before we go over the model response together. The question they were asked to answer is:

"Explain Grice's distinction between what is said and conversational implicature. In your answer, explain how the Cooperative Principle and its maxims generate implicatures, and illustrate your account with an example."

Here is their answer:

Grice distinguishes what is said from what is implicated. What is said is the semantic content of the sentence — the proposition that the sentence expresses by virtue of its conventional meaning. Conversational implicature, by contrast, is not part of the sentence's meaning; it is content that the listener infers on the assumption that the speaker is being cooperative.

The Cooperative Principle says that speakers should make contributions that fit the accepted purpose of the conversation. It is broken down into the maxims of Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. Implicatures arise when a speaker apparently flouts one of these maxims and the listener, assuming the speaker is still cooperating, works out the extra meaning. For example, if someone asks, "How was the job interview?" and the reply is "The parking was free," the speaker has flouted the maxim of Relation. The listener infers that the interview probably went badly, because that is the only way the answer makes sense as a cooperative contribution.

According to Grice, implicatures are defeasible and can be cancelled without contradiction, which is what distinguishes them from what is said. If I say "The parking was free" and then add "and the interview was terrible," the added sentence does not contradict the original utterance, so the implicature was not part of what was said.
