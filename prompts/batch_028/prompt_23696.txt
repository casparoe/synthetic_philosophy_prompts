I am a professor of philosophy (my research is in the ethics of technology) and I have been appointed to the city's Autonomous Vehicle Advisory Board, which oversees a two-year pilot of driverless shuttles operating in a geofenced business district. The shuttles run at SAE level 4, with no onboard driver; a remote operations center staffs one human operator per four vehicles, with typical alert-to-intervention latency of ten to twenty seconds. Last month, in heavy rain, one of the shuttles clipped a cyclist at a crosswalk; the cyclist sustained a minor ankle injury and the city's insurer has paid the claim. The internal investigation found that rain on the LiDAR caused the perception stack to classify the cyclist as a stationary debris object at roughly thirty meters, and that when the remote operator attempted to issue a manual brake command eight seconds after the alert, the system's safety layer rejected the command as conflicting with the local planner's state. The OEM's post-incident review concedes that this rain-degradation mode was identified during validation but accepted as a residual risk, and it offers a software patch within six weeks plus a radar retrofit at its own expense. The pilot agreement contains a clause allowing the city to suspend the pilot after any at-fault incident, and the OEM is arguing that the incident was not at-fault in any cognizable sense, because the system behaved exactly as designed and no individual — engineer, remote operator, or passenger — made a blameworthy decision. The board is split between those who want to terminate the pilot on public-trust grounds and those who want to continue it at full speed.

I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion before Friday's vote on whether to suspend, continue with conditions, or terminate the pilot. I would like the opinion to recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — for instance, an immediate suspension of wet-weather operations, a modified operator-to-vehicle ratio, a defined revalidation period, and explicit termination criteria — and to engage the OEM's position seriously rather than simply asserting that the manufacturer is obviously liable: the core difficulty is that the demand that someone answer for the harm is not matched by any identifiable blameworthy agent, and I want the opinion to say something honest about what this mismatch does to the question of whether we can keep the pilot running. I would also like the opinion to address, without presupposing the answer, whether this is a genuine gap in the structure of responsibility or simply a matter of finding the right bearer of it — say, the corporation as such — and to treat fairly the strongest consideration on the side I do not recommend (if I recommend suspension, that is the argument that the pilot is generating the operational data needed to fix exactly this class of failure, and that a full suspension would leave us no way to verify the fix).
