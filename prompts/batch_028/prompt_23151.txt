So I was at a panel last month and two of the speakers ended up in it during the Q&A, and I kept thinking about it afterwords. One's a game theorist (Mara), the other a philosopher of social norms (Devin), and they were arguing about David Lewis's account of conventions — the one where a convention is basicly a solution to a recurring coordination problem, pinned down by common knowledge and this "prior expectation" that everyone expects everyone else to conform.

Mara's point was that Lewis's whole setup is static. It describes a convention as an equilibrium that's already been selected and is already common knowledge, so it can tell you what a convention is once it's in place, but it can't tell you how one gets started from nothing. To explain the evolution of norms — how a population moves from no convention, or from the wrong one, to an established one — you need a dynamic account, like replicator dynamics or some learning model, that traces the actual path. The "prior expectation" condition just presupposes the very thing you're trying to explain.

Devin pushed back and said that's a misreading. Lewis's arbitrariness condition plus Schelling's focal points already handle the selection problem: even with no shared history, agents can lock onto a salient equilibrium because the salience is itself common knowledge. So Lewis can explain how a convention gets chosen, and the "evolution" over time is just the convention getting reinforced through repeated play, which his framework already covers. The "static" complaint is mostly a category error.

Mara said that even if focal points pick the equilibrium, they don't explain the dynamics of how a population converges to it, or what happens when two conventions compete and one has to displace the other. Then she added that a lot of real norms — fairness, reciprocity, promise-keeping — aren't pure coordination at all; there's a genuine conflict of interest in them, so Lewis's framework (which only handles pure coordination) doesn't even apply to the norms people usually mean by "social norms."

Devin's last move was to say the conflict-of-interest cases are a different phenomenon and shouldn't be dragged into the convention story, and that if you keep the domain to pure coordination — which is what Lewis actually claimed to cover — his account plus focal points is basically complete, and the rest is just empirical work on how fast things converge.

I honestly couldn't tell who had the better case. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific point — not just who wins overall — and flag anything both of them are missing?
