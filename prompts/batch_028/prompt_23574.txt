Two friends of mine, both of whom had recently read a popular article on the hole argument in general relativity, got into an argument at dinner. I'm a software engineer, not a physicist, so I couldn't follow it past the first exchange. Here is my best reconstruction of the main moves.

Alex said the hole argument works like this: take a solution to Einstein's field equations and apply a smooth transformation that is the identity everywhere outside some bounded region (the "hole") but shuffles things around inside it. The result is another solution that agrees with the original on all the initial data but assigns different field values inside the hole. Since nothing observable differs, the points of spacetime inside the hole cannot have any physical identity independent of the fields, and substantivalism is false.

Jamie objected that the argument is invalid: the two "solutions" are the same physical situation described twice, the transformation is just a change of coordinates, and coordinates are arbitrary, so there is no underdetermination to begin with. The argument only has force if you treat the mathematical representation as if it were the physics itself.

Alex replied that the transformation is not a passive relabeling of coordinates: it is an active map that produces a genuinely different assignment of fields to the fixed points of the manifold, and the field equations do not pick out which assignment is the real one. If the points are real, then the initial data really do fail to determine what happens inside the hole, and the theory is indeterministic — which is the reductio.

Jamie answered that this just rephrases the question: whether two diffeomorphically related assignments count as different situations is a matter of interpretation, and the standard interpretation is that they are gauge-equivalent, exactly as in electromagnetism, where gauge-related potentials are not different physical situations. Determinism in general relativity is understood modulo gauge, so there is no indeterminism to explain away.

Alex's last point: in electromagnetism the gauge redundancy lives in the potential, a surplus variable, while the physical field is gauge-invariant. In general relativity the metric itself is the gravitational field, and there is no diffeomorphism-invariant way to say "the matter is at point p." So the gauge response does not factor out a redundancy in the background; it is a claim about what the theory's objects are, and the hole argument is what forces you to make it.

Can you go through their exchange and say who is right at each step — not just who wins overall? In particular, I'm curious whether the "gauge" response is legitimate or question-begging, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
