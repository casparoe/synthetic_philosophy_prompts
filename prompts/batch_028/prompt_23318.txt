I'm a TA for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I've been grading the take-home essays from our unit on mental causation, which has spent the last two weeks on the causal exclusion argument and the responses to it. I'd like a second pair of eyes on one student's paper before I lock in the grade. The question was: "State the causal exclusion argument for mental causation. Then explain how non-reductive physicalists try to resist it, and identify the premise or step of the argument that their reply focuses on." The student's answer, essentially verbatim, is as follows.

The causal exclusion argument has three premises and a conclusion. The first premise is the non-identity thesis: mental states are not identical to physical states, even though they supervene on them — my pain, for example, depends on the relevant brain state but is not the same thing as it. The second premise is causal closure: every physical event has a sufficient cause that is itself physical. The third premise is the denial of pervasive overdetermination: we should not assume that every event is the product of two or more distinct sufficient causes, since that would make causation too indeterminate to be useful. From these, the argument concludes that mental events are causally inert. The reasoning is that if every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, and we do not want to say the effect is overdetermined, then there is no causal work left for the mental to do; the physical cause screens off the mental one.

The argument is aimed at non-reductive physicalism. Reductive physicalists are not threatened, because if the mental just is the physical, then mental causes are physical causes and there is nothing to exclude. Non-reductive physicalists, I take it, respond mainly by attacking the causal closure premise. Their argument is that if the mind genuinely makes a difference — if my deciding to raise my arm really causes my arm to rise, rather than merely accompanying the brain process that does the causing — then there is a non-physical cause of a physical event, and the physical domain is not causally closed after all. Once causal closure is false, the exclusion argument loses one of its premises and so no longer delivers the conclusion that the mental is inert.

The argument is valid, so the dispute is entirely about the premises. The main point of contention is whether causal closure is true, and secondarily whether it is reasonable to insist on rejecting overdetermination.
