I've been going in circles on the sea battle passage in Aristotle's De Interpretatione for a few weeks now, and I can't figure out if I'm missing something or if the standard reading is just... off. I keep seeing people on the forums treat it as a genuine metaphysical argument for determinism, but every time I try to reconstruct it in my head it collapses into something trivial. As far as I can tell, the whole thing is just an application of the law of excluded middle to future-tense propositions: either the battle will happen tomorrow or it won't, and if the sentence is already true or false now, then the outcome is already fixed, so determinism follows. If that's the argument, then anyone who accepts LEM is automatically a determinst, which seems obviously wrong—most logicians and philosophers I know accept LEM while being perfectly fine with indeterminism, quantum mechanics and all.

I've also noticed that the argument gets stated two different ways in different places—something like "the proposition that there will be a sea battle tomorrow is already true" vs. "it is already true that there will be a sea battle tomorrow"—and I don't know if that's a substantive difference or just a difference in phrasing. So my actual question is: is there a version of the argument that doesn't just presuuppose determinism, or am I right that it's all just LEM in a trenchcoat, and the people who take it seriously as a real argument for determinism are making the same mistake I am?
