I spent about 35 years in a chemistry lab before I retired, and I've only just gotten into philosophy in the last couple of years, so I'm definately out of my depth here. A few of us get together on Thursdays to argue about things we've read, and last week two of my friends got stuck in a debate I honestly couldnt referee.

The setup: one of my friends, let's call him Kurt, is a workaholic engineer who says he genuinely gets no pleasure out of family gatherings. When his sister pushes him to come to the holidays, he says, "You're just imposing your values on me; I have no reason to come." His sister says he obviously does — he'd be lonely later, his health would suffer, it's the right thing to do.

Then at our Thursday group, two friends, let's call them Elena and Marcus, got into it about whether Kurt really has a reason.

Elena: "Reasons are internal. If nothing in Kurt's motivational set — what he cares about, what he finds appealing — connects to family time, then he has no reason. Your sister is just talking past him. There's this philosopher, Raz, who argues exactly this: a reason for you has to hook into your 'subjective motivational set' — some motivation you have, which if followed rationally, would lead you to the action."

Marcus: "No, that's too narrow. Consider a person who hates broccoli but has a reason to eat it — health. You can't explain that reason by appealing to his current motivational set unless you assume he has a general desire to be healthy, and what if he doesn't? A pure hedonist who only cares about the next few hours of pleasure — does he have no reason to save for retirement? That makes reasons a function of psychological accidents: people who happened to be raised with concern for the future just have more reasons than those who weren't."

Elena: "But in the broccoli case he does have a motivation — to stay alive, to avoid suffering — so the reason is internal after all. And for the hedonist: if he really has no motivational set that reaches beyond the present, then maybe he really does have fewer reasons. That's a fact about him, not a defect in the theory. Besides, Raz allows that the motivational set can include what you'd be moved by if you were a 'normal' rational person — the so-called normalcy condition — so a temporary deficit doesn't erase reasons."

Marcus: "That normalcy condition is doing all the work, and it's suspicious. If a reason has to be one you'd accept if you were 'normal,' the theory has quietly smuggled in an external standard — the only question left is what counts as 'normal.' And your 'he has a general desire to stay alive' move is ad hoc: I can always find some thin general motivation that hooks onto anything, at which point the theory explains nothing — everything becomes a reason for everyone."

I honestly couldn't tell who was making the better point. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific move — not just who wins overall — and say what, if anything, both of them are missing?
