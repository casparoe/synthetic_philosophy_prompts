I'm in a study group for a social choice course and two of us got stuck in an argument about the discursive dilemma that I honestly cant referee. We worked through the standard 3-juror example: the defendant is guilty if and only if he was at the scene (call it P) and he had the weapon (call it Q), so guilty just means P and Q together. The three jurors vote like this — juror 1 says P yes, Q yes, guilty yes; juror 2 says P yes, Q no, guilty no; juror 3 says P no, Q yes, guilty no. So by majority, P passes, Q passes, but the actual verdict (guilty) fails. Every single ballot is logically fine, but the collective result comes out as "he was at the scene and had the weapon, and is not guilty," which is incoherent.

One of us (call them Alex) says this is a real defect of the conclusion-based rule — i.e., just taking the majority on the final question — and that we should use the premise-based rule instead: vote on P and Q separately, then let the verdict follow from the logic. Alex's core point is that the conclusion-based rule lets the collective endorse its own reasons and then reject the conclusion those reasons entail, which is exactly the incoherence we just saw. The other (Sam) fires back on three fronts: first, the premise-based rule is arbitrary because nothing fixes which propositions count as the "premises" — you could carve up the same case in a different way and get a different answer; second, in our example the premise-based rule convicts (P and Q both pass, so guilty follows) even though the actual majority acquitted, so we'd be overriding what the majority really decided; third, there's an impossibility result to the effect that you can't have an aggregation rule that's logically consistent, respects unanimity, and decides each proposition independently of the others — so some flaw is baked in no matter which rule you pick. Alex replies that the "arbitrary" charge is overblown, since in a real legal case the elements of the offense are pinned down by statute and the premises aren't a free choice, and that Sam's second point is actually the argument *for* the premise-based rule, since the majority's "real verdict" is the incoherent one and shouldn't get to stand.

Can you go through this exchange and say who's right about which specific claim, not just who wins overall? I'm particularly stuck on whether the premise-based rule really is as arbitrary as Sam says, and on whether "each individual is consistent but the aggregate isn't" is a genuine problem or just an artifact of how you slice up the question. And if there's something both of them are missing, I'd like to know that too.
