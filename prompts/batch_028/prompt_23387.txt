I am a novelist, and over the past month I have been re-reading Tolstoy's 1897 essay What Is Art? — not the passages most anthologies select, but the technical machinery of the theory: the definition of art as the deliberate external transmission of a feeling the artist has previously experienced, the test of infectiousness he proposes as the sign of genuine art, and the three conditions he attaches to that transmission — individuality, clearness, and sincerity — which he ranks sincerity above the others, on the grounds that it is almost entirely absent from what he calls the art of the upper classes, produced by artists actuated by vanity or covetousness. I find this framework considerably more defensible than I expected, and it has led me to a question I keep circling in my notes. Tolstoy's account makes the value of art essentially social: its purpose is to produce a union of people in shared feeling, and he treats art that fails to unite as a failure of its defining function. What, then, should this theory make of the algorithmic personalization of art consumption — streaming services that ensure no two listeners or viewers share the same playlist, and a long-tail economy in which niche works find small but devoted audiences rather than participating in any shared cultural moment?

I would be grateful for a considered prediction of how Tolstoy himself would have responded to this development, working strictly from the mechanisms of the 1897 text rather than from his general religious views or from later hagiography. In particular, I would like you to weigh a tension I have not been able to resolve: on one side, the fragmentation of the shared audience seems to defeat precisely the function the theory assigns to art, since personalized curation severs the common ground through which feeling is transmitted from one person to another; on the other side, Tolstoy's praise of local, non-universal peasant and folk art — art that acts powerfully precisely because it is not addressed to a cosmopolitan elite — suggests he might defend small-scale communities of shared feeling as a legitimate, even superior, form of the union. Please distinguish, where you can, between what the theory structurally implies and what we should discount as a product of nineteenth-century assumptions about how people encounter works, and note any place where the modern case seems to strain the theory in a way that reveals its limits rather than its conclusions.
