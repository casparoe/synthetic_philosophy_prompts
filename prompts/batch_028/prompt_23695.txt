I'm working on a research paper for my AP philosophy class on how we should understand biological function, and I keep going back and forth between two accounts, so I'd like you to write an argument in favor of one of them: the etiological account, on which a trait has a given function just in case it was retained by natural selection because it performed that function, as opposed to the causal-role account, on which a trait has a function just in case it reliably plays that causal role in the organism. I'd like the argument to defend the etiological account as the more defensible position.

The part I find most interesting is what happens with traits that are currently doing something they were never selected to do. Think of an organ or structure that, in a new environment, gets pressed into a job it evolved for some other purpose or for nothing at all. The causal-role account seems to say it thereby has that new function, while the etiological account says it does not, since the new role was not what the trait was selected for. I'd like the argument to press exactly this point: explain why denying the new function is not a shortcoming of the etiological view but a virtue, and why the causal-role account's willingness to grant the new function blurs a distinction biology actually needs to draw, for instance when we try to explain why a trait is present in the first place or when we judge that it is malfunctioning.

I'd also appreciate it if the argument anticipated the most obvious objection, namely that the etiological account seems to say a trait can lose its function if its environment changes, and that it makes function depend on a contingent evolutionary history that we can rarely reconstruct. I want the argument to be structured so I could basically adapt it for my paper, so please keep the reasoning tight rather than surveying the whole debate.
