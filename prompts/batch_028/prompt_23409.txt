Okay so I'm working on a novella set in 1930s Vienna and one of the characters is a fictional museum educator who keeps running into Otto Neurath's ideas — the Isotype pictorial language, the little museum of society and economics with its big wall charts, and that whole thing of his about how we are like sailors who have to rebuild the ship at sea, never getting to dry-dock our beliefs. I want my character to be one of the few people in the building who actually disagrees with him, but I keep writing her as a strawman who just says pictures are ambiguous and loses. So please write me a dialogue between her and a sympathetic Neurath supporter (a curator or a young designer from the Isotype workshop would be fine), both of them sharp, with real back-and-forth where each of them responds to the other's actual points.

Here is the crux I want them to fight over. Neurath's view, as I understand it: pictograms sit closer to the facts than words do, they are less ideological and more universal than prose in a particular language, and that is the the whole point of the project — to make social and economic connections visible to people who are shut out of written discourse. My character's counter is that the neutrality is the whole trick: a pictogram of a factory worker is already a decision about what a worker is, and the supposedly universal picture ends up being the most powerful kind of ideology because it doesn't look like ideology, while words at least admit they are a medium and can be argued with. But the Neurath side needs real answers, not a recitation: words are also conventional and carry the baggage of a specific culture; and maybe the ship thing is the answer — if our beliefs have to be rebuilt at sea, never getting to dry-dock, then the best we can do is build a hull that can be inspected from outside, and pictures are more inspectable than prose.

End it without resolution — she should walk away not convinced but unsettled, and I want the reader to feel the same way. Around 800 to 1200 words, stage directions minimal, and please don't let either character recite the history of Isotype at each other; keep it argumentative and concrete, with at least one specific pictogram (a wage flowchart, a factory diagram, something like that) that they actually argue over, wich one of them can use to turn the other's own move against them.
