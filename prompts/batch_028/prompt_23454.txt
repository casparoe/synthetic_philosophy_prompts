I'm working on a long-form piece for my magazine on the moral foundations of promise-keeping, and I've been stuck on a question my editor insists I take a side on rather than give a balanced survey of: why does a promise bind in the first place? I've been talking with a moral philosopher friend who is a committed defender of the will theory — the Kantian idea that a promise is an act of the will that authoritatively assigns to the promisee a right to the promised act, so that breaking the promise wrongs the promisee's will even if no one is harmed and no one relied on it. I find that view compelling in ordinary cases but deeply puzzling in the edge cases, and I've come to suspect that the least popular account in the literature is actually the right one.

Here is the landscape as I understand it, so we're working from the same map. On the will theory, the binding force of a promise is a free-standing normative fact generated by the promisor's voluntary act, independent of consequences. On the reliance or institutional theory, associated with Annette Baier, a promise is an institution that creates a justified expectation in the promisee, and the wrong of breaking it lies in frustrating that expectation. And on the derivative or harm-based account, associated with T.M. Scanlon, there is no distinctive moral wrong of breaking a promise at all: the wrong, when there is one, is entirely derivative from the harm the breaking does to the promisee, so that a harmless breaking is not a moral wrong, or is wrong only in a trivial sense.

I'd like you to argue for the derivative account, and to do it in a way that could plausibly appear in my piece. First, explain why the will theorist's appeal is a confusion: I suspect she is smuggling in the logic of coercion, where what binds is precisely the threat of harm, and that the felt authority of a promise is really the felt authority of a threat in disguise. Second, take on the case I find hardest for her: I promise a colleague to attend a lecture she is giving, and she later secretly cancels; I break the promise. No one is harmed, no one relied. Defend the derivative verdict that I have done no moral wrong, and say something about why the intuition that I "still did something wrong" is misleading — perhaps a matter of etiquette, of self-image, or of the institution's social norms, but not of morality proper. Third, address the pledge case: when I "promise" myself to quit smoking, what exactly binds me on the derivative view, and is it a problem that the derivative account seems to have no room for a wrong directed at no one? I'd like the argument to be tight, to concede what it must concede, and to end with a candid assessment of the derivative account's best remaining vulnerability.
