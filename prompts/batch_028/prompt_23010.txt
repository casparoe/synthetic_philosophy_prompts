I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine — lately mostly forecasting and decision science — and I also sit on the board of a small annual forecasting tournament: about 40 serious forecasters, a $12,000 prize, and forecasts scored on calibration (Brier score, for those keeping score). This year we have a problem. The tournament just closed, the winner and the runner-up are at each other's throats, and the runner-up has filed a formal protest asking us to disqualify the winner on epistemic grounds.

Here's the concrete case. The disputed event was whether a certain infrastructure bill would pass the Senate before January 1, and it did, on December 18. The winner had put 87% on yes; the runner-up, 41%. We've checked the records, and neither of them had a private-information edge — both were working from the public record, wich is what the tournament expects for this category of event. The protest is not about the score, which is mechanical; it's about our own rulebook, which says forecasts must be "made in good faith and in accordance with the rules of rational updating." Her claim is that 87% was simply not a rational response to the public evidence — that no rational person could have assigned that probability, so the winner's forecast shouldn't count. I read somewhere, in a decision-science paper I can't now remember, a result to the effect that two perfectly rational people who start from the same prior assumptions can't rationally keep disagreeing once they know each other's beliefs — and if that's right, one of the two of them must be irrational, which is exactly what the protest is claiming. I don't remember the details, and I'm not sure all the assumptions actually hold in our case.

So: I'd like you to draft the board's decision memo to circulate before Friday's vote. It should take a clear position — uphold the result or grant the protest — and it should give the reasoning in terms the two forecasters, who are both sharp and will read it, can actually respond to. I want the strongest version of the protest treated honestly, because two of my five board colleagues are on that side, and if the memo doesn't steelman their view, they'll just vote no. And if you recommend any rule changes for next year, say what they'd be and why they'd be fair to both sides. I'll be honest, I definately lean uphold, but I keep thinking that if we never police rationality, the tournament might reward lucky-but-unprincipled overconfidence, and I can't tell if that worry is real or just a post-hoc rationalization.
