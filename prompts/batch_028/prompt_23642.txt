I spent my career in experimental physics and have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, so I may be framing this clumsily. I have been following the Bayesian approach to scientific confirmation and have come across what is called the problem of old evidence. The standard illustration is the 1919 eclipse observations of the bending of starlight: these seem to confirm general relativity, yet because they were already part of the scientific community's background knowledge by the time the theory was adopted, the likelihood ratio P(E|GTR)/P(E|Newton) appears to equal one, and by the very calculus Bayesians insist on, old evidence cannot raise the posterior probability of a theory. I am curious what Frank Ramsey would have made of this. Given that his coherence framework treats an agent's credences as the rational response to its information, do you think he would have argued that the eclipse observation, treated as an uncertain outcome of a probabilistic experiment, can still confirm general relativity? Or would he have concluded that the old-evidence problem shows that Bayesian confirmation must be supplemented by some non-probabilistic account of the history of discovery?
