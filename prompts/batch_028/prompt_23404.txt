I've been going down a rabbit hole lately on supertasks — tasks that pack infinitely many steps into a finite span of time — and the topic resurfaced last week when I was talking with a friend who works in control theory. He pushed back on the whole line of thought, insisting that the interesting question was never the one the textbooks ask. I keep coming back to Thomson's lamp, but I'm not interested in the standard framing — is the lamp on or off at t = 1? — that you see in every intro philosophy course. What I actually want to think about is the question those discussions tend to skip over: is a finite-time supertask even physically realizable in the first place, and does the answer to that change what counts as a legitimate response to the puzzle? I'd like you to write me a short story that puts that question in motion rather than just stating it.

Set it in a near-future lab where an engineer has, against every prior assumption, actually built a working supertask device — a mechanism that performs a sequence of operations with durations halving toward a fixed deadline, so that its operating frequency diverges as it approaches the cutoff. The story should turn on the moment she's supposed to read off the device's final state and can't quite settle what it's showing her. I want the narrative to take seriously the view that the final state is genuinely indeterminate — a real absence of a fact of the matter, not mere ignorance — and to set that in genuine tension with the competing view that the diverging switching rate makes the whole apparatus physically incoherent, so that the "paradox" was never a real puzzle to begin with. Give the engineer a concrete stake in the outcome, and let the philosophical tension drive the plot rather than resolve it in a character's explanatory monologue. A few pages is plenty.
