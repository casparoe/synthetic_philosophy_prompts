The student's answer is as follows:

"Gould's 1979 paper argues that adaptationists, following the Panglossian optimism of their paradigm, explain too much: they treat every feature of an organism as if it must have been directly shaped by natural selection for the function we now observe in it. The spandrel analogy supports this by showing how a feature can exist without having been designed for any purpose at all. In San Marco, the spandrels are the triangular sections of wall that arise automatically once the arches and pendentives are built; they were never designed, and yet later painters filled them with frescoes, so observers might mistake these decorative afterthoughts for intentional design. By analogy, many biological features are 'spandrels' in this sense: they are byproducts of other structural or developmental constraints, not products of direct selection.

This is exactly what happens with exaptations as well: a spandrel is simply an exaptation that has been co-opted by selection for a new function, so the two terms are largely interchangeable, with 'spandrel' being the architectural case and 'exaptation' the biological case. Because spandrels arise without selection, their evolution is essentially neutral: selection acts only on the features that produced them, and the spandrels themselves drift freely, which is why they can be so varied across species.

The practical consequence Gould draws is that adaptationist explanations are unfalsifiable, because any feature can be retrofitted with a just-so story about its adaptive function, so the spandrels framework gives us a better default hypothesis: when in doubt, treat the feature as a byproduct rather than an adaptation."
