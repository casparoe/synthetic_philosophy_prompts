I am writing a novel set in a graduate economics program, and two of my characters — a third-year doctoral student and a postdoctoral researcher — argued in the faculty lounge over a 2×2 game. Player 1 chooses between T and B; Player 2 chooses between L and R. The payoffs are (T, L) = (3, 3), (T, R) = (0, 0), (B, L) = (1, 1), (B, R) = (0, 0). The student argued that B is dominated by T and can be eliminated, after which Player 2's unique best response is L, so (T, L) is the sole rationalizable outcome. The postdoc objected that B is only weakly dominated, and iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies is order-dependent, so the conclusion is not robust. The student replied that this game has only one dominated strategy to eliminate, so the order of elimination cannot matter. The postdoc countered that R is strictly dominated by L, so the student's count is wrong; and even setting that aside, relying on weak dominance imports an assumption of perfect rationality that the student's own reasoning does not justify, and a player who assigns even a small probability to the opponent's error might rationally choose B. They both turned to me — I had been eavesdropping over a cup of coffee — and asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange and say who is right at each step, and what, if anything, both are missing.
