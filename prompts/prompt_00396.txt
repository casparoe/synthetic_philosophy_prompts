I've been working on a long-form piece about the higher-order theories of consciousness, and I keep getting stuck on the "targetless higher-order representation" problem that Ned Block and others have pressed against David Rosenthal's classic Higher-Order Thought (HOT) theory: if a conscious state just is a first-order state that happens to be the target of a distinct higher-order thought, what happens when the machinery misfires and produces a higher-order thought about a first-order state that doesn't actually exist? Rosenthal has bitten the bullet and said this would still count as a conscious experience, which strikes several critics as deeply counterintuitive, while self-representational or "same-order" theorists (in the vein of Uriah Kriegel or Robert Van Gulick) argue their view sidesteps the whole problem because the representing and the represented aren't numerically distinct states in the first place.

For the piece, I'd like to include a dialogue that dramatizes this disagreement rather than just summarizing it in my own words. Could you write a short back-and-forth between a committed Rosenthal-style HOT theorist and a same-order/self-representationalist, in which the HOT theorist has to defend the claim that a targetless higher-order thought can still generate genuine phenomenal consciousness, while the self-representationalist presses on whether that concession drains the theory of any real explanatory content? I want the self-representationalist's alternative picture to get pushed on too — for instance, whether collapsing the distinction between representing and represented state can actually account for the apparent subject/object structure of experience, or whether it just relocates the same puzzle. Neither side should get the last, unanswered word; each should have to grapple with the other's strongest move.
