I've been reading through some of the literature on acausal threats in the context of AI safety and decision theory—particularly the proposal that an agent worried about extortion from other agents (including hypothetical or simulated ones it can never causally interact with) can protect itself by adopting a "surrogate goal": a value it commits to caring about instrumentally, purely so that threats get redirected toward that decoy rather than toward its actual terminal goals. I understand the basic pitch, but I keep getting stuck on what seems like a regress or unraveling problem: if a rational threatener knows the surrogate goal was adopted precisely because it's cheap for the agent to sacrifice, why wouldn't it see through the ploy and just threaten the agent's real goals anyway, or escalate by threatening the surrogate goal in addition to something else? And if the agent could instead just refuse to be the kind of agent that responds to any threats at all, what extra work is the surrogate goal actually doing?

Could you walk me through the game-theoretic mechanism that's supposed to make surrogate goals stable against this kind of unraveling—what has to be true about the threatener's incentives and information for the strategy to hold up—and give me a sense of where proponents think the argument is on solid ground versus where it's most likely to break down?
