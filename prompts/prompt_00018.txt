I've been reading about program equilibrium in the context of open-source game theory, and I keep running into two ideas that seem to be doing similar work but come from different intellectual lineages: Tennenholtz's original program equilibrium (where agents submit programs that can read each other's source code, à la mutual cooperation via "if opponent's program equals mine, cooperate") and the more decision-theoretic notion of "modal combat" or Löbian cooperation that comes out of the MIRI-adjacent work on logical decision theory, where agents use provability logic and Löb's theorem to establish cooperation without literally checking for source-code equality. Both are pitched as solutions to getting cooperation in one-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas among transparent agents, but I'm fuzzy on how deep the difference actually goes.

Is the shift from "check if you're playing an identical copy" to "prove that we cooperate together via fixed-point/Löbian reasoning" just an engineering improvement (more robust to slight variations in source code, less exploitable by trivial mimicry), or does it represent a genuinely different philosophical stance on what it means for two agents to "trust" each other's future behavior? I'd also like to understand whether the Löbian approach actually avoids the problems that make naive source-code-equality checking feel unsatisfying — like the worry that it only works for exact clones and collapses the interesting problem of trust into a syntactic accident. Where does each approach actually locate the "reason" cooperation is rational, and does one of them generalize better to asymmetric agents (different programs, different levels of sophistication) than the other?
