I direct a small research program — three to five people for roughly three years — that funds conceptual work on AI alignment: philosophy, decision theory, and theory of computation, not empirical machine learning. My board keeps pressing me to say why we treat "the alignment problem" as a single thing, and I've come to think the phrase bundles at least four separable targets: specifying what a system is supposed to want; ensuring it keeps pursuing that in situations its designers did not anticipate; coordinating many such systems and many principals at once; and deciding, politically, whose specification is allowed to count. I suspect there is a fifth candidate that rarely gets named, namely that the proper unit of alignment might be a single model, or a deployment, or the research community that produces models, since each of those admits a different kind of failure and a different kind of fix.

I want a defended agenda, not a survey. Assume the group cannot run experiments and cannot look anything up; they must settle conceptual questions by argument alone. Tell me which open questions most deserve the group's three years, ranked, and be explicit about what makes a question worth the years rather than merely unresolved. I care especially about which questions are upstream of which — whose resolution would change what else is even askable — and about which candidate problems lack the very thing that would make them answerable, namely some recognizable error signal by which a proposed solution could be judged wrong.

Give me exactly five items, ranked from most to least deserving of the group's effort, each one a sentence or two that names the question, says why it is upstream of other work, and notes what would move it up or down the ranking. No introduction and no conclusion.
