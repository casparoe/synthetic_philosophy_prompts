I'm putting together a fellowship application (two pages, one year) and I've got a half-formed idea I want you to turn into a proper proposal. Domain: just war theory, specifically the moral equality of combatants. Everyone knows the shape of the fight -- McMahan-style revisionists say the traditional equality is false because unjust combatants aren't permitted to kill, traditionalists say the equality survives for various reasons (conventions, reciprocity, the value of restraint). I don't want to re-argue that.

My hunch is that the debate keeps going in circles partly because "moral equality" is doing at least three different jobs and nobody seperates them. There's (a) equality of justification -- both sides are permitted to do the same things; (b) equality of excuse -- unjust combatants are typically not culpable even if they act wrongly; and (c) equality of liability -- combatants on both sides are equally legitimate targets, equally liable to be killed. The revisionists attack (a) and think thats the whole thing; but a lot of the traditionalist defenses (reciprocity, the claim that soldiers can't reasonably be expected to know, the claim that the rules of war are a package deal) really only get you (b) or (c). So maybe the real question isn't "is the equality true?" but "which of these three can be defended, how independent are they of each other, and does the interesting normative work actually all sit in (b) and (c)?"

The bit I think is genuinely in the weeds and under-explored: the liability claim (c) is usually assumed to just fall out of whatever theory of liability to defensive harm you happen to hold, but different live theories -- responsibility-based ones, rights-forfeiture ones, and ones that make liability turn on the target's status rather than the agent's responsibility -- seem to push (c) in opposite directions once you look at unjust combatants who are non-culpably ignorant, which is the standard case, not the fringe one. And the epistemic condition (duress, ignorance, propaganda) is doing double duty: it's openly used to rescue (b), but I suspect it also quietly rescues (c), and if it does, then the revisionist's practical conclusions might collapse back into something close to the traditionalist rules. That would be a deflationary result and I can't tell whether it's the right one or just a trick I'm playing on myself.
