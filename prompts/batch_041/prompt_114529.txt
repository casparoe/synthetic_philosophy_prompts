At our colloquium this week we discussed what we have been calling tranquilism: the thesis that the only thing intrinsically good for a person is the absence of craving and suffering, with everything else mattering at most instrumentally. Two colleagues stayed behind to argue about it, and I would like their exchange adjudicated rather than the thesis summarized.

Dana said the thesis has an absurd consequence. If only the absence of craving and suffering is good, then someone rendered incapable of craving — by sedation, surgery, or lifelong insensate contentment — is doing maximally well; and so is anyone in a dreamless sleep, since an hour of dreamless sleep contains no craving and no suffering. A long enough unconscious life would be the best life available. An account of well-being that cannot distinguish the sage from the anesthetized patient has lost its subject matter.

Ravi said this only shows that Dana is smuggling in the premise that a good state must be felt. Tranquilism claims that the absence of craving is good whether or not it is noticed — a substantive claim, not an absurdity — and that is what lets it explain why someone asleep or anesthetized is not thereby suffering. He added that her examples are not even instances of the good in question: tranquility is a condition of a creature that can crave, so a person whose capacity for craving has been destroyed is not tranquil but blank, and an absence no one could have had is not the absence of craving. What is good is the craving-free condition of a subject who remains the kind of thing in which cravings can arise and be stilled.

Dana replied that this saves the view by giving it up. If tranquility requires an intact capacity for craving, it is not the absence of craving but a relation between a subject and her cravings — a settled calm — which is a positive state of mind, and tranquilism has become a species of hedonism. If the good really is the bare absence, then it needs no subject who could have craved it, and the sleep case stands. Ravi granted that an hour of dreamless sleep is intrinsically good and rather better than an hour of craving and distress, but said he saw nothing absurd in that: Dana's demand that the good life be a life one would choose to live is a different question from what is good for someone while she exists.

Please assess each move in turn — sound or unsound, and why — and then say where the exchange turns on something neither of them has made explicit. Specifically: is Ravi's appeal to the capacity for craving a genuine third option, or does it collapse into one of Dana's two horns; and if it collapses, which horn should a tranquilist accept?
