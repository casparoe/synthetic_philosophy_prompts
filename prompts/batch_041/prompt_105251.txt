So I'm maybe 40,000 words into my fourth novel — it's set in a boys' boarding school in the west of Ireland in the 1950s, lots of rain and Latin and boiled cabbage — and I keep running into a problem I can't think my way through philosophically. The school runs on two practices that sit right beside each other and I genuinely can't tell if they're consistent. First, collective punishment: one boy lets off a stink bomb in the refectory and the whole house gets gated for a fortnight, everyone's Sunday leave cancelled, the lot — and nobody, not the boys, not the masters, treats this as unjust, it's just how it works, it's practically a law of nature. Second, and the school is prouder of this one, there's a kind of volunteer: when a younger boy is due a caning, an older boy can step forward and take it for him, and the place treats this as the noblest thing a boy can do, there's even a little ceremony about it. And then in the same breath the honor code that the headmaster recites at assembly insists that you never punish a boy for what another boy did — every boy answers for his own conduct and nobody else's. That looks to me like a flat contradiction and yet not a single character in my book finds it strange, which is exactly the sort of thing that makes me think the place is running on a picture of punishment I haven't managed to articulate.

Anyway, I've been reading this short thing by David Lewis about penal substitution — the old Christian doctrine that Christ was punished in our place, took the penalty we owed — and I'll be honest, I only half followed it, but I think his question was whether we actually believe that sort of thing, whether we really think punishment can land on someone other than the person who earned it, and whether our own reactions give us away. Which set me wondering whether my school is being inconsistent at all, or just working from some theory of responsibility I'm too close to see.

So what I want from you is the philosophy underneath it. What does punishing a whole house for one boy's prank presuppose — a picture on which guilt is somehow shared or collective, or just a hard-nosed picture on which it doesn't matter who suffers so long as the boys behave? Does the collective punishment actually need that picture, or could a straightforward deterrence story, or a "we're training them to police one another" story, license exactly the same scene? And the volunteer-caning — is that presupposing the same thing as the collective punishment or the opposite thing? I suspect they're opposites and that that's the real puzzle, but tell me if I'm wrong. Then: which rival theories does all of this quietly rule out, and is there any single account of responsibility and desert that could hold all three pieces together — the collective punishment, the admired substitute, the everyone-answers-for-himself code — without contradiction, or do I have to decide which one the school secretly doesn't believe? And if you land on one presupposed theory rather than another, what would the place have to look like to be consistent — what would actually have to change on the page? I don't want reading lists or citations, just the conceptual map, and please flag the places where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, i.e. where several quite different philosophies would all fit what happens in the novel, because that determins how I write the thing.
