I sit on the ethics advisory board of a mid-sized space agency — we're the human-side review, not the engineers, so missions come to us before they get approved. Next week we're taking up a proposal from a private partner: a first crewed settlement on Mars, six people, one-way, with a charter that commits the settlement to begin warming the planet within fifty years of landing so later arrivals can live outside. They want our sign-off. They've also written in a clause they call the Legacy Clause, which binds the settlers and their descendants in perpetuity to leave a designated region of the planet untouched, exactly as found, as a kind of memorial. The settlers sign it freely, so the company says it's fine, and they've set up a Return Fund that anyone at the settlement can draw on to come home, which they say keeps consent ongoing.

Half the board thinks the whole thing is a stunt and the Legacy Clause is empty because you can't bind people who don't exist yet. The other half thinks the one-way mission is the real problem — the adults can take the risk, but any kid born there had no say, and we'd be handing them a planet we've already started cooking plus a promise their great-grandparents made on their behalf. And someone pointed out the Return Fund has no ship behind it yet, which makes me wonder what a right of exit is worth when theres nothing to exit on.

Draft the short opinion I'll circulate before Thursdays meeting. I want a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and if it's conditions, spell them out and say on what timeframe. Don't just restate the Outer Space Treaty stuff, everyone knows that. Engage the strongest version of the side you come out against, and tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the most work in your reasoning.
