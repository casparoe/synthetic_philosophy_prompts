I'm a second-year econ PhD — empirical/behavioral, so in theory terms I'm basically a tourist — and I've been sitting in on a reading group on epistemic game theory this term. We'd just finished the common-knowledge hierarchy (the infinite tower of "I know that you know that I know...") when a friend in the group, the kind of person who reads the proofs for fun, floated a little argument that rational players are never genuinely uncertain about each other, and that all the talk of "strategic uncertainty" is a polite fiction. I wrote it up cleanly afterward and now I cant stop chewing on it, because the conclusion feels obviously too strong, yet every step looks fine to me. Here's the version I reconstructed:

(1) A rational agent plays the action that maximizes her expected payoff given her beliefs.
(2) So if you know your opponent is rational, you know her action will be a best response to her beliefs.
(3) Common knowledge of rationality means: you know she's rational, she knows you're rational, you know she knows you're rational, and so on, and everyone knows the game — the actions, the payoffs, and that you're both in it.
(4) Her action is a best response to her beliefs, so if you know her beliefs, you know her action.
(5) And you do know her beliefs: her beliefs are the output of rational belief-formation, and rationality works the same way in every head, so the process that produced your beliefs produced hers too.
(6) So you know her action, and by symmetry she knows yours.
(7) So in any game with common knowledge of rationality, each player knows exactly what the other will do — there's no residual uncertainty left over.
(8) So no rational agent can ever be genuinely uncertain about, or genuinely surprised by, another rational agent's choice.

The problem is that (8) doesn't just feel wrong, it eats a bunch of things I'd rather keep: if it holds, mixed strategies are theatre, bluffing and deception are impossible, and any trade that rests on two people disagreeing about the odds is off the table, which is a lot of economics to toss. I really want to keep (1), and I don't want to give up the common-knowledge framework in (3), since that's most of what makes the theory do any work at all — I'd much rather find the bad step somewhere else. So: which step is actually doing the illegitimate work, why did it look so innocent, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the honest fix is to bolt on an extra assumption, tell me exactly what it buys and what it costs, because I want to know whether I'm giving up pocket change or mortgaging the house. And please tell me where your own diagnosis is most fragile — what would have to be true, or what would someone have to show you, for you to blame a different step or prefer a different repair?
