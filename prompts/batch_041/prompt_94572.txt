I'm a philosophy grad student and I'm deep in the weeds on the unity of the proposition -- you know, the puzzle of what makes a particular, a property, and a relation add up to *one* proposition, a single thing that can be true or false, rather than just a heap or a list of three items sitting next to each other. I get why the regress starts: if you say the unity comes from a relation that ties the constituents together, then that relation is itself another constituent, so now you need something to tie *it* to the others, and off you go. What I can't decide is whether that regress is a real problem or a fake one. I've heard people say it's vicious and shows there's no such thing as propositional unity at all, and I've heard people say it's harmless because nobody serious ever thought the glue was an extra ingredient in the first place. I don't want a survey of who said what, I want an actual verdict.

Here's the thing that's really bugging me, and I'd like you to focus on it. A classmate floated what she calls the absorption move. Her idea is that the regress only gets going because of a hidden assumption she calls flat ontology: that everything you can mention is an *item* of the same general kind, so anything that does any unifying work can always be put back on the list of things still needing to be unified. She says instantiation isn't an item at all, it's the *act* by which a property gets brought to bear on things -- a doing rather than a fourth thing -- and since a doing isn't a member of the list, the regress never gets started. I think this is either trivially true, in which case it can't be solving anything, or just a relabeling, because the moment you distinguish the doing from the items it operates on you've made it *something*, and something that is something can be listed. But a diffrent friend turned this around on me: he says that even if the act isn't a further constituent, acts are individuated by what they're acts *on*, so the regress just migrates -- instead of a regress of composition you get a regress of individuation, and that one is just as bad. I honestly can't tell which of us is confused.

Please act like a philosopher of language and metaphysics who's worked on this material and write me an essay adjudicating it. Start by brainstorming the live positions and walking through exactly what the regress is supposed to show at each step -- be precise about what's doing the explanatory work at each stage, and about which premises are load-bearing. Then defend a verdict: is the regress vicious, benign, or does it depend on assumptions we're free to reject, and does the absorption move actually get you out or does the individuation worry sink it? I'd much rather you commit to something specific and defend it than hedge, even if the verdict ends up qualified. Write in American English.
