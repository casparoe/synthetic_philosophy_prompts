I am a second-year PhD student in the philosophy of science, having come into the programme from a science background, and I mention that only because it explains why the problem of old evidence has caught me out in the way it has. My thesis is a Bayesian reconstruction of a dispute in my former field, and the chapter I am drafting now turns on a fact that was discovered decades before anyone proposed the theory I am reconstructing — precisely the sort of evidence a Bayesian is supposed to be unable to make any use of, and which the historical record suggests did a great deal of work. I have been arguing about this with my supervisor for months. She has a settled line on the old-evidence cases we have discussed, but I cannot work out what general rule her verdicts are tracking. As best I can reconstruct them: first, in the case of the inverse-square law and the planetary regularities, where the regularities had been established long before and were celebrated, where the law was proposed to unify terrestrial and celestial motion rather than to account for them, and where no rival account then on offer predicted them, she says the regularities confirm the law heavily, and is willing to say the law would have been nearly as well supported had they been announced the week after it was published; second, in a case from population genetics, where a hypothesis proposed on independent grounds entails a census regularity that everyone had expected when it was compiled, and where every live rival now entails the same regularity, she says the regularity confirms it hardly at all and that its real support lies elsewhere; third, in a case from the history of geology, where the regularity was mildly surprising when it was found, the hypothesis was formulated decades later for unrelated reasons, and roughly half of the field's present alternatives also entail the regularity, she says modest confirmation — more than nothing, less than much; and fourth, for a hypothesis proposed first and a striking observation that arrived afterwards and matched, she says that is straightforward confirmation and not the interesting case at all. It has not escaped me that in each of the first three cases the regularity was roughly as surprising when it was discovered as it is discriminating today. She has also said two things that pull in opposite directions, and I cannot tell which of them governs the old-evidence cases: that a Bayesian reconstruction of a historical episode is a model of what a rational agent in that episode could have believed on the evidence then available, and that it is a category error to grade a nineteenth-century scientist by what we know now; and that confirmation is a relation between a hypothesis and the total evidence, so that there is no such thing as evidence for a hypothesis which fails to raise its probability given everything we currently accept.

My own case is this. The theory, T, was proposed in the 1980s by someone working on an entirely different problem. T entails a regularity, R, that had been discovered in the 1950s and was, at the time, a bolt from the blue: on the background knowledge of the day, R was about as improbable as a fact of that kind could be, and for thirty years nobody could account for it. The entailment from T to R was spotted by a third party in the mid-1990s, when no other theory on the table entailed R. Since about 2005, however, four rival theories have appeared, each of them constructed with R in view, and all four entail R. R now discriminates nothing: every live theory in the field predicts it, and it has gone from being the field's great anomaly to being its great platitude. I can see three places from which one might measure how much R confirms T: the standpoint of the 1950s community, before R was discovered; the standpoint of the present community, for whom the only news is the entailment itself; and the standpoint of the mid-1990s community, which had R but not the rivals. I know the first has a wrinkle — the 1950s credence function has to be extended to a theory nobody had yet formulated — and I should like to know whether that wrinkle is fatal or merely technical.

So what will she say about how much R confirms T? I am far less interested in the number than in the rule. Tell me which general rules fit her four verdicts equally well, what each of them predicts here, which prediction you would bet on and why, and what she would have to be assuming about whose credence function is in play for her verdict to be defensible. Finally, name the further case I should invent and put to her in order to separate the rules; I would rather ask one decisive question at our next supervision than six inconclusive ones.
