I direct a graduate seminar on the epistemology of the human past, and a dispute has arisen between two colleagues who regularly sit in on it — one an archaeologist, one a philosopher of science — that I have not been able to settle to my own satisfaction. I would be grateful if you would adjudicate it move by move, rather than simply declare a winner.

The seminar had been working through a case. A settlement of a few hundred people, occupied for several generations, has yielded three classes of find. First, small unfired clay figurines, most of them deliberately snapped at the neck, deposited in pits beneath the floors of particular houses. Second, a set of standardized, deliberately shaped stone tokens — nearly identical in size and form across the whole site — found only in some houses and, in those houses, kept together in a single place. Third, in a few of the pits the figurines are accompanied by the remains of small animals killed and buried whole. There are no texts, no depictions of the practice, and no later tradition to read back from.

Dr. M holds that this material licenses, at most, claims about what the people did: that they broke figurines and buried them in certain ways, that they held or exchanged tokens, and so on. Any step from there to what they believed, or to what institutions they had, she says, is a projection of our own categories; the honest report is a description of behavior plus a confession of ignorance about the mental and the institutional. Her stated principle is that a mental or institutional claim should be asserted only if no purely behavioral description of the traces is available.

Dr. K replies that this standard is not one she applies anywhere else. He points out that Dr. M is perfectly willing to say the site had a trade network, that certain houses were related by descent, or that the occupants had a division of labor — all of which outrun the raw traces just as far as a claim about belief does. If we may infer kinship from the distribution of goods, he argues, we may infer a shared belief from a repeated, costly, normatively sustained practice; the inference to the best explanation is the same in both cases, and the demand to stay at the level of behavior is a rule that, applied consistently, would leave us with nothing but a list of objects.

Dr. M answers that institutions and beliefs are special, because they are constituted by collective acceptance rather than caused by it: a token is money only if people take it as money, and a deposit is a burial rite only if people take it as one. Since the taking-as is precisely what we cannot observe, she says, the objects cannot tell us whether the institution was there at all, whereas a trade network is at least a pattern in the movement of physical things.

Dr. K replies that this is exactly backwards: collective acceptance is not invisible. It shows up as the very features we do observe — the standardization of the tokens, the repetition of the practice across houses and generations, the apparent costs or sanctions that keep it going. Were the traces random we would infer no institution; that they are patterned and costly is evidence of the taking-as, not a barrier to it.

At this point Dr. M makes a concession and a retreat. She grants that some mental or institutional claim may be warranted, but says the content is what is unrecoverable: even if we may say there was a shared belief, we cannot say it was a belief in an afterlife, or in ancestors, or in purity — the traces underdetermine the content, and several incompatible belief-systems would produce identical traces. Dr. K responds that underdetermination is not special to archaeology — every theory is underdetermined by its evidence — and that we are entitled to the best explanation of the pattern, provided we do not pretend it is the only one. He adds that if we refuse every claim that is not uniquely determined, we should also refuse the claim that the figurines were deliberately broken, since that too is an inference to intention.

Please adjudicate. For each of the five moves above — (1) Dr. M's initial behavioral restriction and her principle; (2) Dr. K's consistency objection; (3) Dr. M's constitution-versus-causation reply; (4) Dr. K's reply that collective acceptance is observable in pattern and cost; and (5) the closing exchange about content and underdetermination — tell me who is right, or what is right in each, and where each goes too far. Then say which of the two is closer to the truth overall, and identify what both are missing. In particular, I want your view on whether there is a principled way to say which mental or institutional claims the traces support and which they do not, and on whether the whole dispute has been framed by a false choice between behavior-only description and freely inferred belief.
