I am a third-year student writing a paper for a seminar on early modern philosophy and its critics, and the assignment asks me to stage a dialogue between two positions rather than argue for one. Please write that dialogue for me: a defender of Descartes and a Buddhist philosopher in the Abhidharma tradition, arguing over what the cogito actually proves.

Neither character should be a strawman. The Buddhist should be allowed to grant that there is thinking while denying that any enduring thinker is thereby established — a stream of momentary cognitions will do, and the first-person pronoun is a designation laid over that stream rather than a thing found within it. The Cartesian should be allowed the strongest reply available: that doubt, error, and even the Buddhist's own act of denying a self presuppose a single subject that cannot itself be just another item in the sequence. Have them press each other on the hardest case: the meditator's thought is interrupted, by sleep or by a sudden sound, and then resumes — does the resumption disclose a persisting owner of the thoughts, or only a causal continuity among events that no one owns? They should also take up whether the cogito yields a substance, a bare particular, or merely the thin indexical truth that some subject or other is thinking these thoughts, since I suspect the dispute is really decided there and nowhere else.

Begin with the dialogue itself, with no preface or framing. After it, add a short coda in my voice rather than the characters', stating which single argument, if it turned out to be unsound, would most damage each side, and where the dialogue takes liberties with the historical positions or leans on a reading that specialists would contest.
