Please design a 90-minute in-class activity for a value theory seminar of about fifteen advanced undergraduates, aimed at making the students feel the force of the spectrum argument against the transitivity of better than. The students, not I, should build the chain: arrange the rules and materials so that each of them makes a run of pairwise choices along a spectrum in which every step strikes them as an obvious, negligible improvement, and only afterwards notices that the run has carried them from a state they would call plainly good to one they would call plainly bad. Then let the structure bite: I want a moment in which the student who has accepted every step is exposed as exploitable, with a broker or auctioneer trading them around a cycle and leaving them worse off than they began, so that they feel the pragmatic sting of intransitive preference and not merely its logical oddity. No lecture beforehand, and the students should not hear the argument's name until the debrief. Include the materials, the trade or payoff scheme (nothing that needs real money), a timeline, and roles for the broker and any witnesses. In the debrief, map what happened onto the argument and set out the escape routes as a menu rather than a verdict: denying that the betterness relation is transitive; rejecting one of the adjacent-step premises and identifying which; accepting the endpoint; and appealing to incomparability, indeterminacy, or the thought that some items are merely on a par. Make the debrief separate two questions the game will blur, namely whether the value relation itself is intransitive and whether the students' preferences merely are, and say what would have to change in the activity to test the first rather than the second. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in speech.
