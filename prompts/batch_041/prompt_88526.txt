Alright, I've read enough alignment discourse to land on a position and now I want you to try to take it apart. Here's the position: the only bar that matters for calling a system aligned/safe is a worst-case guarantee. Expected-value or average-case reasoning is basically a cope — if there's any reachable scenario where the system does something catastrophic, then being great in the typical case buys you nothing, because we get one shot and the downside is astronomical. So the target should be: for every environment (or every environment in some well-defined class), the system stays under some harm bound. Expected value is the thing you fall back on once you've given up on actually guaranteeing anything.

I want the strongest honest case against this — not a consequentialism-vs-deontology mudfight. Some threads I specifically want pulled:

The vacuity worry. My nagging sense is the whole thing collapses into "pick your adversary class." If the class is rich enough to contain the real world you can't guarantee anything non-trivial, and if it's narrow enough that you can guarantee something you've assumed away the very uncertainty you were scared of. So every worst-case guarantee is either empty or secretly a distributional claim wearing a costume — I've started calling this guarantee laundering in my notes. Is that right? Or is there a way to earn a non-trivial worst-case guarantee that isn't smuggling in a prior? I think this is the crux.

The pessimism tax. Suppose the world is 99.99% benign and 0.01% adversarial, and the worst-case-optimal policy is mediocre-to-bad across the whole distribution while the expected-value policy is excellent almost everywhere and catastrophic in the tail. When is paying that tax actually correct, and when is it just optimizing for a boogeyman? Does the answer change if the tail event is unrecoverable (we don't get to try again) versus merely bad?

Behavior vs outcome. There's a difference between guaranteeing something about the system's behavior and guaranteeing something about the world, since the world (including other agents) responds. A behavioral guarantee can be airtight while the outcome guarantee is wide open. Does that distinction deflate the position, or just relocate it?

Gaming and unfalsifiability. Worst-case claims are easy to make unfalsifiable by loosening the harm bound ("bounded harm" instead of "no catastrophe") or by defining the adversary class after the fact. How much of the position's apparent strength is real and how much is this? I'd like an honest verdict on whether the position risks nothing at all.

Also: what does my position predict that the expected-value camp doesn't, and are any of those predictions already disconfirmed? And what would the people who actually hold a strong worst-case view concede counts against them, as opposed to what critics throw at them?

Please keep what's genuinely settled among people who work on this seperate from what's contested — dont hand me a contested framing as if it were the consensus, and flag where you're giving me your own lean.
