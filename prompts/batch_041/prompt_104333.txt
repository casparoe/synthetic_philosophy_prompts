I am a senior in high school, and I have somehow ended up responsible for leading next week's meeting of our philosophy club on the question of whether the brain is a computer. I do not want you to explain computationalism to me; I want you to plan how I should explain it. The room will hold about a dozen students, most with no background in either philosophy or cognitive science, though two or three will arrive already certain they know the answer — one because neurons are obviously just biological transistors, another because computers obviously cannot feel anything, so the whole idea is beneath serious discussion. I have forty minutes, a whiteboard, and a bad habit of letting discussion run long, and a skeptical math teacher may drop in, so the plan needs to be defensible to someone who will pounce on loose wording. What I need from you is the sequence: which idea has to be firmly in place before the next one can even be understood, and why the order cannot be shuffled. In particular, I suspect the entire session lives or dies on where I separate the modest claim that the brain does something we can usefully describe as computation from the far stronger claim that thinking just is computation, and I know I will blur those two if I improvise. A teacher once said something about levels of description that I did not follow at the time, and I would like the plan to make that idea do real work rather than sit as a slogan.

The part I am most unsure of is the example I should build everything around. I want one concrete case to carry the whole session, and I would rather it not be the neuron-and-transistor comparison, because I have a suspicion that it does the opposite of what people assume and quietly hands the audience the very conclusion I am trying to make them earn. Tell me what the carrying example must contain — what feature of it has to be visible at a glance, and what it must deliberately lack — and give me at least one alternative if the first candidate is contested. Then give me the misconceptions you expect this audience to form, in the order they will actually form them, with the exact point in my sequence where each should be headed off, distinguishing the ones I can correct on the spot from the ones I should let surface and complicate on purpose. Finish with a single closing question I can put to the room that would genuinely reveal whether they grasped the distinction, rather than whether they can repeat my phrasing, and tell me plainly what I should refuse to cover in forty minutes and how to say so without seeming evasive.
