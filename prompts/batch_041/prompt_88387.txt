I chair the analgesia stewardship committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital. Once a month we must decide whether to approve, restrict, or deny treatments and policies that trade pain relief against other goods: opioids that relieve severe pain but carry sedation and dependence risks; expensive comfort interventions that produce mild benefits for many; and rationing decisions that leave some patients with low-level chronic pain untreated so that a smaller group with severe pain can receive a new therapy. We currently decide ad hoc. I want a reusable decision procedure grounded in lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, with an explicit treatment of the commensurability of pleasure and pain. To make the task tractable, assume the following framework, which I am introducing: suffering and pleasure are not placed on a single cardinal scale. Instead, suffering has lexical priority up to a threshold T; no amount of pleasure can compensate for additional suffering below T. Above T, a bounded exchange rate between suffering and pleasure is permitted. T is not a number but a boundary defined by a set of functional capacities—the capacity to form and pursue projects, to sustain social connection, to maintain basic bodily integrity, and to experience a life as one’s own. The procedure should operationalize a two-ledger audit: one ledger records suffering-states, the other records pleasure-states; the suffering ledger must be cleared to T before the pleasure ledger is consulted, and above T the committee may trade between ledgers only within a pre-declared band. Write the procedure for my committee to run in a 45-minute meeting. The committee includes two clinicians, a pharmacist, a philosopher, and a patient advocate; it will use only a standard ethics consult note and patient-reported outcomes, with no formal cost-effectiveness modeling. Specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as sufficient evidence for each; where the procedure stops with a no; where it stops with a yes; where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts; who signs off (the committee recommends, the chief medical officer may veto but not amend); and what the procedure cannot do. Then run it on three cases: (a) a high-dose opioid for a terminal cancer patient whose pain is severe but intermittent, where the opioid will likely shorten life by a few days; (b) denying a mild analgesic to a large group with chronic low back pain so that a new treatment can be funded for a small group with severe neuropathic pain; and (c) a case where the procedure yields a conclusion I will find uncomfortable—either because it permits a trade-off I regard as degrading or because it forbids a trade-off I regard as obviously worthwhile. Be explicit about where the procedure stipulates rather than derives T, and where it will misfire.
