The idea I need them to feel, in my own words: normally we think a threat only works if it's credible, and it's credible only if carrying it out is in the threatener's interest once the moment comes. But there's a weirder move open to the person being threatened: instead of refusing or paying, they can *become a different kind of agent* — adopt a policy of punishing anyone who threatens them, even though the punishment costs them something and even though, if it all works, they'll never actually have to carry it out. If that policy is known (or at least predictable) on the other side, threatening them stops being worth it and the threat never gets made. So the policy is never used, and that's exactly why it works. The "surrogate" part is that the policy isn't your real goal — it's a stand-in you take on because of how it changes other people's reasoning about you. And the "acausal" part is when the threatener is someone you'll never meet or interact with — an agent that's just predicting what you'd do (maybe a simulation of you, maybe someone reasoning about you from far away), so you can't retaliate against it directly and can't negotiate with it. The only lever you have is what kind of agent you can make yourself be, in advance, in a way a reasoner can figure out.

What I want them to walk away seeing: that a self-harming policy can be instrumentally rational because it changes other people's incentives; that the value of a commitment comes entirely from the fact that it never gets exercised; and that when the other side is only predicting you rather than interacting with you, the commitment has to be something they can actually infer about you, which is a much stranger requirement.
