I'm a grants officer at a small foundation — four of us, roughly £2m a year to give away — and our remit is long-term risk, which means we constantly recieve proposals built on an argument I can't shake and can't use. The shape is always the same: a tiny probability of something astronomically good, multiplied out into a number that swamps everything else on the spreadsheet, and the person sending it is completely sincere. We've had someone ask for £30k to buy lottery tickets, on the theory that the jackpot funds the entire programme; someone else wanting us to bankroll a physics idea that, if it works, unlocks effectively free energy; and last cycle a proposal arguing we should put our whole reserve into a single moonshot because the expected value comes out to something like a googol. I've read enough to know there's a real literature here — Pascal's mugging, fanaticism, bounded utility, the difference between a small chance of a big finite payoff and an infinitesimal chance of something infinite — but I can't get any of it into a shape my colleagues will accept, and the board wants reasons in writing. What I actually need from you is a decision procedure I can run on a proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it has to hand off to a person or the board because the question is one of values rather than facts. Assume an afternoon per proposal, no maths beyond arithmetic, and that I have to write my reasoning up in a page.

Things it needs to handle: what to do when the proposal hands me a probability that's really a guess about whether the whole theory is right rather than about whether a coin lands heads, since those seem to deflate in completely different ways; the fact that if I accept the argument once, I'm committed to funding an infinite regress of ever-more-improbable schemes, because someone will always turn up with a bigger number; and the boring modest proposal where my instinct is just to fund it and get on with my day. It should also be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure — I'd much rather know where I'm the one deciding than have it papered over.

Then run it on two of the ones above: the lottery tickets, and the moonshot that wants the reserves. And give me one case where the procedure tells me something I won't like — say a proposal we've been funding for years that it says to drop, or a long shot it says to take seriously. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my question. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: where it will misfire, and what I should escalate rather than pretend to decide.
