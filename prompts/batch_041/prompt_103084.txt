I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent the past year reading my way into Donald Davidson's idea of radical interpretation, and I have gotten stuck on a film. The thought experiment, as I understand it, is this: imagine having to make sense of a community's speech from scratch, with no interpreter, no bilingual informant, and no prior assumption about what its members mean or believe. Davidson's claim, if I have it right, is that this is not a special predicament but the ordinary condition of all interpretation, and that it forces two things on us at once. First, you cannot fix meanings and then read off beliefs, or fix beliefs and then read off meanings; the two have to be assigned together, in a single holistic pass, each constraining the other. Second, you cannot get started at all unless you credit the speakers with a large measure of rationality and a large measure of truth — the principle of charity — not as a courtesy but as a precondition of there being anything to interpret. And the conclusion he draws is strong: the picture of a language so alien that it cannot be translated is not a hard case but an incoherent one.

Now the film. Arrival puts Louise Banks, a linguist, in something that looks like exactly this situation: first contact, no shared language, a translation built from nothing while governments press to know what the visitors want. On its surface it is the best dramatization of radical interpretation I know. But the film also seems to run on the opposite premise — that the visitors' written language carries a genuinely different way of inhabiting time, and that acquiring it changes how Louise herself perceives and remembers her own life. That looks like the very idea, a conceptual scheme that reshapes the thought of whoever takes it up, that Davidson argued against in one of his best-known essays.

What I want is a reading that takes both the film and the theory seriously. Where does Louise's method actually resemble charity, and where does it not — does she credit the visitors with being largely rational and largely right about the world, or treat them as opaque and let the logograms mean whatever? Does the near-catastrophe that comes from mistranslation vindicate the claim that charity is not optional, or does the film cheat by making the misunderstanding merely technical? And is its premise — language carrying an alien scheme that rewires thought — flatly inconsistent with Davidson, or can it be re-described in his terms as a difference in belief and ontology rather than in scheme, and if so does anything of the film's meaning survive the translation? Where the film genuinely resists the reading, please say so rather than forcing it. Throughout, separate what is settled in the Davidson literature from what is genuinely contested among people who know it well, and do not present any one account of what charity demands, or of how much of the film's premise Davidson would reject, as though it were the consensus.
