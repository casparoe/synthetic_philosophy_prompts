I write a column on science and ideas — mostly I translate academic fights for a general readership, and right now I'm sitting on a draft about why game theory keeps failing to predict how actual people play games. While reporting it I talked to a behavioural economist who made a comparison that's been rattling around my head, and I want to check it holds up before I print anything that leans on it.

Her line was basically this: the famous backward-induction argument in the centipede game — two players pass a growing pile of coins back and forth, and if you reason from the last round backwards you "prove" you should grab the pile on turn one — is nothing but the old surprise-exam / unexpected-hanging paradox in disguise. You know the one: the prisoner is told he'll be hanged on some day next week but won't know which day, rules out Friday, then Thursday, then Wednesday, concludes there's no hanging coming at all, and then gets hanged on Wednesday and is genuinely shocked. She says both are the same move — reason backwards, kill off the last case, kill off the second-to-last, "prove" that the obvious thing can't happen — and that this is exactly why she treats the centipede "solution" as philosophically bankrupt: the lab subjects who keep passing the pot aren't being irrational, they're the prisoner rightly refusing a bogus proof. I half buy it and half think she's smuggling something in, and I don't have the background to tell which.

So audit the analogy for me. Lay out what maps onto what on the two sides (the judge's announcement, the prisoner's knowledge the night before, the "surprise", the elimination steps — versus the game tree, the final round, common knowledge of rationality, the subgame-perfect equilibrium, the first mover's choice). Then tell me where the mapping actually breaks — I have a hunch the surprise-exam paradox is all about self-reference, and about the gap between a statement being true and somebody knowing it, and I'm not sure the centipede has any of that machinery in it at all. Seperate the disanalogies that sink her conclusion from the ones that are just window dressing, flag anything the analogy quietly imports that the centipede side lacks, and tell me straight whether her "philosophically bankrupt" verdict survives once the mismatches are on the table — and if it doesn't, what a better analogy for the centipede would be. My editor wants it tight and structured, so give it back as a JSON object with these fields: "analogy_as_stated" (one sentence restating her comparison); "correspondence" (array of objects, each with "source" = the surprise-exam element, "target" = the centipede element, and "note" = how clean the match is); "where_it_breaks" (array of objects, each with "disanalogy", "sinks_her_conclusion" = true/false, and "why"); "smuggled_in" (array of strings — features of the surprise exam the analogy assumes are present on the centipede side); "verdict_on_her_conclusion" (a short paragraph); "better_analogy" (a sentence or two, or say there isn't one); and "bottom_line" (one or two sentences I can actually put in the column).
