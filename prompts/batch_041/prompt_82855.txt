For the past two years I have sat on the steering committee of an interdisciplinary centre on culture and moral cognition: the psychologists run the experiments, and I am the resident philosopher, whose job is to keep us honest about what the results could possibly show. Last month a visiting speaker walked us through a now-familiar battery of cross-cultural findings on sacrificial dilemmas and concluded that moral truth is parochial; I found myself unable to say crisply what further evidence would have changed his mind, which is part of why I am writing. We have funding for roughly six postdoctoral fellows over three years, and the committee has asked me to circulate a memo ranking the lines of inquiry we should seed first. My difficulty is that the field's obvious priorities and its philosophically load-bearing ones are not the same, and I would rather defend a considered ordering than produce a list.

What I want from you is that memo, or rather the argument for it: an ordering of the open questions in cross-cultural moral psychology by how much each matters for the metaethical payoff, that is, for whether the cross-cultural record gives us any reason to revise our views about whether there are moral truths and whether we could know them. Please treat the following as candidate items, and add or strike as you see fit. First, whether the variation the WEIRD literature documents is variation in verdicts, in the weights assigned to shared considerations, or in the folk concept of the moral domain itself, so that the very category we are measuring turns out to be parochial. Second, whether the moral/conventional distinction and the alleged universal of harm are substantive universals or artefacts of instruments built on Western undergraduates. Third, whether folk metaethical attitudes, objectivism and relativism and the sense that some disagreements are faultless, vary across cultures, and if so whether they are causes or consequences of moral disagreement. Fourth, whether the genealogy-and-debunking route from a cultural explanation of a moral belief to a verdict against it can be made to work without assuming what it sets out to prove. I have come to think the crux for most of these is what I have been calling the calibration problem: variation in judgment tells you that someone is misjudging only if you already know who, so the empirical record threatens realism only given a premise about the reliability of the divergent faculties, and the real question is whether any empirical work could supply that premise rather than presuppose it. Tell me whether that diagnosis is correct or whether it is a philosopher's evasion.

For each item, I want the case for its placement: why it is upstream of the others, in the sense that other questions change depending on how it comes out; how tractable it is for a group with our mix of skills; whether it is genuinely neglected or merely fashionable; and what specific finding over the next three years would move it up or down the ranking. Then rank them, name the two we should fund first, name the ones I should be willing to leave unfunded, and be explicit about the criteria you are applying and where they conflict. If the ordering flips depending on whether we weight philosophical importance or tractability, say so, and tell me which weighting you would defend and why.
