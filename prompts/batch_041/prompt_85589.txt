A friend in my reading group dropped this on me last week and I still can't tell if it's a real proof or a card trick.

We were argueing about whether the probability of an 'if A then B' claim is just the probability of B given A. I said obviously, that's what 'if' means, and he said fine, grant it, and watch what happens. Here's his argument:

(1) An 'if A then B' claim is a claim like any other — it's either true or false, so it has a probability. Call it P(A→B).
(2) Grant the obvious thing: P(A→B) = P(B|A), whenever P(A) > 0.
(3) Since A→B is a claim like any other, you can conditionalize on things. Learn C, and your new probability for A→B is your old P(A→B|C). And the result of conditionalizing is still a probability function, so rule (2) still applies to it.
(4) So for any C with P(A and C) > 0: P(A→B|C) = P(B|A and C).
(5) Now let C be B itself. Then P(A→B|B) = P(B|A and B) = 1, and P(A→B|not-B) = P(B|A and not-B) = 0.
(6) So P(A→B) = 1 × P(B) + 0 × P(not-B) = P(B).
(7) But by (2), P(A→B) = P(B|A). So P(B|A) = P(B), for any A and B, as long as A is compatible with both B and not-B.
(8) That's obviously false — the chance of rain given clouds isn't the chance of rain. So (2) has to go.

Every line looks like it's just shuffling symbols around, but (8) can't be right, so at least one of them has to be doing something sneaky. I'd rather keep (2) than (8) — I lean on (2) constantly, like when I ask myself how likely it is that if I take the job, I'll be miserable. So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? And if the trick is really in (3) or (4), tell me whats left of the equation — can I still use conditional probability to reason about conditionals, when I'm updating or deciding what to do?
