My old PhD supervisor died a few weeks ago and his wife has asked me to give the eulogy at the funeral on Saturday. I'd like you to write it, but you need the full picture first.

He was a yeast geneticist for about forty years, a real bench scientist with a solid publication record, but the thing he'll actually be remembered for -- at least among the small number of people who care -- is a fight he picked early and never put down: he thought it was flatly wrong to say that DNA is a code. Not wrong in the usual way people mean it, where the metaphor is fine as long as you know it's a metaphor. He thought the metaphor had stopped being shorthand and turned into a false picture that even good scientists were now reasoning with, and that the damage showed up in how everyone from doctors to journalists talks about genes. His argument, as best I can lay it out: a stretch of DNA does chemistry -- it gets copied, transcribed, translated, and a protein comes out -- and that's all that happens; there's no meaning anywhere in the room. For something to be a code you need a convention fixing what each symbol stands for, the way a convention fixes that a red light means stop. But nothing fixes what a codon 'means' except the fact that one particular molecule happens to grab another particular molecule. That's like saying the shape of a key is encoded in the shape of the lock. And he'd add: if you're willing to call any reliable causal relationship 'information', then the tide carries information about the moon and the word stops doing any work. He knew the comeback -- that the assignments are arbitrary, a frozen accident, which is supposed to prove it really is a code -- and he'd say arbitrariness gets you a cipher, not a code, and a cipher still needs somebody using it. His line was that the only place a code exists is in the textbooks. What he thought you should say instead is that a gene is something that makes a difference, not something that says anything.

Here's the hard part. The room will be roughly half his lab and his collaborators -- molecular biologists, most of whom use the word code every working day, and two or three of whom spent years telling him this was philosophical nitpicking. The other half is family and old friends who have no idea there was ever an argument and are simply there to grieve. His son is a molecular biologist too and talks about the genetic code constantly in his own work; they loved each other and I don't want the son sitting there feeling like the eulogy is a rebuke to him. And his widow pulled me aside and said keep it to five minutes and dont turn it into a seminar. The department chair speaks after me and will almost certainly say he helped read the book of life, so if there's a light way to make that harmless, I'm listening.

What I want from you: a eulogy in which I never use the vocabulary he spent his life objecting to. No code, no blueprint, no instructions, no program, no book of life, no decoded -- unless it's to mark that he would have hated the phrase. The trick is that the family should not even notice the constraint is there; it should just sound like a warm eulogy about a stubborn, funny, generous man. The biologists should notice exactly what's going on, and it should land with them as a form of respect rather than a gotcha. Don't have me announce that I'm avoiding anything either -- the second I flag it, it's a stunt. It has to be true to what he actually thought without being about the argument; I'm not settling the dispute at a funeral and I'm not pretending he won it. And it needs alot of him in it: the man who put the word code in the first paragraph of every grant application and then grumbled about it over dinner because that was the price of getting funded, the man who taught me to run the control twice and never to trust a pretty gel.

Write the eulogy, about five minutes spoken.
