Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of four hours about whether maximal moral goodness is something worth wanting, and we spent most of it circling the same fork without either of us landing a clean hit on the other. I would like a dialogue that does better than we did.

Write a dialogue between two characters, Iris and Nadia, who hold roughly the positions my friend and I held. Both should be fully competent; neither is a foil, and neither should be made to argue a version of their view that is easier to knock down than the view itself.

Nadia takes the demanding view: there is no principled stopping point short of maximal moral goodness; the goods we invoke when we refuse — leisure, attachment, aesthetic pleasure, an untroubled life — are agent-relative goods whose apparent weight does not survive the move to an impartial standpoint; and our resistance to this is a self-serving distortion rather than a moral insight. She should be permitted her strongest reply: that the objector confuses the goodness of a life with its pleasantness, and that we do not normally treat the costliness of virtue as an objection to it.

Iris takes the critical view, but keep her away from the well-worn moves (that saints are boring, that they crowd out non-moral excellence, that they are insufferable). Her case should turn on two things. First, a distinction I will call output against quality: the demanding view defines maximal moral goodness by what a life produces or discharges — duties met, harms averted, goods delivered — while the ideal under attack concerns the quality of a life, and nothing shows that a maximal score on the one is a maximal score on the other. Second, the second-personal point: a life organized around maximal output cannot be the object of the partial love that is itself a good, and the standard reply — that love is a good instrument for impartial ends — appears to make the beloved a means.

Anchor the argument in a shared exhibit rather than in abstractions: a mutual acquaintance who has given away most of her income, donated a kidney, and is by everyone's account neither happy nor easy to be close to. Nadia should be able to say that this is simply what the ideal looks like from the outside; Iris should be able to say that it shows the ideal has no room for a good she takes to be real.

Then let them fight properly. Nadia should get to reply that the demand for a common currency between moral goodness and the rest of a life is the objection's weak point — that a life can be morally improved without being improved all things considered, and that no one needs to say what a friendship is worth in units of good done. Iris should say what that reply costs her, and Nadia what hers costs. Let each concede something uncomfortable for their own side, and let them arrive somewhere neither expected to be — including, if it turns out to be honest, on whether the verdict of the person living such a life about her own life carries any authority. Neither should cite or quote anyone; they should argue. A reader who has never encountered these debates should be able to follow, but no line should be spent on common ground both characters already share.
