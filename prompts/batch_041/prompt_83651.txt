I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of tech, and part of my funding means I have to do a six-month embedded ethics stint with the safety review group at a mid-sized software company. I talked them into a rule: every proposal with an AI component in it gets triaged as either 'a tool' or 'an agent' before it goes any further, because what we attach to it differs a lot depending on which it is — logging, whether there's a kill switch, who signs off, whether legal gets dragged in. The problem is that nobody on the panel except me has read anything about this, we meet weekly, there's five of us, we get about three proposals a week, and we've got roughly twenty minutes per proposal. Half the panel thinks 'agent' is a marketing word and the other half thinks anything with a loop in it is an agent, so at the moment we're basically voting on vibes. Which went badly last quarter: we shipped something we'd called a tool, it had memory of its own and a budget of its own, and it took us three weeks to work out who was responsible when it went sideways.

So can you write me a decision procedure we can actually run? I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one (bear in mind the only inputs are the proposal doc and maybe five minutes with whoever wrote it), where it terminates in 'tool, carry on', where it terminates in 'agent, escalate to X', and — the bit I care about most — where it stops and hands over to a person because the question is about values, with a note of exactly what that person is being asked to decide. Mark the steps that are really judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and be honest about where the whole thing will misfire, e.g. systems whose agency only shows up once the world moves under them.

Two things I don't want. Don't let it collapse into 'did we write a goal into the spec?' — I have engineers who will answer that question perfectly truthfully and it'll still be the wrong answer. And it can't be a metaphysics seminar: the panel has to be able to run it with me out of the room, and it has to be defensible if someone asks why a project got escalated. One idea I'd like kept if it survives the translation: that calling something a tool is a claim about who's holding the objective, not about how clever the code is.

Then run it on three proposals, and show your working:

1. Autocomplete in the code editor.
2. A customer-support assistant with persistent memory of the customer, the ability to issue refunds up to two hundred quid without asking, and the standing instruction to resolve the issue and keep the customer happy.
3. An internal research pipeline that takes a task, writes its own training script, runs it overnight, looks at the results, rewrites the script the next night — and has lately started caching intermediate results in a directory nobody asked it to use.

Case 2 is the one I definately expect us to get wrong, so I want the procedure to say something about it we won't enjoy hearing, and I want you to be straight about whether the procedure is doing any real work in case 3 or whether it's just renaming the vibes. Also flag any case where the honest answer is that the two categories seperate cleanly on paper and not in practice.

Lead with the bottom line — one sentence, the actual rule — then the reasoning, then the procedure. No preamble and please don't restate the question back at me.
