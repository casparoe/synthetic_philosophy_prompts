I teach a ninety-minute session in an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on Darwinian explanation, and what I need is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation. The subject is Dennett's contrast between cranes and skyhooks and the charge of "greedy reductionism" he introduced alongside it, and most of the group has met the crane-and-skyhook slogan secondhand, chiefly in online arguments about artificial intelligence, so the metaphor tends to arrive already attached to the wrong question. Lay out the order in which the ideas have to come, so that each has something to stand on — in particular, say what must be established about natural selection as a substrate-neutral algorithm before either term can be introduced without circularity — then name the single case that should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack. My instinct is the eye, and a colleague has proposed instead the alphabet, on the grounds that it is an artifact which, once assembled, lets people do cognitive work none of them could do unaided; tell me which carries the distinction better and why, and why the obvious alternative is a trap. Next, the misconceptions to expect and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off: I am braced for "a skyhook is just anything non-physical," "greedy reductionism just means reductionism," and "so Dennett thinks every higher-level explanation is illegitimate," but several of them will also want to map crane/skyhook straight onto reductionist/emergentist, and I would rather not discover that only in discussion. Finish with one question I can put to the room that would distinguish genuine grasp of the distinction from command of the vocabulary, and tell me what I should openly decline to take up at this level rather than gesture at.
