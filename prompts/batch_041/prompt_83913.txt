I teach philosophy of science, and my department's reading group recently spent a session on Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, specifically on whether the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts can do any real critical work once one takes seriously his insistence that a programme may recover after a long degenerating stretch. Two colleagues carried the argument well past the end of the hour, and I have been trying to referee it ever since. They were working with a shared example: a disease-research programme whose central commitment concerns a particular class of molecular aggregate, with the protective belt consisting of claims about which forms of that aggregate are harmful and under what conditions. For roughly two decades the belt was repeatedly adjusted in the face of anomalies in ways that produced no new predictions and merely kept the programme consistent with the data; then the group working in it generated a novel prediction about an unexpected class of cases, which was subsequently confirmed; since then the programme has again produced mostly consistency-saving adjustments. Call the two of them H, a historian of science, and P, a philosopher. H says Lakatos's appraisals are indexed to time periods, so the correct verdict is that the programme was degenerating for the first stretch and progressive during the brief stretch in which the novel prediction was made and confirmed, and that nobody was irrational at any point; on this reading the distinction is not a verdict on a programme as such but on particular problem shifts, and the same programme can earn different verdicts at different times. P replies that this is precisely what makes the framework toothless: if every failure is only a verdict-for-now, and every later success licenses a redescription of the earlier stretch, then no possible evidence could oblige anyone to declare a programme dead, and the appraisal becomes a promissory note rather than a judgment. H counters that time-indexing does not make a verdict indeterminate, since a dated appraisal can be perfectly definite and still revisable, much as a weather forecast can be specific and later superseded, and that the recovery clause is what licenses the sensible advice to keep working on a currently degenerating programme when the rival is immature and the anomaly is stubborn. P then presses two further points. First, if the rational advice is to keep going anyway, the progressive/degenerating distinction is doing no work in decisions about where to invest effort, and the real explanatory labor is performed by sociological facts about training, funding, and prestige. Second, the hard core generates an identity problem: in the shared example the practitioners gradually changed what they meant by the programme's central commitment, and if the core can be reinterpreted without the programme ceasing to be the same programme, then the unit of appraisal is fixed by communal say-so rather than by any logical structure, which means the thing being appraised is a social group and not a research programme. H answers that this conflates the hard core with a set of propositions that must remain literally unchanged: the core is whatever practitioners refuse to abandon, and sameness of programme is a matter of heuristic continuity, meaning the lineage of problems, techniques, and exemplary results, which the example preserves. H adds that P's unfalsifiability charge boomerangs, since a verdict of progressive is itself falsifiable: if the promised novel predictions never pan out, the verdict is simply wrong. Neither would concede, and the group broke up with each convinced the other had changed the subject.

I want a genuine adjudication rather than a summary of the two positions. Please go through the exchange move by move — H's time-indexing claim, P's toothlessness objection, H's weather-forecast analogy, H's persistence argument, P's decision-theoretic point, P's identity problem, and H's heuristic-continuity reply — and rate each move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating and flagging any place where a move is right under one defensible reading of Lakatos and wrong under another. Then address what I take to be the questions at the bottom of the dispute: (a) Is the recovery clause best read as a claim about the rationality of continuing to work on a degenerating programme, or as a license for retrospective redescription, and can these two readings be separated without loss? (b) Does Lakatos's framework contain any resources for saying, at a given time, that a programme is dead, and if so, who bears the burden of proof, the programme's defender or its critic? (c) Is the identity problem fatal to research programmes as units of appraisal, or can heuristic continuity do the work that logical content cannot? Finally, tell me what both colleagues are missing, including anything either would have to concede if the other's strongest point were pushed as far as it will go. Please write in American English, and end with a single final line in the form \answer{...} containing a compact verdict of no more than thirty words that names which of the two, if either, is more right overall and identifies the single point on which the dispute should turn.
