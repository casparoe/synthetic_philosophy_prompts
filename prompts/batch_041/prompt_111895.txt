I am the philosopher on a government behavioural insights advisory panel, and I want to test a distinction I have been pressing on my colleagues. The ethical debate about nudging is organised around belief and consent — whether the person would endorse the intervention, whether their options were misrepresented — but most of what actually moves people in these programmes operates on attention rather than belief: a default, a placement, a reminder, a change of font leaves the option set and the agent's beliefs about it intact and changes only what she notices. Please describe the most important implications of treating attention-directed influence, rather than deception or coercion, as the central case for the ethics of nudging. I am most interested in what follows for the permissibility of opt-out defaults, for where the burden of justification should fall as between the state and the person influenced, and for whether manipulation remains a distinct moral category once belief and consent are held fixed. Please close by naming the single consideration that would most likely lead you to revise your answer, and by saying where in your reasoning you take the greatest risk of being wrong.
