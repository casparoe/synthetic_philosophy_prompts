I'm giving the best man speech at my brother's wedding in a few weeks. He had a corpus callosotomy when he was eight — the surgery that cuts the corpus callosum — and he's been seizure-free and totally fine since, but a chunk of our family has read enough pop neuroscience to be weird about it. They'll say things like which one of him is getting married, or ask his fiancée, who is a philosopher of mind, whether she's marrying one person or two. She studies unity of consciousness, and about half the wedding guests are her department, so they'll catch every reference; the other half are our aunts and uncles who just want a nice toast and maybe a laugh. I need a four-minute toast that is warm and funny and doesn't treat him as a case study, but that actually uses the split-brain material to say something true about marriage. The philosophy has to do real work here, not just be a cute reference. I definately don't want it to turn into a lecture.

Here's the stuff I want you to build it around, and please explain it in the speech so the non-philosophers can follow: the classic split-brain findings where the left hemisphere confabulates a reason for an action that was actually triggered by the right hemisphere; cross-cuing, where the two hemispheres can learn to coordinate through external cues; the difference between unity of consciousness (it's all one experience) and unity of agency (it's all one agent); and the debate over whether split-brain patients have two streams of consciousness, one fragmented stream, or something in between — partial unity, unity that comes in degrees. Then I want you to use a half-baked idea I've been kicking around, which you can name the shared checkbook view of unity: two sub-systems count as one person to the extent they can co-sign for each other's commitments, not because they share a single stream of experience. On that view, unity is something you do, not something you have. Marriage, I think, is a lot like that — two people who don't merge into one consciousness but who become one in the eyes of the law and the bank and the family by co-signing each other's life.

So: write the toast. It should open with something that gets the room on side, have at least one real laugh (not a groaner), include one moment where the philosophers in the room can tell you actually understand the material, and land on something my brother and his fiancée would actually want said in front of their families. Don't quote any real texts or cite any papers — no one wants a bibliography at a wedding. Don't use the phrase two halves of a whole unless you're making fun of it. Keep it around 600-700 words. End with a single line that reads \answer{...} and inside the braces put a one-sentence summary of the toast's central claim.
