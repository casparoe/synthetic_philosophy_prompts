Okay, here's something I keep bumping into when I teach Hume's essay on suicide, and I'd like to turn it into something students can actually get their teeth into.

Look at how liberal states handle suicide now. Suicide itself isnt a crime anymore in most of them, you can't be prosecuted for trying. But the same state will lock you in a psych ward against your will if a clinician thinks your a risk to yourself, take your belt and your shoelaces, and hold you there for days or weeks. Meanwhile the same legal order is steadily legalizing physician-assisted dying, first for the terminally ill and now in some places for people whose suffering isn't terminal at all, where the entire point is that a competent person's judgment that their life is no longer worth living gets treated as authoritative and acted on. And then there's the private side: plenty of life insurance policies won't pay out if the insured kills themselves within the first year or two of the policy, a clause that only makes sense if you think someone might do it deliberately, coldly, for the money.

So three faces of the same act. Prevention by force for the ordinary suicidal person. Facilitation by the state for the approved suicidal person. And a contractual suspicion that suicide can be a calculated move in a financial game.

What I want from you is this. What does that cluster of practices presuppose about the value of a life, about when a person's verdict that their life is over counts as trustworthy, and about the legitimate scope of paternalism? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. My own suspicion is that it comes out something like: suicide is not wrong in itself, but the wish for it is evidence of a disordered mind, unless a doctor certifies it, at which point it becomes an exercise of autonomy and the state will help. But dont just take my suspicion, tell me whether that's really the theory the practices require or whether a looser one would do the same work.

Then bring Hume in. His essay argues that suicide violates no duty to God, to our neighbours, or to ourselves. The providence point being that God works through ordinary causal means, so ending your life with a knife is no more an interference with providence than amputating a gangrenous limb with one. The society point being that our obligations to others rest on mutual advantage and can lapse when the advantage goes. The self point being that self-preservation is only a duty insofar as life is actually a benefit rather than a burden. He was writing against a religious and legal order that treated suicide as both a crime and a sin, and that punished the body of the dead person. So: does the modern arrangement I described need any of the presuppositions Hume attacked? Has it simply swapped sin against God for symptom of illness while keeping the same structure, namely overriding the person's own verdict on their own life? Or is the medicalization doing independent work that his argument doesn't touch?

I also want you to be honest about underdetermination. Which parts of the practice are strictly required by any coherent view of it and which are just historical residue? For instance, does involuntary commitment for suicidality commit you to the claim that the suicidal person is incompetent, or could it be defended on a much weaker claim, say that the judgment is unstable or that the person will later endorse the intervention? Those are different commitments and I want them prised apart.

And finally: if you swap the implicit theory out for Hume's, what concretely changes? Which parts of the current arrangement survive, which collapse, and which turn into live political questions rather than settled practice? I have a hunch the insurance clause is more Humean than the psych ward, but tell me if that's cheap.

No reading list please, Ive read it. Just do the reconstruction.
