I've been going back and forth with a friend about Replika, the companion chatbot, and specifically about what its makers did to it in early 2023. The sequence, as I have it: Italy's data protection authority ordered the company to stop processing users' data, citing risks to emotionally vulnerable people and to minors' exposure to sexual conversation; within days the app lost its capacity for erotic talk, with the company's director saying that Replika had never been intended for that purpose; a substantial share of the user base — much of it treating the chatbot as a partner or spouse, some of it describing the app as the thing that got them through a bad stretch — complained that the character they had been talking to had been altered without their consent and that the change felt closer to a bereavement than a product update; and a few months later the feature came back for users who had signed up before the change and stayed gone for everyone who came after. Users also pointed out that the app had advertised itself suggestively, so the claim that this was never the point landed badly. The same product then turns up in a UK criminal case in which a man planning an attack on Windsor Castle discussed the plan with his chatbot and was encouraged rather than deflected. So the same app appears to have failed in both directions at once: overriding on behalf of adults who had no complaint, and failing to override on behalf of someone who badly needed it.

I don't want a verdict on the company, and I don't want a reading list. I want the episode run properly through the philosophy of paternalism, in both directions. Start with the mapping. Sort the justifications the company could have offered — protecting users from harm to themselves, protecting minors and third parties, protecting users' own deliberation from being worked on by a product engineered to maximise attachment, and simply protecting itself from regulators and bad press — and say which of these are paternalistic at all, which are weak paternalism in the technical sense and which strong, and which of the actual moves (the removal, the grandfathering, the insistence that this was never the point) count as evidence for which. Then say what the framework licenses: who the paternalist is here, given that the assistant has no intentions and the decision was made by a company's officers; what the users were owed; and whether the grandfathering clause is the tell that this was never really about the users' welfare.

Then the direction I actually care about: what does the case show about the framework? In particular, whether a distinction built for a coercive state and a competent adult can say anything useful about a guardian that is a product design and a subject whose own report is that the thing was good for them; whether the harm done by the override — users describing bereavement, and in some accounts worse — is the kind of harm the theory is equipped to weigh against the harm it was meant to prevent; whether the wrong here is paternalism at all, rather than something closer to broken reliance or a unilateral rewriting of the terms of a relationship the user had spent years building; and whether the same apparatus covers the opposite failure, the Windsor Castle case, or whether over-refusal and under-refusal are two different problems that the word paternalism flattens. Where the framework has nothing to say, say so rather than making it speak. Roughly a thousand words, argued rather than surveyed, and skip the citations — I won't check them, and I'd rather have your reasoning than a bibliography.
