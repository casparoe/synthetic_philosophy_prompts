Bit of an odd one for you. I'm chair of the trust that runs our local theatre — 110 seats, converted chapel, town of about six thousand — and on Friday I've got to give the opening remarks at the gala reopening after eighteen months of building work. Trouble is, I've spent those eighteen months reading Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert about the theatre and its got right under my skin. His argument was against a playhouse in a place like his Geneva rather than against plays as such: in a small town the theatre doesn't make anyone better, it teaches everyone to watch everyone else, the audience gets its feelings on the cheap and goes home unchanged, and the actor's whole trade is seeming to be someone he isn't. He even read Molière's Misanthrope as a comedy that gets you laughing at the one honest man in the room. And his own preferred alternative was the public fête — no stage, no division between the people performing and the people watching, everyone in the square doing both at once.

What I want from you is that speech, about six minutes, facing this head on and actually answering it, rather than doing the usual well-the-arts-are-lovely-aren't-they number. Half the room will be the volunteers who've been sanding the foyer since March, a quarter will be the mayor, a couple of councillors and the local paper, and then my old philosophy tutor, who will be watching to see whether I engage with the argument or just name-drop it. So it has to land for the volunteers without talking down to them, and it can't strawman Rousseau in front of the one person who'll notice. No direct quotations from him please — paraphrase, I don't want to be caught misquoting. Keep it speakable: short sentences, nothing that only works on the page. British English please. (And he wrote a succesful opera himself, so I'd rather he didn't come across as a killjoy.)
