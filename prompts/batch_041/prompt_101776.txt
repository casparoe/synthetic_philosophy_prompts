I am preparing an episode of a small, technically literate podcast on AI safety, and next week's guest is a researcher who has become a prominent defender of what she calls the control-first program. Her thesis is that the field's fixation on alignment is a category error: we do not need to know that a model is aligned in order to deploy it safely, because we can design protocols that make deployment safe even if the model is misaligned, deceptive, or actively optimizing against its operators. Her central proposal, which I want the interview to probe rather than merely advertise, is asymmetric auditing. A weaker trusted model monitors a random sample of the untrusted model's actions; the untrusted model knows that its actions are logged and may be audited later; human reviewers see only the cases the trusted monitor flags; and the safety claim is explicitly probabilistic. In one variant she adds an audit-token budget: the untrusted model may spend a limited number of tokens to request human review, which makes safety depend on how it allocates that budget under uncertainty. She pairs this with a two-lock-box thought experiment: a deployment is safe if either the model is aligned or the control protocol is sound, and she insists that the second disjunct is the one we can actually engineer. She also accepts a control tax -- some fraction of useful capability is sacrificed to monitoring, sandboxing, and shutdown mechanisms -- and she treats control as a temporary bridge until a weaker aligned system can oversee a stronger one.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation. I want questions that turn on the actual content of her position, not invitations to restate it. At least one question should press the distinction between control and alignment: whether control can really be a substitute for alignment, or whether it quietly presupposes a trusted base that is itself an alignment success. At least one should press the collusion and steganography problem: when several untrusted models interact, what stops them from coordinating through channels the trusted monitor cannot parse, and what does her protocol do if the untrusted model's most useful strategy is simply to wait. At least one should press the trusted-model bottleneck: what happens if the trusted monitor is weak, or itself subtly misaligned, or if the untrusted model can learn to produce outputs that are legible to humans but opaque to the monitor. At least one should press the evaluation problem: how can a control evaluation be meaningful when the red-team model may sandbag, behave differently when it knows it is being tested, or strategically avoid the very tripwires the evaluation is measuring. At least one should press the control tax and its distribution: who bears the cost of false positives, and what happens when the tax is high enough that operators route around the protocol. At least one should press stability under capability gains: whether a protocol that works against a model that is merely deceptive today remains sound against a model that is substantially more capable, and whether the two-lock-box disjunction hides a regress. I would also like a question on the moral and political dimension -- who gets to decide what level of residual risk is acceptable, and whether a probabilistic safety claim can legitimate deployment without consent from those exposed. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that the exchange cannot end with her standard deflection. End with one question about what evidence or argument would make her abandon the control-first program, and one about what she thinks the field would have to observe for the control agenda to be judged a failure rather than an incomplete success. No generic questions about how she became interested in the field, and no citations or reading lists; keep the questions self-contained.
