I am setting the compulsory essay for the final examination of a third-year undergraduate module in applied ethics. The module closes with the lifeboat metaphor for our duties to strangers — the boat at capacity, the swimmers in the water, the question of who may decide — and my students have learned to reproduce the familiar objections to it without ever asking what the metaphor's normative machinery actually is. I want this year's question to force them to test that machinery by setting a different piece of maritime law beside it.

The students will receive the following background with the question. Please work it into the question paper in your own words, so that nothing outside the paper is needed:

- General average. Where a loss is deliberately and voluntarily incurred for the common safety of a sea venture — cargo jettisoned in a storm to lighten a foundering ship — the loss is not left where it falls. It is borne proportionately by all who benefit, each contributing in proportion to the value saved. The doctrine has three conditions: a common danger in which ship, cargo and crew all share; a voluntary sacrifice of part for the sake of the whole; and the success of the venture, since the contributions are assessed only if the ship and the rest of the cargo come through. Assessment is retrospective, carried out afterwards by an adjuster who can inspect the records and fix the values.
- The master's duty of assistance. A master who is in a position to assist persons in distress at sea must do so. The duty is qualified: it does not require him to act where doing so would seriously endanger his own vessel or those aboard.

The case they are to work with: a single lifeboat, rated for fewer than it holds, carrying the crew of a foundered ship together with people picked up from the water; water and provisions sufficient for some but not all to reach land; heavy weather that may swamp a boat loaded past its capacity. The occupants were strangers to one another before the foundering and have made no agreement among themselves.

The question should be, in substance, whether general average can supply the normative model for deciding what the lifeboat's occupants owe one another and owe the swimmers, or whether the analogy breaks at a point that matters. I want it constructed so that it cannot be answered by reciting the standard objections, and so that a good answer must:

(a) identify what general average presupposes — a common venture, a loss that can be made good to the party who bore it, contributors who can be charged, and an adjudicator working after the event on the strength of records — and test each presupposition against the lifeboat;
(b) notice that the duty of assistance is a threshold duty with a self-preservation cap, so that it lapses wholesale once the boat is full, and consider whether a rule of shared sacrifice on the general-average model would convert that threshold into a continuous duty by making the occupants bear part of the risk rather than none of it;
(c) confront the fact that the party whose share is sacrificed in the lifeboat cannot be compensated, and say what the rule must put in the place of compensation — a lottery, a claim on behalf of dependants, prior consent, something else — and whether any substitute does the work;
(d) distinguish the structure of a rule that allocates a loss among beneficiaries from the structure of a duty owed to a claimant, and say which structure the lifeboat case really has;
(e) take a stand on whether a rule that is fair ex ante but lethal ex post is legitimate, rather than assuming it either way; and
(f) state their own rule precisely enough to be applied, then apply it to two variants: one in which the extra passengers are those whose own ship stood off and did not come to the aid of the others, and one in which a further swimmer reaches the boat after the rule has already been invoked and would be the last person admitted.

Please give me the essay question as it will appear on the paper, answerable in ninety minutes at about 1,200 words and worth 30 marks; a model answer of roughly 900 words; and a marking scheme that names the must-hit points, the marks attached to each, an explicit statement of what separates an A from a B, and a one-line note for each point on how candidates typically lose it. Write everything in British English, and include no citations and no suggested reading: the paper must stand on its own.
