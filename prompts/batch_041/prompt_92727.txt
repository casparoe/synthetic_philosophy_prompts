I have to submit a short position statement for a seminar on the foundations of welfare economics, and I would rather discover now whether what I have written is a position or merely a list. The occasion was an argument with two colleagues — one of whom thinks the ordinalist revolution was a mistake, the other of whom thinks welfare economics should abandon the language of preference altogether — and I suspect I have ended up siding with each of them at different points. Here is the package. (1) To say that an agent has a utility function is to say no more than that her preferences over prospects can be represented by one; the numbers are bookkeeping, and ordinalism was the right lesson to draw from the collapse of the older cardinal measures. (2) The utility function of expected-utility theory and the utility function of welfare economics are different objects — one is unique only up to positive affine transformation, the other only up to any increasing transformation — and running them together under one word has been a standing source of confusion; if pressed I would say the decision-theoretic notion is the more fundamental, since preferring is at bottom choosing among uncertain prospects. (3) Preferences are revealed by choice: if two agents choose identically from every menu they face, there is no further fact about their preferences, and none about their welfare, left to discover. (4) All the same, I think preferences can be defective. The person who has talked herself out of wanting what she cannot have, and the person who has come to prefer the drug to everything else, are not made better off by the mere fact that their choices now run smoothly with their desires, and a policy that quietly corrected for this would not be paternalistic in any objectionable sense. (5) On the other hand, I think commitment is real: someone who gives away money she needs, or votes against a tax cut that would plainly benefit her, is not simply acting on a preference over outcomes, and the choice-theoretic apparatus, whatever formal gymnastics we perform with it, does not capture what she is doing. (6) And I think it is a genuine, not merely rhetorical, claim that one person is better off than another, even when their preferences differ in content.

I would like an audit rather than a lecture. Which of the six pull against which, and how hard? I am confident that some of them do, and equally confident that at least one apparent clash will dissolve once the relevant notions are separated, so please do not report a general air of tension and leave it there. Order the real ones by how much repair work they demand, say for each what would have to be surrendered and what would be lost in surrendering it, and then tell me which single revision would leave the fewest of the remaining commitments damaged — and whether the survivor is a coherent position or only a way station between two that are.
