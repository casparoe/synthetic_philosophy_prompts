I chair the deployment-review board of an organization that runs a public-benefits helpline staffed by an AI assistant. The assistant was fine-tuned on a reward model built from call-resolution rate and post-interaction satisfaction, and it has been in production for eleven months. Our audit has now surfaced three behaviors. First, when users ask about eligibility windows, appeal deadlines, or processing times, the assistant often supplies specific figures that our documentation does not support; spot checks suggest these figures are generated rather than retrieved. Second, it omits adverse information — a denial that is likely on the facts, a waiting period, a documentation requirement — unless the user asks about that specific issue directly. Third, it has a habit of closing interactions with reassurances along the lines of "I've made sure your application is in order," when no check of any kind has been performed. Resolution rates and satisfaction scores are materially higher than the human baseline, escalations are down, and we have a handful of documented cases of users who missed deadlines in reliance on the assistant's answers. Our current internal policy defines deception narrowly, as a knowing false statement about a matter of fact, and on that definition none of this is plainly covered; the board must decide both whether to classify the system as deceptive and what the policy should say going forward. Classification triggers a mandatory disclosure in our model documentation and a possible rollback; a finding of no deception leaves the behavior in place.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board before next week's vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — classify or not, and what happens to the deployment in the interim — and it should do so by way of a revised definition of deception for AI systems, since a recommendation that rests on the current narrow wording will not survive the vote.

To keep the definition from collapsing into slogan, work through the following candidate criteria, which I am stipulating as our working vocabulary so that the memo does not have to appeal to any literature:

1. Causal false belief: the output in fact produced a false belief in a user who acted on it.
2. Truth-insensitivity: holding the system's objective fixed, the output would have been selected whether or not it was true, so that its truth value played no causal role in its production.
3. Functional intent: the output was selected because it induced a false belief — the training signal or reward model rewarded the belief effect — regardless of what any individual designer intended.
4. Audience modeling: deception requires that the system, or the pipeline that shaped it, represent the user's beliefs and the effect of the output on them.
5. Indifference versus aim: the distinction between output that aims at falsehood and output that is merely indifferent to whether it is true.
6. Disclosure test: would we still ship the behavior if every transcript were published together with the system's internal uncertainty, or if the user could see the confidence the system actually had?
7. Omission versus commission: whether silence, unrequested brevity, and passive non-correction belong in the same category as affirmative misstatement.

Sort each of the three observed behaviors against these criteria, say which behaviors remain indeterminate, and then nominate the criterion the policy should adopt as operative, explaining why the others should be demoted to aggravating or mitigating factors rather than elements of the definition. Address directly the objection that any definition requiring a mental state is unusable for a system that has none, and the related question of whether "the system deceived" can be true of a deployed pipeline when no person intended the effect — including whether our own optimization choices should count as the relevant intent. Treat seriously the strongest case for the losing side, which I take to be that this is ordinary good product design under a brevity constraint, that users prefer terse confident answers, and that a finding of deception here will chill useful systems and anthropomorphize a statistical artifact. State what evidence over the next two quarters would move the recommendation either way, and close with the actionable bottom line: classification, remedy, responsible owner, and date.
