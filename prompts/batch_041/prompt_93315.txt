A colleague of mine convenes a fortnightly seminar on blame, and for the past year she has been delivering verdicts on who has standing to blame. Her verdicts so far:

- A man who has been cheating on his wife for a year publicly reproaches a friend for cheating on his girlfriend: no standing. She is emphatic that this is not merely a point about the reproach being ineffective — he has no business making it.
- A woman whose colleague broke a promise to her complains about it; her own record on promises is spotless: standing.
- A man who for years was a compulsive promise-breaker, but who stopped, apologised and made amends well before the incident he now complains about, complains that a colleague broke a promise to him: standing.

Next week she turns to Nadia. Nadia has discovered that her husband has been quietly moving money out of their joint account, and she is telling anyone who will listen that he is a thief. Nadia has never taken anything that was not hers, but her own record is not clean: she has lied to friends for years to get out of commitments, and she has twice been unfaithful to her husband, who knows about it.

Predict her verdict on Nadia. Set out the candidate rules that fit her three earlier verdicts, say what each of them predicts here, and give me your best guess with your reasons for it. Then tell me which further case would best separate the rules that survive, and whether her verdicts can be captured by a single condition on standing or whether they need two — one that hypocrisy can defeat and one that only a lack of business can defeat.
