- The forfeiture move: the murderer, by aiming at an unjust end, gives up any claim to be told the truth, so misleading him isn't lying in the first place.
- The silence move: you just don't answer. Refusing to speak isn't lying, so no duty gets violated.
- The literal-truth trap: you say something true but arrange things so he draws the false conclusion you want — like "I saw her heading toward the market an hour ago" when you know she's since come back.
- The maxim-rewrite: you describe what you're doing at a level of generality that passes the universalization test — "I help people in deadly danger" rather than "I lie to murderers."
- The collision move: treat the duty not to lie and the duty to protect innocent life as two real duties that genuinely collide here, and just say the life-saving one wins.
- The consent move: ask whether the murderer could will as a universal law that anyone may deceive someone who is trying to murder.
- The self-deception move: you actually manage to believe your friend isn't home, so you're not lying when you say she isn't.

Rank them into tiers (S/A/B/C/D or whatever you like) by how good they are as *Kantian* fixes — i.e. how much they'd satisfy someone who wants to keep the absolute prohibition on lying fully intact while still getting the right answer at the door. A "fix" that secretly just abandons the absolute ban should get dinged even if it gives the answer we all like.
