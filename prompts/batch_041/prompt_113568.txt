ok so I'm in a seminar on early Chinese philosophy and for the last unit we have to hand in something creative, and I've decided I want to do a poem about the Mencius vs Xunzi fight over human nature. Heres why it bugs me: everyone in my class treats it as a definately clean split — Mencius says we're born good and just have to not ruin it, Xunzi says we're born bad and have to be built into something good by ritual and effort — but when I actually read them side by side they agree about basically everything that matters. Both of them think anyone can become a sage (Mencius's claim that any man could be a Yao or a Shun, Xunzi's that anyone standing in the street could become a Yu), both are obsessed with ritual and with the daily grind of cultivation, and neither of them thinks you get there by just relaxing into yourself. The only real disagreement is about where the shape comes from. Mencius's pictures are all of something already leaning the right way: water that runs downhill by itself, which you can strike and make splash up over your own forehead, but thats you fighting it rather than water being water; the little sprouts of compassion that only need to not be trampled; Ox Mountain, where the trees got cut for firewood and the new shoots got eaten by grazing cattle until the slope looked bald and people assumed it had never been wooded at all — for him the good stuff is already there and the axes are the problem. Xunzi's pictures are all of raw material that has to be forced: the crooked plank that only comes straight under the clamps and the steam, the dull blade that only bites after the whetstone, the wood that once steamed and bent into a wheel will never go straight again however long it dries. His word for what makes us good is wei, usually translated deliberate effort or conscious activity, as opposed to xing, what we're born with. So they end up in the same place — a good person, a person of ritual — and the fight is really about whether the goodness was found in us or made out of us.

Heres what I want from you: a two-voice poem, somewhere in the 22-30 line range, and the thing I actually care about is that the two voices get opposite verse textures. Mencius gets loose, long, spilling, heavily enjambed lines that seem to be running downhill under their own weight; Xunzi gets tight, metered, rhymed lines that visibly do not come easy, so every rhyme lands like something pressed into place rather than something that just happened. Then somewhere in the last third let the two textures start leaking into each other — I want a turn where it stops being obvious who is speaking, or where the same image gets claimed by both of them, so the reader starts to feel they might be describing one single process from two ends. Please dont let it settle into a both-sides-have-a-point shrug or a tidy synthesis; end on the thing they still can't agree about. And no name-dropping the analogies like flashcards — someone who's read the sixth book of the Mengzi and the Xunzi chapter on human nature should nod along, and someone who's read neither should still come away with a real poem. Use the images I gave you above and nothing you're not sure of; no invented quotations from either text, no sages who aren't already in the story.

Think of yourself as a poet who has also spent real time with these two texts — someone who would notice if the philosophy in a poem was off. Before you write anything, brainstorm in 3-5 bullets: which images you're using, where the turn falls and what it turns on, and how the formal contrast is going to work. Then give me the poem.
