Write the joint statement that a committed Self-Sampling Assumption theorist and a committed Self-Indication Assumption theorist could both sign after a sustained good-faith adversarial collaboration. Understand the two positions as follows. The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) holds that, other things equal, an observer should reason as if randomly selected from the set of all observers who actually exist, past, present, and future, within the relevant reference class; on this view the fact that you exist is not by itself evidence for any hypothesis, and a hypothesis earns no credit merely for implying more observers, since only the fraction of actual observers who would share your evidence matters. The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) holds that, other things equal, an observer should reason as if randomly selected from the set of all possible observers, weighted by their probability of existing; on this view the fact of your existence is itself evidence, and a hypothesis on which more observers exist is, to that extent, more probable. Assume both parties are competent Bayesians who agree on all ordinary physics, on the formalism of conditionalization, and on the mathematics of the cases below; they disagree only about observation selection effects — that is, about how evidence filtered by the condition that some observer was present to receive it should be treated. Neither party may lean on named authorities, and the document must contain no citations; it should stand on its own reasoning and on the cases.

Produce the document in four parts.

1. Agreed claims, numbered. Include at least: a shared account of what an observation selection effect is and why it arises; what both agree a rule for handling such effects must do, together with the ordinary cases in which SSA and SIA deliver the same verdict; what both agree about the structure of the presumptuous philosopher argument — the argument that, because a hypothesis on which the cosmos is vastly larger, and contains vastly more observers with our evidence, predicts vastly more such observers, it is vastly more probable, so that a philosopher may settle from the armchair a question physics is supposed to settle, namely whether the universe is small or enormous; which cases both accept as discriminating between the two rules; and what both agree a satisfactory rule must not do.

2. A disagreements table, one row per disagreement, with columns for: the question at issue; the SSA side's formulation of its own view, in wording that side would accept; the SIA side's formulation of its own view, in wording that side would accept; the crux, that is, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the case or argument both sides agree bears on that crux; the SSA prediction; and the SIA prediction. Cover at least: whether your existence is evidence; whether the presumptuous philosopher's argument is a reductio of SIA or a conclusion its defenders should accept; whether randomness of selection is a primitive commitment or a derived one; and the reference class — whose observers count, whether all human observers, all sentient beings, all observer-moments, or all possible observers — and how much of the dispute is carried by the choice of reference class rather than by the two assumptions themselves.

3. A stipulated new case, which is not drawn from the existing literature. You know that exactly one of two things will happen tomorrow. Under Solitary, the world continues with you as its only observer, and no further observers ever exist. Under Colony, a machine will tomorrow produce a thousand exact copies of you, each of which will thereafter live a full life with exactly the memories and evidence you now have, while you yourself continue to exist unchanged as well. Assume, for the sake of the case, that every copy is a distinct observer, whatever the truth about personal identity. You have no further evidence bearing on which hypothesis is true, and you assign them equal prior probability. Both parties must state in advance, in the table's format, what each predicts about Solitary versus Colony on the strength of your present evidence alone, and must record what would follow for their own view if the other party's prediction proved correct. Then take the variant in which each copy is instead given, from the moment of its creation, a different set of memories and a different stream of experience, so that no copy ever has the evidence you now have; state for each side whether its verdict changes, and if its rule is silent on the variant or needs a further stipulation, say so explicitly rather than choosing silently.

4. Residual disagreement and diagnosis. Say which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal — for instance, disagreements about what kind of probability is being assigned, or about which question is really being asked — and, where a part is verbal, how the two parties should reformulate their claims so that what remains is a genuine disagreement. State for each side what argument or result would move it, and whether there is anything both sides agree would settle the matter.
