In popular accounts of cliodynamics — the quantitative, Turchin-style program that treats history as a science and claims to uncover recurrent "secular cycles" in the rise and fall of states — one analogy keeps recurring, and I want it audited rather than applauded or dismissed. The analogy runs roughly as follows: a society is an ecosystem, and its history is a predator–prey cycle. The general population is the prey, the elites are the predators. When labor is scarce the producers prosper, the population grows, and the surplus sustains a swelling elite; once the population is large and labor cheap, the elites extract a growing share through rents, taxes, and the like, immiserating the producers, while elite numbers outrun the positions available — elite overproduction — so that elite factions turn on one another and the producers can no longer reproduce themselves. The cycle then resets. The work the analogy is asked to do is substantial: it is used to argue that these multi-century boom-and-bust waves are not loose historical patterns but an endogenous ecological dynamic, and therefore forecastable — which is what licenses predictions of coming instability decades ahead.

Give me a full audit. First, set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of predator, prey, carrying capacity, the functional response, the phase lag, the oscillation itself — and say which elements of the ecological model have real counterparts in the historical story and which are idle. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the merely picturesque ones; a defender of the analogy will want to wave some of them away as incidental, so tell me which ones he can actually afford to concede. Third, name the features of the predator–prey source that the analogy quietly imports into the history — above all the assumption that the dynamics are autonomous and lawlike, driven only by the internal interaction of two closed populations. Fourth, tell me whether the forecasting conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. And if it cannot carry the conclusion, describe the analogy that could: one that preserves whatever genuine recurrence there is without pretending that the mechanism is that of a closed ecological system.
