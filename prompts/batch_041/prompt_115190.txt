I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and I have spent some months now working on anthropic reasoning. A difficulty raised by a colleague after a reading group has rather stalled me, and what I want from you is its consequences rather than a direct verdict on it. The difficulty concerns the doomsday argument. In its familiar form, the argument asks me to treat my position in the sequence of human births as though it were a random draw from the whole sequence, past and future alike; since my position falls late in the portion of the sequence that has already run, the argument concludes that the total number of humans ever born is probably not vastly greater than the number born so far, and so that the end of the species is not remote. My colleague's objection is that the same pattern of reasoning, applied without alteration, delivers conclusions in cases where we can check it and find it wanting. He offers three. First: I am watching the fourth episode of a television series and have no idea how many episodes were made; by the reasoning, the series probably has few episodes left to run. Second: I am the second child my parents have had, and I do not know whether they intend to have more; by the reasoning, they probably will not. Third: a laboratory has made eleven attempts at a synthesis that it will go on attempting until it either succeeds or is abandoned; by the reasoning, the attempts are probably near their end. He concludes that the reasoning is a fallacy and that the doomsday argument simply inherits it. I suspect the cases are not all alike, and that whatever separates the doomsday case from the others is the philosophically interesting part, but I have not managed to say precisely what it is.

What I should like from you, then, are the most important implications of this, pursued in either direction. Suppose, first, that the reasoning survives in the doomsday case but fails in cases such as the second and third above: what are the most important implications of that asymmetry for the use of anthropic reasoning in cosmology — for instance, in estimating how long technological civilisations tend to persist, or in reasoning about how many observers a universe of our kind should be expected to contain? Suppose, second, that the reasoning fails across the board: what are the most important implications for the way doomsday-style considerations are deployed in work on existential risk, where they are often invoked to argue that near-term risks deserve unusual weight, and for the more general question of what work a reference class can legitimately do? I should also like your view on the most important implication for how the argument ought to be presented and criticised — whether it belongs with live, unresolved problems or with instructive fallacies — and for the wider question of when reasoning from one's own position in a sequence can be evidence about the length of that sequence at all. Please argue from first principles rather than summarising the state of the literature; I am trying to grasp the structure of the problem rather than who has said what, so I do not need a reading list or citations. Please write in British English.
