I'm a doctoral student in philosophy, working mostly in epistemology, and I've been trying to get the new riddle of induction straight. A handout that circulates among our incoming students—written by someone competent, though I don't know who—summarizes Goodman's grue roughly as follows:

"Goodman introduces the predicate grue, which applies to a thing just in case it is examined before some future time t and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue. Suppose every emerald examined so far has been green. That same evidence supports 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' equally well, yet the two hypotheses disagree about the next emerald, so the evidence cannot tell us which to believe. This is really just Hume's problem of induction restated in the formal mode: no amount of past regularity can justify projecting into the future. Goodman's own solution is that green picks out a natural kind while grue does not; nature carves itself at the joints, and the predicates that answer to real kinds are the ones that support induction. Grue is an unnatural, gerrymandered, disjunctive predicate, no more projectible than bleen, so the paradox dissolves once we see that only natural predicates can be confirmed. The upshot is that induction is justified after all, provided we restrict it to natural properties."

I suspect this is largely right but wrong in a way that matters, and I want an audit I can actually use rather than a vague "it's complicated." Go through it claim by claim. For each, say whether Goodman would accept it as stated, whether it is a defensible simplification a careful expositor might make, or whether it is a genuine error—and if it is an error, what it obscures.

I am especially interested in three points. First, is it fair to present the grue problem as Hume's problem of induction in another idiom, or is the new riddle doing work that Hume's riddle does not? Second, is the natural-kinds resolution actually Goodman's, and if not, what is his proposed resolution and what work is it supposed to do—in particular, is it offered as a justification of induction or as something weaker? Third, does the charge that grue is unnatural, gerrymandered, and disjunctive have any force as Goodman understood the predicate, given that green can be defined in terms of grue just as easily as grue in terms of green?

Finally, tell me whether the handout's closing claim—that induction is justified so long as we restrict it to natural properties—follows from anything in the accurate parts of the summary, or whether it is an overreach that the rest of the summary does not license. Where the summary is wrong, say what a defender of Goodman would put in its place. I don't need a reading list or citations; I want the substance.
