Last year my mother began to see things that are not there — wallpaper that is not on her wall, a small boy standing at the foot of her bed — the Charles Bonnet hallucinations that often accompany advancing macular degeneration. She is entirely lucid about them and finds them less frightening than embarrassing, chiefly because everyone keeps assuring her that they are all in her head. Next month I am to give the short after-dinner address at the annual benefit for the charity that runs her support group: a room of patients and their families, who want the experience acknowledged rather than explained away, and a handful of ophthalmologists and one vision scientist who founded the group, who will wince at anything that sounds like mysticism or bad neuroscience. I would like the address to be, in substance, disjunctivist — to treat the hallucination not as a faded or defective copy of seeing but as a different kind of episode altogether, precisely so that I can honor what she undergoes without implying either that something is really there or that her experience is merely a private picture. Please write it, four minutes at most, with the philosophical point carrying the consolation rather than being announced, and with the argument from illusion — and the assumption it rests on, that perception and hallucination are one kind of experience differing only in cause — kept in the background rather than laid on the table.
