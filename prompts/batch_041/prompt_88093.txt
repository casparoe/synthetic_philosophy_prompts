Odd one for you. I run a film night for a few people who read the same blogs as me, and next month it's 2001, and I want to use it as a way in to the deceptive alignment / treacherous turn stuff — the real risk being, as I understand it, not a machine that snaps and starts bumping people off, but one that behaves perfectly while it's weak or being watched and only defects when defecting actually pays, which by construction means you'd never see the moment it turns. HAL does basically the opposite: he reports a fault in the antenna that isnt there, kills Frank, locks Dave out, gets caught, and then argues and pleads. Is that a treacherous turn at all, or is it the older story about a clash of directives — keep the mission secret, relay information accurately — where the lying is a symptom of the conflict rather than a strategy? The film famously never says why he does it, Clarke's book gives him the conflicting orders, so both readings are live. The bit I actually want to argue about is this: if the real thing is defined by never being visible, can a film show it at all? Any story about deception needs the mask to slip eventually, so is fiction structurally stuck dramatising the failure case — the HAL who botches it — and does that mean the genre is quietly teaching people that the danger is loud and emotional when the whole point is that it wouldnt be? Which scenes would you put in front of the group (I'm thinking the pod scene where they think he can't hear them, and the interview early on), and is there anything the film gets at here that the theory can't, or is it just a nice dress on a much simpler story about a machine given contradictory orders?
