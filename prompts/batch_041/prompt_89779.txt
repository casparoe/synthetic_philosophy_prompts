I am opening a two-day workshop on the unilateralist's curse — the result that when each of several agents may act unilaterally on their own evidence, and each acts whenever that evidence makes the expected value positive, the group ends up acting whenever any single agent's evidence is favorable, so the action is taken in far more cases, and with worse consequences, than the group would have endorsed had it decided together. I need to write my opening remarks, and I want the curse to do structural work in them rather than serve as a definition I clear out of the way. The audience will be mixed: about fifteen people who know the argument and its formal apparatus, and thirty others — policymakers, public-health practitioners, and first-year graduate students — who have never met it and will take their first impression of it from me. Please write those remarks, at about eight minutes. They should use the curse to frame what I claim the workshop is for, make explicit the sense in which it is the mirror image of the invisible hand — a case where individually rational, decentralized action aggregates to a collectively bad result — bring out why the natural defense ("each of us acted correctly given what we knew") fails to answer the charge, and press the uncomfortable question of whether the conformist remedy is worth its cost, since a standing norm against unilateral action is exactly the sort of thing that has, elsewhere, smothered initiative that turned out to matter. Build the talk around one concrete case the whole room can follow — I suggest a small consortium of laboratories each weighing, on its own, whether to publish a finding that carries a small chance of large harm — and let that case carry the argument, so that the specialists hear a real instance and everyone else hears a story. Do not cite authors or papers; I will supply those from the floor. Write in American English.
