I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years — long enough to have absorbed the move where a moral question is dissolved into a predictive one: stop asking whether someone deserves your anger, start asking what behavior you want to reinforce. I've made that move on other people and felt clever doing it. I'm writing because I've now been on the receiving end of it, inside a community that has organized its entire culture around it, and I can't tell whether my resistance is leftover bias or an accurate perception.

The community is a shared house and coworking space — six adults — that grew out of a reading group and has been running for about six years. The norm we're proudest of is that we don't blame each other. When someone behaves badly, we look for the mechanism: what in their history, their current load, or their habits produced the behavior. Then we adjust the situation rather than the person. We half-joke that we care about prediction, not condemnation. Two members describe the norm slightly differently. One says that in the state they were in, the person really couldn't have done otherwise, so fault simply doesn't attach. The other says the person is less like a colleague who let you down and more like a car with a failing alternator: you don't resent an alternator, you plan around it.

Then there is R, who has been with us for four years. In that time: promises made sincerely and dropped; a project two other people sank a year into before R quietly stopped answering messages; and, most recently, something that cost me personally and that I still cannot describe without getting angry. After each episode R has offered an account of the underlying mechanism, and each time the house has accepted the account, adjusted the situation, and moved on. I have done the same, and I have defended R to people outside the house.

My difficulty is that I do not feel the way I talk. I say "let's look at the mechanism," and I mean it, and then I lie awake furious. And I have noticed something else: the same people who will not entertain a syllable of moral criticism of R will, in the group chat, condemn a landlord or a rival group with a fluency that would embarrass a Victorian clergyman. There is a reading on which that is principled — R is one of us and the landlord is not, and the norm is about how we treat our own. There is another reading on which the norm is a costume, and what actually sorts people into mechanism and agent is something we would rather not examine.

Two arguments made in the house bear on this, and I want to know what is wrong with each. The first: the resentment is the unjustified part; we can keep saying "that was wrong," keep imposing consequences, keep holding R accountable in every outward respect, and simply stop feeling the anger, because the anger is debris from a pre-scientific picture of agency. The second, from the person who rejects that: it is incoherent, because if you do not feel it you are not holding anyone accountable, you are managing them. The whole point of "that was wrong" is that it carries a demand, and a demand you do not feel is not a demand.

So, concretely: I have a conversation with R on Thursday, and I need to decide what to ask for. Should I be pushing the group's norm toward consistency — which would mean extending the mechanism treatment to outsiders as well — or abandoning it, which would mean holding R fully to account? Or is the asymmetry itself evidence about what the norm really is, and if so, evidence of what? And the fact that I cannot stop being angry no matter how much I agree with the argument that I should not be: is that a fact about my psychology to be managed, or is it telling me something about the argument?
