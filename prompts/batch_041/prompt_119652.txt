You are a philosopher of language and a metaethicist who has spent years writing and speaking for people who are not philosophers. I need a speech from you, and I need it to take a side rather than survey the terrain.

The occasion. I teach the Year 12 unit in reasoning and ethics at a large state secondary school, and on Thursday I give the opening remarks at curriculum evening: about eighty parents, a handful of my own students, the head teacher, and one school governor who happens to lecture in philosophy at the local university. I will be reading it aloud, and then the parents disperse to their groups. The unit opens with four weeks on what I call, in the classroom, arguing about right and wrong without asserting anything. A parent has written to the head teacher complaining that this teaches children to argue for conclusions they do not hold and to treat moral questions as an exercise in logic, and the head teacher has asked me to answer the complaint directly, in the speech. Some of the parents are devoutly religious and will be listening for signs that I am quietly teaching relativism. Several are scientists and engineers who will notice hand-waving. Most have a cup of tea and twenty minutes before they can reasonably leave.

The puzzle the unit is built around — in the department we call it the Frege–Geach problem, though I will not use that name in front of parents — is this. Take a sentence like: if it is wrong to keep a dog chained up all day, then it is wrong to sell a puppy to someone who will. That sentence has a meaning, stands in logical relations to other sentences, and can be reasoned with. It is inconsistent with accepting both that it is wrong to chain a dog up all day and that it is not wrong to sell a puppy to someone who will. It can be agreed with, disputed, and true or false in the way that any conditional can. But the person who says it need not think that chaining a dog up all day is wrong; she may be arguing hypothetically, or working out what her friend is committed to. So the moral words in it do their work while nobody asserts anything. That is awkward for any account on which calling something wrong is not describing a fact but expressing an attitude, prescribing, or committing oneself to a plan: if the word expresses an attitude, no attitude is expressed in the conditional, and it becomes hard to say what the word contributes or why the sentence has the logic it plainly has. This is not a curiosity of interest only to philosophers. Almost all moral argument has this shape: we reason from premises we reject, we point out that someone's moral claims cannot all stand together, we ask what follows from what. The puzzle is the reason that works, and the reason it is not simply a game.

What I want from you. Write my opening remarks. Roughly seven hundred words, about five minutes, and they must carry the practical announcements as well — the trip consent forms and the deadline for the extended essay titles — because this is the only slot in the evening when I have all of them in one room. The speech should make the parents feel the puzzle rather than hear it described, and it should end with me taking a definite position about what moral claims are, with reasons, not with an account of who thinks what. Whatever position you put in my mouth must be one you are prepared to defend, and it has to be one that a serious scientist and a serious religious believer could each hold without insult to their intelligence. The head teacher will not forgive a speech that sounds like anything goes; the governor will not forgive one that makes the puzzle disappear.

Two things I want the speech to do concretely. First, I want one worked conditional about a moral question the parents will have strong feelings about, in which I make it unmistakable that I am not endorsing the antecedent — the demonstration is the argument — and which I could use in front of eighty parents without the evening turning into a referendum on the example itself. Second, I want the speech to concede something to the complaint rather than merely rebut it: there is something real in the worry that this material can turn moral seriousness into a game of logic, and I would rather name that myself than have it named at me.

Before you draft, take a few lines to work out which view you are committing me to and why it is better than the obvious alternatives you considered. Then write the speech. Then add a short note addressed to me rather than to the parents, saying where the speech leans on a contested reading of the puzzle, or buys its rhetorical effect at some cost in accuracy — I would rather hear it from you than from the governor.

Constraints: no citations, no reading lists, no quotations from any text, and no name-dropping of philosophers. You and I know what we know, but a speech that gestures at the literature will lose the room. No term of art that a non-philosopher cannot pick up from context. Keep it in my voice: a teacher, not a lecturer.
