I am preparing the opening section of a paper on whether beauty is objective, and before I assess any of the standard positions — Hume's sentimentalism, Kant's subjective universality, the various realisms — I want the specification settled first: what, exactly, must an adequate account of taste deliver? Treat a theory of taste as any account of what it is for a judgment of beauty to be correct rather than merely felt, better rather than merely different.

Write the desiderata such a theory has to satisfy, and for each give the case or consideration that puts it on the list. I want the list built from the phenomena rather than from the vocabulary of any one camp, so it should include at least the following:

- the requirement to accommodate the manifest phenomenology of aesthetic judgment — that it presents itself as non-optional, as something one can be wrong about and can argue for, rather than a report of a private sensation;
- the requirement to accommodate the equally manifest fact of persistent, intractable disagreement among competent judges, without declaring most of the disagreeing parties simply confused;
- the requirement to explain how a change in one's own response — acquiring a taste for something once found repellent — can count as a correction rather than a mere change;
- the requirement to say what the standard is *for*: whether its point is to guide first-order judgment, to adjudicate disputes, or to rank works, since these pull toward different theories;
- the requirement to explain the difference between a judgment that is mistaken and one that is merely unusual or unfashionable;
- the requirement to make sense of aesthetic deference — why we sometimes treat another's verdict as authoritative and sometimes do not — without reducing the standard to the opinion of whoever is most confident;
- the requirement to explain how a judgment can be supported by reasons, some feature of the object, while not being derivable from rules, so that the standard collapses neither into a decision procedure nor into arbitrary taste;
- the requirement to account for the status of the judge: what it takes to be one, and how the theory identifies good judges without presupposing the very standard it is trying to ground;
- the requirement to say how the standard bears on works that no competent judge has yet encountered.

Then do the two things I most need. First, mark which requirements are genuine constraints and which are merely the commitments of a particular camp in disguise. Second, flag any subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — and if the list is jointly unsatisfiable as it stands, say which requirement should be weakened and why.

Finally, briefly assess Hume's own standard against the list, noting which requirements his account of the good critic and of the joint verdict of competent judges meets and which it strains to meet. Keep that assessment short; the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard.
