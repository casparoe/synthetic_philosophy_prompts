So I've gotten into this long-running argument with a couple of friends about whether its fine to boil a lobster alive, and whether the shrimp in my freezer should be bothering me more than they do, and somewhere in the middle of it I realized I'd actually landed on a position rather than just a vague discomfort, and now I want to stress-test it properly.

The position, roughly: the evidence about fish and decapod crustaceans — that they don't merely withdraw reflexively from noxious stimuli but make trade-offs that look like they're weighing the noxious thing against other priorities like food or shelter, that injuries leave lasting behavioural changes, that analgesics shift all of it — is good enough that we should treat them as welfare subjects, put the burden of proof on anyone who wants to deny it, and let that reshape what we do in aquaculture, where we're the ones doing the harming.

What would actually count against this? I don't want the "it depends what you mean by pain" shrug; I want the findings, arguments, and intuitions that should move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward, including which of them someone who already holds the view would concede as a genuine cost versus wave away. I especially want to know whether the position has quietly become one that can't lose — if any behaviour at all can be read as evidence, or if the precautionary framing means no possible finding could count against it — and whether that's fatal or just the ordinary shape of a view like this. Does it prove too much (insects, bivalves, and if not, what's drawing the line and is that principled)? Does the asymmetry argument — worse to wrongly deny welfare than to wrongly grant it — actually survive, or is it a shield that blocks exactly the evidence I'm asking for? And what does the view predict that its rivals don't? Honestly, the thing that would move me most is a case where the very criteria we apply to fish, applied without cheating, force us to accept something we can't bring ourselves to accept.
