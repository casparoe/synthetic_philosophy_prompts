I've just started as a grad on the cat risk desk at a reinsurer and I've realised I don't actually understand what my own firm thinks a probability is. Maybe you can help me work it out.

Here's what happens. We price flood cover off a catastrophe model that spits out a "1-in-100 year" loss figure for each site, and everyone here talks about it as a fact about the world — the client is in a 1-in-100 zone, full stop. But the number comes from simulating tens of thousands of synthetic years in a computer, and there's alot of judgement in it, and it gets revised every time the vendor updates the model or we get another bad winter, and the climate is shifting the distribution underneath it anyway, so what exactly is it a frequency *of*? Then last month we wrote a big line on a risk with no loss history at all (I'd rather not say what), and the actuaries ran a "structured expert elicitation" — asked six people for their 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles, pooled them somehow — and that number went into the same spreadsheet, in the same cell type, and is now used for pricing and for the capital we report to the regulator, who wants us to hold enough to survive a 1-in-200 year loss. Meanwhile there's a standing instruction from the head of underwriting that we must never write "there is a 1 per cent chance of flooding at your site next year" in a client email, because "it either floods or it doesn't" — but we're allowed to say "1-in-100 year event" in the same email, and we do, constantly.

So: what theory of probability does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct it for me. I want to know which bits are frequentist, which are subjectivist, which look like a propensity view (that there's a real chance out there in the world and the model is trying to read it off), and which bits don't commit us to anything in particular. Is the whole thing actually inconsistent, or is there a reading — calibrated credences, or "the model is just our best guess and the numbers are shorthand" — under which it hangs together? Is the "1-in-100" talk even intelligible if the underlying process isn't stationary? Does the reference class problem do real damage here, given we're applying a model fitted on one population to one particular site? And tell me what would have to change if we took either horn seriously: a thoroughgoing frequency view (only write business with a long, stable loss history?) or a thoroughgoing subjective view (whose credences? does the pooled elicitation number have any claim to being *the* number, and if not, what are we reporting to the regulator?). Don't give me a reading list — I just want the implicit theory laid out, with a verdict on which parts of it the practice genuinely needs and which parts it could drop without anything changing.
