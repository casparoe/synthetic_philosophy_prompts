I teach an upper-level seminar in democratic theory and I'm putting together this term's final. The boundary problem -- who gets to be part of the demos that does the deciding? -- keeps getting the same canned answers: students recite "all affected interests" vs "all subjected to coercion," mutter that both are vague, and stop. I want one essay question that actually makes them work, and that can't be passed by summarizing the literature.

Heres the setup I want to build it around. A mid-sized city is voting on whether to annex a neighboring township. Under the charter only current city residents vote. If it passes, township residents fall under city taxes and ordinances but got no vote. A third group -- people who work in the city but live outside both areas -- also got no vote, though city growth and transit decisions hit them too. And the charter requires a two-thirds supermajority for annexation, a rule an earlier generation added precisely to make future annexations hard, so today's voters are trying to lock in the boundaries of future demoi.

A colleague of mine thinks the whole thing is a pseudo-problem. Her line: a demos never needs justifying "from outside"; it's legitimate so long as (a) insiders can in principle exit and (b) the arrangement stays contestable over time, so any excluded party can petition in and any included party can petition out. On her view legitimacy is retrospective and dynamic -- a running property, not something earned at a founding moment -- so there's no circular founding act to worry about, because there is no founding act; the demos is always mid-stream.

Write the exam question so students have to (i) judge whether my colleague's move dissolves the boundary problem or just relocates it, (ii) run the annexation case as a test of her view, and (iii) deal with the entrenchment wrinkle -- whether a demos can legitimately fix its own future boundaries. Should be doable in about 50 minutes. Then give me a model answer of roughly 700 words plus a marking scheme out of 20 that cleanly separates an A from a B, naming the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks.
