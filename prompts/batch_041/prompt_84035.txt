I am assembling a unit on creativity for an interdisciplinary seminar, and I want my students to work on the specification rather than on adjudicating between named theories. Before we examine any account of what creativity is, write the desiderata: what any adequate account of creativity must deliver, given that it has to cover both human and machine-made work and be usable for settling disputes about who or what deserves credit — the model, its user, or no one.

Include the cases it must get right, with the case behind each requirement: the curator who writes a prompt and then selects one output from two hundred, discarding the rest; the photographer who spends a week coaxing a single image out of a model, reworking it and printing it; the model whose output is a novel recombination of training images made by people who never intended any such thing; the system that generates, scores, and discards its own candidates with no one caring which survives; and the unrecognized maker whose genuinely novel work no community ever credits. Add the distinctions the account cannot collapse — in particular between novelty and answerability (the capacity to say why this rather than that, and to revise in light of criticism), between novelty and stake (something being at risk for the maker), and among creativity as a property of products, of processes, and of persons. Mark which of these requirements are genuine and which are merely commitments of one camp — the romantic picture of the lone genius, or the contrary prejudice that only social recognition confers creativity.

Then the part that matters most to me: identify any pair or subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the accounts we would otherwise take seriously. Skip the survey of positions; we will do that in class.
