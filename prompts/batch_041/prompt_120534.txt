For most of my working life I was an atmospheric chemist; since retiring I have spent six years reading philosophy, mostly applied ethics and political philosophy, with more appetite than training. I convene a monthly discussion group — a dozen retired scientists, plus two philosophers who tolerate us — and next term we are taking up paternalism and nudging. I would like the group to leave with something better than a vocabulary list. What I want from you is a decision tree.

The instrument should begin at a fork that everyone in the room can answer honestly, and I propose this root: I accept that an intervention can be morally wrong even when it is not coercive, is fully disclosed, harms no one's interests, and makes the person's life go better even by her own later lights. Those who say no travel down a welfarist branch; those who say yes need an account of what the objection is, and the tree should then sort them. Adjust the root if you can do better, but keep it answerable without knowing where it leads.

Build the tree from the logical space first and locate the named positions in it afterwards. Each node must be a question about a concrete case or a specific claim, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which label it produces; no node may take the form of asking which doctrine you favour. Every leaf should carry the position's name (or be marked unnamed), one sentence stating its central commitment, and one sentence naming the case below that it handles worst and what it must deny about that case. I want between eight and fourteen leaves, none more than six questions from the root, and the whole thing usable in a single sitting. At least one leaf should be habitable by a Kantian and at least one by a welfare economist with no interest in autonomy talk.

The tree must separate positions along at least these axes, and I care most about the first three, because I think they do more work than the usual soft/hard distinction:

1. Whose good is being served — the nudged agent's, a third party's, the institution's, or the nudger's. I suspect a great deal of what gets called paternalism is really externality policy wearing a paternalist coat, and I want the tree to expose that rather than smooth it over.
2. Whether the intervention works by exploiting a bias the agent would disavow or by supporting a heuristic she would endorse. My own working labels, which you need not keep: exploitative versus scaffolding nudges. Whether a position even registers this difference is itself diagnostic.
3. Whether the intervention changes the option set or the chooser's competence; and whether the agent's capacity for self-government is treated as fixed or as something the intervention can improve or degrade over time. I have in mind the possibility that a nudge which works is one that leaves the agent less able to choose well unaided, and I want to see which positions have anywhere to put that worry.
4. What grounds permissibility: actual consent, hypothetical consent, the agent's present good, her future good, respect for her authority over her own life, or the retention of a cheap and known exit.
5. Whether the intervention must be announceable in advance without defeating itself.
6. Whether nudger and nudged are the same agent — self-nudging and pre-commitment — or a collective, such as a committee or a firm.

Please use all of the following cases, and no others, as the matter of the nodes:

- A payroll default that raises the contribution rate with every salary increase; opting out takes one click, at any time, and the employer chose the scheme without asking anyone.
- An organ-donor register that is opt-out, but where opting out requires a witnessed paper form at a government office, while opting in is a checkbox online.
- A clinician who presents the treatment she thinks best as what we will do, mentioning the alternative once and misrepresenting nothing.
- A car that chimes and dims its display when you exceed the speed limit by a margin, calibrated to be irritating enough to change behaviour, never limiting speed, and not described in the manual.
- A utility's default thermostat schedule that pre-heats in the afternoon and cools at peak, advertised as comfort optimisation, which mainly serves the grid and lowers the household's bill.
- A betting app required by law to display cumulative losses as prominently as winnings, a requirement the app's own designers now warn may drive some users to chase losses.
- A hospital procurement committee defaulted toward the cheaper of two equally effective suppliers.

Three further requirements. First, where a node turns on an empirical question — whether the intervention works, whether reliance erodes the capacity, whether the exit is genuinely cheap — say so, phrase the node as the empirical claim together with what the position holds if the claim is false, and keep the tree answerable by someone who does not know the evidence. Second, mark combinations in the logical space that no one occupies, and say for each whether you judge it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Third, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say what further case would separate them.

Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I will add the references myself, and I would rather you name positions by their commitments than by their sponsors.
