I am in the final year of my philosophy degree and am taking a specialised option on suffering-focused ethics and the measurement of wellbeing. I need to choose a dissertation topic within the next few weeks, and I have one semester of full-time work, a supervisor who will support anything in this area but can spare only two or three meetings, and no empirical data of my own.

The cluster I keep circling is the one around lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and the commensurability of pleasure and pain, and I am overwhelmed by how many distinct questions live inside what looks like a single issue. Let me state the position I find most attractive, so that you can see what is genuinely at stake. Call it the threshold view: disvalue has lexical priority over value above some threshold of intensity, so that no finite quantity of mild pleasure can outweigh a single episode of severe suffering; but below that threshold, mild pains and mild pleasures may be traded against one another on something like a common scale. I want to know which open questions this view raises are the ones worth a semester, and in what order.

The questions I can see — though I suspect my list is both incomplete and badly ordered — include: (i) whether the threshold is a feature of the experiences themselves, a level of intensity on a single hedonic scale, or a feature of our moral priorities, in which case it might survive even if pleasure and pain turn out not to be commensurable at all; (ii) whether pleasure and pain can be compared in the first place, and if they cannot, whether the threshold view collapses or instead becomes the only coherent option available; (iii) what fixes the height of the threshold, and whether any answer to that is more than stipulation; (iv) whether the view can accommodate the heterogeneity of suffering, or whether the whole apparatus presupposes a false uniformity in which grief and physical agony are treated as points on one axis; and (v) what the view implies for the population cases that usually drive these debates.

I would like a defended agenda rather than a survey. Rank the open questions by how much other work in the area depends on them; say which are tractable for a single student in a semester without empirical data, and which would require experimental psychology or formal decision theory that I do not have; and tell me what would move a question up or down your list. I am particularly interested in whether the commensurability question is genuinely upstream of the threshold question, or whether — as I half suspect — one can have a defensible threshold view without ever settling commensurability, in which case the field may be directing its effort at the wrong target. Please also say what a semester's work could realistically settle, and identify any question on my list that is a trap: one that looks deep but would not change how anyone actually reasons.
