I've ended up in a long-running argument with a mate at work about whether disease is a real thing in the body or just a label we stick on bits of biology we don't like. His line, roughly: an organism is a machine, the genome is its source code, the environment is the runtime it runs in, and a disease is a part that isn't doing the job it's supposed to do — a bug. So 'healthy' just means 'meeting spec', and working out who's diseased is a question for biology, not for values, ethics, or what anyone happens to find unpleasant. He reckons this is the serious, grown-up version of the position and that all the 'disease is a social construct' talk is just people being squeamish about admitting that some states of a body are simply worse than others. I can see the pull of it — I write code for a living, so 'malfunction' is a word I use every day and I know exactly what it means when a service starts throwing 500s. But I keep thinking the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bare, and I can't put my finger on where.

So: how far does it actually hold? Give me the mapping first — what's meant to correspond to the machine, the spec, the source code, the compiler, the runtime, the bug, the patch — then the places the mapping breaks, and say which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion he's using it to reach (that disease is a value-free biological fact) and which are just picturesque. I'm especially interested in what the machine picture quietly imports that a body hasn't got: a designer, one canonical spec, a fixed environment, parts with a single well-defined job each, and a difference between working and broken that doesn't depend on what anybody wants. I gather the two camps here are supposed to be naturalism and normativism, though I couldn't tell you what the distinction is meant to amount to. Then tell me straight: if the analogy goes, does his conclusion go with it, or is there some other route to it that doesn't need the machine at all? And if the picture is wrong, what would a better comparison be — or is any single comparison going to fail in this area?

Things I'd like you to actually chew on rather than wave at, because they're the bits that make me think the machine story is in trouble: when I've got flu and I'm shivering and coughing, is my body malfunctioning? As far as I can tell the fever and the coughing are the system doing exactly what it evolved to do, and yet the state I'm in gets called a disease — so where's the bug? A broken leg is a part failing to do its job and nobody calls that a disease. Most adults in the world lose the ability to digest milk properly, which makes the milk-digesting version look like the odd one out rather than the norm — so what's the reference class, and who gets to pick it? And the same gene variant that causes sickle-cell disease protects against malaria when you've only got one copy, so whether a bit of code counts as a bug seems to depend on where its running, which the machine picture treats as mere background. Also, abnormal compared to whom: a pregnant woman's blood sugar, a marathon runner's resting heart rate, a 90-year-old's kidney function. Don't bother with who said what or pointing me at books — I'm definately not going to read them — I just want the argument laid out properly, and I'd honestly rather you told me his conclusion survives for some reason I hadn't thought of than pretend it doesn't.
