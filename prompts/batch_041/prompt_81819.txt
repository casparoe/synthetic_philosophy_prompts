I'm a third-year PhD student in the philosophy of technology, and I've reached the point in a paper on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems where I need to stop arguing and start illustrating. The core problem, as I'm sure you know, is that when a system's behavior is produced by learned parameters that no single engineer fully understands or intends, the standard conditions for attributing fault — control, foreseeability, an identifiable agent — come apart, and every party downstream can truthfully say that the decisive call wasn't theirs. In a seminar last week a colleague half-joked that the gap is really a staffing problem, and that any firm could close it simply by hiring someone whose entire job title is the responsible one. I want to write, absolutely deadpan, the recruitment advertisement that such a firm would post.

Set it in a fictional company operating a fleet of autonomous delivery vehicles that make routing and right-of-way decisions in real time, hundreds of milliseconds before any human could intervene, under the terms of an invented meaningful-human-control licensing rule. The position — let's call it the Designated Responsible Operator — is the named human in the loop: contractually answerable for the fleet, yet forbidden from inspecting the decision logic and physically incapable of reacting in time. The humor must come entirely from the accuracy of the ethics rather than from winking. Every advertised duty should be one that, read literally, either cannot be performed by a human at all or can be performed only after the fact, by absorbing blame; and I want the real friction points to do the work — the control condition on moral responsibility, the problem of many hands, the distinction between being answerable and being at fault, the quiet slide from fault-based to strict liability, and the way a designated human can serve as a moral crumple zone, absorbing the force of a crash so that the system itself remains structurally blameless. The ad must never read as a strawman of the company; it should come across as reasonable, even humane — that is precisely what will make it land. Please give me only the advertisement's numbered duties: exactly five of them, each a sentence or two, with no heading, no sign-off, and no commentary around them.
