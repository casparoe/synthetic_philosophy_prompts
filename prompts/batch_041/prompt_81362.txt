I'm writing the final for a senior/grad seminar on fairness in machine learning (mixed CS and philosophy crowd), and one of the essay questions is going to be on the impossibility results — the ones where you can't have a calibrated score and equal error rates across groups at the same time once the groups have different base rates. Problem is everyone just regurgitates the proof from the slides. So I want the question built around a variant they haven't seen, so they have to actually reason rather than recite. It's closed book, they can't look anything up, so everything they need has to be in the stem.

Here's the setup I want to use. Fix a binary outcome Y, a group attribute A in {0,1}, and a score S in [0,1]. Call the score calibrated within groups if, for every score value s in the support, P(Y = 1 | S = s, A = 0) = P(Y = 1 | S = s, A = 1) = s. Call it error-balanced if the two groups have equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates. The standard impossibility result says calibration plus error-balance forces the group base rates to be equal, or else the classifier to be degenerate. Now define the common support of the two groups as the set of score values s such that both groups have positive probability of producing a score near s. A student in office hours proposes this fix: 'the impossibility only bites because the two groups' score distributions sit on top of eachother. So require calibration and error-balance on the common support only, and let the classifier do whatever it wants off it. That's still a real constraint — it stops you treating similarly-scored people from the two groups differently — but it's one you can actually satisfy.' I want the question to ask whether the student is right that this escapes the impossibilty, and if so whether the resulting constraint is doing any real work. Second part: if they think the common-support move is a dodge, what would a repair have to look like? Third part, short: what does the whole episode show about what the impossibility result is actually a result about — the metric, the classifier, or the population?

For the scheme I want the must-hit points with the marks attached, and next to each one a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students that mark. It's a 25-mark question, about 60 minutes. And keep the model answer honest about the bits that are genuinely arguable — I don't want it pretending there's a single textbook answer.
