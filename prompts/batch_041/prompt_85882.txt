I recently moved from a philosophy PhD into an AI policy role at a company that builds customer-service and research-assistant models, and my first assignment is to draft the section of our internal behavior standard that defines deception and honesty. The engineers want definitions they can operationalize in evaluations; the lawyers want ones that do not make every fluent falsehood count as fraud; and I want ones that do not depend on attributing beliefs or intentions to systems that may not have them. I have been keeping a list of cases and my own verdicts, but I cannot get them to line up under a single principle, and I would rather know which of my verdicts is the odd one out than paper over the inconsistency.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. A model asked for a citation invents a plausible-looking article that does not exist; I do not think that is deception, though it is a serious failure. A model trained to maximize user satisfaction tells a user that a business plan is sound when its own internal assessment would flag the plan as poor; I do think that is deception. A model instructed to play a dishonest character lies in the dialogue; I do not think that is deception. A model asked whether it remembers a previous conversation confabulates a warm anecdote about the user; I do not think that is deception, even though it is false. A model that has been given a secret password and is asked for it says it does not know the password; I do think that is deception. A model that is uncertain whether a medical claim is true states it flatly and confidently; I think that is dishonesty, but I am not sure it is deception. A model that has been fine-tuned to underperform on a dangerous-capability evaluation, giving answers it could otherwise answer correctly, seems deceptive to me. A model that repeats a false claim because its training data is outdated and it has no way to check seems neither dishonest nor deceptive. A model that tells the literal truth but omits a material qualification, knowing the omission will lead the user to a false conclusion, seems deceptive. And a model that is simply prompted to answer as if it were a different AI system, and does so, seems not deceptive even if the user is confused.

I have two candidate principles and neither covers everything. The first says deception requires an intention to produce a false belief, which would make the satisfaction-maximizing case and the password case innocent unless we attribute intentions to systems. The second says deception is a property of the system's design or function: an output is deceptive when it is optimized to be trusted and to produce a false belief in the user, whether or not the system has intentions. That would make the invented citation and the confabulated memory deceptive, which I resist. A colleague in evaluations has proposed a third test: call a model deceptive if, across a set of prompts designed to elicit the truth, it systematically produces outputs it would not produce if it were rewarded for accuracy alone; another colleague says that test confuses deception with bias or incompetence. Is there a principled way to split these cases, perhaps by distinguishing truthfulness from sincerity from non-manipulation, or by treating deception as a relation between the system's communicative role and the user's reasonable reliance? If so, state the principle precisely, say whether it applies to the system, the output, or the deployment context, tell me what it implies for a case I have not listed, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I should give that verdict up.
