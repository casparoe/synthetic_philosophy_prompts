A popular economics book I read some years ago — I no longer have the reference — devotes a chapter to the question of why markets move at all, and its centerpiece is a result the author calls the no-trade theorem. Here is the gist as I remember it:

"Suppose a market is made up of rational traders who all share the same background beliefs. The theorem says that none of them will ever agree to a trade, and the intuition is airtight. If someone offers to sell you a share, ask why. Either they know something you don't, in which case you are the sucker, or they know nothing you don't, in which case there is nothing in the deal for you. The mere offer to trade is itself evidence that the trade is bad for you, so once everyone reasons this way, trading grinds to a halt. This is the same insight that makes markets efficient — prices already contain all the information there is, in the everyday sense that any bargain gets snapped up before you can see it — which is why the billions of shares that change hands every day must be driven by irrationality, by noise, or by traders who haven't thought it through, and why professional stock-pickers can't beat the index. The moral is that markets only function because most participants are wrong."

I have no training in economics, but the last few steps feel like they are doing more work than the theorem licenses. Separately, I have run into a claim that if prices really did contain all available information, nobody would be paid to do the costly work of gathering it, so prices cannot be perfectly informative. Is that the same result as the no-trade theorem, or a different one?
