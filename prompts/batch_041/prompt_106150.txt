My grandmother died last year and left me a sealed letter; when I finally opened it, the note itself was trivial, but the recipe's margins, its initials, and its line breaks turn out to spell out a second and far more personal message. My brother and I now disagree about what she meant, and the argument has pushed me into the older question of where a message's meaning actually lives — in the frame message (the signal that this is a message to be decoded), the outer message (the structural key that tells you how to decode it), or the inner message (the decoded content). I want your credence in one precise claim: that a text's meaning is fully fixed by its inner message, so that any two readers who recover the same inner message have understood the text equally well, even when only one of them also recovers content carried by the outer message. Give me a number and its decomposition — the probability that meaning is properly analyzed as decoded propositional content at all; the probability that the outer message contributes to content rather than merely to access; the probability that the frame message is a separable layer rather than a precondition for there being any content; the probability that understanding equally well is a coherent notion once the inner message is held fixed; and any further terms you think belong. One line of justification for each, then the combination, then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most.
