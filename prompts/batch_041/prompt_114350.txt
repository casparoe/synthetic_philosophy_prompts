I am a novelist, about two years into a book set in a port city in the 1660s, in which a young woman is told by a man who has just finished a treatise on the mathematics of motion exactly what will become of her; and because I could not decide whether I was writing a scene about knowledge or a scene about fate, I went looking for the philosophical literature on future contingents and ended up in a fortnightly reading group with a retired logician, a medievalist, and two graduate students. The argument there runs on rails. One camp says that a prediction about a contingent future event — will there be a sea battle tomorrow — is already true or already false, and that this does not make the event inevitable, since truth and necessity are different properties and the step from one to the other requires a further premise that nobody in the room has ever managed to state without assuming it. The second camp says that if the prediction is already true then the future is already settled, and that the honest move is to deny that such predictions have a truth value until the event decides the matter, accepting that bivalence fails where the future is concerned. A third position, which interests me most and whose adherents cannot agree among themselves, holds that the whole quarrel is an artifact of a theory of truth we dragged in with us, on the grounds that a sentence about tomorrow has no truth value in isolation but only relative to a context of utterance and an occasion of assessment, and that the first two camps are quietly answering different questions about which of those two occasions is doing the work. Every six weeks someone says the argument only goes through if you conflate the necessity of the consequence with the necessity of the consequent, every six weeks someone else replies that the distinction is merely a restatement of the position it was meant to defend, and we adjourn.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, to be argued with rather than granted — is that this is not really a disagreement about logic at all but about time: whether the future is a region that exists and has merely not been visited, or a region that does not yet exist, and that each camp's semantics quietly encodes its own metaphysics of time, so that the arguments are not so much invalid as question-begging in a structurally predictable way. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal dispute about what the word true is being asked to mean, or two groups answering different questions under one heading — one about the logic of tensed sentences, one about the openness of the future? Is some hidden question of fact or of formalism doing the work, and is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely that it is hard? Engage the positions as I have described them rather than surveying the field. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue honestly that nothing should be expected and why. Close by telling me what this leaves me able to write: whether a prophecy can be true at the moment it is uttered without being inevitable, or whether my sense that I must choose between those two is itself a consequence of a position I did not know I had taken.
