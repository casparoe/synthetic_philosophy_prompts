I am a graduate student in philosophy preparing a term-paper proposal (one semester, roughly 6,000 words) and I would like the topic to sit in the fine structure of the debate over open borders and the state's right to exclude rather than in its familiar framing as a contest between freedom of movement and state interests. My half-formed idea is that the debate repeatedly argues past itself because the parties never say which Hohfeldian incident the right to exclude is supposed to be. A liberty to bar entry, a claim-right against interference with the border, a power to alter the status of would-be entrants, and an immunity from having one's admissions policy overridden by others are four different things, with four different correlatives and four different burdens of justification; and I suspect that several of the standard grounds for exclusion—an analogy to property, an appeal to freedom of association, a claim about collective self-determination, and a claim about the conditions for a stable political community—establish an incident of one kind while their proponents argue as though they had established another. Please turn this into a proposal.

The proposal should state the question so that it has a determinate answer: for the state's right to exclude to be justified on each of the main grounds, which incident must it be, and can a state in fact stand in that relation to non-members, given that the correlative party is someone who is not yet subject to the state's authority? Explain why the existing positions leave this open—why the familiar justifications do not already settle which incident they are invoking, and why the familiar critiques do not already show that no such incident can be held. Lay out the approach concretely: what I would actually do, in order, over the semester, and why this conceptual decomposition of the right rather than the obvious alternatives, such as directly weighing freedom of movement against state interests, or turning to empirical work on the effects of immigration. Say what a finished paper would look like, what a real result would consist in, and what would count as the project failing—including the possibility that the decomposition turns out to be merely terminological and changes no verdict.

Take a definite position on the framing: choose which incident you think is load-bearing for the strongest case for exclusion, and which answer the project should aim to establish, and give your reasons, rather than laying the options side by side and leaving the choice to me. Keep the scope honest for one semester. Do not include citations or a bibliography; refer to positions and arguments by description and I will supply the references myself.
