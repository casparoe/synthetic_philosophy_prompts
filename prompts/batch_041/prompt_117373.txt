Her answer:

Williamson's rule is that you may assert p only if you know p. It is a norm of permissibility rather than a description of what people do, and it is meant to be constitutive of assertion in roughly the way the rules of chess are constitutive of chess: an assertion that flouts it is still an assertion, just an improper one. It is stronger than the truth rule and stronger than the belief rule, because knowing entails both being right and being convinced; so the knowledge rule forbids everything the other two forbid, and more besides.

The best support for it is the lottery. I believe my ticket won't win, and I'd be a fool not to; but it is plainly out of order for me to stand up and announce that my ticket won't win. If truth were the only requirement the announcement would be fine, and if justified belief were the only requirement it would be fine too, since my belief is as well grounded as a belief can be. What's missing is knowledge, which is why the case is usually read as showing that the standard is knowledge and not something weaker.

The rule also explains the oddity of Moorean conjunctions. 'It's raining, but I don't know that it's raining' is not contradictory the way 'it's raining and it isn't raining' is. All the same, it is a contradiction of a sort: strictly speaking the two halves cannot both be true, since the first half asserts that it's raining, which the rule permits only if I know it's raining, and the second half says I don't. Grice had noticed something in the same neighbourhood, incidentally; his maxim of Quality, which forbids saying what you lack adequate evidence for, is basically the knowledge rule in different clothes, since adequate evidence is exactly what turns a true belief into knowledge.

On Dana: the statement she reads is true and rests on a real survey that is sitting in her office's files, and knowledge is standardly transmissible by testimony. She therefore knows the dam is sound, disbelief notwithstanding — knowing doesn't require me to have checked the survey myself, only that I got it from someone who did. So the knowledge rule permits her to read the statement out, and the case gives us no reason to replace the rule with a truth rule or a belief rule. If anything it is a point in the rule's favour: the impropriety, if there is any, lies with the director who is using her as a mouthpiece, not with her.

As for whether the rule is constitutive: it tells us when an assertion is in order, not which utterances count as assertions. A breach is like a foul in football — the game carries on, and the offending move still happened. That is why the rule can be stated as a norm rather than a definition, and it is also why Dana's case leaves the rule untouched.
