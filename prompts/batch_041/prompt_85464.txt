ok, i've been going back and fourth on this for weeks and i need someone to just tell me what rule i'm actually running. when is it ok to beleive something you don't have the evidence for? my gut verdicts, for what they're worth:

- my uncle won't get on a plane because he's sure it'll crash. he has no reason for this. not ok, he should get on the plane.
- a climber about to try a move right at her limit, who has to believe she'll stick it or she'll hesitate and fall. fine, i want to say.
- a friend who joined a church as an adult because the practice gives her life a shape and the arguments on both sides look like a wash to her. fine.
- a juror who votes guilty because the guy 'seems like the type'. not fine, obviously.
- a woman with a bad prognosis who is certain she'll recover. i want to say fine — and i notice i'm saying the opposite of what i said about the juror.
- a man who's sure his wife is cheating because she's been distant, and there's nothing else. not fine.
- the same man, same distance, deciding instead that she's faithful, because he thinks that's what being married means. and i want to say fine?? which can't be right, the evidence is identical in both of those.
- a researcher who keeps believing her hypothesis through five years of null results, because if she dropped it she'd never find out whether it was true. fine. though if she's wrong she's burned five years and maybe somebody else's grant money.

is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? if there is, tell me what it says about the one i keep dodging: believing your kid is going to be ok, when you've got no real reason to think so, and when you suspect that if you stop believing it you'll stop being able to do anything for them. and be honest about which of my verdicts the principle makes me give up.
