I spent thirty-five years as a population biologist, working mainly on resource-limited growth and the mathematics of carrying capacity, and I have spent the years since retirement reading philosophy of science. I would like a credence from you rather than a survey, and I would like the reasoning decomposed into its parts.

The question I cannot settle is whether the Malthusian prediction is a theory that was right but early, or one that the historical record has simply refuted. Let me state the proposition precisely enough that it can have a truth value. Call it R: the central Malthusian claim — that population has a persistent tendency to grow faster than the means of subsistence, so that any sustained improvement in living standards is eventually absorbed by further population increase unless something of the kind Malthus called the preventive checks intervenes — is best classified as a mechanism that the subsequent record has borne out, the theory's error being confined to the timing of the check, rather than as a claim that the record refutes.

Before the number, I would like one thing disambiguated, because I suspect much of the disagreement in this area is verbal. Malthusianism is not one claim. Give me a rough probability for each of the following: the mechanism claim, that fertility and mortality respond to the means of subsistence; the equilibrium claim, that there is a population level at which living standards are pinned; and the impossibility claim, that sustained long-run growth in living standards is impossible. Say which of the three you take to be load-bearing, and which have different fates in the light of the last two centuries.

Then give me your credence in R as an explicit combination of the sub-questions it rests on, with a number and one line of justification for each term. I want at least these:

(a) the probability that the mechanism reading of the thesis is the correct reading, rather than a rescue mounted after the fact;

(b) the probability that the record confirms the mechanism on that reading — in particular, whether the demographic transition is better read as the preventive checks finally operating, which is the escape route the theory itself names, or as evidence that no check was ever binding;

(c) the probability that a defensible criterion exists for distinguishing a premature prediction from an unfalsifiable one. My own tentative proposal: a prediction may be called premature only when the factor that displaced its timing is independently attested and is one the theory had no antecedent reason to exclude; where a factor is posited for no reason other than to absorb the anomaly, the honest verdict is that the theory was refuted and subsequently rescued. Tell me whether that criterion survives scrutiny and where it would place Malthus;

(d) the probability that the theory, on any of its readings, forbade anything we observe — that is, what the world would have had to look like for it to count as refuted.

Then the combination; then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and which observation or argument would move it most.

For what it is worth, my own credence in R is about 0.7. A friend of mine, a philosopher of science who is otherwise a fairly strict Popperian, puts it near 0.15. Tell me where our disagreement most likely lives — whether it is a disagreement about the facts of the last two centuries or about what falsification demands of a theory that makes long-run projections.
