I'm in my third year and taking a seminar on early modern philosophy that has spent the past few weeks working through Hobbes, and I've found myself more interested in a remark my lecturer let drop almost in passing than in the political theory we are ostensibly there to discuss. Her claim was that for Hobbes geometry is the model of certain knowledge not because its first principles are self-evident but because we construct its objects — we draw the figures, and so we grasp them as effects we ourselves have produced — whereas in natural philosophy we can only reason from visible effects back to causes we never see. She then observed, with some relish, that Hobbes's own forays into geometry, including his claim to have settled classical construction problems and his long quarrel with the professional mathematicians of his day, are generally treated as a cautionary tale rather than a contribution. I would like to understand what the most important implications of this package of views are, and I would far rather have two or three implications developed carefully than a broad survey.

The first thing I cannot get straight is what Hobbes's constructivism actually commits him to about mathematical truth: is he best read as a proto-conventionalist, as a constructivist in something like the modern sense, or as neither — and what follows from his position for the old question of whether mathematics is discovered or made? This matters to me because a friend in the seminar argued that if geometrical certainty rests on our constructing the objects, then Hobbes ought to have been perfectly content with non-Euclidean geometries, since we can construct those too; I suspect this is a confusion, but I cannot articulate why, and I would like the implications laid out.

The second thing I want to press is the standing of his civil philosophy. He advertised it as demonstrable in the way geometry is, and if the demonstration is meant to borrow its certainty from the fact that the commonwealth, like the figure, is something we make, then the analogy looks load-bearing rather than decorative. So does the analogy hold, where exactly does it break, and what does the answer imply for how much of the political theory stands or falls with the epistemology? Third, and relatedly, given how badly the actual mathematical practice went, I want to know what to conclude about the relation between a philosopher's professed method and his results — does the failure of the geometry undercut the epistemology, or is that simply a confusion between doing mathematics and justifying it? Please make the tensions explicit rather than smoothing them over, and if some contemporary position in the philosophy of mathematics fits his view better than any label he would have recognized, tell me which and why.
