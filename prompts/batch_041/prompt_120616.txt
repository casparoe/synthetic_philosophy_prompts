You are a philosopher specializing in mereology and the metaphysics of material objects, and you know the literature on the special composition question—the question of when, if ever, some things compose a further thing—in fine detail. I am preparing a podcast interview and need your help drafting questions. Before writing, please think through the view described below carefully: identify where it is most vulnerable, what its defender's most natural lines of reply are, and which standard objections will be hardest for her to absorb.

The guest defends a restricted, non-standard answer to the special composition question. She calls it the joint-capacity account, and she states it as follows: some things compose a further thing just in case they are jointly and non-accidentally disposed to exercise a capacity that none of the parts could exercise alone and that is not merely the aggregate of their individual capacities—and this joint capacity must be sustained by the mutual causal dependence of the parts, not by their spatial arrangement alone. On her account, a living organism composes (its parts jointly sustain capacities such as metabolism and self-repair that no part has on its own), and a functioning machine may compose, but a rock, a heap of sand, or a drifting cloud of dust does not, since any capacities they have are either aggregates of their parts' capacities or depend on mere arrangement. She also insists that composition is never vague: at any moment it either determinately occurs or determinately fails to occur, because the relevant capacities are themselves fully determinate. She regards this last claim as a virtue, since it lets her deny that there are borderline cases of composition.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press this account rather than letting her simply restate it. Aim for questions a specialist audience would find genuinely probing, and make the sequence progressively harder for her to deflect by gesturing at future research. I would like at least one question on each of the following:

- the problem of the many, and whether her determinate boundaries survive the microphysical continuity of ordinary objects;
- the coincidence problem—how the account handles a statue and the lump of clay that seems to compose it, and whether it wrongly multiplies or wrongly denies objects;
- her rejection of the standard argument that any restriction on composition must leave borderline cases, given that capacities can come in degrees and seem to admit of vagueness themselves;
- whether the account is covertly conventionalist or anthropocentric, since which capacities count as non-aggregative may depend on our interests;
- the deflationist challenge that there is no single special composition question to answer;
- causal exclusion: whether the whole's joint capacity does any causal work over and above the causal work already done by the parts;
- how we could ever come to know which aggregates possess joint capacities, and whether the account is therefore epistemically inert.

For each main question, supply a follow-up that anticipates her most likely reply and presses further. Avoid generic or biographical questions. End with a single closing question about what evidence or argument could count against the account, such that a rational defender would abandon it.
