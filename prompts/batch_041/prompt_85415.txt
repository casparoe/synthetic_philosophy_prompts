I've been having a running arguement with my mate Dan — MA student, thinks he's Aristotle reincarnated — about the statue and the clay, and it's got to the point where I've written my position up as a short essay and want it taken apart before I show him. Can you give me comments on it? Three things I want to know: (a) have I got the actual problem right, or have I wandered into a made-up version of it that's easier to solve, (b) is the move I make interesting or is it a well-known dodge in fancy dress, and (c) where does it break — I'm fairly sure it breaks in the last paragraph but I can't see the crack from the inside.

The essay, in gist. The standard set-up: there's a lump of clay; the sculptor shapes it; now there's a statue; statue and lump sit in the same place, are made of the same matter, and from now on share every future. Yet they seem to differ — the lump was around before the statue was, the lump could have been made into a teapot while the statue couldn't have been anything other than itself, and squashing the statue leaves the lump (or does it? that's the fight). So either they're two things, which offends against the idea that you can't have two material things made of exactly the same matter in exactly the same place, or they're one thing, which offends against them having different properties. My move is to say the whole puzzle is a counting problem in disguise. Every how-many question gets asked under a kind — how many statues, how many lumps, how many things full stop — and the question how many things are on the plinth has no answer until you say which kind you're counting under, because thing isn't a kind, it's just the widest word we have. On that reading the contradiction is an equivocation. Except it isn't quite, because the museum's insurance schedule needs an answer that isn't relative to a kind and isn't one the registrar gets to choose. So my line ends up being: the puzzle is real, but it's about which kind the context forces on us, and that's a metaphysical fact rather than a semantic one, since the registrar can simply be wrong about it. Dan says I've reinvented the relativist move and then stapled a mystery to the end of it, and I think he might be right.

What I actually want from you: is that last step defensible, or is it a promissory note dressed up as a conclusion? If it's defensible, what would it take to make it non-circular — what would a case even look like where the context forces a kind and the registrar is wrong about which one? He also wheeled out the cat whose tail you pluck hair by hair, and the ship that gets its planks replaced one at a time, and I don't have a good answer for either; tell me whether my counting line even reaches those cases or whether it's a solution to one puzzle wearing the clothes of a general theory. And if you reckon the whole thing is well-trodden, say so plainly, but then tell me which exit I should take instead and what it costs me, because I'd rather drop my line for a good reason than defend it out of stubbornness. One more thing, on accuracy: I've a nagging feeling I've made the modal point — what the thing could have been — do work the temporal point, what it actually was, was already doing, and muddled the two; check that for me. Be blunt about the prose as well if it's waffly, and about the spelling and grammar, since I'm definately sending this to him.
