I've been turning over something that came up at a book club meeting a few weeks ago, and I still can't decide what to make of it. The claim was that in countries that score highly on measures of gender equality — more women in parliament, narrower pay gaps, more equal sharing of domestic labor, and so on — men and women often end up making more divergent career and life choices than they do in poorer, less equal countries; the split between who goes into engineering versus nursing or primary-school teaching, for instance, is sometimes wider in the egalitarian countries rather than narrower. One friend took this as strong evidence that the differences in what men and women are drawn to are at least partly natural: once you clear away the obstacles and the economic pressure, people are free to follow their inclinations, and the inclinations turn out to differ. Another friend was sure this couldn't be the right lesson, but neither of us could say why in a way that didn't sound like hand-waving, so I'd like your help working it through properly.

Please take the role of a careful philosopher of social science — someone equally comfortable with the empirical literature and with the conceptual questions about what such a pattern could possibly demonstrate — and write a dialogue between two characters who hold these two views. Both of them should be genuinely competent; I don't want a strawman on either side, and I don't want them talking past each other. Each should engage the other's strongest point directly, concede where a concession is honestly warranted, and press where it isn't. Have them begin by getting clear together on what the pattern actually is, and on what would and wouldn't follow from it, before they start quarreling about its cause.

To keep the exchange concrete and stop it from becoming a recitation of the usual talking points, I'd like you to fold in an idea I've been chewing on, even if one of the characters ultimately rejects it. The idea is this: when material constraints and formal barriers are removed, choices stop being dictated by survival and start functioning as expressions of identity, and in a society where people are relatively free to become whoever they want, the choices that remain salient as identity-markers — gender being among the most salient — may actually pull people apart rather than together, so that greater freedom could widen the observed gap even with no underlying difference in aptitude or disposition. One character should find this idea illuminating and try to build it into a defense of the cultural explanation; the other should test it hard, asking what would have to be true for it to work and whether it can really bear the weight being placed on it. End with the two of them identifying what they take to be the crux of their disagreement — the point at which the dispute would have to be settled by evidence, or perhaps the point at which it turns out not to be an empirical dispute at all. Write it as natural, substantive back-and-forth, roughly a page or so, and feel free to give the characters names and a setting.
