I am a novelist about to begin a book whose central character has spent her working life arguing over what an emotion actually is. I want her intellectual world to ring true: the questions that occupy her should be ones that are genuinely unsettled among people who study emotion for a living, and they should be the kind that ramify, so that where she comes down on them quietly governs how she reads her colleagues, her marriage, and herself. I have a few months of reading and thinking before I start drafting, and I would rather spend them on the questions that will matter most to the book than on the ones that merely sound profound.

Within the debate over whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, or feelings, then, which open questions would you place at the center of her work, and why those rather than the others? Give me an order and defend it. I care less about the list than about the reasoning: which questions are upstream of which, which are most likely to remain genuinely open a decade from now, which a careful reader with no training in the field could follow without my stopping to lecture, and which would actually change what happens in a scene or how she treats someone she loves. Tell me, too, what would move a question up or down your ranking.

And please be candid about the reverse: if some of the grand-sounding questions are effectively closed, or are open only in a way that specialists find interesting and almost no one else would, say so plainly rather than padding the list. She is not a caricature of a scientist, and I don't want the book to turn on a question that was settled thirty years ago.

Write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and in American English.
