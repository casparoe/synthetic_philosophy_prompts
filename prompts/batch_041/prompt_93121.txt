I am a novelist, and I am finishing a long book about a marriage: two people who over thirty years restore a house, raise a family, and run a small business, and who at the end cannot agree whether any of it was done together or merely side by side. The last chapter turns on whether the wife's claim that they built it together is true, or a consoling story she tells herself, and so — naively, as I now see — I went to the philosophers of joint action, and found them as divided as my characters are. Bratman's camp assures me that a shared intention is nothing over and above a suitable web of individual intentions and beliefs: each of us intends that we do the thing, each intends it in part because of the other's corresponding intention, and each is committed to supporting the other's part, and that this exhausts the matter. Searle's camp insists that this is precisely what cannot be done — that the 'we intend' is a primitive mode of intentionality which no assembly of 'I intend' plus beliefs about the other can reconstruct, and that it is a biological capacity of individual minds, one that may even be exercised by a single person alone. Gilbert's camp holds that a joint commitment brings a plural subject into being, something that owes and is owed and that neither party can dissolve unilaterally; Tuomela's draws a line between a 'we-mode' and an 'I-mode' of thinking and acting. They have been at this for decades, over a small stock of cases — painting a house, carrying a piano — and no one has conceded an inch; a friend who teaches philosophy of mind and I re-fight the whole thing about once a month and always end in the same silence.

My hypothesis, which I offer to be tested rather than adopted, is that the impasse is not really about those cases and not really about the mind, but about two things at once: what is to count as a reduction, and which of two different questions is being asked. One side seems to be asking how a single brain could represent an act that is ours; the other seems to be asking what makes a plan ours in the sense that licenses the word 'we' and the apportioning of blame. If that is right, the dispute may be a merely verbal one about 'intend' and 'reducible', or it may be two questions travelling under one name. But I am not confident, and there may be a genuine metaphysical disagreement underneath — about whether the social is autonomous from the psychological — that no distinction will dissolve. There is also a case that troubles me, and that I have not seen treated in the form I need: the 'we' that survives. My characters' business outlives the husband; the wife still says 'we' and means it, and I believe something is genuinely true when she does — yet no shared psychology remains, only obligations, records, and expectations. If a plural subject can persist with no minds left to share it, does that not suggest the quarrel has been about the wrong thing all along?

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and not a survey: what kind of disagreement this is, what each side is actually committed to, and — given your diagnosis — what would count as progress, whether a distinction, a fresh case, a formal result, or a candid admission that none is coming. And since I must decide how much of this to let my characters say aloud, tell me where your diagnosis is most likely to be wrong, and what you would have to be shown in order to abandon it.
