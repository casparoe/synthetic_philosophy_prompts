I work on the recommendation and ranking side of a large social platform, and I have no formal training in philosophy — my vocabulary is cohorts and segments, ranking objectives and engagement proxies, virality coefficients, network effects, persona models, moderation queues, and the constant background fact that "the user" in my dashboards is a statistical construct rather than anyone in particular. I have been reading Kierkegaard on and off for a couple of years, mostly his late attack on the press and on what he calls the public, and I keep having the uncanny feeling that he is describing something my industry has industrialized without ever naming it. I would like you to take his position on the press and the public — the idea that the press manufactures a "public" that is an abstraction rather than any collection of actual people, the process he calls leveling, and his insistence that the individual and the crowd are qualitatively different kinds of thing — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Treat it as a genuine translation exercise, not a metaphor hunt: when you say that the public maps onto something in my world, specify the mechanism, the metric, and the level of aggregation, and do not quietly substitute my framework's assumptions (that engagement is the good, that scale is neutral, that the aggregate is the reality and the individual the abstraction) for his. Where his claims presuppose something my vocabulary cannot express at all, say so plainly rather than rounding it to the nearest metric.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of his claims translate cleanly into platform mechanics — the phantom public, leveling, the press as an apparatus that produces an audience it then addresses — and which translate only under distortion, and what precisely gets bent in the process? Which parts have no counterpart whatsoever: is his talk of inwardness, of becoming a single individual, of the ethical demand that cannot be aggregated, something my framework is simply blind to, or is it the kind of claim that dissolves once you ask what it would predict or measure? And for each item of residue, tell me whether the failure is a defect of my vocabulary — something real about human beings that my instruments cannot register — or a sign that his original formulation was vaguer or more rhetorical than it looked, and that a sharper version would have survived the translation intact. I want a verdict, not a both-sides summary: on balance, does the platform vocabulary lose something that matters, or does it expose something Kierkegaard never made precise?
