Write a poem of about twenty lines in the voice of the traveller in Parfit's hitchhiker case: the man given a lift across the desert on the strength of a promise to pay on arrival, who now comes to the edge of town and sees that payment can no longer make any difference to whether he was carried. Let the form do the arguing. Cast it in terza rima (aba bcb cdc …), or another chain in which every line incurs the next, so that the reader feels a reasoning that is faultless step by step and ruinous in sum, and give me at least one moment in which he wishes he were duller than he is, since the disposition that would have served him better is not one he can acquire by argument. Split the final tercet between the driver, who must judge the promise by the man, and the rider, who knows what his word was worth; stop before the rhyme that would have joined them, so that the poem ends owing exactly what he owes. Then add a short note on where the poem takes liberties with the case — and say so plainly, before writing anything, if I have misdescribed it in some way that matters, or if a poem that breaks its own form is too neat a device to carry the point I am after.
