I have been trying to extract the philosophy of collective agency that sits buried inside a pair of legal rules, and I keep tangling myself up. I read a fair amount of the rationalist and decision-theory blogs, so I am comfortable with the thought that an institution can be running a metaphysics it never avows; what I want from you is a careful reconstruction of that metaphysics, plus a warning wherever I am over-reading the evidence.

Here is the practice, as I understand it. In a number of common-law systems a company can be guilty of a crime only if the guilty mind belonged to someone senior enough that the law treats that person as the company itself — a director, or whoever effectively ran the relevant part of the business; the doctrine is usually called identification. A junior employee who knowingly smuggles goods or deliberately falsifies a record does not, by that fact alone, make the company a knowing smuggler or a deliberate falsifier, even though the employee did the deed and the company took the profit. Yet the same company, under tort and much regulatory law, is liable for whatever any employee does in the course of employment, however junior, however ignorant the board, and there the company's conduct seems to be nothing over and above the acts of its employees.

So in one setting the company's intention is identified with the intention of a privileged subset of its members; in the other the company is an agent whose deeds are simply the deeds of whoever acts for it. I want you to reconstruct the theory of collective intention the criminal rule presupposes. Does it require a summative or individualist picture, on which a group can only intend what some individual intends and the corporation is a fiction for tracking liability? Or is it compatible with a non-summative picture, on which the corporation is an agent in its own right whose intentions are not reducible to any member's? Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well. I suspect much of the seniority requirement is doing epistemic work — we can reliably locate intent only in the people who run things — rather than metaphysical work, and I would like that disentangled. And tell me where the practice is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories of joint action would license exactly the same rules.

Finally, the counterfactual I keep circling: suppose we took the tort rule and generalized it to crime, so that the company's intention is just the aggregated intentions of whoever acted on its behalf. What would be lost? Would we be adopting a different theory of group intention, or merely admitting the one we already act on? I am also curious whether the popular idea of team reasoning — each participant asking what we should do rather than what I should do — fits the identification rule or competes with it. And since the law also lets the corporation own property, contract, and sue, does the criminal doctrine have to deny that the company is a person, or only that it is a person of the relevant kind?
