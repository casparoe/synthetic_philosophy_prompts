I'm in a decision theory seminar this term and we've spent two weeks on money pumps, and I've hit a wall I'm hoping you can get me over. I understand the basic trick well enough: if my preferences go in a circle — tea over coffee, coffee over juice, juice over tea, whatever — then someone who knows that can trade with me round and round taking a small cut each time, and I end up paying for the priveledge of walking in a loop. So intransitivity is a bad idea. Fine. But the second half of the reading is about "diachronic" money pumps, and I can't tell whether that's the same argument in a new hat or a genuinly different one, and nobody in the seminar seems bothered by the question.

As far as I can make it out, the diachronic version doesn't need my preferences to be inconsistent at any single moment. It only needs them to shift in a predictable direction as time goes by — the classic being the small-now versus bigger-later trade-off, where I always say in advance that I'll wait for the bigger one and then always cave when the now actually shows up. The exploiter isn't exploiting a loop in my preferences, they're exploiting the fact that the person doing the choosing later isn't quite the person who made the plan. That strikes me as a different complaint, and possibly a weaker one: someone whose tastes simply change as they age seems like they could be pumped in that sense without ever being inconsistent about anything. But here's my other worry: the first argument also takes time to run, since you can't trade with me in a circle at a single instant, you have to catch me at different moments — so maybe "diachronic" is doing completely different work in the two cases and everyone's just being sloppy about it.

What is each argument supposed to establish — transitivity of preference, stability of preference over time, or only something about choices rather than preferences? Flag any cell where the answer is genuinely contested rather than settled.
