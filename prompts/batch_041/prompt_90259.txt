I'm a TA for a grad metaethics seminar and I've somehow been volun-told to make the handout for the week on the Frege-Geach problem. Every year the same thing happens: everyone can say that "murder is wrong" can't just mean the same thing in "murder is wrong" and in "if murder is wrong, then telling your brother to murder is wrong," but when I ask what a non-cognitivist should actually say about the embedded occurrence they just list names -- quasi-realism, minimalism, hybrid expressivism -- and can't tell me which disagreement is which. So I want a decision tree / taxonomy that sorts the live positions by their commitments, not by the labels. Start from a rough commitment like: someone who sincerely utters "lying is wrong" is not describing a fact but expressing a practical attitude. Then every branch node should be a diagnostic question about embedded moral sentences, phrased so a student can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. Please build the tree around a distinction I've been calling the embedding profile of a moral predicate: which syntactic environments -- negation, conditional antecedent and consequent, disjunction, propositional-attitude reports, questions -- the predicate can enter while still doing whatever expressive work the theory assigns it. Use at least these test cases: (i) "if lying is wrong, then getting your sister to lie is wrong" uttered by someone who doesn't actually care about lying; (ii) a community I call Moralish whose speakers only ever use their moral term inside conditionals and negations, never in flat assertions; (iii) a machine that prints "murder is wrong" but never forms any attitude; (iv) "Sally believes that lying is wrong" where Sally is a committed liar.

The nodes should cover at least: whether the embedded clause expresses an attitude or is merely mentioned; whether "not" and "if" are truth-functional or force-operators; whether logic is truth-preserving or inference-rule-governed; whether "true" is a robust property or a device of endorsement; whether the view accepts moral truths/facts or tries to earn them; and whether the problem is solved locally or by rejecting the premise that moral sentences have truth-conditional content. I want leaves for quasi-realism, minimalism, hybrid expressivism, inferentialism, expressivist accounts of logic, fictionalism, error theory, plain cognitivism, and a "the problem is a pseudo-problem" leaf -- each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied, with a quick diagnosis of whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Aim for maybe 12-16 nodes/leaves. No citations or reading list, please -- just the tree, the diagnostic questions, the leaves, and the diagnoses of the empty branches. The audience is grad students who know the Frege-Geach problem but not the taxonomy.
