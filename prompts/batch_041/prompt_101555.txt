ok so I'm putting together the take-home final for my capstone seminar on comparative memory politics — the four-case version (Germany and Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan, Turkey, and the American South) — and I need one essay question that students can't just answer by reciting the standard collective-memory literature. The seminar's been running for years and by now they all know how to name-check "coming to terms with the past" and then list the usual national milestones. I want the final to force something else.

So here's the distinction I've been working with and want the question to be built on. It's mine, not from a book, so don't treat it as established vocabulary. Split memory into two kinds:

(1) memory that is *about* the wrong — the past is narrated, represented, taught, musealized, commemorated, admitted in official speech; and
(2) memory that is *answerable to* the wrong — the present order has to actually change something (territory, law, curriculum, who controls the land, what the state calls itself, who pays) as a condition of the memory being honest.

Call them 'representational reckoning' and 'structural reckoning' (I may rename them, so don't get attached).

The question I want: take two of the four cases and argue whether representational reckoning without structural reckoning is a stable resting point or a self-undermining one — i.e., whether a society can fully admit what it did while leaving the distribution of advantages inherited from it untouched, or whether that kind of admission always turns out to be either a live demand for structural change or a way of inoculating against it. I want them to have to say what would count as evidence for either verdict, and to face the case that looks like a counterexample: a society with near-maximal representation and near-minimal structural change, or the reverse.

Please write me three things:

(a) the question itself, phrased so it can't be answered by summarizing the four national literatures;

(b) a model answer of roughly 700–800 words that actually takes a position and shows what a strong student would do with it; and

(c) a marking scheme out of 25 that separates the top band from the middle — name the specific moves a first-class answer has to make (how it handles the maximal-representation/minimal-structure case, whether it distinguishes symbolic from material repair, whether it treats the distinction as descriptive or as normative, how it guards against just asserting the conclusion) and the specific mistakes that should cost marks (collapsing the two kinds into one, treating acknowledgment as a single scale that just goes up, moralizing instead of arguing, picking two cases that make the comparison trivially easy). For each must-hit move give me a one-liner on how students usually get it wrong.

Dont have them cite anything — no reading list, it's take-home and I don't want a bibliography arms race. It should be answerable cold.
