I have settled on a view about hard choices, and I want to know what would count against it.

The view: when two options each realize a different great good — a life of scholarship against a life of political commitment, loyalty to a friend against fairness to a stranger, one child's prospects against another's — the difficulty is not ignorance of a common scale. There is no fact of the matter about which option is better. There are genuine reasons on both sides, the choice can be made well or badly, and regret afterward can be rational rather than a sign of confusion. But the values are incommensurable, and more information, more empathy, or more time would not converge on a ranking that was there all along.

I want this position's exposure mapped: which observations, arguments, intuitions, and formal results should move me, sorted by severity, from the ones that ought to sink it to the ones that are only a nuisance. I also want an honest statement of which of these its defenders should themselves concede rather than deflect.

Specifically:

1. The small-improvement worry. If A and B are incommensurable, and A-plus is a clear improvement on A, what is the status of the comparison between A-plus and B? If A-plus is also incommensurable with B, then arbitrarily small improvements never accumulate into a verdict, which looks absurd; if A-plus is better than B, then A was worse, which contradicts the original claim. Which horn should I take, and does either leave incommensurability as a stable category rather than a way station on the road to a complete ranking?

2. Rivals. How do I distinguish my view from (a) the claim that comparisons are determinate but vague, (b) the claim that they are determinate but unknown to us, and (c) the claim that the options are comparable but neither better, worse, nor equal? For each, what would have to be true for it to be the better explanation, and what would I thereby be committed to that I might not want?

3. A test I keep toying with. Present an agent with a lottery that yields one good with probability p and the other with probability 1 − p. If there is some p at which the agent is genuinely indifferent, does that show the goods are commensurable after all, or only that the agent's preferences are complete while the values are not? If the latter, what work is the notion of a value doing that the notion of a preference is not?

4. Predictions. What does my view predict that a complete-ranking view does not? I would take seriously any case in which competent judges converge, for the same reasons, on a verdict about which of two allegedly incommensurable goods is better, and the convergence is best explained by the goods themselves rather than by shared convention. Would that be a refutation, or is it compatible with my view under some reading I have not yet considered?

5. Insulation. Has this position, in the hands of its ablest defenders, been qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, by re-describing every apparent ranking as commensurability in some respect, so that no case could ever count against the general thesis? If so, is that a defect, or is it what we should expect of a thesis about the structure of value rather than about our psychology?

Finish with what you think should move me most, and what I should stop worrying about.
