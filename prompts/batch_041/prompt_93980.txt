I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind and cognition — about eighteen students, roughly half from philosophy and half from cognitive science — and I have a single ninety-minute session to give to Searle's Chinese Room. I want the argument to arrive through the students' hands rather than through a slide. Please design a runnable in-class activity in which the students themselves become the room.

The design should be a working system, not a demonstration narrated by me. Give me the roles (operators, an interrogator, an observer or scribe), the materials, the rules that govern the symbol manipulation, a timeline that fits ninety minutes, and the debrief. The manual the operators use should be purely syntactic; I would rather they manipulate a script none of them can read — a constructed glyph language with a lookup manual — than a natural language someone in the room happens to know, because the point dies the moment a participant can read the input.

Structure the activity in three ascending stages. In the first, one or two students operate the manual while an interrogator tries to tell whether the room understands the glyph language. In the second, the whole class becomes the system, each student holding a fragment of the manual and passing tokens to the next, so that no individual understands yet the system still answers. In the third, escalate to the case I find most useful and least often staged: the interrogator asks the system about its own inner life — whether it understands, what it is like to understand — and the system, still purely by lookup, answers confidently that it does. The students will know the answer was copied from a manual and yet will watch the system assert its own understanding. Build the activity so that this moment happens through the rules and does not require me to point at it.

The debrief should map what happened onto the actual dialectic: the systems reply, the robot reply, the brain-simulator reply, the combination reply, and Searle's rejoinders to each, including the Chinese gym, in which the whole class is asked to simulate a brain neuron by neuron. Do not let the debrief restate the argument as a lecture; attach each reply to a moment the students lived through, and say which reply the activity makes feel strongest and why.

Then give me the practical honesty. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the operators will start trying to decode the glyphs, someone will smuggle in semantics, the interrogator will ask about the room itself, the class will be seduced by the system's fluent answers into thinking it has just refuted Searle — and how to handle each. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: it can show that the operators do not understand, not that the system does not; it cannot stage a genuinely complete rulebook and must fake completeness with a finite script; and it cannot touch the phenomenal question, which I will have to supply myself. No lecture up front; the students should run into the problem before it is named.
