I teach a third-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I am writing the final examination. I want a single essay question on Strawson's reactive attitudes that a student cannot answer by reciting the participant/objective attitude distinction or by reproducing the usual summary of his essay. The crux I want them to reach is a problem I have been calling, in lecture, the switching problem: whether the objective attitude is something a person can adopt at will, or whether—on Strawson's own account—it is something one finds oneself in, often against one's own wishes, and what that implies for the voluntariness of our responsibility practices. I was put onto the case below by a colleague on our hospital's clinical ethics committee, and it has stuck with me.

So that they cannot simply reach for a stock example, I am giving them a case rather than a reading. A woman has been the primary caregiver for her brother since a traumatic brain injury left him with a personality change; he is frequently cruel to her. She used to resent him. Over the years, however, she reports that she can no longer summon resentment at all: she has come to see him as a machine that sometimes says hurtful things. She is disturbed by this and has tried deliberately, more than once, to recover her resentment, without success. At the same time she insists that she still holds him responsible and continues to expect better of him. I want the question to force students to say whether this condition is intelligible within Strawson's framework, whether her insistence on responsibility-without-resentment is coherent or merely confused, and whether her inability to switch attitudes by an act of will tells against the view that our responsibility practices are something we opt into.

Please give me three sections, in this order and with these rough lengths. First, a section titled The Question, about 150 words, addressed to the students, which supplies the case and the task but does not telegraph the answer. Second, a section titled Model Answer, roughly 700 words, written as a strong script that a top student could plausibly produce under exam conditions, making the moves I have described and arriving at a defended verdict. Third, a section titled Marking Scheme, roughly 400 words, built around five must-hit points; for each point give the marks it is worth out of a total of 20 and a one-line note on the characteristic error that costs candidates that point. End the marking scheme with a short paragraph on what separates an A answer from a B answer.
