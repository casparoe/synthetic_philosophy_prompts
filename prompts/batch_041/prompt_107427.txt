Okay, I keep going back and forth on whether taxation is theft, and I want to come at it from a weird angle: not from first principles, but from what the tax system actually *does*, and what it has to be assuming about ownership and obligation for that to make sense.

Two things about how income tax works where I live. First, it's taken out of your pay before you ever see it — your employer just sends it off. And yet the law is oddly insistant that you recieved your whole salary and then paid the tax out of it yourself, so that the payment counts as yours. Second, the tax office spends real money on things that only make sense if paying is a moral matter and not just a legal one: letters telling you most of your neighbours have already paid, adverts about tax being what you pay for a decent society. And if you simply don't pay, eventually it stops being a letter.

So what philosophical commitments does this bundle presuppose?

Does the withholding fiction presuppose that the pre-tax wage was genuinely yours — that there's a moment when the money is definitely yours and then isn't? Or is it just as compatible with the state having a standing claim on your earnings from the start, so that nothing is ever taken from you at all? If the authority feels it needs to persuade you, what does that presuppose about where the obligation comes from — a duty you're meant to recognise, or a price you're charged? Can one institution coherently both threaten and cajole? And if we flipped it — you get paid gross and post the state a cheque every quarter — would that make the claim more like theft, or does the mechanism not matter at all? What does it tell us that the present setup is built to avoid exactly that moment?
