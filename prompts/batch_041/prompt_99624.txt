I chair the selection committee of a foundation that awards an annual prize for new work in the Black literary and visual arts, and our founding brief instructs us to judge by Du Bois — by his contention that all art is propaganda whether or not the artist intends it, taken together with his insistence that this gives no licence to sacrifice beauty, and that the artist must be free. Over the years we have settled four cases. We rejected a slick, market-tested novel that traded in exotic stereotypes of Black life. We awarded the prize to a plainly political and formally accomplished history of racial terror. We rejected a hectoring protest play that made every correct point and moved no one. And we gave a special citation to a first book that was technically uneven but whose candour no reader could doubt. The shortlist now before us has one obvious front-runner: a technically superb, unflinching novel of betrayal and violence within a Black community, written with no brief from us or from anyone else, and already being excerpted with evident relish by a newspaper hostile to the people it depicts.

Tell me how the committee should vote if it means to stay consistent with its own record, which of its earlier decisions is the closest analogue, and what it would be committing itself to in each direction. I am less interested in the verdict than in the reading of Du Bois the record commits us to. I can see at least three available: that propaganda is to be judged by what a work does to our standing before a hostile public; that propaganda is truth-telling, and truth-telling is judged by craft and candour rather than by what a hostile reader makes of it; and that the real test is whether a work was made freely rather than to an audience's order. Say which of these the record fits equally well, which past decision each would have to be explained by, what each predicts about the front-runner, which one you would run yourself and why, and which single case not yet before us would best tell them apart.
