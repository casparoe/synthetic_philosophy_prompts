I teach a graduate seminar that pairs animal ethics with conservation biology, and I'm writing the take-home final for its closing unit, which asks whether destroying habitat might actually reduce the total suffering of wild animals. I want to avoid the obvious essay question, because students can answer a general "assess the debate over wild animal suffering" prompt by recycling a summary — the r-strategist point, the sheer number of invertebrates, the asymmetry between pleasure and pain — without doing any reasoning of their own, and because the topic invites hand-waving in both directions. So instead of asking for a survey, I've drafted a specific version of the argument for students to attack, and I'd like the exam question built around it. For the sake of the exercise I'll tell them to treat the ecological figures as stipulated, so the essay turns on the reasoning rather than on contested population estimates: a hectare of primary tropical forest supports vastly more individual animals than a hectare of cattle pasture, which in turn supports more than a hectare given over to a solar array; the great majority of the forest animals die before reproducing, and many die by predation, parasitism, starvation, dehydration, or disease; a typical wild animal's life contains more suffering than enjoyment; and, other things equal, a world with less total suffering is better than one with more. From those premises the argument concludes that converting forest to pasture — and pasture to something still more barren — reduces the world's suffering and is, to that extent, a good thing.

Please write the question as it would appear on the paper, with the argument set out in numbered premises and a note that the ecological figures are stipulated, for an in-class final of ninety minutes and roughly a thousand words, worth twenty-five marks. The question should force students past the summary: it should make them separate the empirical premises from the evaluative ones and say how confident one can reasonably be in each; make them confront the fact that the conclusion is driven by animals — invertebrates, above all — whose sentience is exactly what we are least sure about, and say how one should reason under that uncertainty; and make them take a position on the limiting case in which the same logic recommends a near-sterile landscape, saying whether that outcome is a reductio of the argument or a bullet the welfarist should bite. I'd also like them to say what would have to be true for the conclusion to follow and whether the argument, so understood, is self-undermining.

Along with the question, give me a model answer of about nine hundred words and a marking scheme out of twenty-five. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make — for instance, the exact point at which the arithmetic and the evaluative premise interact, or the reason the argument's conclusion is sensitive to facts about invertebrate welfare rather than to the numbers alone — and not generic virtues like "clear structure." For each move, add a one-line note on how a merely competent answer typically falls short, so that the A/B boundary is legible: what a first-class script must contain, and what a solid but unremarkable one will typically leave out. The model answer should itself perform those moves, so a grader can see what hitting each point looks like in prose.
