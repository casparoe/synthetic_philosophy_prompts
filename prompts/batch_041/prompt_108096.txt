I need a critical reading of Matt Haig's novel The Midnight Library through the lens of David Lewis's modal realism, for a graduate seminar I run on metaphysics and literature. The novel's premise is familiar enough: Nora Seed, at the point of suicide, wakes in a library frozen at midnight, presided over by a manifestation of Mrs Elm, her old school librarian, where each volume opens onto a life she might have led; she samples these lives, finds each flawed, and finally chooses her own. Most discussion of the book treats it as a fable about regret. I want you to take it instead as a piece of modal metaphysics and see how much of it survives contact with Lewis.

Bear several points in mind. For Lewis, possible worlds are not stories, models, or ways things might have been; they are concrete universes, spatiotemporally and causally isolated from one another, and the word 'actual' functions indexically, like 'here' or 'now', picking out the world the speaker happens to inhabit rather than conferring any special status. Because no object exists in more than one world, Nora cannot literally be the same person in two of the library's lives; at most she has counterparts there, resembling her in certain respects. Lewis also insists, against consolatory appropriations of his view, that the reality of other worlds offers no compensation for anything that happens in this one, and that modal realism is not a theory of the meaning of modal talk but a metaphysical hypothesis argued for on grounds of theoretical utility — the price of the plurality of worlds being, in his view, worth paying. It is also worth saying plainly that none of this is the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, and that the branching in the novel is not a physical process.

With that in place, I want you to address the following. Does the novel presuppose an ersatz picture — worlds as options a single continuing subject can try on, inhabit, and step out of — and is that picture simply incoherent, or is it a variant of the many-worlds idea that Lewis overlooked or dismissed too quickly? Where does the book's treatment of regret and of the value of one's actual life trade on precisely the consolation Lewis disavows? Is there any moment where the novel approaches something genuinely Lewisian — the thought that the actual world is privileged only indexically — or does it always retreat into a human-scale moral? And what, if anything, does the novel contribute that the philosophy cannot: does it dramatize why we cannot help treating our own world as privileged, in a way the indexical account of actuality leaves unexplained?

Keep the reading honest in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a thesis, and do not bend Lewis's view to fit the novel's arc. If the reading breaks down at some point, say where and why. No quotations from the novel are needed; I care about the argument, not the prose. Please answer in no more than 1,200 words.
