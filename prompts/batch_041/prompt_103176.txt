I am the ethics and compliance lead at a mid-sized engineering firm, and I sit on a sustainability committee that meets monthly. The committee keeps bringing proposals that ask employees to make personal changes—removing beef from the cafeteria, reducing business air travel, voting in local elections, turning down the thermostat—and every proposal runs into the same objection from the works council and the chief financial officer: one person's change makes no measurable difference, so why should the firm ask, require, or even encourage it? I need a reusable decision procedure that I can run on each proposal in under half an hour, that is defensible to a skeptical finance officer and to a works council wary of moralizing, and that the works council and the CFO can sign off on as a standing policy. The procedure must operationalize the following framework, which our committee has drafted but cannot turn into practice. The framework distinguishes three structures of collective harm. An additive harm is one where each act adds a small increment to a total, as with individual flight emissions. A threshold harm is one that occurs only once a critical mass is crossed, as with an election or a climate tipping point. A constitutive harm is one where the act is not an increment but part of what makes the harmful practice what it is, as with buying meat from an industrial system or voting for a candidate whose platform one endorses. For additive harms, the framework asks whether the act belongs to a pattern that is collectively harmful and whether the agent has an available alternative. For threshold harms, it asks whether the act moves the group closer to or further from the threshold and whether coordination is feasible. For constitutive harms, it asks whether the act expresses or perpetuates a practice the agent rejects and whether withdrawal is possible without disproportionate cost. The framework also includes a counterfactual-irrelevance clause: the fact that the harm would occur without the agent's act does not by itself exonerate the act; the procedure must then ask whether the act is constitutive rather than merely contributory.

Write the procedure. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark the points where the procedure will misfire, where it will produce an answer the sustainability committee will not like, and where it cannot settle the matter at all. Translate the framework into plain questions that a compliance officer without philosophical training can ask and defend. Then run the procedure on three proposals: removing beef from the cafeteria; asking employees to vote in a national election; and a ten percent reduction in business air travel. At least one of the three should come out in a way that the sustainability committee will find unwelcome, and you should say why the procedure nonetheless requires that result. Keep the whole thing to a length a busy officer can actually use, and do not include citations, reading lists, or references to the academic literature.
