ok, I run a small reading group out of a bookshop and we're finishing Kripke's Naming and Necessity next month, the session on the necessary a posteriori — the identity statements that are necessarily true if they're true at all, and that you can only find out by going and looking. I want to close that night with a poem instead of a summary, and I want it to do the thing rather than describe it.

Here's what I need. Exactly five numbered stanzas, six lines each, and nothing before or after them — no title, no epigraph, no footnote explaining what you meant. Every stanza has to end on the exact same line, word for word, and that line has to contain a name: a person, a river, a town, a stone, anything that picks something out. The line never changes. What changes is what it's doing. In stanza one it should read like a report, something somebody went and found out. By stanza five it should land as the other kind of claim, the one about what couldn't have gone otherwise. Stanza three is where the finding out happens; its the one that has to carry the a posteriori. And please dont do water and H2O, or Hesperus and Phosphorus, those are worn to death.

Steal your case from somewhere else instead. I keep thinking about jade — one word, and underneath it two completely different minerals, nephrite and jadeite, so that every description anybody would have handed you to pin down what the word meant was just wrong, and the word went on working anyway. That goes in a stanza. And if the neat story snags there (one word, two things — does it even name one thing?) let the snag show; I'd rather the poem wobble honestly than smooth it over.

Accuracy matters more than prettiness: someone who's never read a word of Kripke should come away knowing the difference between we found it out and it had to be so, and someone who has read him shouldnt wince.
