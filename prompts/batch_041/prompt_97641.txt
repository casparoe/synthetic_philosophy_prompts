I belong to a small philosophy reading group, and at our last meeting two of the other members — Rowan and Imani — spent most of the session locked in an argument about the knowability paradox. I followed it closely, said very little, and have been chewing it over since without settling on who had the better of it. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange for me. Please take the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in epistemology and philosophical logic, think carefully before you commit to anything, and, before you evaluate the moves, spend a short paragraph setting out your own understanding of the argument they were arguing about and of the principles it turns on.

The background, as it was laid out in the group. The knowability principle says that every truth can in principle be known — for any truth, it is possible that it be known. The paradox is that this modest-looking claim appears to entail that every truth actually is known. The reasoning runs as follows. Take a truth that no one in fact knows — the example used in the group was the exact number of hairs on Julius Caesar's head at the moment of his death. Call it p. Then the conjunction "p, and it is not known that p" is also a truth. If every truth is knowable, so is that one: it is possible that it should be known. But whoever knew it would know both of its conjuncts; knowing the second conjunct entails that p is not known, while knowing the first entails that p is known. So the conjunction cannot be known, and therefore is not knowable, and the knowability principle is false. (I gather this is standardly called Fitch's paradox, but I am reporting the argument as the group gave it, not vouching for anything.) The principles they both accepted without argument were that knowledge implies truth, and that knowing a conjunction implies knowing each conjunct.

The dispute itself, as best I can reconstruct it, went through five moves.

(1) Rowan opened by saying the paradox is an artefact of an over-generous statement of the principle. The anti-realist, he said, only ever needed to claim that truths about the world are knowable, not that truths about our own ignorance are. Restrict the principle to what he called "worldly" truths — those whose content does not itself mention what is or is not known — and the derivation has no instance left to run on. No one who holds that reality is in principle fully available to thought has to hold that the bare fact of their ignorance is knowable; the paradox exploits a sentence whose subject matter is the knower rather than the world.

(2) Imani replied that the restriction is ad hoc and, worse, self-defeating. The anti-realist's claim, she said, was that no truth lies forever beyond us; if you now concede that some truths — the ignorance-involving ones — are unknowable, you have already granted the realist everything of substance. The restriction is also unstable: for any worldly truth p that happens to be unknown, the conjunction "p, and it is not known that p" is not worldly, and is therefore, on Rowan's view, possibly unknowable. That is a truth no one could ever know, and she could not see how a position admitting such truths still deserved the name anti-realist. Any principle, she added, can be rescued from contradiction by carving out the cases that do the damage.

(3) Rowan's rejoinder was that he accepted there are unknowable truths under his restriction, but denied that this concedes anything of substance. Such truths are not truths about how the world is; they are truths about the state of our knowledge, and the anti-realist's thesis concerned the world. He then offered a distinction which he said was the real lesson of the whole affair: there is a weak sense of "knowable" — there is some possible world, however remote, in which someone knows it — and a strong sense — there is a course of inquiry available from our actual situation that would end in someone's knowing it. The anti-realist, he said, needs only the strong sense; it is the weak sense that Fitch's argument refutes, and no anti-realist was ever committed to it.

(4) Imani answered that the strong sense is either empirical or empty. Read literally, whether a truth is knowable in the strong sense depends on contingent facts about our instruments, our lifespans and our cognitive limits, so the principle becomes a contingent generalisation about human enquiry — and a false one. She offered her grandmother's dream on a particular night in 1953: whatever its contents were, they are (if determinate at all) a truth, and no course of inquiry available from where we now stand will recover them. Read generously, on the other hand, "available from our situation" stretches until it means "available to any suitably placed inquirer", at which point the strong sense collapses back into the weak one and the paradox returns in full. She added that a knowability that varies with the enquirer's situation looks like a fact about us rather than about truth, which is a strange foundation for a theory of truth.

(5) Rowan's last move was that the derivation commits a subtler error than either of them had yet named. The knowability principle, he said, is something one asserts from a context. It is asserted from the standpoint of enquiry, at which no particular truth is stipulated to be permanently out of reach; and Fitch's derivation obtains its contradiction only by importing that assertion into a context in which some particular truth is stipulated to be unknown. That is a context in which the principle is not assertable at all, so no genuine counterexample has been produced. Imani retorted that this saves the principle only by making it unassertable precisely where it would matter, and that by the same manoeuvre any paradox turning on one's own ignorance — Moore's, for instance — would dissolve, which is too much to swallow; she said she would rather conclude that the knowability principle, as the anti-realist wants it, is simply false.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. I would like you to do the following.

First, rate each of the five numbered moves on a scale running from "clearly mistaken" through "defensible but overstated" to "clearly correct", and briefly justify each rating. Do not collapse them into a single verdict; the moves are separable and I want them separated.

Second, say whether the derivation as stated is actually valid, and which element of it — if any — is the genuine weak point. If you think the derivation is valid and the knowability principle therefore false as stated, say so plainly.

Third, identify what both of them are missing. In particular, consider whether the whole dispute presupposes something about the knowability principle that ought to be rejected, and whether the two of them are really disagreeing about logic, about the meaning of "knowable", or about what anti-realism was ever supposed to amount to.

Fourth, say who has the better of the argument overall, if either. If you judge that neither does, say what a better position would look like.

A few notes. You need not cite any literature, and I would rather you did not; I am interested in your reasoning, not in who has said what, and you should not feel obliged to attribute any of these positions to named authors.
