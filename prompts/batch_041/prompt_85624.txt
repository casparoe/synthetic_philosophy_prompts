I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm midway through a long piece about monuments -- statues, street names, the whole thing -- and I keep discovering that my own gut reactions don't add up to any one view. I'd like you to figure out what, if anything, I actually believe. Here are my verdicts, going on gut.

Confederate generals in courthouse squares: get rid of them. Not a close call, and I don't buy the "it's history" line -- a museum is where history goes.

The Colston statue in Bristol, the slave trader, when the crowd pulled it down and rolled it into the harbour: I was glad it went and I still am, but the fact that it was a mob and not a council bothers me. I want to say the outcome was right and the method was wrong, and I have a feeling that isn't a coherent thing to believe.

Cecil Rhodes at Oriel: should come down. The awkward part is that his money funds scholarships that a lot of African students actually use. I don't think that saves him -- which is odd, because my usual instinct is to ask who benefits now.

Here's where it gets messy. There's a street near me named after a man who was a full-on eugenicist, wrote pamphlets, the works. My verdict: leave it. Renaming a street is a paperwork nightmare, nobody thinks about who a street is named for, and the name has basically worn off into being just a location. But there's a park statue of the same guy, same crimes, and I want that gone. Opposite verdicts for the same person, and I can't tell you why, except that a statue feels like an active claim and a street name feels like a leftover.

Two more that I think should come out differently even though they look identical. A merchant who's honored in my city specifically for his philanthropy, which was funded entirely by the slave trade -- that one should go, no question, because the thing we're celebrating is the thing that was built on it. But a nineteenth-century botanist with a statue, who also happened to own slaves, and whose plaque is about the ferns? I lean keep it, maybe with a second plaque. I know that's exactly the distinction everyone accuses everyone else of fudging.

And then the cases that don't fit the frame at all: a war memorial that's about an event rather than a person -- do the same rules apply? A statue of a famous nonviolence hero who, as a young lawyer, wrote genuinely vile things about Africans (I think there was a campaign to pull his statue off a campus in Ghana). And a big bronze nude in a roundabout that isn't commemmorating anybody -- I assume the question doesn't even apply, but say why.

Here's my half-formed theory, tell me if it's salvageable: a monument isn't a statement about the past, it's something we're doing now, every day we leave it standing. So the test isn't "was he bad" or "did people then think it was fine," it's "does keeping this up make us say something about ourselves that isn't true." Under that test I think I get Colston and the merchant and Rhodes right, but I think it forces me to give up the street name, and maybe the botanist too.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, and tell me which of my verdicts each clause is doing the work for. If you have to overturn one of my calls, say which and why. And tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed -- a statue of a man who did nothing wrong, but whose statue has been adopted as a rallying point by a group he'd have despised, and a monument to a public health campaign that turns out to have been built on experiments on people who couldn't consent. One last thing: should age matter at all? I don't think it should, but everyone I say that to looks at me funny.
