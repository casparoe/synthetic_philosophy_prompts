I'm finishing the third year of a PhD in philosophy of mind, and for the past few months the material I teach has stopped staying in the classroom. What began as an ordinary reluctance to leave the house has hardened into something I recognize from the literature: a system that has discovered it can keep prediction error low by declining to encounter anything, and for which each cancelled plan makes the next cancellation cheaper. The dark room objection is no longer an objection to me; it's a Tuesday.

For the final session of my seminar I want to close with a poem that takes the machinery seriously, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. Please write a pantoum: four quatrains, sixteen lines, following the strict rule that the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, and that the closing quatrain completes the circuit by taking up the first quatrain's third and first lines — so that the poem's last line is its first. That recurrence is the point. Each time a line comes back it should be doing slightly different work, so that a reader feels both the thrift of a system that reuses its own predictions and the suspicion that a principle capable of returning to itself from anywhere may be unfalsifiable rather than true.

The two lines that recur should be the two ways the framework allows prediction error to fall — revising the model, or acting on the world — and the poem should be honest about precision: what decides which of the two occurs is not insight but a weighting, and that weighting is estimated rather than chosen. Give one quatrain to the rock, to the thought that if merely persisting is enough to give something a boundary, then a rock has one, and let the poem feel the cost of that generosity in the moment rather than resolving it. Then let the loop close on the dark room, so the ending reads less like a cadence than like a room the poem has walked back into. Please don't name the framework or cite anyone; someone who knows it should hear it in every line, and someone who doesn't should find only a poem that is uncomfortably accurate about staying in.
