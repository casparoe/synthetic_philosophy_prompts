I'm a novelist and I've gotten myself stuck on something that's really your kind of question, not mine. My main character has taken Franklin's scheme for moral perfection and made it the whole center of his life. The thirteen virtues, one virtue per week, thirteen weeks to a course, four courses a year. He keeps the little book — a page per virtue, ruled with red ink, seven columns for the days, and he puts a little black spot in the column every time he catches himself slipping. (He's also got a phone app that graphs the whole thing, which he tells himself is just for convenience.) The novel is about him slowly coming to love the ledger more than the virtues, so I need to know exactly what the ledger *beleives*, because I want the reader to see the crack before he does.

So what does the practice presuppose? Here's what I jotted down. That virtue comes in thirteen seperable pieces. That a fault is a countable event you can date and log. That you can reliably tell, from the inside, when you've committed one. That you're the same person across years, so the columns add up to something. That repetition wears a groove and the groove is the virtue. But I don't know which of those the practice actually needs and which it just happens to sit next to. Could a man keep the book and believe none of them? Could he keep it while thinking virtue is one thing, not thirteen?

And here's where I get tangled. He's doing all this in order to become humble — but doesn't the scorekeeping itself assume humility is the kind of thing you can rack up points at? Franklin tacked humility onto his list last, after a friend told him he came across as proud, and by his own account he got the look of it but never the thing. So is the practice self-defeating for at least one entry on its own list, or is that just a bad week for the guy? I'd like to know whether it's a bug in the practice or a bug in the practitioner.

And what does it assume about where moral improvement even comes from — effort and method, or grace, or just being the sort of person you already are? My character's grandmother was a strict Calvinist and he's turned into the opposite without noticing: he thinks you save yourself by trying harder, the old Pelagian move, though he'd never call it that. I want the book to notice before he does.

The thing I actually need, though: if the practice doesn't really presuppose the list I gave — if it's compatible with a whole range of views about virtue and self-knowledge — then tell me which ones, and for each, tell me what he'd have to change about the practice itself to keep it honest. What would the ledger look like for someone convinced you can't audit your own character from the inside at all?
