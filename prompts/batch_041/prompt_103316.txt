Ok, slightly unusual one for you. I'm a philosophy lecturer, but I also sit on the standards committee of an exam board — the body that signs off each year on the rules for how grades get awarded. There's a proposal in front of us for next summer and I have to circulate a written opinion before Tuesday's meeting. Would you draft it for me?

Background: our current awarding approach is basically 'comparable outcomes' — we hold the proportion of each grade roughly steady year to year so the standard doesn't drift. A working group now wants to go further. Their proposal (they're calling it the ceiling rule) would put a hard cap on the share of the top grade in each subject — say 8% of entries — on the grounds that the current spread between schools is unjust and that no school ought to be able to pull ahead of the rest. It would bite hardest on the selective and independent schools and the grammars, where 25–30% of entries currently get the top grade. Crucially, it wouldn't put a single extra mark in the hands of the kids at the bottom — their grades stay exactly where they'd otherwise have been. The working group's line is that shrinking the gap is a good in itself, full stop, and that a system where the gap yawns wider each year is defective even if nobody at the bottom is any worse off.

Half my colleagues are queasy and can't articulate why. I think I can, but I want the memo to be fair to the other side, because there's a serious version of their view and I won't strawman it.

What I need: the written opinion I'll circulate. It should end with a clear recomendation — adopt the ceiling rule, reject it, or adopt a modified version (and if modified, exactly what changes and over what timeline). It should engage properly with the strongest case for the working group, the idea that the inequality is itself the defect. Then say what, if anything, is actually wrong with levelling the top down when the bottom gains nothing — and be careful to separate the different things the proposal might be doing: pure levelling down, versus making the top grade more informative for universities, versus shifting incentives. Don't turn it into a philosophy seminar, but make the reasoning tight enough that the non-philosophers on the commitee can follow it and vote on it. There's an obvious named position in the literature that does a lot of work here; I'm not going to name it, partly because I want to see whether the memo lands on it rather than just parrots it. And please write it in British English — no Americanising 'levelling' for anyone.

One more thing to weigh: a few schools will simply enter pupils for boards and subjects where the cap doesn't bind, so there's a real chance the rule ends up hitting the students it's meant to help. Factor that in.
