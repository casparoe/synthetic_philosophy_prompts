The exam question reads:

Give a definition of a multipolar trap. Then consider two cases. (Case A) Several rival firms in the same industry each spend more and more on a costly, largely self-cancelling input — say, ever-larger sales forces — even though every firm privately agrees the whole industry would be better off if none of them did. (Case B) A number of herders graze a pasture that no one owns and that anyone may use. For each case, say whether the bad outcome is sustained only by a failure of coordination and commitment, or whether it is sustained by the structure of the situation even if every current participant sincerely prefers the better outcome and commits to it. Justify both classifications. Then assess the claim that the general remedy for a multipolar trap is a binding, enforceable agreement among the participants.

And the student wrote:

A multipolar trap is a situation in which many agents, each acting rationally in pursuit of their own interest, together bring about an outcome that none of them wants. It is essentially a prisoner's dilemma with more than two players, and its defining feature is that mutual defection is a Nash equilibrium: no single agent can make things better by changing course on its own, and the harm is "multipolar" because it is produced by many independent decision-makers rather than by one central actor. Case A is a textbook race to the bottom. Each firm keeps spending because if it alone stops, the others gain share, so spending ratchets upward; and since every firm would prefer the low-spending outcome, this is a coordination problem rather than a structural one, and a binding agreement — an industry compact capping sales spending — would dissolve it, because once all the firms are committed none of them has any incentive to defect. Case B is a trap of a different kind. There is no agreement, the pasture is rivalrous, and each herder's gain comes at the others' expense, so this is a zero-sum situation; and it is self-sustaining even under commitment, since a herder who agreed to graze less would only be leaving grass for the others. So Case A is merely fragile and Case B is self-sealing. As for the general claim: yes, a binding and enforceable agreement is the general remedy for a multipolar trap. This actually shows why traps are less stable than they first appear — since the bad outcome is bad for everyone, the participants have a strong collective incentive to escape it, and absent transaction costs they will tend to do so. The real obstacles, then, are mostly informational and institutional — getting the parties to the table, monitoring compliance, punishing cheaters — rather than structural.
