I am working through Yates v. United States, the 2015 Supreme Court case in which a commercial fisherman in the Gulf of Mexico was found with undersized red grouper in his catch, was instructed by a federal agent to keep the undersized fish segregated until the vessel returned to port, and instead had a crew member throw them overboard. He was charged under 18 U.S.C. § 1519, a provision added by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, which punishes anyone who knowingly alters, destroys, conceals, or covers up "any record, document, or tangible object" with the intent to impede or obstruct a federal investigation. The Court reversed his conviction by a 5–4 vote. Justice Ginsburg's plurality opinion, joined by the Chief Justice and Justices Breyer and Sotomayor, held that a "tangible object" within the statute's compass is one used to record or preserve information, and so does not include fish; it leaned on the statutory heading, the section's placement among the financial-fraud provisions of Title 18, the canon that a word is known by the company it keeps, the canon that general words following specific ones take their color from the specifics, and an argument that the government's broad reading would render superfluous a neighboring provision. Justice Alito concurred only in the judgment. Justice Kagan dissented, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy, and Thomas, reading "tangible object" in its ordinary sense as any discrete thing possessing physical form, and appealing to what an ordinary reader would take the words to cover.

I want an analysis of this episode as a case study in the dispute between textualism and purposivism, and I want it run in both directions.

First, the theory onto the case. Sort the opinions: is the plurality's method recognizably textualist, or is it contextualist in a way that quietly imports purpose? Do the canons it invokes actually constrain, or do they merely supply respectable language for a result reached on other grounds? What should we make of the fact that the Court's most committed textualists ended up on the broad, dictionary-driven side, while the justices usually associated with a more purposive or living-constitutional sensibility produced the narrow, context-sensitive reading? Where does the legislative purpose behind Sarbanes-Oxley — protecting investors and restoring trust in financial markets after a corporate accounting scandal — genuinely bear on the case, and does the plurality need it or only gesture at it? What work, if any, is the rule of lenity doing, and should it have been doing more, given that the provision carries a maximum of twenty years?

Then the case onto the theory. Does this episode confirm that "textualism" names a family of methods rather than a single method, and if so, is that a defect or just what any interpretive theory looks like when it meets a hard case? Does it show that the real disagreement is not text versus purpose but which contextual materials count as part of the text's meaning? Is there a coherent version of textualism that would have decided this case either way, and if both readings are available, what is the theory for choosing? Finally, say what this does to the familiar claim that these methods differ mainly in how much they constrain judges, and whether the case is better read as evidence for or against that claim.

Give me a verdict rather than a survey, and calibrate your confidence in it explicitly. If my framing is confused — for instance, if treating this as a textualism-versus-purposivism case misdescribes what actually divided the Court — say so plainly rather than working around it, and tell me what the real axis of disagreement was.
