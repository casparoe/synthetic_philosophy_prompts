I have been trying to keep hold of the ordinary thought that the museum case contains one thing, a statue, and I keep writing myself into an argument that seems to show it contains indefinitely many. Here it is.

(1) The lump of clay on the bench would have survived being flattened; the statue would not.
(2) So there is something true of the lump that is not true of the statue.
(3) If a is identical with b, whatever is true of a is true of b.
(4) So the statue and the lump are not identical.
(5) Yet they consist of the same clay in the same arrangement, in the same place at the same time. So what separates them is only what each would and would not survive — and if they really are two, there must be something over and above that which makes them two, on pain of the difference between them being brute.
(6) The lump also sat on the bench for a week before the statue was finished. So they do not even have the same history; but "the statue was already there, shapeless, on Monday" sounds false, and so does "the lump came into existence on Tuesday."
(7) Nothing in (1)-(5) is special to statues and clay. Take the hedge in my garden that has been clipped into the shape of a bird: it would have survived a season of neglect, the bird would not. Whatever the modal difference does in the first case, it does here.
(8) So for every description under which the same matter falls — statue, lump, bowl it might have been, doorstop, paperweight — there is a distinct object, and nothing in the argument says where the list stops.
(9) So the museum case contains as many objects as there are descriptions of its contents, and no fact of the matter fixes how many.

I would rather keep (1) and (4) than accept (9): I do think the clay would survive the flattening and the statue would not. But I cannot find the slide. Which step should I give up, why did it look innocent, and what do I pay for giving it up? If the honest answer is that (1)-(8) are sound and (9) is what I must abandon, say so and tell me what I then owe — in particular, what becomes of "there is one statue in the case," and of the claim that the statue is worth a fortune while the lump is worth a few dollars, when they are the same clay in the same place.
