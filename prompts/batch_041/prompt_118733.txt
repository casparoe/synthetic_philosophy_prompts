A friend and I spent last night arguing about whether it is ever worth arguing with someone who seems unreachable, and we kept circling Twelve Angry Men: she thinks it is the great dramatic proof that patient reasons can turn a hostile room, and I think the reasons are almost decorative. I would like a reading that adjudicates between us. Work through the film with the vocabulary of the polarization and echo-chamber debates, and with a distinction I have been chewing on — disagreement that tracks a difference in what people are motivated to believe versus disagreement that tracks a difference in how they weigh the same evidence. Which one is that jury room actually dramatizing? Notice how the first defection follows not an argument but a prop, a switchblade Juror 8 bought himself; how one juror votes for acquittal because he wants to get to a ballgame; how the last holdout breaks over his own son rather than over anything said. Then notice the oddity that this is a sealed room with no incoming information — the paradigm of the epistemic bubble — and yet it ends in what looks like success. Is that a genuine counterexample to the pessimists, or does it depend on features of a jury room that do not survive outside it? Say too where the film resists this reading, if it does, and what it shows that the theoretical literature cannot.
