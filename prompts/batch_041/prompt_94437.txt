I'm giving the commencement address at my alma mater this June — a small liberal arts college where I did a philosophy degree before drifting into forecasting, and where I now sit on the board, which is presumably why they asked. The invitation is uncomfortable in a way I'd like the speech to make a virtue of. I am, professionally, a person who takes the outside view: my job is to build reference classes and tell agencies what usually happens to undertakings like theirs. The commencement genre, meanwhile, is the purest inside view there is — this class is exceptional, their futures are theirs to shape, the base rates don't apply to them. I won't give that speech. But I also won't give the knowing, deflationary version, in which I stand up and recite the statistics for how many of them will be doing something else in ten years; that isn't honesty, it's the opposite error dressed up as rigor, and it would misrepresent what I actually believe.

What I want the address to argue — and to be built around, not merely to mention in passing — is that the outside view is a discipline and not an oracle. Base rates are priors, not verdicts; they have whatever grip they have only relative to a reference class that somebody chose, and choosing it is an act of judgment that can be every bit as self-serving or as lazy as any inside-view story. Forecasters ping-pong between reference classes until they find one that flatters the answer they wanted, and the correction for that is not to abandon the outside view but to notice the ping-pong. So the two views aren't rivals standing on either side of a question; they run in a loop, and the honest form of epistemic modesty is modesty at both turns — about the narrative you'd like to tell about yourself, and about the class you've sorted yourself into in order to correct it. I also want to resist the obvious closing moral. The lesson of the outside view is not "expect less." If anything it licenses bolder, higher-variance choices, once you see how wide the distribution really is and how little of it anyone controls; timidity, too, is usually an inside-view story.

The audience will be about a third parents who paid tuition, a third faculty who will notice if I fumble the epistemology, and a third students who have been sitting in folding chairs in the sun for two hours and would like this over in under ten minutes. Please write the address: seven or eight minutes as delivered, composed to be spoken aloud, with no citations, no quotations, and nothing that presupposes the listener has read anything. It should be funny once or twice without becoming a comedy routine, and it should be sincere — the students should leave feeling addressed rather than processed.
