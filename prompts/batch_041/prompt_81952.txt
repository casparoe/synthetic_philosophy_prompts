I'm a science journalist — I cover AI and cognitive science for a general-interest magazine, and for the past fourteen months I've been embedded part-time with a small company out here while they built their first real consumer product, an assistant they're shipping next month. They've asked me to give the toast at the launch dinner, which is flattering and also a trap, because the room splits three ways and each third wants something different out of my four minutes. A third of the room are the engineers and research staff — they've spent a year and a half staring at evals and loss curves, and they visibly wince whenever anyone says the model "wants" or "believes" or "is trying to" anything; one of them keeps a running tally on his phone. Another third are sales, support, and a bunch of early users with their partners in tow, and they talk about the assistant as if it has moods, preferences, opinions — and they're not being sloppy or sentimental about it, that's just how the thing is usable; they ask it what it thinks and then act on the answer, and it works. The last third are investors and spouses and parents who've heard me on the radio and are expecting, I think, that I'll explain AI to them for a bit.

What I'm stuck on is that I don't want to give either of the two toasts that are actually available to me. The first is the deflationary one — it's autocomplete, dont get attached, there's nobody in there, everyone nods — which is cheap, insults the second third of the room, and worse, doesn't describe how the product actually works. The second is the sentimental one, where the assistant cares about you and has your back, which I'm fairly sure is false and which the engineers would rightly resent hearing from me. What I want is a toast that can say what the assistant "thinks" and "wants" all night long — because you cannot describe its behavior otherwise, and the room's daily experience of it is exactly that — while being completely scrupulous about what that language commits us to regarding whether anybody is home. I've been reading around the Dennett idea about the intentional stance and I think that's my spine: treating something as a believer and a wanter is a predictive strategy, it can be a wildly successful one, and adopting it isn't the same as positing a little person inside; and there's a ladder of these stances (you can also predict a system by its mechanism, or by what it was designed to do), and most of the confusion in this room is people standing on different rungs and shouting at each other. I'd like the toast to make that visible without ever turning into a lecture about it, and to be honest enough that the engineers don't think I sold out and warm enough that the users don't think I'm calling them suckers.

You're a philosopher of mind who also writes speeches for a living, so you know the difference between a toast with an idea in it and a lecture wearing a tuxedo.
