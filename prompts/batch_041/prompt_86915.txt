I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of social science and I keep running into the same move from a colleague, and I want to know whether it actually works before I try to pull it apart in a seminar next month.

The move: whenever someone says you shouldn't generalise about a people — that "the Italians are like this" or "the Japanese are like that" is either meaningless or pernicious — he says it's exactly like saying men are taller than women. The distributions overlap, loads of women are taller than loads of men, but the claim about the averages is perfectly true and perfectly useful. So national character claims, he says, are just ordinary statistical generalisations: true of the group, not of every member, and the squeamishness about them is moralising dressed up as methodology. He leans on this pretty hard — it's what lets him say quite strong things about cultural differences in a seminar without anyone being allowed to object.

What I want is an audit. First, what actually maps onto what — what's playing the role of height, of the group means, of the overlapping distributions, of the sample? Then where the mapping breaks. I've a nagging feeling the height case has features the nation case doesn't. The reference class is contested and shifting (who counts as "the Italians"?). There's the ecological fallacy lurking — group-level and individual-level claims coming apart. There's Simpson's paradox — the comparison flipping depending on how you slice the population. And there's the way a label can feed back into the people it's applied to, so the "trait" is partly produced by the classifying rather than merely measured by it. Height is also a single dimension on a ratio scale with a fairly clean causal story behind it; "orderly" or "warm" is... decided by whom, measured how, and stable across what?

So: give me the correspondence table, seperate the disanalogies that matter for his intended use from the ones that don't, and say what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks (a single dimension? a fixed population? value-free measurement?). Then tell me straight whether his conclusion — that national character talk is just statistics and the objections are moralising — survives once we're done, or whether the height analogy was carrying it the whole way. If there's a better analogy for what he's actually entitled to, say what it is. And a small thing, but I'd like to know how much of this turns on whether we're making a descriptive claim or smuggling in a causal/essentialist one, because I think that's alot of where the fight really is.
