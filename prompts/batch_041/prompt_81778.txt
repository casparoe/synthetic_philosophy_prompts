I've been thinking about driverless cars, and it has left me with a case I can't settle. Suppose a delivery company runs a fleet of self-driving vans: the route-planning software was written by one firm, the collision-avoidance system was trained by a second on data bought from a third, and the vans go on retraining themselves on their own driving once they're on the road. One van makes a maneuver that emerged from the interaction of these parts, that no engineer at any of the three firms had imagined, and that none of the components would have produced alone. A pedestrian is badly hurt. Nobody lied, cut corners, or brushed aside a risk they knew about; the maneuver simply never occurred to anyone.

What is your honest credence that in this case no human being is morally to blame for the injury — that responsibility really has fallen into a gap rather than onto someone we simply haven't looked hard enough for? My own gut puts it near 0.2, but I care far more about the reasoning than the number. Break it down: how much rides on whether being to blame requires that you could have foreseen the harm or could have done something to prevent it; how much on whether blame can be divided and shared, so that three companies each somewhat at fault can sum to full fault with nothing left over; how much on whether a person can be to blame merely for taking on a role — running the firm, signing off on the deployment — whatever they happened to cause; and how much on whether being to blame is a single thing here at all, or splits into fault, liability, and having to answer to someone, which can come apart. Give me a rough probability for each piece, say how you'd combine them, and tell me which piece the final number is most sensitive to — and what you'd have to learn to move it.
