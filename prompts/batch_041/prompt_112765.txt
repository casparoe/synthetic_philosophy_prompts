I am a doctoral student in philosophy writing a paper with my supervisor on weakness of will. She has published nothing on the subject, declines to state a general account, and insists that she judges cases one at a time. I have therefore been keeping a record of her verdicts in order to work out what she is in fact committed to, and I need your help with the next entry.

Her verdicts so far.

Rosa had settled that morning on finishing her grant report and continued to hold that the report was the better thing to do; when a friend offered her a cinema ticket she hesitated, then went. Verdict: weakness of will.

Nadia had formed no plan about the matter; standing on a crowded train she judged that she ought to give up her seat and, preferring to sit, did not. Verdict: weakness of will.

Tomas had intended for years to remain in the city, but over several months of reflection came to think that moving to the country was better for his family; he moved, with no inner struggle, and continued to endorse the move. Verdict: not weakness of will, on the ground that he had changed his mind.

Priya judged it best to testify truthfully, then lied at the hearing after her employer told her she would lose her job otherwise; she experienced the choice as forced. Verdict: not weakness of will; her will was overborne.

Marek had never formed any view about whether to attend his brother's dinner; passing a bar on the way he went in, missed the dinner, and felt no reproach. Verdict: not weakness of will; there was no judgment to betray.

Béla judged it best to accept a job he disliked and accepted it, though he would far rather have declined. Verdict: not weakness of will; he did what he judged best.

The new case, which will be the centrepiece of the paper. Fatima decided on Sunday, deliberately and after reflection, to spend Saturday finishing her thesis rather than accept a standing invitation to a party. On Saturday, with the party an hour away and her deadline a fortnight away, she sincerely and calmly judges that going to the party is what she has most reason to do now. She would not say that the party is more worth having than the completed chapter; nothing has happened to her, no new information has arrived, and the change has simply kept pace with the nearness of the party. She does not struggle: the Sunday decision occurs to her, she acknowledges it, and she goes. She recognises the pattern from many previous Saturdays, each of which she later regretted. A behavioural economist friend describes her as hyperbolic in her discounting and maintains that this is not a failure of will at all but merely the preference reversal any time-inconsistent agent undergoes; I cannot tell whether that is a rival account of the case or the same phenomenon in another vocabulary.

Please address the following. First, your prediction of my supervisor's verdict on Fatima, with your reasons. Second, the two or three most plausible rules that fit her entire record, and for each what it implies about Fatima and which entry in the record strains it most. Third, which rule you would bet she is running, and why. Fourth, the single further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, and what each predicts about it. Fifth, whether the economist's description, if correct, commits her to treating every preference reversal as weakness of will, or whether her record leaves a reading on which it does not.

Set the candidate rules out in a compact table with one column for the verdict each yields on Fatima and one for the record entry that strains it, and keep the whole reply under 700 words.
