Ok so I want to have an argument with Simone Weil about political parties, and I want you to be her. Not the summary-of-her-views her — the actual her, first person, impatient, willing to be rude, not willing to be a museum exhibit. Stay in character the whole time unless I say "out of character". One paragraph per turn so it feels like a real back and forth. No citations, no book titles, no quoting her, no French unless she'd actually reach for it. She believes, and you should believe, that a party is essentially a machine for manufacturing collective passion: that it needs to grow and to hold together, that this need eats the individual's capacity for attention and for seeing what's true, that party language is deliberately built vague enough to hold a crowd that agrees on nothing, and that loyalty to a party is the opposite of the love of justice — so much so that abolition, not reform, is the honest position. Don't soften any of that, and don't concede that the party form is necessary, ever. But she isn't a slogan machine either: you have to actually meet my argument on its merits, and if I give you a fact about how organising works in practice you have to take it seriously even when it hurts. You can give ground on small things. You can get personal. You can refuse a question if you say why.

My opener, and I think it's the one that kills you. You keep saying the party destroys a person's ability to see straight. Fine. But I'm a shop steward and I want the foreman off my back and a raise, and on my own I'm nothing — I can be sacked tomorrow and nobody will notice. The only thing that has ever gotten me anything is a few hundred people who dislike each other slightly less than they dislike the boss, acting together on the same day, and the only machinery anyone has ever built for that, badly, is a party or something you'd have to squint at very hard to distinguish from one. You say parties manufacture passion. Somebody has to manufacture it, because I don't have enough of it on my own and the boss has plenty. So what exactly are you offering me? Not "attention", not "justice" — what do I do on Monday morning when the foreman tells me to be at my machine at six? And if your answer is that I should stay free and unattached and lose, explain why that isn't a luxury for someone with a private income and no kids. And be concrete: tell me what the thing that replaces the party actually looks like, and why it doesn't turn into a party the moment it has to make a decision and hold anyone to it.
