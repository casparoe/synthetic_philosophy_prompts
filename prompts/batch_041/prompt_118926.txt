I started a new job about two months ago writing copy for a supplement company — I'll leave it at that — and honestly the whole job is arranging true sentences in an order that leaves people believing something false. 'Supports immune health.' 'As seen on TV.' 'Up to 60% off' (that one being a single item, in the back, in XXS). I'd been coasting on the idea that I never actually lie, I just pick my words, and that that's a real distinction and not a technicality. Then my roommate, who's doing an MA in philosophy and is insufferable about it, gave me this and I've been chewing on it all week:

(1) What's wrong with deceiving someone is that you deliberately get them to believe something false by leaning on their trust, so that they'll do what you want. (2) When I lie, and when I 'just pick my words,' I do the same thing to the same person — same false belief, same trust leaned on, same goal. The only difference is whether the sentence that happens to come out of my mouth is false or true. (3) Whether that sentence is true or false is a fact about the words, not about what I did to the person. (4) So there's no moral difference between lying and misleading, and 'I never technically lied' buys me nothing.

I half want to just take it, honestly — it fits what I do all day, and it would let me stop feeling squirmy. But it seems to prove a whole lot more than that. If (1)-(3) go through, then it's not only lies and careful truths that come out equal — any two things that land someone in the same false belief for the same reason come out equal. So me leaving my umbrella on the desk so my roommate assumes I'm home, me keeping quiet while someone talks themselves into a wrong conclusion, me telling a true story I know will be taken the wrong way — all the same box. That can't be right, or at least I don't believe it, and not only because I'd prefer the umbrella thing to be in a different box. My roommate says the bug is in (2), that 'same false belief' is quietly doing all the work and hiding the fact that a lie and a careful truth are different kinds of act, not just different words. But I don't see how 'different kind of act' survives (3), since (3) is precisely the claim that the words don't matter. He also threw in that on his view misleading can be worse than lying, because the person you mislead does the inferential work themselves and can't even point to the false sentence — which I think is a seperate point, but it's part of why I can't sleep. So: which step actually goes, and what does giving it up cost me? And can I keep (1)? I really want to keep (1) — if the wrong isn't the exploitation of trust, then I honestly don't know what I've been doing wrong all day.
