My friend and I spent most of Saturday arguing about indicative versus counterfactual conditionals — he says the whole difference comes down to tense plus whatever the speaker happens to believe about the antecedent, and I think that's wrong in a way that matters — and afterward I sketched the essay I should have written. I'd like your comments on the sketch rather than on a finished draft. Please tell me (a) whether the criterion I propose actually sorts the cases I use it on, (b) whether my problem case is fatal or can be absorbed, (c) where my friend's position is stronger than I've allowed, and (d) what to cut. I don't need citations or reading suggestions; I want the argument pressure-tested. Please answer in American English.

The sketch runs like this. Thesis: the indicative/counterfactual distinction is not marked by verb morphology and not by the truth value of the antecedent. It marks which possibilities the speaker is treating as live, with "counterfactual" naming the limiting case in which the antecedent is excluded from that live set.

The criterion I want to defend is a vindication test. Assert "If A, then B." Then learn that A is true. If the natural response is "then I was right" — the assertion is vindicated — the conditional was indicative. If the natural response is that the assertion must be withdrawn or restated ("that isn't the case I was talking about"), it was counterfactual.

Cases I run through it: (1) "If the butler did it, the knife is in the greenhouse" — vindicated on learning he did it, so indicative. (2) "If the butler had done it, the knife would have been in the greenhouse" — on learning he did do it, the sentence seems sometimes to survive and sometimes to be voided, and I can't tell whether that instability is a defect in the test or a fact about counterfactuals. (3) "If you touch that, I'll scream" versus "If you were to touch that, I'd scream": the backshifted version is used for tentativeness with a fully live antecedent, so backshifting cannot be what encodes falsity. (4) Future contingents: "If it rains tomorrow the picnic is off" versus "If it were to rain tomorrow the picnic would be off" — same live antecedent, different felt remoteness.

The conclusion I want to draw is that counterfactuality comes in degrees, as epistemic remoteness, rather than being a binary, and that the textbook counterfactual is just the endpoint of the scale where the antecedent is presented as settled against. What I can't decide is whether the remoteness scale explains anything or whether I've merely renamed the phenomenon, and whether case (2) shows that even the endpoint is unstable.

Give me your comments — what holds up, what doesn't, and whether this is worth finishing.
