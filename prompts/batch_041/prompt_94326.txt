I'm drafting the intro chapter of a thesis on chance and credence and I've decided to write the spec first, before I look at any of the actual theories. So: what does a theory of the credence-chance link actually have to deliver? Target to aim at is the principle everyone starts from -- if you know the objective chance of A is x, and your evidence is "admissible" (it doesn't already tell you whether A, and it isn't information that the chance itself already encodes), then your credence in A should be x. Everyone nods at that sentence and then the whole paper is a fight about the small print. I want the small print turned into requirements.

Two things to keep off the page: no bibliography, no citations, no "as so-and-so argued" -- I know who argued what, and I want the list to stand on its own as a list. And dont sneak in requirements that are really one camp's taste. If a requirement only bites if you already think chance is a primitive, or only if you're a Humean about chance, then it goes in the second section, labelled as such, not in the list.

Sections, in this order:

**Bottom line.** Two or three sentences: on your reading, is there any theory that can meet the whole list, or is the list itself already a no-go result? I'd rather have the verdict up front than at the end.

**The list.** 600-900 words. Numbered requirements. For each one: state it in a sentence, then the consideration that forces it -- a case, a counterexample, a feature of how we actually use the word "chance", whatever does the real work. Give me the case with the requirement, not a gesture at the case. Things I'm already worried about, and want placed properly or told they dont belong, plus whatever I've missed: what makes evidence admissible, given that "don't use information about how it turns out" starts looking circular once you notice the chance itself is information about how it turns out; futures that are live possibilities now but that would, if they happened, have made the present chances different; chance-1 propositions that fail to happen and chance-0 propositions that happen anyway; chances about chances, and how credence in a chance proposition should move as time passes and the chance changes; cases where "the chance for me, now" is indexical rather than about the world out there; deterministic worlds, where it's not even clear there are any chances for the principle to be about; and whether deferring to chance is really like deferring to a better-informed expert, given that experts can be wrong and chances (whatever they are) can't.

**Requirement or preference?** 150-250 words. Go back through the list and mark which items are non-negotiable for any theory at all, and which are artefacts of a particular picture -- and say which picture, in a clause, not an essay.

**The squeeze.** 250-400 words. This is the part I actually want. Find the subset of requirements that can't be jointly satisfied -- if there's a trilemma or quadrilemma buried in there, name the three or four things and lay out what you'd be giving up on each way out. Where a pair is unsatisfiable, give me the derivation, not just the verdict.

**Which requirement does the most work.** 100 words max. The one requirement that, if you hold it, kills off the most candidate theories -- and what it kills.
