I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist and have come to philosophy only recently, which may explain the habit that got me into this: I keep asking what the instrument actually measures rather than trusting the label on the front of it. A reading group I have joined has been working through questions about the funding of higher education, and I find that the two arrangements I know best are defended in vocabularies that seem to commit their defenders to incompatible pictures of what a university education is. In most German states, and broadly across the Nordic countries, a public university charges the resident student no tuition at all: the state pays the institution, the price the student faces is zero, and her cash outlay is a small administrative fee plus the cost of living. In England and in most of the United States, the same kind of institution charges a substantial fee, financed by loans repaid out of the earnings the degree makes possible, and the standard defence is that the student is the principal beneficiary and should therefore be the principal payer. It has not escaped me that the first arrangement subsidises the supply and the second subsidises the demand: in one, the money goes to the university on the student's behalf and she is never a purchaser; in the other, she is handed purchasing power and passes it on.

The free arrangement is defended in the language of public goods and equal opportunity, yet the same states that refuse to charge tuition means-test the money a student needs to live on, and frequently lend it rather than grant it, which looks like treating her as an investor in a private asset; if the education really is a public good, I cannot see why the maintenance grant should be a private loan. The fee arrangement is defended in the language of private benefit, yet the fees are capped, subsidised, and forgiven under various conditions, which suggests the state does not quite believe the benefit is wholly private. There is also an awkward fact I take to be reasonably well established, namely that abolishing tuition has not flattened the gradient in participation by parental background in the countries that did it, which would suggest the policy does not rest on the belief that price is the binding barrier, whatever its defenders say. To sharpen the question, consider a scheme a colleague once described to me: a state abolishes tuition but levies a fixed percentage of the earnings of everyone who graduates from one of its universities and remains in the state for ten years. That is not the old policy under a new name; it says something different about who the beneficiary is and what the state believes it is buying.

So I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of each arrangement — what view of the good being distributed, and of the relation between student, institution, and polity, it only makes sense under — and to tell me which of its presuppositions are strictly required and which are merely congenial to it, since I suspect a good deal of the rhetoric is decoration. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several quite different theories would license the very same arrangement, say so rather than choosing for it. And tell me what would have to change if the presupposed picture were replaced by a rival: if education were a positional good whose value is largely exhausted by being ahead of others, or a right whose exercise must not be rationed by price, or simply an investment in a tax base the state expects to recoup. Where the parts of an arrangement presuppose inconsistent things, I would much rather you said so plainly than smoothed it over. Please state how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of. Write it as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold, or tables; I read these things the way I read a letter.
