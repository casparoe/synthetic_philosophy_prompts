Ok so I've been reading a lot of popular history about why the industrial revolution took off in England and not somewhere else, and separately digging through local records about why my town ended up the county's mill town while the one twelve miles down the road stayed farms, and I keep running into the same shape of argument in both, and I dont have a word for it.

The shape is this. Somebody explains the split by pointing to something the winner had and the loser didn't — coal, a navigable river, a dissenting chapel, a habit of tinkering. You say, fine, but the other one had that too — the other town had the same river, the other country had coal as well — and the explanation doesn't die. It just gets more specific. The winner had coal in the right place, or the river happened to join the right two points, or the tinkerers happened to already know each other. And half the features being cited are things the winner only acquired after it had started winning — the banks, the technical school, the reputation for being enterprising — but they're offered as the reason it won. What really nags at me is that the story is reversible. If the other one had won, I'm fairly sure somebody would be writting the mirror image, with the same confidence and the same kinds of features, just with the signs flipped. So the account can't lose: there's no fact about the losing case that would make anybody drop it, because any fact about the losing case can be re-described as one more difference. My brother in law does something adjacent for a living and just says it's complicated, which is not helpful.

I know the usual warnings — correlation isn't causation, survivorship bias, just-so stories, hindsight — and I've heard people say the whole why-England-and-not-China question is badly posed, but none of those is quite the thing I'm circling. Is there an established name for this specific move, where a comparison between two cases quietly turns into two separate one-off descriptions, and where the outcome is doing the work of deciding which of a thousand differences count as causes? And is there a name for the related suspicion that the comparison looks obvious in hindsight but maybe wasn't a comparison of two of anything until it was over? If nothing established fits, coin something, give me the definition and the boundary cases, and tell me where the line runs between this and just doing comparative history honestly — I don't want to come away thinking the whole field is a con, and I'm not asking for a reading list, I want the carving. Lead with your answer in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no wind-up, and don't restate what I asked.
