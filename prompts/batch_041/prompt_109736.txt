I've got a stats PhD and these days I do causal inference on observational data — potential outcomes, identification, confounding, DAGs, the usual. Through no fault of my own I've ended up in a reading group on the historical Jesus, and I keep wanting to translate everything into my own language, so I'd rather you just did it properly. I know basically no theology and I'm not a philosopher, so don't assume I'll catch you if you fudge the source side.

The fight in the group is about the 'criteria of authenticity' — the embarassment criterion, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, and whatever else is on the standard list — and whether they can settle which sayings go back to the man himself. One camp thinks that if you apply them carefully you can carve out a core of material you're entitled to use. The other camp is sceptical: the criteria are circular, question-begging, and don't actually discriminate.

Restate the whole thing in the vocabulary I actually think in — treatments, potential outcomes, identification, confounding, selection, collider bias, and so on — as faithfully as you can. Go criterion by criterion. Don't quietly upgrade either side into claims they wouldn't accept just because they happen to fit my language nicely.

Then the residue report. What maps cleanly? What only maps if you distort something? And what in their argument has no counterpart at all in mine — no treatment, no outcome, no estimator it could be? Include the obvious question at the end of it: does any of this add up to an answer to the question of who he was, or only to a list of bits you're willing to keep?

Two suspicions I want you to push back on, because I genuinely can't tell if they're real or just me. First, the dissimilarity criterion looks like a botched attempt to control for confounding: you want to net out anything that could have come from his Jewish setting or from the early church, but the way it's done feels like conditioning on something you really shouldn't be conditioning on. Second, the embarrassment criterion looks like it's leaning on a monotonicity assumption — the thought that the early church would only have preserved things that flattered it, so anything awkward must be early. Is either of those a fair way to put it, or am I importing my own hang-ups?

Last thing, and this is the bit I care about most: if the residue turns out to be large, is that my vocabulary failing to capture something real about how historians actually reason, or is it a sign that the criteria people never had a well-defined question in the first place?
