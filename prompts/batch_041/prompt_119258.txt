I'm a philosophy graduate student writing a seminar paper on probability in the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, and I've hit a wall that I now suspect is more interesting than the paper I originally set out to write. On one side of the literature there's a well-developed project of deriving the Born rule from the branching structure of the theory together with constraints on how a rational agent ought to bet — the thought being that anyone who accepts the Everettian ontology and is internally consistent about her preferences will end up assigning exactly the Born-rule weights. On the other side, critics press what look to me like a rotating set of objections: that the derivation assumes what it is supposed to prove, that a notion of "measure" over branches is doing unjustified work, that a rational agent in a fully deterministic theory has no reason to care about how many successor branches contain copies of her, or that probability-talk simply doesn't belong in a theory in which nothing is chancy. My seminar has now spent three weeks circling this and nobody has moved an inch, which is what makes me think the disagreement may not be where everyone thinks it is.

My own suspicion — and I'd like you to treat it as a hypothesis to test rather than a conclusion to endorse — is that the two camps are answering different questions under a single heading: one side is asking a normative question about what a rational agent should believe and how she should bet, while the other is asking a descriptive question about what the theory itself says concerning physical frequencies, and the apparent clash is really a clash between a credence question and a frequency question that never quite meet. I'd like you to diagnose the dispute rather than adjudicate it. Is this a verbal quarrel, a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of probability, a technical gap in the derivations that someone could in principle close, or genuinely two questions wearing one name? Use what each side actually claims as your evidence, and be specific about where the friction is.

Two further things I want from the diagnosis. First, tell me whether the increasingly popular move of recasting the problem in terms of self-locating uncertainty — treating the agent as merely unsure which branch she occupies, rather than as facing genuine chance — actually resolves anything, or whether it relocates the mystery without discharging it; if the latter, say precisely what remains unexplained. Second, given your diagnosis, say what would count as real progress here: a theorem, a conceptual distinction, an experiment, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed and ought to be abandoned. Be concrete about what each side would have to give up for the dispute to advance, and whether you think that is likely.
