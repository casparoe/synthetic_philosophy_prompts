Two of my friends got into an arguement at a party last month and it's still bugging me, so I'm outsourcing it. Nadia keeps ants — three colonies in tanks in her spare room, she can talk about them for hours — and her biggest colony she calls Aunt Hillary after some book she likes. Owen is in a philosophy PhD program. Someone made the mistake of asking what Nadia does for fun, and the two of them went at it for forty minutes about whether an ant colony can have a mind. I stayed out of it because I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, and I'd like you to referee.

Here's how it went, as best I can reconstruct it.

Owen opened: no, obviously not. An ant is a few hundred thousand neurons doing something simple, and the nest is thousands of ants running local loops. Nothing in the colony is in a position to receive what all the rest are doing. A brain has a place where the signals converge — one stream that the relevant stuff gets written into — and that convergence is what it is for there to be something it's like to be you. The colony has no such place. It's parallel and it's local. So "the colony decides" is a metaphor in the same way "the market decided" is a metaphor.

Nadia: by that argument no brain has a mind either, because no single neuron in your head receives the whole brain's signals, and there's no little room with a screen in it. You're pointing at the thalamus and calling it a screen.

Owen: that's not the same thing. There's a difference between a system that's massively parallel but mutually constrained — A's state changes B's, B's changes C's, C feeds back into A, so the whole thing is one tangled state that evolves together — and a system that's just a lot of agents bumping into each other. Your ants are barely coupled. Ant 40,000's state is close to irrelevant to ant 39,000's.

Then Nadia pulled out the case I think is the real crux. She said: fine, here's a test. Last spring a branch fell across the middle of Aunt Hillary's tank and split the colony in two for about a week. Each half kept foraging on its own trail, each half reacted separately when she dropped a different intruder near each end, no coordination between them. Then she took the branch out, the trails reconnected, ants mingled, and it's one colony again. So: does each half have its own mind while it's split? If yes, then Owen has to explain how two minds become one mind when she lifts a branch out, with neither of them dying or merging — minds don't do that. If no, then a half-colony of ten thousand ants is mindless and the whole colony of twenty thousand is minded, and he owes me an account of where exactly the line falls, because it can't just be "however many ants Nadia happens to have."

Owen said that's not a problem, that's split-brain patients — one mind that comes apart into two under surgery, and the fact that they're fragile and can be re-coupled is a feature of minds, not an objection. Nadia said: then "has a mind" is a matter of degree of coupling, and if coupling is all it takes, my thermostat with a feedback loop is on the scale somewhere. Owen said: right, which shows coupling can't be all it takes, so we're back to needing the extra thing.

There was also a stretch about the queen. Owen said she's the closest thing to a head in there, and Nadia said no, she's an ovary with legs, she doesn't direct anything, and the actual "decisions" — when to swarm, where to move — are made by the ants' statistics. Her favorite example: the pheromone trail. It's a message no ant wrote and no ant reads in full, but the next ant acts on it. Owen's reply was that a message with no author and no reader is a chemical gradient, and calling it a message is where the smuggling happens.

And near the end Owen offered a criterion: the colony has beliefs if the colony-level story predicts what the ants will do better than the ant-level story does. Nadia said great, then let's actually run it. Owen said no, wait — that test makes "the colony has a mind" true relative to an observer who wants a short story, which makes it a fact about us, not about the ants.

For the verdict, I wrote the moves down afterward so you can rate them one by one. Here's my mangled numbering:

1. Owen: minds need a place where the signals converge; the colony has no such place.
2. Nadia: the brain has no such place either, so the criterion kills brains too.
3. Owen: what matters isn't a screen, it's mutual constraint — the whole thing evolving as one tangled state.
4. Nadia: the split case, and the dilemma she builds from it (two minds that merge, or an arbitrary numerical threshold).
5. Owen: the split is just split-brain, and re-coupling is normal for minds.
6. Owen: the trail is a gradient, not a message; the message-talk is the mistake.
7. Owen: beliefs are whatever earns its keep in prediction; Nadia: then minds are relative to observers.

Go through these and tell me who's right about which, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify the ratings briefly. Then tell me who's more right overall, and what both of them are missing — I suspect there's something neither of them said that would dissolve half of this, and I'd rather hear it than hear that it depends what you mean by mind. No reading list please, neither of us is going to read it.
