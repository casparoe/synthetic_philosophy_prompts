I'm a novelist — four books, all of them the sort where nothing much happens and everyone talks about their feelings — and I've got a craft problem that I suspect is really a philosophy problem wearing a coat.

Let me give you my kit first, because it's the only vocabulary I actually trust. When I build a character I think in terms of an **engine**: the standing thing in them that makes them do what they do. An engine only shows itself on an **occasion** — a situation that gives it something to do. The scene where the reader first sees the engine work I call the **audition**; after that they read everything else through it, which is unfair but there it is. Some traits I've decided a character has never get an occasion at all — I call those **off-stage**. A **blocker** is something in the scene that stops the engine firing without touching the engine. A **trapdoor** is nastier: the very situation that would show the trait is the situation that takes it away, so the reader can never catch the character in the act. And **set-dressing** is the weather, the state of the kitchen, the way someone holds a cup — stuff that has nothing to do with the engine, that the reader cannot help reading as evidence about it anyway.

Now: years ago I read two or three philosophy articles about dispositions and half-understood them. The gist, as I remember it: saying a glass is fragile isn't the same as saying it would break if you dropped it, because you can rig the case — something that changes the glass the moment it's dropped, or something that catches it, or a thing that would only break if it were *not* fragile. Some people took this to show that no amount of conditionals about manifestations adds up to the disposition itself, and that a disposition needs some kind of basis that isn't just a summary of what happens. I can't remember who said what and I don't need the names.

What I want from you: restate that whole debate, as faithfully as you can, in my kit — engine, occasion, audition, off-stage, blocker, trapdoor, set-dressing. Then the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical position come across cleanly, which I've had to bend to fit, and which simply have no expression in my vocabulary at all — and same in reverse, what my kit says that the philosophers' apparatus can't. I'm especially interested in whether my audition and set-dressing ideas have any counterpart there, because it seems to me the philosophers take the conditions under which a trait shows itself as given, whereas for me choosing them is the whole job. My hunch is that the philosophy is the sharper of the two and my kit is just the same thing with mood lighting. Tell me if that's wrong.

And then answer the question I actually care about, which is whether there's any honest way to say a character *has* a trait that never gets an occasion in the book, or whether, in a novel, where everything is a scene, a trait is only ever the sum of its scenes and the off-stage ones are my private sentimentality.

Don't hedge and don't give me a both-ways list. Take a position, say which way the residue cuts, and give your reasons. Write it in British English.
