I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science, and I help run a small student podcast that sits somewhere between a reading group and an interview series. Next month our guest is a researcher who works on concept induction from sparse examples and who has spent the last few years arguing — against what he treats as the received framing — that the interest of Bongard-style puzzles has almost nothing to do with the fact that they present only a handful of labelled figures. His line, as I've reconstructed it from abstracts and one rather dense preprint, is that what a solver actually has to do is re-describe the raw figures in a vocabulary of parts, orientations, and spatial relations, and that a puzzle's difficulty is set by what he calls the "description gap": the distance between the most natural perceptual description of the figures and the description under which the target concept comes out simple. He likes to say this is why adding more examples to a puzzle usually buys you nothing, and why some puzzles with a single contrasting pair are trivial while others with a dozen instances stay hard. To make the discussion concrete I've been toying with a puzzle of my own: on one side, figures that each contain a triangle whose apex points toward the nearest edge of an implicit bounding frame; on the other, triangles whose apex points away from the nearest edge. Intuitively nothing in the figures as drawn changes — only the frame-relative orientation does — which seems to be exactly the kind of case his account is built to explain, though I'm not sure it isn't also the kind of case that makes the account circular.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the substance of the view rather than let him restate the slogan? I'd like at least one question on why he thinks the number of examples is a red herring, one on how the description gap is supposed to be defined and measured without tacitly presupposing the very hypothesis the solver is meant to find, one on how his position differs from Bayesian program-induction and minimum-description-length accounts of the same puzzles (including whether it is meant as a rival to them or a reframing of the same data), and one on the frame-relative puzzle above, since I suspect his answer there will be the most revealing. For each of the central questions, please add a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give — so that if he retreats to the claim that re-description is a "capacity" rather than a measurable quantity, or insists that the gap is fixed by the solver's prior vocabulary rather than by the figures, the next question already has him in view. Please close with a question about what evidence could count against the account, and make it one he can't answer by pointing to further confirming puzzles.
