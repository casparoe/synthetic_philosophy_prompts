In a reading group on the foundations of probability we keep colliding with a maxim that one of the members treats as self-evident: that where you have no reason to prefer any one of several possibilities to the others, you should treat them as equally likely, and that any assignment which does not do so is simply arbitrary. He deploys it in two ways that I suspect are not the same. First, when I raise Bertrand's paradox — the family of cases in which a uniform distribution over one natural parameterization of a setup yields a different answer than a uniform distribution over another, as with the several standard ways of choosing a chord at random in a circle — he says the paradox only shows that such questions are ill-posed, and that a genuine probability question always arrives with enough structure to fix a unique answer, so the maxim never actually lands us in trouble. Second, he uses it positively, to license flat priors over whatever partition of possibilities happens to be in front of us, most recently over a list of rival hypotheses about some historical event, on the grounds that we have no evidence favoring any one of them.

I would like you to treat that maxim as a piece of language to be taken apart rather than a doctrine to be defended. Lay out the distinct precise principles it could be standing for — for instance, a purely formal constraint on rational degrees of belief, a symmetry or invariance requirement, a rule of maximum entropy or least commitment, a heuristic for practical betting, or a claim about what a well-posed question must contain — and for each say what exactly it asserts, whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false as stated, and what it presupposes about the existence of a privileged reference class or parameterization. Then tell me which of those readings, if any, would actually support his two moves: which one would make Bertrand's paradox a sign of an ill-posed question rather than a reductio of the maxim, and which one would license the flat prior over his list of historical hypotheses. I am especially interested in whether the two uses require different readings, and in what the maxim would have to be weakened to if it is to survive the chord example at all. Please give me a menu with a verdict on each entry rather than a single recommended reading, since what I want is to see, at the next meeting, which reading he is actually relying on and whether he can have both.
