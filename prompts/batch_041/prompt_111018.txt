I am a novelist, some way into a book that follows a single family through three generations, and the hinge of the plot is not a death or a marriage but a change in the law: the moment a state decided that parental leave belonged to a parent rather than to a mother. I have the human material. What I lack is a disciplined way of thinking about what the policy actually did inside the household, and I would rather not smuggle an economic theory into the novel unexamined.

Take the following as given, since I do not want the analysis to turn on dates or figures I might have wrong. In 1974 Sweden replaced maternity leave with a parental insurance formally available to either parent, paid at a high replacement rate and financed publicly rather than by the employer. For roughly two decades the entitlement was almost entirely transferable within the couple, and mothers took the overwhelming majority of the days. In the mid-1990s a single month was reserved for each parent on a use-it-or-lose-it basis; the reserved period was later lengthened; fathers' share of the days taken rose substantially without approaching parity. A comparable shift occurred in Quebec after 2006, when a non-transferable paternity entitlement was introduced and fathers' take-up rose sharply.

The apparatus I have been using, and which I would like you to test rather than accept, is this. An entitlement has two independent properties. Its liquidity is how freely it can be reallocated between the two parents; its legibility is how visibly it is assigned to a named person, in public record and in workplace conversation. Transferable leave is liquid but anonymous; an earmarked month is legible but illiquid. I have been assuming that liquidity works through a bargaining or opportunity-cost channel — leave goes to whoever's absence is cheaper, or to whoever has less power to refuse — and that legibility works through a norm channel, by making a father's non-use a visible and nameable fact.

Analyse the two episodes through this apparatus. Tell me which channel each reform actually engaged, and whether the case can discriminate between two versions of the price story: the household allocating leave to the lower earner because that is genuinely the cheaper arrangement, versus the higher earner simply having the stronger bargaining position. Note that because the cost is socialised, the employer's incentive to penalise leave-takers is muted, which is awkward for the cruder versions of both stories. Then run the analysis in reverse: what do these cases show about the apparatus? In particular, what does the stubborn residual gap in the most generous and most thoroughly earmarked regimes do to it, and is there a case the framework never anticipated — the single parent by choice, the same-sex couple, the household in which the mother out-earns the father, the parent who does not live with the child — where it does not merely fit badly but has nothing to say? I would rather be told where the theory is silent than watch it be forced to speak.

Finally, and this matters more to me than the diagnosis: on whichever reading you settle, what would count as evidence against it, and what should I not claim in the book?
