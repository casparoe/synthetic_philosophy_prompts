I run the evaluation team at a lab training a large model whose outputs we can no longer check by hand: it writes code, drafts expert-level technical answers, and summarizes bodies of literature that nobody on my team has read. To supervise it we have adopted a procedure I will call arbitration. For any question we care about, two copies of the model are prompted to defend opposite answers and to see each other's moves; a judge then reads the full exchange and picks a winner. The judge is deliberately much weaker than the debaters. In our current setup it is a small model; in the version we are scaling toward it is a crowdworker with no relevant expertise and only a few minutes to spare. We then treat the judge's verdict as the label: it goes into the fine-tuning set as though it were the truth.

Two features of this arrangement bother me. First, we are confident the judge cannot answer these questions itself, and we do not trust either debater's unaided answer; yet we treat the judge's verdict as a better guide to the answer than anything the debaters produce on their own. Second, we treat everything the debaters say about themselves — that they are confident, that they have checked the arithmetic, that their argument is sound — as worthless testimony, while treating the judge's equally unverifiable verdict as authoritative. I suspect both features are doing real philosophical work and I cannot say what it is.

What does this procedure presuppose, and do the presuppositions hold together? What view of truth and evidence does it take to treat a weak judge's verdict as a training label — is the verdict functioning as evidence about the answer, as a stipulation of it, or as a proxy for something else entirely? What must be assumed about the asymmetry between defending a true answer and defending a false one for the procedure to be sound at all, and is that assumption empirical (about how these systems happen to behave) or conceptual (about what arguing is)? Does the procedure require a debater who has the right answer to be able to tell that it has it, and to produce a defense a much weaker judge will recognize as decisive — or only to be able to produce some defense? And is there a coherent epistemology on which the judge's verdict is privileged over the debaters' self-reports, or does that privilege rest on something non-epistemic, such as the fact that the judge is the only party whose output we can afford to collect at scale?

Please lay out the candidate readings of the procedure's implicit theory rather than settling on one: which views of knowledge, justification, and testimony would license it, which would forbid it, and between which of them the practice is simply underdetermined. Separate what the procedure strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say which parts of it are doing genuine epistemic work and which are conveniences. Finally, suppose we replaced the presupposed view with a deflationary one — the verdict is only a proxy for human approval, not a guide to truth — and tell me what would then have to change: the protocol, the way we describe it, or the training objective.
