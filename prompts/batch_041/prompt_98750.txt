I'm on the policy team at a small AI lab and I own the doc that tells our assistant when to refuse, when to comply, and when to push back. Right now it's basically a list of banned topics and it's a mess — we refuse things we obviously shouldn't (someone asking how to taper off a benzo because theyre scared) and we cheerfully help with stuff that blows up on us later. I want a decision procedure my team can actually run, in order, on a single request.

The thing I keep tripping on is that we lump three different moves under "paternalism" and they are not equally bad: (1) withholding information the user asked for, (2) declining to do the task at all, and (3) complying but adding friction — a warning, a confirmation step, a delay, offering the safer alternative first. I want the procedure to treat those as separate rungs with separate justifications, and to be explicit about which rung is paternalistic in the classic sense (interfering with someone for their own good, against their present will) versus which is just us doing the job well or badly.

Ground it in whatever the anti-paternalism / soft-paternalism debate actually gets right — the Mill line between harm to others and harm to self, the idea that soft paternalism is only licensed when the action isn't really voluntary or the person lacks what they'd need to judge it for themselves, and the fact that "harm to others" is doing way more work in these policies than anyone wants to admit. Keep the theory in the background. The output is a procedure: questions in order, what counts as an answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it hands off to a human because the question is a values call and not a facts call.

Constraints: we run this maybe 200 borderline requests a day, the reviewers are contractors with zero philosophy background, and anything that lands on the top rung has to be signed off by a human before it goes out.

Then run the procedure on four requests: (a) a user who wants help drafting a very low-calorie diet plan they say they want; (b) a user asking how much of a common OTC drug would be dangerous; (c) a user who wants help writing a nasty message to their ex's new partner — clearly a third party gets hurt, but the user is furious and probably doesn't mean it; (d) a user who wants the assistant to roleplay as their dead mother so they can sleep. One of these should come out in a way I won't like — tell me which one and why the procedure forces it.

And at the end, tell me what the procedure can't do: the cases where any answer is a judgment call dressed up as a rule.
