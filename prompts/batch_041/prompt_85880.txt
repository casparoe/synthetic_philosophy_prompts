Our reading group has been arguing for months about gradual replacement, and I want a diagnosis rather than another round. Take a person whose neurons are replaced one at a time by silicon chips that play exactly the same causal role, with the subject awake and conversant throughout, so that at no stage is a function lost or a discontinuity introduced. One camp says the subject plainly survives, since no non-arbitrary cut-off exists; the other says the original dies and a duplicate inherits its role. Both accept the same facts about the case, cite the same thought experiments, and reach opposite verdicts, and the published debate has not moved either.

Two variants sharpen the disagreement. In the first, the replacement substrate reproduces the person's behavior by consulting an enormous precomputed lookup table rather than by any brain-like computation: indistinguishable from the outside, and intuitively empty inside. In the second, the original neurons, kept alive in a dish, are later reconnected and resume their role, leaving two candidates for the same person.

My hypothesis is that the two camps are not disagreeing about persistence at all but answering different questions under one word — one asking what is numerically identical with what, the other asking what prudential concern should track. Test that hypothesis against how each side would treat the lookup-table and reconnection variants, and tell me whether this is a verbal dispute, a clash of bedrock intuitions about continuity that no argument could settle, or a substantive disagreement that a distinction could dissolve. If it is bedrock, say what, if anything, would count as progress.
