I write about ideas and public life, and for the past several years I have been reporting on protest movements, whistleblowers, and the criminal courts. I have been asked to contribute a short essay setting out where I actually stand on the ethics of breaking the law, and before I file it I want to know whether the position holds together. Here it is in the plainest form I can manage:

(1) I do not believe there is any general moral obligation to obey the law as such. Where I have reason to comply, the reason is an ordinary moral one that happens to point the same way — that I would be harming someone, breaking a promise, or free-riding on the compliance of others — not that a statute commands it.

(2) I do believe that a law produced by a fair democratic process is entitled to a presumption in its favour, so that breaking it carries a heavier burden of justification than breaking a rule imposed by decree. Part of my reason is epistemic: a rule that has had to win over a great many people is some evidence that it is not a blunder.

(3) I believe civil disobedience — public, non-violent, aimed at changing the law, and undertaken in the knowledge that one will be punished for it — is sometimes not merely permitted but owed.

(4) I believe the same of leaks and whistleblowing, even though they are covert, and even though those who undertake them generally do everything they can to escape the penalty.

(5) I believe the permission to break the law is not the same for everyone. Someone whom the law excludes or wrongs — an undocumented worker, a prisoner, a family whose benefits have been cut — stands in a different position from someone like me, who breaks the same law out of conviction with nothing at stake.

(6) I believe a democratic state is within its rights to prosecute and punish lawbreakers even when I think they were right, and that jurors who refuse to convict them are also doing the right thing.

Nearly everyone I have shown this to says the trouble lies in (1) and (2), and I can see the shape of that objection. I suspect the real fault lines are elsewhere. What I want is an audit rather than an essay: which of these pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be; how severe each tension is, ranked; what the cheapest repair would be in each case and what it would cost; and which single commitment, if I surrendered it, would clear up the most.
