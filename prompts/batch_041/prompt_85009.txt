I am in my final year at secondary school and am writing an extended project on the ethics of belief, which has taken me into the disagreement between W. K. Clifford and William James. I think I have the outlines of it: Clifford's claim that it is wrong, always and for everyone, to believe anything on insufficient evidence, illustrated by the shipowner who talks himself out of his doubts about an unseaworthy hull and loses his passengers; James's reply that the twin commands to know the truth and to shun error are two different commands which can pull in opposite directions, that where a question cannot be settled by evidence and the option is live, forced and momentous the passional nature may legitimately decide, and that there are cases in which believing something ahead of the evidence is part of what makes it true. What I cannot work out is where Yann Martel's *Life of Pi* sits within that argument. Pi practises Hinduism, Christianity and Islam at once; he gives the officials investigating the sinking two incompatible accounts of his time on the lifeboat, one in which the animals are real and one in which they stand in for human survivors; and when the officials admit that they prefer the version with the tiger, he replies that this is how it goes with God.

I would like a reading of the novel through the Clifford–James debate rather than a summary of either. Treat the two-story structure as a piece of philosophical apparatus and tell me whether it is a genuine instance of what James called a genuine option or a counterfeit of one: the officials cannot decide between the stories on the evidence, but does that make the choice live and momentous for them, or only for Pi? Does the claim that one story is better than the other confuse the value of holding a belief with the truth of what is believed — which is precisely the confusion Clifford's shipowner is guilty of? What would Clifford say about a man who keeps three faiths because they sustain him, and what would James say about the shipowner: is the difference between the two cases the presence of harm, the presence of inquiry, or something else altogether? And does Pi's multi-faith practice expose a limitation in the debate itself, in that both philosophers treat belief as a proposition held in the head, whereas the novel presents it as a practice — a set of observances, habits and attachments that one lives inside?

Please come down firmly on one side rather than surveying the options: tell me whether the novel is on balance a Jamesian book, a Cliffordian one, or a work that shows both philosophers have misdescribed the thing they are arguing about, and defend that verdict against the strongest objection you can raise to it. I have the novel to hand, so there is no need to quote from it or to recommend secondary reading; I would rather have your own argument, in British English.
