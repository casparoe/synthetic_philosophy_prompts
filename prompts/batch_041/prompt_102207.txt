I have been following debates about imprecise probabilities for a while, and I keep running into a disagreement that I would like you to adjudicate in essay form. The disagreement is not the familiar one about whether imprecise probabilities are useful in principle, but a narrower one: whether the formalism captures a genuine feature of epistemic situations or is best understood as a bookkeeping device for sensitivity analysis. Suppose we distinguish two ways a set of probability functions can arise. In the first, there is a determinate chance or credence, and the set represents our uncertainty about which precise value is correct—imprecision as uncertainty about a precise target. In the second, the situation itself is indeterminate, or at least our evidence does not single out any unique probability, and the set is meant to represent that there is no fact of the matter to be uncertain about—imprecision as genuine indeterminacy. A friend argues that this distinction collapses: any second-kind case can be redescribed as a first-kind case with a higher-level probability distribution, and any first-kind case can be redescribed as a second-kind case by treating the precise value as merely one member of a set. Another friend thinks the distinction is real and that imprecise probabilities earn their keep precisely by refusing to force a unique higher-level distribution.

The test case I find most puzzling is dilation: learning information that seems irrelevant to a hypothesis can nevertheless widen the range of probabilities assigned to it, at least under some imprecise-probability update rules. I want an essay that takes a stand on whether dilation is a feature or a bug, and more generally on whether the first-kind/second-kind distinction can be maintained. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, but do not end on a non-committal note; defend a verdict about what imprecise probabilities are actually representing. I am not asking for a literature review or citations—I care about the conceptual argument and the conclusion.
