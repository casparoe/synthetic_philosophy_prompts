Exam question: Consider a two-person housing cooperative that must choose one of four social states, each specifying the color of Alice's door and Bob's door. The states are: S1 = (Alice green, Bob green), S2 = (Alice blue, Bob green), S3 = (Alice green, Bob blue), S4 = (Alice blue, Bob blue). The cooperative charter includes two liberal rights: (R1) Alice is decisive over the pair of states that differ only in the color of her own door; (R2) Bob is decisive over the pair of states that differ only in the color of his own door. The charter also includes the weak Pareto condition: if every member strictly prefers state X to state Y, then the cooperative's social preference ranks X above Y. The members' preferences are: Alice: S1 > S2 > S3 > S4. Bob: S2 > S3 > S1 > S4. Does this setup instantiate Sen's liberal paradox? If so, identify the cycle and explain which condition generates each step. Then discuss whether the paradox can be dissolved by distinguishing between rights as side constraints and rights as goal rights.

Student's answer: Yes, this is an instance of Sen's liberal paradox, which is essentially Arrow's impossibility theorem applied to individual rights rather than voting. The cycle is S1 > S2 > S3 > S1. The first step, S1 > S2, comes from Alice's right: S1 and S2 differ only in Alice's door, and Alice prefers S1 to S2, so her preference is decisive. The third step, S3 > S1, comes from Bob's right: S3 and S1 differ only in Bob's door, and Bob prefers S3 to S1, so his preference is decisive. The second step, S2 > S3, comes from the weak Pareto condition: both Alice and Bob prefer S2 to S3. Now, notice that the real conflict here is between Alice's right and Bob's right: Alice's right ranks S1 above S2, and Bob's right ranks S3 above S1. The Pareto step just fills in the gap. So the paradox is fundamentally a conflict between two liberal rights, not between liberalism and Pareto. In fact, if we dropped the Pareto condition, we would still have a tension, though not a full cycle. The deeper lesson is that when people have meddlesome preferences—preferences about each other's private choices—liberal rights can conflict. If we restricted preferences to be self-interested, the tension would disappear, because Alice would get her preferred door color and Bob his, and there would be no conflict. So Sen's theorem really shows that we should not count meddlesome preferences in social welfare.
