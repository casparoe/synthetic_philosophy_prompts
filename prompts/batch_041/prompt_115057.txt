I host a podcast — science-and-ideas stuff, not academic — and next month's guest is a quantitive historian who's spent a few years modelling how long states and empires last. His thing, which I find half bracing and half nuts, is that the lifespan of a polity is basically memoryless. You pick the widest reference class you can defend — all sovereign polities in the historical record, or all of them above some size/complexity threshold — estimate a single constant hazard rate off that class, and then refuse to update on age. So the fact that some empire has already lasted six hundred years tells you nothing about how much longer it has; it's radioactive decay, or a coin that's come up heads ten times. He's explicit that this is just the outside view taken seriously, and that everything we call decline — the fiscal crises, the elite overproduction, the invasions — is the inside view, a story told about a sample of one. He also says a reference class only counts as principled if it's the one picked out by a symmetry, i.e. the features you're indifferent about shouldn't move the hazard rate.

I want questions that make him actually work. Draft me eight to ten. I need at least one each on: (a) why I shouldn't just gerrymander the class until the frequency comes out how I like, and whether his symmetry talk is anything more than the principle of indifference wearing a lab coat; (b) the empirical side — what would have to be true in the data for his memoryless claim to be false, and whether the standard complaint that state lifespans look more like a power law (loads of short ones, a handful of very long ones) is a real problem for him or just a fact he can absorb; and (c) the upshot — if age carries no information, what is he telling a policymaker or a funder to do differently, and isn't that just defunding the study of history? Also one on the Lindy effect, since he must get that constantly, and I want to know if he thinks it's a real regularity or a selection artifact.

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. I've heard him talk enough to know his moves, and I don't want to be left flat-footed when he says that's the inside view again. Where he has a standard dodge, the follow-up should close it off rather than just restate the question louder. No generic stuff like what got him into history.
