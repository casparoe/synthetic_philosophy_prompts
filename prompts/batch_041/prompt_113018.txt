I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of language preparing an advanced seminar on the semantics and pragmatics of derogatory language. I want a dialogue that is driven by a small artificial dataset rather than by familiar examples, so that students must reason from the linguistic facts instead of recalling a literature. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of language, A and B, who defend opposing accounts of how a derogatory term gets its force.

The term is "grel," a noun used to refer to members of a fictional group, the Calderans; both the term and the group are invented. Assume the following about its use:

1. "Mara is a grel" is normally understood as derogatory toward Calderans.
2. "Mara is not a grel" denies that Mara is Calderan; many competent speakers report that the contempt survives the negation, though some report that it does not.
3. "If Mara is a grel, she will probably be unreliable" also seems to carry the contempt, even though the speaker is not asserting that Mara is a grel.
4. "The dean said that the grels are lazy" can report what the dean said and seems to convey that the dean used or endorsed a derogatory characterization.
5. In some Calderan communities, "grel" is used as a reclaimed term of solidarity, and in those uses it is not derogatory.
6. In linguistics papers, "the grels are a Calderan subgroup" can be used in a purely descriptive, non-derogatory way.
7. "The word 'grel' is a slur" is not itself derogatory toward Calderans.
8. Replacing "grel" with "Calderan" in "Mara is a grel" preserves the claim about group membership but removes the contempt.

A defends a semantic account: the derogatory content is part of the term's conventional meaning—perhaps a presupposition, a conventional implicature, or a component of its truth-conditional content. B defends a pragmatic account: the term's conventional meaning is neutral with respect to derogation (or at most includes the group predicate), and the contempt is generated by use, context, social convention, or speaker intent. Each should press the other on the data above. For example, A may argue that projection through negation and conditionals is evidence for semantic content; B may reply that pragmatic presuppositions and conventional acts also project, and that reclamation and descriptive uses show the meaning is not fixed by the word. A may then argue that B cannot explain why the term is derogatory even when the speaker does not intend to derogate; B may distinguish semantic meaning from social meaning, or argue that the relevant conventions are conventions of use rather than truth-conditional content.

Make both interlocutors equally sophisticated. Neither should be a strawman. They should respond to each other's actual formulations, revise their positions when pressed, and distinguish carefully between semantic content, presupposition, implicature, expressive meaning, and social practice. The dialogue should not end with a tidy resolution unless one is genuinely earned by the exchange. I am less interested in a verdict than in seeing exactly where each view has to spend theoretical resources to accommodate the embedding, reporting, reclamation, and descriptive data.
