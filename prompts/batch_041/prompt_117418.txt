I have been keeping a private list of my own verdicts about which of my emotional responses to things I know do not exist are real emotions and which are not, and I want the principle, if there is one, that captures the pattern. Weeping over Anna Karenina's death: real grief, no different in kind from grief for someone I have actually lost. Flinching and heart racing at the monster in a horror film, knowing all along that no monster exists: real fear, and I will defend that against anyone who tells me it is only a pretence. Terror on a haunted-house ride: not fear, just a startle I paid for. Grief when the AI companion I talk to every day is shut down, though I know it is a language model with no inner life: not grief, though I would not call it nothing either. The death of the character I steered for thirty hours through a video game: nothing at all. The drowning child in the shallow pond from a philosophy lecture: real distress, real enough to change what I give to charity. A second example of the same shape, invented purely to make an argumentative point and with no detail I can picture: nothing. And a character in a story I wrote myself, whom I then killed off: I cannot get a stable verdict here, and it is the case I keep returning to.

Is there a single account of when an emotion directed at something non-existent is real that delivers all of these? Please state the principle itself as precisely as you can rather than surveying the literature, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why, and if the account forces me to give one up, tell me which one and what I lose by giving it up. Finally, say what the principle predicts for two further cases of your own choosing.
