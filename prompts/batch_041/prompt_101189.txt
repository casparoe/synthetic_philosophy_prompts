I teach a second-year undergraduate ethics module, and I have one 90-minute session with about eighteen students in a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard. I want to run a staged activity that makes the group feel the force of the demandingness objection to consequentialism from the inside, rather than hearing it described. Please design it for me, in enough operational detail that I could run it next week without having thought it through further.

The target is not the familiar trolley-style dilemma and not the bare thought that consequentialism asks a lot. What I want them to collide with is the instability of any stopping point. Set up a situation with a small real stake (a token redeemable for something trivial at the end, or points toward a prize — nothing that requires money or makes anyone uncomfortable), in which the cost of each additional step of sacrifice is small and the benefit of each step is held constant. Then let the demand escalate one small increment at a time, with each participant privately recording the point at which they refuse to go further and the reason they give for stopping there. Collect the refusal points anonymously and put them on the board. The pedagogical payload is the moment when they see that the reasons offered for stopping at different points do not line up with one another, and that most of them cannot say what makes their own threshold the right one rather than merely the one they happen to have.

Build in a second phase, after that discomfort has landed, in which some of the tokens are needed for something the participant has already committed to and cares about — a role in the scenario that stands in for a personal project or relationship. This should let the room feel the difference between "the theory asks more than I want to give" and "the theory leaves no room for me to be the person with these commitments at all." The point where the two complaints pull apart should emerge from the second phase, not from anything I say.

Some constraints. The idea must live in the rules, the escalation procedure, and the stakes; there should be no framing lecture beforehand and no naming of the objection until the debrief. The stipulated exchange rate between tokens and the benefit should be explicitly fictional and obviously so, so that nobody can win by disputing a real-world number. Participants must not be able to read off my own view, and the procedure should not rank them morally or reward the ones who hold out longest. Nothing should depend on the room having read anything in advance.

Give me the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, a short facilitator script covering only what has to be said out loud, and the debrief. In the debrief, map specific moments in the activity onto the distinct things that get run together under this objection: the plain claim that the theory demands too much; the separate fairness complaint about how the burden of those demands is distributed across agents; and the integrity or alienation complaint that the theory leaves no room for the agent's own commitments — plus the standard replies (that the world is demanding rather than the theory, that a scalar or degree version of the view avoids the objection, that ordinary morality is also demanding in extreme cases, and that the objection may be about psychology rather than about truth). Say which moments in the activity correspond to which of these, and which ones the activity cannot stage.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the performative maximal giver, the first speaker anchoring everyone else, the student who tries to short-circuit the exercise by declaring the exchange rate unrealistic, the risk that the refusal round reads as a moral leaderboard — and how to handle each. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: in particular, that real demandingness is unbounded and ongoing rather than a single resolved choice, and that whether demandingness is a wrong-making feature of a theory at all is a question the activity will not settle and I will have to supply.

End your answer with one sentence beginning "Bottom line:" that states the single thing the activity has to get right for the objection to land as more than a slogan.
