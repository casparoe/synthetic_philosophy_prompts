My closest friend from university, Nadia, and I went through a bad year together in our late twenties: she was being treated for a serious illness, I had just moved abroad for work, and most of our friendship ran through long phone calls at strange hours. During one of those calls she asked me to give her my word on two things — that if her ex-partner, Tomas, ever tried to reach me, I would tell her about it before I told anyone else, and that I would never let myself become a quiet channel between the two of them. She was afraid, I think, of being watched from a distance by someone she had cut off, and she wanted an assurance rather than a favour; I gave her my word, and for the next two years I kept it without much difficulty. Tomas moved abroad, remarried, and fell out of both our lives; Nadia recovered, and in the seven years since she has mentioned him perhaps twice, always lightly. If I asked her tomorrow to release me from that undertaking, I am fairly sure she would laugh and tell me I was being ridiculous — and I am also fairly sure that the asking itself would cost her something, since it would put Tomas back in her head for a week and make her wonder what I was planning to do.

That is the practical question I would like help with. I have been asked to contribute a personal essay about that period of my life, and Tomas belongs in it; nothing I would write would harm him, embarrass Nadia, or reach either of them in any way that matters. So I want an actual verdict on whether I am still bound by what I said, and if I am, what I should do about it: keep to the letter of it, ask for a release I know she would grant and would rather not be asked for, or treat the whole thing as having quietly expired along with its point. Three friends have given me three answers — one says that a commitment with no point left is no commitment at all; one says that the fact that she would release me is itself the release, so I need not trouble her; and one says that I am confusing the reasons I had for promising with the promise, and that I should simply do as I said. What puzzles me most is my own reaction: I would have felt less bad about breaking my word back when it mattered and she would have been hurt by it than I do now, when nobody would be. That seems backwards, and I suspect it is the clue.

Please tell me what is structurally going on here rather than offering general encouragement about keeping my word — if there is a name in the philosophical literature for the thing I am circling, use it, and explain how the competing accounts of what a promise does would each handle my case, including which of my friends they side with. Commit to an answer, and then say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to be mistaken.
