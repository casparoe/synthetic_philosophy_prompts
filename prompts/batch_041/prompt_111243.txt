I'm in my third year of a medical ethics program, and my seminar has asked each of us to write down our settled position on advance directives so the rest of the group can try to pull it apart. Here is where I've landed, stated as flatly as I can, so that you can see how the pieces fit or fail to. (1) A competent adult's advance directive — take the standard case of a refusal of artificial nutrition and hydration should advanced dementia set in — ought to bind the people caring for that patient, even if, once demented, she now seems placid and contented and tugs at the tube. I take the earlier, competent self to have the authority to settle this, and I would want my own directive enforced against a later, contented version of me. (2) On personal identity I am a reductionist in Parfit's sense: there is no further fact about whether the demented woman is the same person as the one who signed, and what matters in survival is psychological connectedness and continuity, which comes in degrees and can be more or less interrupted. (3) I hold that a life can go worse for someone only through how it feels from the inside — through suffering, or through the frustration of desires the person actually has at the time — so nothing that befalls someone who no longer has the relevant desires can set her back. (4) I think autonomy is essentially the present capacity to reflect on and govern one's own life, and that overriding someone whose capacity is intact is straightforward paternalism and wrong. (5) The deepest reason a directive has force, I think, is that a life has a narrative shape and the person is entitled to author the whole of it, ending included; I don't want the last chapter of my life written by someone who can no longer read the book.

What I am after is an audit, not a lecture: tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to and in fact sit together fine, and, for each real conflict, what would have to give and at what price. Please rank the conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list, and if a single revision would resolve the most, tell me which one it is and what it costs me to make it. Two closing requests: say what would change your mind about your own verdict — which consideration, if I turned out to be right about it, would reorder your ranking — and flag the step in your reasoning that you would trust least. I have no internet access, so please argue this out from the commitments themselves rather than sending me off to read anything.
