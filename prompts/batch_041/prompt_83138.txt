I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and for about a year I have been the reluctant referee in a running fight between two friends in my reading group. One of them holds a view she calls quantifier variance: existence claims, on her account, are always made relative to a language or conceptual scheme, and there are many schemes whose existential quantifiers range over different domains — some with composite objects in them, some with only simples — with no scheme-independent fact about which scheme is correct. So the question whether composite objects exist has no answer until you say which language you are asking in, and the apparent dispute between someone who says composites exist and someone who denies it is an artefact of that. The other holds what he calls ontological realism: there is one privileged set of concepts, one structure that reality has, and existence questions have answers that are true or false independently of what language we happen to speak; when the two of them disagree about whether some composite thing exists, he says, they are disagreeing about the world and not about words. They have now spent eleven months on this, and each thinks the other's position is either trivially true or straightforwardly incoherent, depending on the day.

I would like you to write the joint statement they should have produced — the document they could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration — and I want it exact enough to be worth the eleven months. Please include a numbered section of agreed claims: what both accept about what existence claims do, about what is meant by saying that something exists, about what would count as one scheme being better than another, and about the status of their own disagreement. Then give a disagreements table, one row per disputed question, and for each row: a formulation of each side's position that its own side would accept as accurate, a verdict on whether that row is a genuine disagreement about the world or a merely verbal one, the crux underneath it, the argument or case that both sides agree would bear on the crux, and each side's prediction about how it would come out. The case they keep returning to is a heap of stones that a community, over several decades, rearranges stone by stone into a wall: she says the question whether the wall came into existence at some point, as opposed to the stones merely having been rearranged, is a question about which language you are using, while he says there is a fact of the matter about whether a new thing came to be. Use that case to make at least one crux concrete.

One exchange in particular is a crux and I want both formulations of it in the table. The deflationist's move is to say that a dispute over an existence claim is merely verbal just in case each side's claims can be translated into the other's language in a way that leaves the two sides agreeing about all the facts; the realist's reply is that this test is far too permissive, since one can always gerrymander a translation that makes the other side agree. Be explicit about which rows of the table turn out to be verbal, and if some do, say whether recognising that dissolves the dispute or merely relocates it — and if you think her position threatens to make even this collaboration's own question (whether their dispute is verbal) verbal as well, say so and say whether that regress is vicious. I do not need citations, a reading list, or references to who said what; I need the formulations themselves to be as exact as you can make them, and I would rather have a short table whose rows are genuinely precise than a long one that stays vague.
