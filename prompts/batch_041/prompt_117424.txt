I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and last week our reading group broke into an argument that I ended up being asked to referee, which I couldn't do without simply picking a side. The dispute was between Ines, a fourth-year in the department, and Rafael, a visiting student sitting in for the term. Ines has a lemma in her third chapter — an elementary but fiddly piece of algebra that she says she has derived three times and knows to be true. A draft has been circulating from a group in another department claiming the lemma is false and pointing at where the derivation is supposed to go wrong; the relevant passage is about two paragraphs long, and Ines has not read it. She said she isn't going to spend a weekend on it: she knows the lemma holds, so whatever is in that draft is a mistake, and she is entitled to treat it as a mistake without hunting down where. Rafael said this is the dogmatist's move, and that a rule licensing you to wave off unwelcome evidence on the strength of the very belief under attack is precisely how people end up defending something false for a decade. Ines replied that he has the order of explanation backwards. Knowledge is factive: if she knows the lemma, the objection is false; a rule requiring her to keep treating a false thing as a live threat would mean she never knew it in the first place, and then "knowledge" would name a state no one is ever in. Rafael said that this at most shows she cannot both know and be required to doubt — it does not show that she knows — and that she is the last person whose assessment of her own proof can settle that question. She said that if the objection arrived with an actual counterexample she would read it, but as it stands it is a promissory note, and she is not obliged to do other people's work for them. He said that was a different argument, and that if that had been her point all along, the factivity business was decoration.

He then added, going back on the offensive, that even if the draft only made it more probable that she had slipped somewhere — and slipping is a kind of error she has made before — she would have reason to lower her confidence, and nothing in the factivity argument touches that, since "I slipped here" can be probable even when the lemma is true. She said that is a florid way of saying she might be wrong, which is true of everyone about everything and cannot by itself be a reason to do anything; if the bare possibility of error defeated knowledge, nothing would ever be known. He said he had not been talking about bare possibility, and that the difference between them at that point was the whole question. (One detail that may matter: he offered to work through the passage himself, and she said that was fine, but that it would not change her view either way, since she had checked the derivation three times.)

I'd like you to adjudicate this move by move, in the order it happened: for each move, say whether it is right, wrong, or right only under a restricted reading, and give a short justification — including, where a move is ambiguous, which reading you are rating and why. Then say who is closer to the truth overall, and finish by naming what both of them are missing; I have a suspicion that one distinction would separate the defensible parts of both positions, and I want it stated and then actually put to work on the case rather than gestured at. I'm asking for a committed answer rather than a survey of positions: take a definite line on each move, defend it, and flag clearly wherever you think your verdict is a judgement call rather than something the arguments force.
