Okay, odd request, but I've been roped into bringing something creative to the last session of a reading group thats been going through Grice all term — the conversation stuff, where a huge amount of what gets communicated isn't in the sentence at all but in the fact that you chose to say that sentence, so the other person has to do a bit of arithmetic on why you'd bother saying it, and they can do the arithmetic wrong, and you can always take it back and claim you only meant the words. We spent a whole session on the idea that you can break the rules of conversation on purpose (don't say more than you need to, be relevant, don't say what you can't stand behind, be clear) and that the breaking is the message. Anyway, I've decided I want to bring in a poem, and I want it to be a pantoum — or if thats too fussy, any form where whole lines come back word-for-word and not one word of them can change as they return. Four or five quatrains.

Here's the thing I actually care about. The repeated lines have to be flat and boring and literally true every single time they come back — the kind of sentence that on its own carries nothing, like a remark about a boiler or a bus route — and the entire meaning of the poem has to live in what those sentences imply in each new place they land, never in what they state. Nothing may be announced. No line gets to explain the feeling, no title that gives it away, no epigraph. The reader has to figure out from what the speaker chooses to mention, and what they conspicuously leave out, that something is badly wrong (or that something is being asked for, or that somebody is being forgiven — your call), and I want the last stanza to come back around to the first line of the poem so the sentence is identical and the meaning is not. Set it on the phone: one person telling another about an apartment they've just gone to see — a parent, a partner, an old friend, whoever makes it sting most. Someone who's read Grice should be able to go through it stanza by stanza and tell me which rule is being broken in each and why the implication is worked out from context rather than carried by the words themselves, and someone who hasn't should just find it a good poem about two people who used to be closer. A few more constraints: don't use the words implicature, imply, inference, or any of the maxim names inside the poem; give me one stanza where the other person responds to what was implied rather than to what was said, since the whole thing only lands if they take it up; and somewhere let the speaker deny that they meant anything by it at all, because thats the bit that shows the meaning was never in the words to begin with.
