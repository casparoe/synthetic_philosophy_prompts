Halloran and Vane are two novelists whose working methods I have been thinking about for years, because they seem to be running opposite programmes on the same problem — what a writer does with the gaps in the historical record. Vane has said that her job is to fill what the archive drops, that a gap is an invitation rather than an instruction, and that when a ledger omits a life it is the novelist's task to restore one. Halloran has said the reverse: that the silences are the record, that to fill them is to launder what happened, and that the honest novel about an archive is mostly ellipsis. I am less interested in which of them is right in the abstract than in what each would actually do with a specific document, so let me set out four cases where I know how they ruled.

In the parish register from the workhouse that Halloran used for *The Ledger of Small Mercies*, a woman is entered as 'a woman, name not taken' beside the birth of a son. Halloran refused to invent her a name, and said in interview that the omission was the whole point of the entry — that the book was built to record her as a number, and that handing her a name would tidy away the very thing the document does.

In *Cinder Lane*, Vane's novel about a family of modest means in an English county town, a daughter is simply absent from the family's own papers after 1817; no death record, no marriage, no mention in a long series of letters that otherwise accounts for everyone. Vane invented her a whole later life — a move to a port, a trade, a child — and defended it on the grounds that the family had tidied her out of its own story and someone ought to put her back.

Halloran's third novel turns on a survey of a colonial district in which an entire settlement is marked as empty land. He kept the settlement off the page for two hundred pages, and then had a surveyor's assistant note, in a document that exists only inside the fiction, that he had been told to leave it off. Halloran has said that he wanted the reader to sit inside the erasure rather than be rescued from it.

Vane, in *The Salt Road*, gave a name and an interior life to a woman who appears in a shipping company's accounts only as a unit of cargo, listed by number, and said afterwards that she took the risk of being wrong about the woman's life rather than leave her as a number.

Now the new case, and it is mine rather than theirs. I have an advertisement from an estate office in a colony, dated 1761, in which an owner offers a reward for the return of a man who has run away. The man is described at length — stature, country marks, a missing finger, the clothes he took — and he is not named. The owner is named, his estate is named, his other transactions are fully documented elsewhere, and there is a long paper trail for everything he owned except the people he owned. The man is the only figure in the whole file who has no name, and the advertisement is the only trace of him I have.

If I build a chapter around this advertisement, what will Halloran do with it, and what will Vane do with it? What I want is not a verdict on the man himself but a prediction of each novelist's ruling on the compositional question — do they name him and give him a mind, or keep him as the advert keeps him — together with the rule each is implicitly running, the other rules that fit each track record just as well, and which of those candidate rules would be told apart by the next document, say a later estate inventory that lists the same man, still nameless, among the livestock.
