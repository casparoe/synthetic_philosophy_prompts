I got into a stupidly long argument about assisted dying at a dinner party a few months ago, and the weird thing was that the four of us agreed on almost every concrete case and still ended up with totally different principles, which I found more unsettling than the disagreement itself. So I've been writing down what I actually think, case by case, and now I want someone to tell me what I've committed myself to. Here's the list. Woman in her sixties, pancreatic cancer, spread everywhere, pain the hospice can't really touch, clear-headed, has asked three times with a month in between: fine to help her die, and honestly I'd call it the decent thing. Same woman, except the pain could be managed if she'd accept being sedated to the point of barely being conscious for the last few weeks, and she says she'd rather go: still fine by me. Same woman a third time, but now she's also severely depressed the way a lot of people are right after diagnosis, and she wants to go this week: not fine, and I'd want the depression treated first even though the cancer isn't going anywhere. Her brother has the same diagnosis and the same prognosis, but he's not in much pain and he's not depressed; what's eating him is that his wife has to help him in the bathroom and he can't stand being seen like that. He wants to go while he still has some dignity. I think that's not fine, and I notice the only thing separating him from case one is which part of the situation he's suffering about. Then jump to the other end: my great-aunt is 94, physically pretty healthy for her age, outlived her husband and both her close freinds, says she's done and doesn't want another five years of this. Nothing wrong with her head, no depression I can see, nobody pressuring her. I don't think anyone should help her die, and I'd be uneasy about a doctor who did. Also, for what it's worth, a 19-year-old who's had anorexia for years and says she's finished: not fine, obviously, though I couldn't tell you why that's more obvious than the great-aunt. But here's the one I keep going back and forth on: mid-thirties, complete quadriplegia from a diving accident, no pain, sharp as ever, two years of asking, and everyone around him thinks he has a genuinely good life available if he wants it, and he doesn't want it. Some days I think of course it's his life. Other days I think there's something off about the fact that we could probably make his life better and haven't really tried, and that if we'd built the world a bit differently he might not be asking at all.

So what I want is the principle behind this, if there is one. Not a slogan — actual machinery, with the parts labeled. What makes a request authoritative in the first place? What defeats it? I seem to treat depression as defeating a request but not grief, not exhaustion, not humiliation, and I can't say why. And is the irreversibility thing I keep leaning on doing any real work, or is it just a proxy for something else I haven't named? I suspect not all my verdicts can survive together, so tell me which ones I should give up and what a cleaned-up version of me would say instead. And then please run the surviving principle on a case I haven't listed, because I think it's the one that decides between the candidates: someone with early dementia who signed a request years ago while she was competent, and who now, at the stage where she still recognizes her family and seems happy enough, says she wants to live. I genuinely don't know what I think there.
