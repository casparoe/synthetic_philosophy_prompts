I am a high school student writing an independent-study essay on consciousness, and I have come to accept a version of the higher-order theory: a mental state is conscious just in case a suitable higher-order representation of it is present, and what makes the state conscious is not its first-order content but its being targeted by that higher-order representation. The version I am defending adds two claims: the higher-order representation must be non-inferential and may be dispositional rather than occurrent, and it need not be accurate—so a creature could be conscious of red even if its first-order state is of green, provided the higher-order state represents red. Please criticize this position. What would count against it? I want the strongest defeaters, ordered by how much damage they do, including cases the view cannot describe, empirical findings that would embarrass it, and arguments that it collapses into a rival account. Which of these would a committed higher-order theorist concede as genuine problems, and which would they dismiss? I am especially interested in whether the misrepresentation and dispositionality claims make the view unfalsifiable, and whether that is a serious problem. Finally, tell me what would change your mind—which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict—and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
