I'm preparing an episode of a small philosophy-of-religion podcast, and my guest defends Gödel's modal ontological argument — the version on which a being is God-like just in case it has every positive property, positivity is an undefined primitive governed by axioms (among them that positivity is itself positive in every world), and the conclusion is drawn in a modal logic at least as strong as S5. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the argument's machinery rather than on whether its conclusion is consoling, and attach to each a follow-up of a sentence or two, keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give. At least one question should raise the objection that the axioms are so strong that they collapse the modal distinctions, making every true proposition necessarily true, so that the modal apparatus is idle and the conclusion follows from the bare possibility premise; its follow-up should ask whether weakening or dropping the axiom that positivity is itself necessary repairs the argument without costing it the step on which the necessity of the conclusion depends, namely that God-likeness is the essence of any God-like being. At least one question should ask whether the axiom that God-likeness is positive comes to anything more than the possibility premise it is used to generate, with a follow-up on what independent reason there could be for counting a property positive. At least one question should ask whether the proof delivers the God of theism at all — uniqueness, personality, and the Kantian worry that the axiom that necessary existence is positive treats existence as a predicate — with a follow-up pressing whichever of these the guest concedes. Please skip anything biographical or of the what-first-drew-you-to-the-argument variety.
