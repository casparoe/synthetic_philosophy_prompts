I am a high school student enrolled in an interdisciplinary seminar on the European Enlightenment, and this term we are reading Hume's Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary. For our unit on "Of National Characters," an essay Hume first published in 1748 and then kept revising for the rest of his life, I have been assigned a close reading of a single paragraph, and I have hit a wall. Hume's general position seems clear enough to me: he says the vulgar carry national characters to extremes, but that sensible people still allow that each nation has its own set of manners, and he traces those manners to what he calls moral rather than physical causes. He defines the former in this way: "By moral causes, I mean all circumstances, which are fitted to work on the mind as motives or reasons, and which render a peculiar set of manners habitual to us." He then lists government, revolutions, plenty or penury, and a nation's situation with regard to its neighbours as examples, and remarks that since a nation is nothing but a collection of individuals, its character must depend on causes of this sort. He also grants that animals plainly differ by region — English bull-dogs and game-cocks, heavy Flemish horses, light Spanish ones — and that transplanted breeds lose the qualities their native climate gave them, yet he denies that anything comparable holds for human beings.

What I cannot make sense of is the paragraph in which he explains why, if climate does not do the work, whole peoples nevertheless come to resemble one another. Here it is: "The human mind is of a very imitative nature; nor is it possible for any set of men to converse often together, without acquiring a similitude of manners, and communicating to each other their vices as well as virtues. The propensity to company and society is strong in all rational creatures; and the same disposition, which gives us this propensity, makes us enter deeply into each other's sentiments, and causes like passions and inclinations to run, as it were, by contagion, through the whole club or knot of companions. Where a number of men are united into one political body, the occasions of their intercourse must be so frequent, for defence, commerce, and government, that, together with the same speech or language, they must acquire a resemblance in their manners, and have a common or national character, as well as a personal one, peculiar to each individual." My difficulty is that this looks like a different kind of explanation from the one he set out at the beginning. Imitation and contagion are not "motives or reasons" that a form of government or a level of prosperity could supply; they seem to operate whatever the government happens to be, so long as people live near one another and talk to one another. Is the definition of moral causes meant to be broad enough to swallow up contagion, and if so, what is left of the contrast with physical causes? A classmate in my study group suggested that Hume is not really explaining national character in this paragraph so much as explaining why people come to share manners at all, and that the two halves of the essay are doing different jobs; I cannot tell whether that rescues Hume or convicts him of changing the subject. I would also like to know whether this passage commits him to thinking that a national character is a genuine property of a people, or only a summary of how frequently certain qualities turn up among them, since he seems to say both things.

Could you help me understand what Hume is arguing in this passage and how it fits together with his earlier account of moral causes? Please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your reading, and identify the point in your interpretation that you are least sure of — I would rather know where the reading is shaky than have it smoothed over.
