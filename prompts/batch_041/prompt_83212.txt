Ok, I'm running the book club discussion on Pynchon's The Crying of Lot 49 and I said I'd do the philosophy part, so now I have to. Everyone wants to litigate whether Oedipa is paranoid, which is the boring question. I want the reading that takes the epistemology of conspiracy theories seriously, and I want you to treat two features of the book as real epistemic problems rather than decorations.

First: she never has a theory she's testing. She has a pile of stuff — the muted post horn graffiti, the watermarks on Inverarity's stamps, the line in the Jacobean play, the older censored edition of it, the W.A.S.T.E. bins — and the question is whether to posit an agent behind it at all. Ordinary conspiracy theorising runs the other way: there's an official story, someone offers a rival story, and the fight is over the evidence. She has no official story to beat. So the standard question — is this belief warranted? — may not even get a grip on her situation. Say what does.

Second: nothing she learns ever comes from a person. Every scrap of evidence is a document, a symbol, a text, or a stranger who won't explain himself. She never meets a conspirator. So is a conspiracy with no conspirators a coherent object of knowledge — a pattern that lives in the archive and nowhere else? I've been calling it the "archival conspiracy" and I genuinely don't know if that's a real category or a phrase I made up. Test it. And if the hidden-agents-coordinating-in-secret picture can't survive contact with this book, what replaces it? Is the real subject here not belief but interpretation — reading the world as a pattern, where the pattern is recognisable because it's narratively satisfying? That's the accusation people make against real conspiracy theorists, and the book seems to half-endorse it. Note also that the novel parks a real institution (the Thurn and Taxis postal monopoly) next to an invented one, so half of it is checkable — which is exactly the move real conspiracists make.

Then the honest part. Does it actually matter whether Tristero exists? I don't think the book thinks it does — the anonymous group of heartbroken people quietly using the mailboxes, plus the fact that she's executing the estate while the estate keeps handing her the evidence, so the search is circular and maybe a prank by a dead man. But I want the places where the reading breaks. Where does the novel cheat, if it does? If I'm teaching it next to real work on conspiracy theorising, where do I have to put up a sign saying "literary conceit, don't generalise"? And what does the novel give this debate that the arguements can't — or is it just a novelist having it both ways by never letting lot 49 be called?
