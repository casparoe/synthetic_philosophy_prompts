I sit on the grant panel of a foundation that has committed roughly £2.5 million over three years to a programme on epistemic dependence on AI systems and the alleged erosion of human understanding. The money is for conceptual and philosophical work — epistemology, philosophy of mind, social epistemology, ethics of technology — and not for building or benchmarking models. We expect to fund six to eight researchers, mostly at postdoctoral level, and we would rather fund a small number of questions pursued deeply than a broad survey of the field. I have drafted the candidate questions below and want your judgement on which deserve the money, in what order, and on what grounds.

1. Specification. What exactly is supposed to be eroded — a skill, a disposition, a body of propositional knowledge, a capacity to evaluate explanations, or a standing social arrangement? Does the erosion thesis survive being made precise, or does it fragment into several distinct claims, some of which are trivial and some of which are false?

2. The distinctiveness of machine deference. Ordinary reliance on testimony is usually thought to be unproblematic because the competence relied upon remains somewhere in the community and can in principle be interrogated. Is reliance on an AI system different in kind, or only in scale and speed? If it is different in kind, what is the structural feature doing the work — the absence of a locus of accountability, the opacity of the system, the fact that it cannot be asked to defend itself, or something else?

3. The verification regress. Checking an AI system's output typically requires either the very competence one was relying on it to supply, or a second system whose reliability one has the same grounds to doubt. Is there a general result here about when a relation of reliance can be self-stabilising rather than self-undermining?

4. The treadmill. As systems improve, the competence needed to check them may rise alongside them, with the result that erosion is systematically invisible from the inside. Is this a genuine dynamic with identifiable mechanics, or a rhetorical trope? What would distinguish the two?

5. Production versus uptake of explanations. AI systems generate explanations in great volume that no one has evaluated. If the relevant epistemic good is the uptake of an explanation rather than its production, does the distinction generate a distinctive harm, or is it a restatement of familiar worries about testimony?

6. The redistribution hypothesis. On this view nothing is eroded; understanding simply migrates from individuals to socio-technical systems, and the interesting questions are about accountability, liability and institutional design rather than cognition. Is this a genuine rival to the erosion thesis or a redescription of it?

7. Testability. Is the erosion thesis an empirical hypothesis, a conceptual claim, or an implicitly normative one in empirical dress? If it is conceptual, what would it take to convert it into something evidence could bear on, and is that conversion worth attempting?

8. Normative payoff. Suppose the erosion thesis is true. Does it matter? Is understanding valuable intrinsically or only instrumentally, and would the answer license anything — paternalism about tool use, duties of competence, restrictions on delegation — or nothing at all?

Please give me a defended agenda rather than a survey. Specifically:

- State the criteria you are applying — importance, tractability for philosophers working without machine-learning engineering, neglectedness, how many other questions in the area depend on the answer, and anything else you think should count — and say how you trade them off when they conflict.
- Rank the questions and make the dependency structure explicit: if one were settled tomorrow, which others would become easier, and which would simply dissolve?
- Where a question looks intractable as posed, reformulate it into something a small team could make real progress on within three years.
- For each question near the top of your ranking, say what developments over the next two years — conceptual, empirical, or in the technology itself — would move it up or down, and roughly what you would expect to see.
- Tell me which of my candidates are overrated, ill-formed, or duplicates of one another, and which questions I have omitted that belong near the top.
- Finally, assume for the sake of argument that the erosion thesis is false. Which questions would still be worth funding, and does that change your ranking?

If you could fund only two of these, which two, and what would you be betting on? I do not need a reading list, a bibliography, or a potted history of the field; assume I know it. Please write in British English.
