I'm teaching an upper-level value theory seminar (mostly 3rd-4th years, a couple of MA students) and I have to write the final, which is a single 90-minute essay question worth 20 marks. The topic is the transitivity of 'better than'. This came up because my friend Ada and I spent like two hours arguing about it after a party — she says it's definately just a precondition of the words making sense that if A is better than B and B is better than C then A is better than C, and I kept saying yeah but have you actually looked at the spectrum cases, and we never resolved it, so now I want to weaponize the whole thing as an exam question.

Here's the case I want the question built on, and I'd like you to keep it concrete rather than abstract. Imagine a dial running from 0 to 100, where each setting describes a whole world. As you turn the dial up, each of a fixed group of people gets a tiny bit more of one good (say income) and a tiny bit less of another (say free time), in increments small enough that no single click looks like a big deal. At 0 everyone has lots of free time and little income; at 100 everyone has lots of income and no free time. The stipulation is that each click from n to n+1 is, all things considered, an improvement — you'd rather live at n+1 than n — but almost nobody thinks the world at 100 is better than the world at 0; most say they're on a par, or even that 0 is better. So something has to give: either transitivity, or the step-by-step judgments, or the endpoint judgment. I don't want an answer that just recites that some philosophers think 'better than' is intransitive and calls it a day — the question has to force them to say precisely which thing they're giving up, and to notice that the options carry different costs. If you keep transitivity by denying that every step is an improvement, you owe an account of where the chain breaks and why that isn't an arbitrary cutoff; if you keep it by accepting that 100 is better than 0, you owe a defense of the repugnant-ish conclusion; if you reject transitivity, you owe an account of what 'better than' now means, whether it can still guide choice, and whether you're really just talking about vagueness or about incomparability/parity under a new name.

So: write me the exam question (a sentence or two of setup is fine, since they'll have seen the dial case in class), a model answer of about 700 words that a strong student could actually produce in 90 minutes, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific moves that should cap someone at a B, plus a one-line note on how students typically go wrong on each. Keep the level at "smart undergrad who's done the reading" — don't assume they know the fancy terminology, and don't let the question be passable by name-dropping.
