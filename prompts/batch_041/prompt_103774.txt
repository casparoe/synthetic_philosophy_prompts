I teach a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and I'm assembling a problem set on eliminative materialism — in particular on whether eliminativists and their realist critics are even disagreeing about the same thing. The stock illustrations both sides reach for have been worn smooth by overuse (the giant lookup table; the creature with a belief-shaped box in its head; the future neuroscience that quietly drops propositional attitudes), and my students reproduce them without having to think. So I want you to do something harder. Take the role of a philosopher of mind who knows this debate well and who is good at building cases, and design me a new thought experiment to a specification. Start by brainstorming several candidate systems and giving me a sentence each on why you rejected the ones you dropped; then give me the case itself, in one paragraph, at whatever level of biological or engineering detail is needed to make the mechanism credible. Tell me at the outset, in one line, what the case is for, so that I can judge whether it cheats: I want it as the centrepiece of a section arguing that eliminativism and realism about belief may not be rival answers to a single question, and therefore that a case which wins only by making the system's behaviour too thin to test anything is worthless to me.

The case has to meet all five of the following at once, and I mean all of them. (a) The system is realistic: an actual kind of organism, artifact, or institution, or a modest variation on one — no brains in vats, no homunculi, no stipulated new physics, no imagined technology. (b) Ordinary belief–desire prediction genuinely works on it: someone who ascribes beliefs and desires in the usual way, and revises those ascriptions in the usual way as evidence arrives, can anticipate its behaviour in novel situations of indefinitely many kinds — so it cannot be a lookup table, a lookup table with a very long menu, or anything whose behavioural repertoire is small enough to be written out as a finite list. (c) The internal states that actually do the causal work fail the marks realists treat as constitutive of belief: they carry no truth-evaluable content, they do not compose, and — the part I care most about — they are not redeployed the way a belief is; a state that, if it were the belief that p, would be drawn on in indefinitely many further inferences and actions is here drawn on in one, or in a closed family of them. (d) The case must not stipulate its own conclusion: nothing in the description may simply announce that the states lack content or that there are no beliefs, and the verdicts have to fall out of the mechanism you describe. (e) The case should sit one small step away from a perfectly ordinary case of believing, so that changing a single feature hands the realist the win by default. If you come to think two of these conditions cannot be met together, tell me which and why — but don't use that as a way of not building the case; give me the closest thing that satisfies the rest.

Once the scenario is on the table, do the bookkeeping. First, state the verdict each of the following delivers on your case, with the reasoning that gets it there: the eliminativist; a realist who holds that belief–desire psychology is a roughly correct empirical theory and that its success on novel cases is itself the evidence that the internal states have content; and an instrumentalist who thinks the question whether there are really beliefs is settled by whether the intentional description picks out a real pattern, and who therefore expects to land neither where the eliminativist lands nor where the realist lands. Second, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work — the one whose removal turns the case into an ordinary case of believing — and demonstrate the collapse by describing what the case looks like without it. Third, put on the hat of whichever position comes out worst and give me its strongest reply, the move that would show my case is a trick rather than an argument; I am especially interested in replies like the charge that the prediction is parasitic on the real beliefs of whoever built or trained the system, that the ascriptions are doing no predictive work at all, or that you have merely relabelled a representational system. Close by telling me honestly whether you actually satisfied (a) through (e) or had to fudge one, whether your case yields a genuinely new argument or only a vivid restatement of an old one, and where you are leaning on a particular philosopher's position — describe any such view in your own words, and don't cite anything, since I'll add the references myself and don't want papers cited at me.
