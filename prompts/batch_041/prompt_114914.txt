I've been reading about the Fischer controversy -- the scrap among West German historians in the 60s over whether imperial Germany deliberately started the First World War -- and I want to see the whole thing run through a bit of philosophy of causation rather than through the historiography, which I already know.

Here's the machine I want you to use. When somebody says X caused the war, that's never a bare causal claim, it's a contrastive one: X caused the war rather than [something else]. The contrast class is doing hidden work, and different choices of contrast class give you different answers to what looks like the same question. And when people pick out one thing as the cause, they aren't doing physics, they're doing selection, and the selection follows a couple of regularities: we reach for the abnormal deviation rather than the standing background condition (the match, not the oxygen, is the cause of the fire), and we reach for voluntary human action over non-agential processes even when the non-agential process was just as necessary. So there's a live question whether cause-talk here is explanatory -- picking out the fact that explains -- or attributive -- picking out the agent to blame. Keep those two jobs seperate, I think they get conflated constantly.

Now map it onto the episode. Facts so you don't have to guess at anything: Franz Ferdinand is assassinated at Sarajevo in June 1914; Vienna decides to crush Serbia and gets a blank cheque from Berlin; the ultimatum to Serbia is drafted to be unacceptable; Russia mobilises, Germany mobilises, the Schlieffen timetable drags the army through Belgium, Britain comes in, everyone's in. The war nobodys plan actually wanted, or so the older story went. Then in the early 60s Fischer, a Hamburg professor working from the archives, argues that Germany had been aiming at a war of expansion and used the crisis on purpose -- not a slide, a bid. Firestorm, partly because it made 1914 continuous with what came after rather than a break from it. The counter, which has come back strongly since, is the sleepwalking picture: alliance commitments, mobilisation timetables, misread signals, nobody in control, responsibility diffused all round. And then there's the treaty clause that pinned responsibility on Germany and its allies for the loss and damage of the war -- a liability document, not a work of history.

What I want:

1. Take the Fischer claim and spell out the contrast class it presupposes. Germany caused the war rather than ___. Do the same for the sleepwalking claim and for the treaty clause. Then tell me straight: are these people disagreeing about facts, or disagreeing about the contrast class? Be honest if a chunk of the fight is verbal.

2. Run the selection rules over the July Crisis. What gets picked as the cause, and do the abnormality rule and the voluntary-action rule actually converge here? I suspect the blank cheque and the Schlieffen timetable are exactly where they come apart -- the timetable is the most mechanical, least voluntary thing in the whole story, and it's also what turns a Balkan war into a world war. Say what that does to the Hart-and-Honore-style picture.

3. The nation question. Even granting that Germany-in-the-abstract did it, can a nation be the kind of thing that has intentions and bears responsibility? Lay out the two readings -- the responsibility is just the sum of what the individual decision-makers did, versus the state as a corporate agent with intentions none of the individuals held -- and say which reading the Fischer thesis actually needs. His own story is about elites and pressure groups and an establishment, which smells more like a distributed thing than a single agent.

4. Now run it backwards. What does the episode show about the theory? The debate has run 60 years without resolution and the positions track the politics of the 60s more than the archives -- does that support the view that causal selection is pragmatic and blame-driven, or does it just show that historians are sloppy? Is there a version of the theory that predicts this, and is that a virtue or just a way of making it unfalsifiable? And the anomaly I care about most: is there a case where the selection rules point at someone and our moral judgment says no, or the reverse?

Keep the mapping tight -- who's the agent, what's the contrast class, which rule is firing -- and where the theory has nothing to say, tell me that instead of forcing it. No reading lists or citations, I care about the argument.
