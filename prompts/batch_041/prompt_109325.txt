I have no academic training, but I have been thinking on my own about simultaneous discovery and have argued myself into a corner. I should be grateful if you would diagnose the argument below. Treat each numbered step as a claim I actually want to defend. Tell me which step I should give up, why it looked so plausible, and what giving it up costs me. I am especially interested in whether the answer differs between the calculus case and the natural selection case, and whether it matters that one pair ended in a priority dispute while the other was announced jointly.

(1) To deserve credit for a discovery, a person must have made a difference: if that person had not existed, the discovery would not have been made, or at least would not have been made when it was.
(2) Newton and Leibniz each arrived at the calculus independently, and Darwin and Wallace each arrived at natural selection independently. In each pair, if either one had not existed, the other would still have made the discovery, at roughly the same time.
(3) So in each pair, neither person made the relevant difference.
(4) So neither Newton nor Leibniz deserves credit for the calculus, and neither Darwin nor Wallace deserves credit for natural selection.
(5) But the same reasoning applies to any discovery for which there could have been an unactualised independent discoverer. Since for almost any discovery there could have been one, almost no one deserves credit for anything.
(6) That conclusion is absurd. Darwin and Wallace both deserve credit, and so do Newton and Leibniz. Therefore (1) is false.

I want to keep (6). I also want to keep the ordinary thought that if one person had not made a discovery, someone else might have. What I cannot see is how to reject (2) without losing that thought, or how to reject (5) without letting in a great deal of luck. I have come across the idea of overdetermination, as when two arrows hit the same target, and I suspect it is relevant, but I cannot make it do any work here.

Please tell me which step is the weak one. If I keep (1), what must I say about the two historical pairs? If I give up (1), what account of credit can still distinguish a discoverer from a talented bystander? And does the residue of the argument point to something genuinely mysterious about credit, or only to an ambiguity I have been sliding over? Please answer in British English.
