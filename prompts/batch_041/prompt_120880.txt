I've been covering the longtermism beat for a couple years and I've got two sources who've been circling each other for months over infinite ethics. One says that the moment you admit the far future could hold infinitely many good lives, expected value stops being able to rank anything — if there's any nonzero chance the future is infinite, then every policy has infinite expected value, so the tiny probability you thought you were optimizing gets swamped and the whole prioritize-the-long-term program quietly sinks. The other says thats a conversation-stopper dressed up as a proof: you can still rank infinite futures by something like how densely the good lives are packed, or by what happens as you take the limit of a growing finite horizon, or by noticing that anything I do only ever shifts a finite amount of value anyway — and the first guy is just confusing the math being weird with there being no fact of the matter.

I want the joint statement these two should have written instead of another year of going back and forth. What can they both sign about what infinity does and doesn't break for comparing policies? Where do they actually split — my guess is one crux is whether a choice of mine now can change the probabilty of an infinite outcome at all, and another is whether more good lives means anything when both futures are already infinite — and for each crux, a case or argument they'd both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Numbered agreed-claims first, then a table for the disagreements. And if part of their fight is really just about what they each mean by value, say which part.
