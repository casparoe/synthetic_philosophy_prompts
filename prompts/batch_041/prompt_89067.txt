You are an expert in the theory of statistical evidence and the philosophy of probability, with hands-on experience in insurance underwriting. I sit on the underwriting committee of a mid-sized life insurer, and we keep having the same argument under different names: when we set a rate for an applicant, which class of past policyholders should the number come from? A colleague calls this the reference class problem. Before answering, brainstorm the distinct rules our past decisions might be implementing, then work out what each rule says about the case in front of us. I want the argument, not citations or reading lists.

Our record so far:

- A 26-year-old man with no other risk factors: we rated him on our experience for men aged 18–34, not on our experience for all 18–34s, and not on our experience for rural drivers, even though he lives rurally.
- A long-haul trucker with a clean medical record: we rated him on our experience for long-haul truckers with clean records, a group of a few hundred lives.
- A 52-year-old woman with a well-managed but serious chronic illness: we rated her on our experience for 50–54-year-olds with that illness, a group of about two hundred lives.
- A 40-year-old man flagged with a lifestyle proxy that our medical adviser says is merely correlated with mortality and for which no mechanism is known: we rated him as standard, ignoring the marker, even though our books contain thousands of lives with it and they do die somewhat more often.

The case now before us: a 47-year-old applicant, otherwise standard, carries a rare inherited cardiac variant. Our medical adviser says carriers face roughly three times the standard mortality hazard. Our own books contain 14 carriers, one of whom has died. A reinsurer offers a large dataset on a different but closely related variant, showing a similar hazard ratio, and our committee has in the past blended thin in-house experience with outside data when the mechanism was understood. The applicant has asked us in writing for the reasoning behind whatever rate we set, so our decision has to be defensible on its face.

Think carefully about the candidate rules, including at least one that fits our whole record as well as the rule you favor does. Then give me a brief paragraph of reasoning followed by a JSON object with these fields:

- candidate_rules: an array of objects, each with name, statement (the rule in one sentence), predicted_verdict (what it implies for the new applicant), and why_it_fits_our_record.
- preferred_verdict: a concrete outcome — standard rate, or a specified multiple of standard, or a range — stated so the committee could adopt it.
- preferred_rule: the rule that best explains our record, in one sentence.
- confidence: a number between 0 and 1.
- distinguishing_cases: an array of objects, each with case (a hypothetical applicant described in a sentence or two) and how_the_rules_diverge (what each candidate rule predicts and why that case separates them).
- strongest_counterargument: the best case against your preferred verdict, stated fairly.
- underdetermination: if our precedents genuinely fail to settle the case, say so here and state what principle we would be committing to under each choice.
