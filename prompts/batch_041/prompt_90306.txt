Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on the "historical Jesus" question — trying to sort out which sayings and deeds in the gospels actually go back to the guy and which got added by the early church — and there's a pattern in how the arguments work that I can't find a name for.

The basic move is always: you've got a rule for sorting the material. Like 'if a story would have embarassed the early church, it's probably old, since why would they invent something that made them look bad.' Or 'if a saying sounds unlike both the Judaism of the time and the church's own concerns, it probably goes back to him.' Or the one that really gets me: 'if it coheres with the stuff you've already decided is authentic, count it as authentic too.'

Fine, sure. But heres what's bugging me. To use the first rule I have to know what the early church would have found embarrassing — and I know that from the same documents I'm trying to sort. To use the second I have to know what the Judaism of the time was like and what the church's concerns were, and those reconstructions lean on the same texts. So the rule needs the answer in order to do its work. And when two of the rules disagree about a passage — one says yes, one says no — nobody tells you how to weigh them. So in practice I watch people reach for whichever rule lands where they already wanted to land, and the rule turns into an announcement of the verdict rather than a way of arriving at one.

There's a bigger version of it too: if you keep only what's unlike its setting, you end up with a figure who doesn't belong to any setting at all, which strikes me as a reason to distrust the rules, not a discovery about him. And I've read people who run the second rule exactly backwards — more likely authentic the *more* it fits its Jewish setting — presented with the same air of obviousness.

So: does this have a name? My hunch is it's a version of something general in philosophy, something about needing the criterion to sort the cases and the cases to justify the criterion. But I don't know what that thing is called, and I'm not sure it's really it, because my version seems to have extras: the rules fight each other, and they were all sold to me as neutral. What are the nearest concepts, how does what I'm describing differ from each one, and which fits best? If nothing quite fits, name it yourself and tell me where the edges are — what's inside it and what's next to it but outside. Don't just hand me a label, I want the thing carved. (If the honest answer is 'this is just a fallacy,' say that, but my instinct is it isn't, since in small doses the move seems reasonable.)
