I'm a graduate student in history and philosophy of science, writing a seminar paper on instrumentalism about models, and I keep hitting a puzzle I can't get past. I want to work through one concrete episode as a test case: the rise and fall of the stable Phillips curve in mid-century macroeconomics -- its incorporation into the large-scale Keynesian macroeconometric models that the Council of Economic Advisers and the Federal Reserve leaned on in the 1960s, the stagflation of the 1970s that the models did not see coming, and the rational-expectations reconstruction that followed. The theory I want to read it through is the methodology of positive economics, the position usually labelled the "F-twist": the claim that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to its assessment, that only predictive accuracy matters, and that we may legitimately treat agents as behaving "as if" they satisfied assumptions we know to be false -- Friedman's expert billiard player, who sinks his shots as if he had solved the relevant equations, being the canonical illustration. Take the mapping seriously and don't just retell the textbook story, which I know: who the players were, what the model's options and predictions actually were, what counted as evidence, and where exactly the instrumentalist defence of the Phillips curve gets its grip. One feature I want you to weigh is that the curve's "unrealistic" assumption -- that workers and firms respond to nominal rather than real magnitudes, and that the trade-off is stable -- was not a convenient fiction tucked away in a derivation but precisely the feature that made the model a policy instrument, a menu the authorities believed they could order from. That seems to me to break the clean separation between predicting and assuming on which the F-twist depends, and I'd like you to say whether it does.

Then the reverse direction, which is the part I actually care about. Does the episode confirm the F-twist, refute it, or leave it untouched -- and here I'd like you to be careful about a distinction I suspect is doing all the real work: between idealization in a strict sense, an approximation with a well-defined limiting case, such as treating a gas as a collection of point particles, and a structurally false assumption, a missing variable, a wrong functional form, or a claimed invariance that does not hold. If the F-twist can license only the former, and stagflation is an instance of the latter, then the methodology is either trivial or false, and I want a verdict on which. I'd also like you to consider whether the natural-rate response is itself an application of the F-twist or a quiet abandonment of it: the predictions failed, the assumption was blamed, and the assumption's falsity turned out to be the very thing that mattered -- which sounds like realism winning the argument, except that the same episode has been used to defend the same essay. Finally, tell me what the F-twist simply cannot say about this episode: why it was reasonable for policymakers to steer the economy with a model whose assumptions many of them privately doubted, and what "predictive success" can even mean when the predictions are of the effects of the very policies the model recommends, so that the world's conformity to the model is partly manufactured by the model's own use. I want the disciplined mapping, the places where it strains, and a considered verdict on whether false assumptions are ever innocent.
