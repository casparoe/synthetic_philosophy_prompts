You are a philosopher of history and a historian of historiography, with particular expertise in the long-running debates about Eurocentrism, periodization, and the place of narrative in historical explanation. I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar and would like you to draft a staged dialogue for me. Please think carefully and work in two clearly separated stages.

Stage one — brainstorming. Before writing any dialogue, produce a few paragraphs of working notes for yourself. State the strongest version of each of the two positions sketched below, list the arguments, distinctions, and counterexamples each side has at its disposal, and flag the places where the two are most likely to talk past each other. In particular, watch for equivocation on terms like narrative, center, universal, and the same time. Do not begin the dialogue until these notes are complete.

Stage two — the dialogue. Write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words between the two figures described below.

The occasion is this. Our department has been asked to advise on a proposed digital platform, the Synoptic Atlas, intended for both research and teaching. The Atlas would allow a user to select any given month and see events from every inhabited region of the world displayed together, with no region treated as the default reference point. Two design questions are contested. First, the interface as currently specified sorts everything onto a single continuous timeline keyed to the Gregorian calendar, with local reckonings retained only as metadata attached to each entry. Second, the editors must decide whether the Atlas will support any synthesized narrative at all — a connective account linking the events on display — or whether it will present only un-narrated juxtaposition, leaving the user to draw whatever connections they can.

The two interlocutors are both senior historians, and both are firmly opposed to Eurocentrism in its conventional forms. They disagree about what the Atlas should be.

Dr. Ama Boateng defends the synoptic ambition. On her view, the past can indeed be told from everywhere at once, provided the telling is built out of connectivities — trade, translation, pilgrimage, migration, the movement of texts and techniques — rather than out of any single vantage point. She holds that to abandon the synoptic aim is to cede the universal to those who will claim it on their own terms, and that a genuinely polycentric account is not merely possible but obligatory.

Dr. Lev Sokolov argues that the synoptic ambition is either incoherent or self-defeating. On his view, every narrative is told from somewhere, and the god's-eye view is itself an inheritance rather than an escape; the demand for one account, however polycentric, still presupposes a single reader, a single present from which all pasts are surveyed, and a single narrative time. He does not think this is an accident of history. He recommends that the Atlas be an instrument for producing situated, partial histories rather than a container for a global one, and that the juxtapositions it generates be treated as provocations to further local inquiry.

As a concrete test case to keep the exchange from drifting into abstraction, give them the following. An event in the fictional port city of Marawa — a set of coordinated grain riots, say — is attested in four sources: a merchant's log, a court register, a monastic chronicle, and a traveler's memoir. Each dates it by a different reckoning, and the four reckonings do not align cleanly with one another or with the Gregorian year to which the Atlas would assign it. Ask the two interlocutors to work through this case together and to disagree about what follows from it.

A few constraints on the dialogue itself. Both figures must be equally competent: neither is a strawman, and each must be allowed the strongest form of their view. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth — each speaker should respond to the actual points the other has just made, rather than restating their own position or talking past the other. Let them press each other on the hard cases and concede where a concession is warranted. The exchange need not end in agreement, and it should not resolve too neatly; if it ends in a clarification of what exactly is at stake, that is enough. There is no need to cite literature, name scholars, or supply a reading list; I am interested in the argument, not the bibliography. Keep both speakers in character throughout, and use stage directions sparingly.
