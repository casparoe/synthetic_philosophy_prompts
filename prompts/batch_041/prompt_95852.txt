I'm a novelist — three books, the last one about a family in a drought. No philosophy training to speak of, but I read alot of it, and I dont like being talked down to.

Here's what I'm stuck on. I'm starting a book about a woman who hasn't done anything especially bad, except that she eats meat, takes two flights a year, and votes in a seat that's safe for the party she votes for. None of it changes anything: if she stopped tomorrow, the world would not notice. And yet I want the reader to feel she's implicated, and I can't do it with the tools I've got, because all my tools are for deeds. In my line of work a character's guilt has to be earned on the page — the reader has to be able to point at the thing she did. So I've been working with a distinction I made up, and I want you to tell me whether it holds up:

the deed — a discrete act, filmable, with but-for necessity in the plot: if she hadn't done it, the story goes somewhere else. My entire craft runs on these.
the standing — what she is by virtue of being one of the many, a member of the aggregate whose combined behaviour produces the harm. Nothing filmable here. No scene. Only atmosphere, or a self-justification she half believes.

The argument I keep running into is that if my act makes no difference to the outcome, I'm not responsible for the outcome. There's a pile of replies to that — that each participant is responsible for a share of the harm; that what you're accountable for is participating in a practice rather than causing an outcome; that there usually is a difference, because thresholds are fuzzy and somebody's contribution crosses them; that the right unit is the expected consequences of the practice rather than of the act. (That last one, incidentally, sounds like it turns my book into a different genre — a whodunit where the killer is somewhere in the crowd — which I'm not sure I want, but never mind.)

Put each of those replies into my two drawers, one at a time, as faithfully as you can, and tell me where you have to force it. Then the part I actually want: what doesn't fit in either drawer. Is there something the philosophers are saying that I have no way to say at all? And if so, is that because my vocabulary is missing something real, or because the claim is thinner than it sounds and only looks substantial because of how it's phrased? One complication you should factor in: I write close third person, so I can only give the reader what she can see of herself, and she is not a reliable narrator of her own case.
