I am a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on epistemic logic and paradoxes of knowledge, and I am grading the take-home midterm. The student whose answer follows is a first-year MA student who usually writes clearly and has a good grasp of the formal material, but I need to be fair about the details rather than give a global impression. The exam question was: Consider a propositional language containing a factive knowledge operator K and a possibility operator ◇. Let the knowability thesis be the claim that for every true proposition p, it is possible that p is known—formally, p → ◇Kp. (a) Reconstruct the Fitch-style argument that this thesis entails that every true proposition is actually known. Make explicit each principle about knowledge and possibility that the argument relies on. (b) A seminar handout distinguishes the knowability thesis from a weaker thesis: for every true proposition p, it is possible that someone or other, at some time or other, knows p, not necessarily the same person or time for different truths. Does the Fitch-style argument still show that this weaker thesis entails that every truth is known? Explain why or why not. (c) A classmate claims that the argument shows the knowability thesis is simply false, and that the only way to avoid the conclusion is to deny that knowledge is factive. Assess that claim.

The student's answer was: Fitch's argument starts with the knowability thesis: every truth can be known. Take the proposition p and not known p. If that proposition is true, then by the knowability thesis it is possible that someone knows p and not known p. Since knowledge distributes over conjunction, anyone who knows p and not known p knows p and also knows not known p. But knowledge is factive, so knowing not known p entails not known p. That contradicts the first conjunct, knowing p. So the proposition p and not known p cannot be true. Therefore every truth is known. This shows that the knowability thesis is false, because it entails omniscience, which is obviously false. The only escape is to give up the factivity of knowledge, since if knowledge did not entail truth, knowing not known p would not conflict with p. The weakened thesis does not help, because the Fitch argument only needs the possibility of some knower or other, and the proof never identifies the knower with a particular person or time. So the same contradiction arises. The argument is a reductio of any attempt to say that all truths are knowable; the real culprit is the factivity of knowledge.
