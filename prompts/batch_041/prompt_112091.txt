I've been trying to teach myself the free will stuff on my own and I think I finally cracked it with these Frankfurt cases. The setup as I understand it: a guy is going to do something bad anyway, but a scientist has wired up his brain so that if he starts to hesitate she'll step in and make him do it regardless. He never hesitates, so he does it entirely on his own, and everyone agrees he's responsible — even though, because of the machine, he couldn't have done otherwise. So the lesson is that being morally responsible doesn't require being able to do otherwise. Which seems to mean determinism can't threaten responsibility either, right? If I don't need an open future in order to be blamed for what I do, then it shouldn't matter whether my choices were fixed by my brain chemistry from birth. So hasn't this basically settled things for the compatibalists? Why do people still go on about alternative possibilities as if it's an open question? Every time I think it's done the arguements just keep going and I can't tell what's left to fight about. I did see someone online say that the guy could still have done otherwise because he could have at least started to waver before the scientist cut in, but that seems like a dodge to me — the point is the scientist would have stopped him either way, so in the end he definately couldn't have done otherwise. Am I missing something?
