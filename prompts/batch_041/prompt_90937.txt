I'm putting together a special section of a departmental journal devoted to satire that specialists can enjoy without wincing, and I'd like you to draft one piece. The target is the standard analysis of convention in analytic philosophy — the account on which a convention is a regularity in behaviour that solves a recurring coordination problem, that each party conforms to only because the others do, and that is arbitrary in the sense that some other regularity would have served just as well and been conformed to just as readily. That account, together with the questions it leaves open about how conventions and norms arise and change, is what the piece should be quietly about.

Write the text of a guidance handbook issued by a fictional public body — the Office for the Registration of Conventions — whose statutory task is to determine whether a proposed regularity of behaviour qualifies as a genuine convention and may therefore be registered, published in the gazette, and (within the limits of its powers) maintained. The voice is that of an underfunded, slightly defensive regulator: procedural, deadpan, fond of cross-references, forms, and the phrase 'for the avoidance of doubt.' Aim for about 700 words, laid out as numbered sections with headings. The piece must not name any philosopher or cite any book or article; the reader should be able to reconstruct the theory from the rules alone.

The comedy has to come from the machinery being taken seriously, so build the sections on the real joints of the account rather than on invented absurdities. The Office should refuse to hear arguments that one solution to a coordination problem is more natural or more fitting than another, and should require instead an affidavit that a competing regularity would have done equally well — applications concerning situations with only one feasible course of action are rejected as non-conventions. It should insist that a registered convention be sustained by conditional conformity, so that parties who would conform whether or not anyone else did are referred elsewhere as ritualists and are not counted toward the requisite expectations. It should conduct a common-knowledge audit of the regularity, the expectations, and the availability of alternatives, and should be candid that no audit in the Office's history has been certified beyond a small number of iterations. It should draw a firm jurisdictional line at regularities that sustain themselves, assigning anything held up by guilt, shame, or third-party punishment to a separate department, whose methods the handbook describes in terms that are neither admiring nor a commitment to any particular story about how such things come to exist. Include a section on amendment: the Office cannot create or abolish a convention by decision, it can only alter which options are salient, and it should admit how little it understands about why some registered conventions drift while others persist untouched for decades. A section on sanctions should observe, without visible embarrassment, that the Office takes no position on whether anyone ought to comply with a registered convention, that this question cannot be posed in the framework it administers, and that its only available penalty — withdrawal of registration — would alter nothing whatever.

Keep the irony flat throughout: no asides to the reader, no jokes about the Office being absurd, no character allowed to step out of the document's voice. A reader who knows the literature should be able to point to the specific claim behind each rule; a reader who does not should still find a plausible and faintly alarming bureaucracy.

After the handbook, add a short note of roughly 150 words on where the piece takes liberties. It should separate what is settled in the literature on convention from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it — for instance how demanding the common-knowledge requirement really is, whether a coordination-equilibrium account can capture anything normative at all, and whether conventions are best modelled as equilibria that get selected or as things that arise and mutate by other means — and it should say plainly where the satire leans on a contested reading rather than a standard one. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
