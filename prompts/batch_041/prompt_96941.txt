I am a high school student preparing a paper for a history seminar, and I have been reading a popular psychology book about the ways our minds distort the past. Its chapter on historical explanation summarizes the relevant research roughly as follows.

'Hindsight bias, the familiar knew-it-all-along effect, is the finding that once we learn how something turned out, we cannot help but see that outcome as inevitable, and we quietly revise our memory of what we previously expected so that it matches what happened. This is the same phenomenon as the narrative fallacy: the mind demands a story with causes and motives, so it invents one, and then mistakes the story's coherence for proof that events had to unfold as they did. Because a well-told narrative always fits the facts it was built from, historical explanations can never really be tested, and the forecasting studies confirm as much, since experts do no better than chance at predicting how events will go. The most sophisticated historians have therefore abandoned the search for causes and content themselves with describing what happened.'

I would like to know how much of this I can rely on. Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a psychologist or a philosopher of history would accept, which are oversimplifications or conflations of two different ideas, and which are simply mistaken. Where a defender of the research would object to the book's wording, I would like to know what they would say instead. Finally, please tell me whether the book's conclusion — that historians should stop looking for causes — actually follows from the findings it cites, or whether it is doing something else. I am inclined to believe the passage, since it matches my own impression that history classes make everything sound obvious in retrospect, but I do not want to build a paper on a summary that my teacher would mark up.
