I'm a retired atmospheric chemist -- 34 years of measuring trace gases and arguing with climate modelers -- and since I hung up the lab coat I've been co-hosting a small philosophy podcast with a former postdoc. Next week's guest is a philosopher of physics who defends a view she calls the closure objection to Maxwell's demon. I've read her draft chapter and I want to press her, not just let her rehearse the usual history. Her line is roughly this: the standard resolutions all treat the demon as a bench demon -- a little agent that sits outside a small gas, measures molecules, and is allowed an external memory or an external entropy dump. She says the real demon is a wild demon: it has to be a physical subsystem of the same closed universe it's trying to sort, with no external ledger, and its own microscopic degrees of freedom get entangled with the gas. On her view, Landauer erasure costs and measurement-disturbance arguments are red herrings, or at best they only show that a bench demon needs a power cord; the wild demon is ruled out by a structural closure condition, something like: any record of the gas that the demon keeps is itself a part of the gas's phase-space trajectory, so the sorting is undone by the demon's own thermalization. She also thinks the reversible-computing escape hatch -- make the demon's operations logically reversible so no erasure is needed -- just relocates the bookkeeping into the environment, and the environment is the gas. And she claims a demon that never erases, say one that writes to a fresh tape and dumps the tape into a black hole, still can't win, which is the part I find hardest to swallow.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me, with a follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give for each? I want at least one question that makes her say precisely why the erasure/measurement accounting is a red herring rather than a partial answer; one that distinguishes her closure objection from the older objection that a demon would just be exploiting a rare fluctuation (I can never keep those straight); one on whether her view makes the second law merely statistical, or something stronger, and what hangs on that; and one on what would count against the closure objection -- what observation, thought experiment, or formal result would make her abandon it. Also, if she starts leaning on the idea that the demon's memory is just another thermal system, I'd like a follow-up that asks whether that doesn't simply re-describe the problem instead of solving it. Keep the questions concrete enough that a listener who knows the basic demon setup can follow, but not so basic that she's just restating the view.
