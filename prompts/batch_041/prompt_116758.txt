A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether the axiom of choice is obvious, and we got nowhere, so I would rather put the question in front of students than keep rehashing it. She holds that a choice function is nothing over and above the mundane act of taking one thing out of each box, so there is nothing there to deny; I hold that whatever licenses a selection from a family like the equivalence classes of binary sequences under eventual agreement is not an act of taking at all, since there is no saying which element has been taken and no two people can agree on a taking.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics: fourteen students, one seventy-five-minute session, no computers, only paper, cards, and a whiteboard. They have seen naive set theory and a little cardinality, nothing more. Design an activity that makes them feel the force of the axiom before they hear its name. What I want them to come away with is a double feeling: that of course you can choose one element from each of a family of sets, and that nobody can actually do it or share it.

My rough idea, which I have not managed to make work, is a game in which teams must select an element from every set in a family, with the twist that the teams must make the same selections as one another without communicating, so that nothing short of a rule will do. It should include rounds they can win, where a rule is available, and at least one round in which no rule exists and the facilitator can defeat every rule the students write down by a construction the students can check for themselves. The part I cannot solve is how to present the sets so that the presentation does not smuggle in a canonical element: if the card names a member, the players simply name it back and the game collapses. Solve that, or tell me it cannot be solved and design around it.

Give me the rules, the materials, the scoring, a timeline for the seventy-five minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the axiom, onto the difference between a finite family and an arbitrary one, and onto why some of us find the axiom indispensable while others find it a license for monsters. I would like the debrief to reach the point that a selection from those equivalence classes is not merely undescribed but provably not measurable, so that the students see the obstacle is not a shortage of cleverness. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
