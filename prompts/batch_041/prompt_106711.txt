Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about whether two different neural networks can implement the same mechanism, and we ended exactly where we started. My friend holds the intuitive view: if two models have different weights, different architectures, or were trained on different data, then they implement different mechanisms, full stop, even if they compute the same function on every input we try. I hold the view that sameness of mechanism is not settled by weights or even by input-output behavior; it is settled by a chosen class of interventions, so that two systems count as the same mechanism when they respond equivalently to every intervention in that class. The argument got especially tangled when we tried to connect it to mechanistic anomaly detection, the project of flagging when a model's internal causal structure has changed in a way that matters, for example when a backdoored model uses a different internal route to the same output. If mechanism identity is relative to an intervention class, then what is an anomaly relative to? And if it is not relative, then what fixes the class of interventions that defines the mechanism? I want to interview someone about this on a small podcast I co-host.

The guest is an unnamed researcher, but treat her as a real defender of a specific position: she holds that mechanism identity is intervention-relative, that there is no fact of the matter about whether two systems implement the same mechanism apart from a specified intervention class and a specified granularity, but that once those are fixed, sameness is an objective equivalence relation. She also thinks this view is compatible with mechanistic anomaly detection, because anomaly detection can be made objective relative to a reference intervention class. She is not a skeptic about mechanistic explanation; she thinks the relativity is a feature, not a bug. She is, however, vulnerable to the charge that her view makes anomaly detection circular, because the intervention class that defines the mechanism may be the very thing that the anomaly is supposed to reveal. Please act as an expert interviewer in philosophy of science and mechanistic interpretability. Before drafting, think carefully about the dialectic and brainstorm the strongest objections to her view: the trivialization objection that any two systems are equivalent under some intervention class; the gerrymandering objection that the choice of intervention class is unconstrained; the multiple-realizability and degeneracy objections; the objection that anomaly detection needs a mechanism identity that is not chosen after seeing the anomaly; and the objection that her view cannot distinguish a model that has learned a new mechanism from a model that has merely learned a different implementation of the same mechanism. Then draft eight to ten interview questions for the episode.

The questions should be self-contained and should not ask her for papers, citations, reading lists, or names of researchers; if a technical idea is needed, state it in the question itself. They should go beyond restating her view. I want the questions to form an arc. At least one question should press on what fixes the identity of a mechanism across two systems with different weights or architectures, and should include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. At least one question should press on how an intervention class is selected without circularity, and should include a follow-up that anticipates her reply that the class is fixed by explanatory interests. At least one question should press on what mechanistic anomaly detection is detecting if mechanism identity is relative, and should include a follow-up that anticipates her saying it detects divergence relative to a reference class. At least one question should press on degenerate or redundant implementations, where the same input-output function is computed by two very different internal causal structures, and should include a follow-up that anticipates her saying the structures are equivalent under the relevant interventions. At least one question should press on whether her view collapses into behavioral equivalence, and should include a follow-up that anticipates her distinguishing intervention-based equivalence from input-output equivalence. Finally, include one closing question that asks what would count as evidence against her own view, and one follow-up that asks whether any actual experimental result in interpretability could play that role. For each question, add a brief note on what she is likely to say and the follow-up you would ask next. Keep the tone collegial but unrelenting.
