Our department's philosophy of mind colloquium is hosting a visiting philosopher whose work defends a strong reading of the commissurotomy literature. Her claim is not the modest one that the disconnected hemispheres process information separately; it is that at least some patients who have undergone section of the forebrain commissures simultaneously sustain two streams of experience, and that the ordinary impression of a single subject is in their case a reconstruction rather than a datum. I am moderating the Q&A after her talk, and I want questions that treat the position as a live empirical and conceptual claim rather than as a curiosity.

Please draft eight to ten questions, ordered so that the pressure builds rather than plateaus, each followed by the single follow-up that her most likely answer invites. Some of the items below will generate more than one question; work them into the sequence in whatever order makes for the best dialectic.

- The methodological objection that the apparent disunity may be an artifact of the testing situation: residual interhemispheric pathways, peripheral cross-cuing through posture, gaze, or inadvertent experimenter signals, and the thin response repertoire available on the non-speaking side. Press her on what pattern of results would have counted as evidence against her reading rather than merely being consistent with it.

- The inference from two streams of information-processing to two subjects of experience. Ask what she takes to turn a stream of experience into a subject, and whether she will accept the corollary that the two hemispheres are two persons, with whatever moral and legal standing that brings, or whether she needs a thinner notion of subject than the one that does the work in personal identity.

- Report and confabulation. Most of what is known about the disconnected right hemisphere comes from left-hand responses and from the speaking hemisphere's commentary on actions it did not initiate. Ask how her view survives the possibility that the speaking hemisphere is a confabulator, and how she distinguishes disunity at the level of access and report from disunity at the level of phenomenal experience.

- Whether unity comes in degrees. If the hemispheres are only partially unified, with some contents shared and others not, ask why the normal case should not be described the same way, and whether her criterion, applied to a partial commissurotomy in which only some modalities are disconnected, still predicts two subjects; if it does not, ask what work the disconnection is actually doing.

- The social or forensic dimension. Ask whether the manifest unity of these patients is partly constituted by our practice of addressing them, and being answered, as one person, and whether that practice does any metaphysical work or is merely a convenience we extend to a subject that was already there.

- Finally, ask what she would regard as decisive evidence against her reading, and whether any conceivable outcome of a commissurotomy study could play that role.

Please keep the questions free of throat-clearing and pleasantries, and do not cite or recommend literature. I know the literature, and a reading list is not a question.
