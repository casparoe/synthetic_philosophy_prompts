I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and for the first meeting I want a "which position do you actually hold about belief and desire?" quiz — an instrument that sorts participants by what they are actually committed to rather than by the labels they would apply to themselves. Most of my students will have read something about eliminativism and will happily say they are "not eliminativists" while answering the cases in ways that commit them to it, so the instrument has to sort by answers to cases, not by self-description. Please write it.

Specifications:

- Ten questions. Each is a concrete case or a conditional prediction, with two or three answer options. No question may ask whether beliefs exist, or ask the respondent to pick a school of thought. Every question must be answerable by someone who has read nothing on the topic, and the options must be phrased so that choosing one does not announce which position it belongs to.

- The instrument should end in eight to ten outcomes. At minimum, these positions must be reachable:
  (a) descriptive eliminativism — belief and desire do not exist and a mature science of the mind will not posit them;
  (b) revisionary eliminativism — whether or not such states exist, we should stop ascribing them. This must be separable from (a), since it is a claim about what we should say rather than about what there is;
  (c) partial eliminativism — for instance, beliefs go but desires stay, or the personal-level attitudes go while subpersonal representational states stay;
  (d) instrumentalism or fictionalism — belief-talk is a useful fiction that should be retained;
  (e) interpretivism, or stance-relative realism — beliefs exist relative to an ascriptive stance, and ascription is partly constitutive of what is ascribed;
  (f) realist retention on functionalist lines — folk psychology is roughly correct, and beliefs are whatever occupies the relevant causal role;
  (g) refinement — "belief" survives the way "gene" survived, its content revised rather than abandoned;
  (h) the normative-status view — "belief" names an epistemic status or commitment rather than a natural kind, so the empirical case against it misses the target;
  (i) content-revisionism — keep beliefs, drop propositions, so that a belief is map-like rather than sentence-like;
  (j) the meta-level view that the debate turns on a semantic question — whether "belief" is a natural-kind term whose reference is fixed by whatever folk users were tracking — so that the empirical findings underdetermine the answer.
  Add any further outcome the logical space requires, and use "unnamed" for a position the questions reach that nobody has defended.

- The questions must genuinely separate (a) from (b), (a) from (d), (e) from both (a) and (f), (f) from (g), and (h) from everything else. If ten questions cannot separate some pair on that list, say which pair and why, rather than papering over it.

- The cases must include, at least: a small child or non-human animal whose behaviour is best predicted by ascribing a belief; a person who sincerely avows one thing while a contrary implicit disposition predicts their behaviour better; a patient who gives a sincere but demonstrably wrong reason for an action caused by something else; a system that produces fluent, self-consistent reports about its own beliefs; and a hypothetical future science that predicts all behaviour from subpersonal mechanisms without any belief-talk. Include at least one case that could push a respondent to treat belief and desire differently, and make clear which outcome that lands them in.

- Scoring: give a rules-based key rather than an exhaustive table, and then list every answer pattern that reaches no outcome, saying of each whether the combination is incoherent, merely unoccupied, or a position I should have included. Flag any outcome that the ten questions make nearly unreachable, and any pair of outcomes the instrument cannot separate.

- Give each outcome one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

- Keep the whole thing self-contained: no citations, no reading list, and no reliance on the respondent recognizing proper names. Where a position has a standard name, use it, but state every commitment in plain language so that a working scientist who uses belief-talk daily without having thought about its metaphysics can take the quiz and understand every result.
