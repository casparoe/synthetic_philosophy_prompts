A colleague of mine in the department — a political philosopher whose commitments I have never quite been able to pin down — has spent the term leading our reading group through a sequence of cases about freedom, and I have been keeping a record of her verdicts. So far:

1. A prisoner confined to a cell, physically restrained and moved about at the guards' discretion: not free.
2. A person who lives as she chooses, subject to no one's interference and no one's authority: free.
3. A woman whose partner tracks her movements, threatens her when she dissents, and vetoes her decisions: not free.

The case she has set for next week is this:

4. A man whom no one coerces, no one dominates, and no one even observes; he is left entirely to himself. He is nonetheless in the grip of a compulsion he disowns and cannot master — it governs his days, and he would give almost anything to be free of it.

Predict her verdict on case 4. Set out the conceptions of liberty with which her record so far is consistent, and say what each of them predicts here. Then give me your best guess, with your reasons for preferring it; and if the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, tell me which further case would force the issue, and how she would have to come down.
