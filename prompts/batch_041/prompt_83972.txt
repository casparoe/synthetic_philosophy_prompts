Before we open any of the candidate theories, I want the specification. The target is what I shall call threshold lexical negative utilitarianism, fixed as follows: there is a severity level on the scale of suffering — the threshold — such that any suffering at or above it is to be averted in preference to any quantity of pleasure, however large, while suffering below it is commensurable with pleasure and may be traded against it. The theory must fix the threshold itself; it may not be stipulated for it.

Give me the desiderata such a theory has to meet: the cases it must get right, the puzzles it must dissolve, the distinctions it may not collapse and the findings it may not contradict — with the consideration behind each, and a verdict on which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp. The cases I want on the list, at minimum, are these. The Drip: an episode of suffering is lowered by a hair from just above the threshold to just below it, while unbounded pleasure is available as a counterweight. The Ratchet: everyone in a large population gains one more minute of mild discomfort, all of it well below the threshold, so that no episode ever crosses it. The Two Scales: a single episode scores high on the sensory intensity of pain and low on its unpleasantness, the two dimensions that pain measurement standardly separates. The Partition: the same total quantity of suffering, delivered as one continuous episode or as many brief ones, and at steady moderate intensity or as intermittent severe spikes of equal area under the curve. The Hollow Trade: one person accepts a small pain now for a large pleasure later and reports no regret, while the impartial planner must still rank the two lives. The Thin End: a chance of one in a billion of a lifetime of agony can be reduced at the cost of a certain small loss of pleasure for everyone. The Empty Option: the theory must say whether it implies that a world containing no sufferers is better than any world containing one, even when the first contains nothing else.

So far as it can, the theory should treat the value of an outcome as fixed by the anonymous multiset of experiences it contains, and its ordering as transitive, complete, impartial and weakly Paretian, with no discounting for time or for personal identity; say plainly where these have to be surrendered. What I most want from you is the part of the list that cannot be jointly satisfied — the pairs or triples of requirements that pull against one another, and which member of each such set a defender of the view would have to give up. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the standard contenders, and close with a sentence each on how total utilitarianism, classical negative utilitarianism, a critical-level prioritarianism and an incommensurability or parity view fare against the list.
