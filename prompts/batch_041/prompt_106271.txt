For the framing note of my graduate seminar on explanation in history, I want to test a thesis before I commit it to the syllabus. I have come to think that the covering-law (deductive-nomological) model and narrative explanation are not genuine rivals: every narrative explanation is, at bottom, a covering-law explanation whose general laws have been left tacit, and the narrativist objection—that history explains by emplotment rather than by subsumption—is therefore a claim about rhetorical presentation rather than about the logic of explanation. A colleague has proposed what she calls the explanatory-debt view, on which a narrative explains an event exactly to the extent that it traces a causal chain, and the narrative form is merely a concession to finite memories; I find this persuasive. So my question is whether the fact that historians almost never state laws should be read as vindicating the covering-law model as the correct underlying account, leaving narrativism as a stylistic preference. I suspect I am conflating the demand that an explanation be reconstructable with laws and the demand that it actually cite them, but I want the diagnosis.
