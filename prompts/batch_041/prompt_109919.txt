Our reading group has spent the better part of a year circling a single disagreement, and I want a diagnosis of it rather than another round of it. The case is a grant panel of five that funds a proposal only if all three of its criteria — feasibility, rigor, ethics — are satisfied. Four of the five approve each criterion, only two approve funding, and every panelist who voted against funding had voted against one of the criteria. So each panelist's own judgments are consistent, the majority's are not, and the panel's answer depends on whether you poll the criteria or the conclusion. I am told this is the discursive dilemma, and everyone in the room grants the example.

What keeps the argument alive is what the example shows. One position: it is an instance of a general impossibility — fix a few conditions that look like the bare minimum for any defensible aggregative procedure and no procedure can guarantee a consistent collective outcome, so something must be abandoned and every abandonment costs something real. A second: the condition doing the damage is the one forbidding a procedure from treating logically related propositions differently, and that condition is a formal convenience rather than a requirement of fairness, so dropping it loses nothing. A third: a group need not be consistent, because consistency constrains individual reasoners; a group's output is whatever its procedure yields, and talk of what the group believes is a manner of speaking that has been allowed to harden into a demand. A fourth: the whole apparatus misdescribes its subject, since real bodies have no single well-defined judgment set, and the dilemma is the old voting paradox in new clothes — a claim the formal people in the room reject, after which the exchange returns to where it started.

My own suspicion is that the parties are not disagreeing about the theorem, which they all accept, but about what a group's judgment is: a summary of the members' judgments, in which case consistency is inherited and must be preserved, or a decision-theoretic construct, in which case consistency is one virtue among several and may be traded against responsiveness. If that is right, the dispute is verbal at bottom but not innocuous, since the word is doing normative work.

One further thought I would like tested, because I cannot tell whether it is a diagnosis or a redescription. Each panelist's ballot can be read not as an unconditional assertion of every proposition she endorses but as a conditional commitment — yes to feasibility on the assumption that the other two criteria are met. On that reading the five ballots are jointly satisfiable, and the panel contradicts itself only when the antecedents are detached, which they must be the moment the panel has to act. If so, the impossibility results may be theorems about the wrong object: not about whether groups can hold consistent judgments, but about what happens when commitments have to be converted into assertions.

So tell me what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, a hidden formal question, or two camps answering different questions under one name — and, given that diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, or nothing at all.
