I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of language, and in two weeks I am responsible for the session on two-dimensional semantics in a cross-departmental reading group. The membership is awkward in a productive way: roughly half are semantics students who are comfortable with possible worlds and with Kaplan's character/content distinction, and the other half come from cognitive science and philosophy of mind and will hear the word intension as a loose synonym for meaning. The session runs seventy-five minutes, with a whiteboard and no formal apparatus beyond what I can write up on the spot. I do not need the view explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it.

Please give me, first, the order in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit. In particular: what has to be in place before the primary/secondary distinction can be stated at all; where the a priori / a posteriori contrast must enter relative to the possible-worlds machinery; and exactly why the move of considering a world as actual rather than as counterfactual has to be secured before the two intensions can be pulled apart.

Second, the carrying example: the one case that should hold the whole session. Tell me what it must have and what it must lack. I want an example that is not the evening star, not water, and not the Gödel/Schmidt case, since the group has seen all three, and that does not require the cognitive scientists to bring any empirical science they don't already have. It also has to survive the shift from the epistemic reading of the dimensions to the metaphysical one without my swapping it out mid-session.

Third, I want to run this as an argument rather than a survey. My hook is a claim of my own: that the two-dimensional apparatus earns its keep only for someone who wants to get from conceivability to possibility, and that if you give up on that inference, the two levels quietly collapse back into something like character and content. Build the sequence so that this claim becomes assessable by the end, including the point at which I should acknowledge that different two-dimensional frameworks disagree about what the dimensions represent — a semantic rule, an a priori accessible truth-condition, or a device for interpreting a partially mistaken speaker — without letting the session degenerate into a taxonomy of positions.

Finally, give me the misconceptions you predict. I would guess the first will be that the primary intension is just whatever the speaker has in mind, and the second that the diagonal is just the actual-world truth condition; tell me where in the sequence each should be headed off. Add one question I can put to the room at the end that would discriminate between having followed the argument and having merely enjoyed it, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and how to say so without making it sound as though something is being withheld. No reading list, and please don't hang the plan on who said what.
