I serve on the editorial board of a small, closed knowledge repository maintained by a research group. To keep the repository honest we have adopted a house rule I will call Finite Knowability: a proposition may be entered as true only if some contributor could, in principle, come to know it using only the methods and materials already in the repository, within a finite span of working time, and without postulating agents, instruments, or inferences we do not already have. The rule is anti-realist in spirit -- we want the truth we store to be tied to what our community can come to know -- but the tie is meant to be practical rather than metaphysical.

A colleague warns that any principle of this shape is liable to the instability exhibited by Fitch's paradox, and that our bounded version may fare either better or worse than the idealized one. Please give me the most important implications of Fitch's paradox for how we should actually run the repository, treating the paradox as a design constraint on our rule rather than as a puzzle to be summarized. I have a first course in modal logic, so you may use boxes, diamonds, and the standard derivation, but flag any step that depends on a choice of modal system.

In particular: does the derivation that converts the principle that every truth is knowable into the conclusion that every truth is known survive once knowability is bounded by finite time and the real resources of a finite community? Which steps lean on idealization -- unbounded time, logical omniscience, closure of knowledge under conjunction, the normality of the possibility operator -- and which of those our rule can shed and which it cannot? If the bounded version escapes where the unbounded one fails, is that a genuine escape or a change of subject, i.e. has the bound quietly replaced knowability with something weaker, such as checkability or warranted assertability under our present methods? Of the usual repairs -- restricting the rule to propositions whose knowability is not self-undermining, weakening the closure of knowledge, or abandoning the idea that the repository records a single truth rather than a set of standpoints -- which is most defensible for a working community, and what does each cost us? And what is the single most important practical implication: what should we change about how we admit, revise, and retire claims?
