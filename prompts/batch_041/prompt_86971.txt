I've spent the past year coordinating a small volunteer fact-checking desk out of our county library system: patrons drop off a claim at the reference counter or through a web form, and my team has to decide within a day or so whether the claim gets a short written explainer we publish under the library's name, whether we send the patron back with guidance on how to check it themselves, or whether we say nothing at all because engaging would only spread it further. I got pulled into this after watching a close relative sink into a theory about our water utility that I could never reason him out of — every piece of evidence I brought him was absorbed and turned into further proof of the cover-up — and that experience convinced me that the usual advice to check the source and check the date is close to useless against a claim that has already built the debunking into itself. We handle four or five submissions a week, we get about fifteen minutes of research per claim with no access to court records or public-records requests, and anything we publish is signed off by the library director, so I need a procedure that is defensible rather than clever.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my volunteers can actually run on a claim, built out of what the epistemology of conspiracy theories genuinely knows rather than the folk version. The tension I keep running into is this: there is one tradition that treats a conspiracy theory as just an ordinary causal claim, to be weighed evidence by evidence like any other, and another that treats conspiracy theories as a distinctive epistemic kind that can be discounted in advance on structural grounds — and I need a procedure that uses the structural grounds as triage without becoming a machine for dismissing true claims, since real conspiracies and real institutional wrongdoing exist and often look, at first, exactly like this. So the procedure should have an explicit step that forces genuine engagement with the leftover anomalies that official accounts leave unexplained, because a good conspiracy theory's whole selling point is that it explains them, and it should treat the following as indicators to be operationalized rather than as verdicts: a structure in which disconfirming evidence is reabsorbed as confirming; a demand for a higher evidentiary standard from the official account than from the theory itself; an assumption of near-perfect secrecy across a large number of participants; and the habit of treating coincidence as something that must be explained away. I also want the procedure to keep separate the epistemic question (is this claim warranted?) from the strategic and ethical question (should we, a public institution, respond to it at all?), because those come apart and I don't want my volunteers resolving a values question with a checklist.

The deliverable should be the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a definite no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts — and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three submissions I'll give you so I can see it work: first, my relative's claim that the utility is adding something to the water as part of a deliberate plan to make the population docile, which I personally find absurd but which keeps arriving from people who are not absurd; second, a claim that our regional hospital is quietly rationing care to hit a budget target, which is an under-evidenced institutional allegation that could simply be true and that I would not want us to flag as a conspiracy theory; and third, a claim that last year's county-wide emergency-alert test was a pretext for standing up a federal surveillance program, which is one I find more plausible than I'd like to admit, so I want to see the procedure tell me something I won't enjoy hearing. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do and the cases where it will misfire.
