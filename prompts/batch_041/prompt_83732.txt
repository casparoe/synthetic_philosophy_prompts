I run a 90-minute session in an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on explanation in the social sciences; the room seats about twenty, the chairs move, and I can split the group in half if the design calls for it. I want the session to leave students with the felt difference between two claims that are usually run together: that an outcome was contingent — that things could have gone otherwise, and that the going-otherwise could have stuck — and that an outcome was merely unforeseen, or inefficient, or slow in arriving. My aim is that they feel the first claim as a real force before they hear it named. So please design a runnable activity in which the group, through a sequence of ordinary choices made under mild time pressure, ends up in a stable arrangement nobody would have picked if they had seen it coming, and in which the cost of switching to something better has become prohibitive by the time the better thing is visible. Early individually sensible choices should raise the price of later ones, and the pressure should come entirely from the rules and payoffs rather than from anything I say: I do not want to announce the theme, use the phrase "path dependence," or hint at the shape of the endpoint before the debrief.

The second thing I want from the design is that the counterfactual question becomes unavoidable rather than a closing flourish. Whatever device you use — a rerun under a trivial perturbation, a parallel group playing the same game in the next room, an alternative that the rules made permanently unavailable — I want the students to end up arguing about whether the same endpoint would have arrived anyway, and to notice how little the activity itself gives them to settle that with. Concretely, I need: materials and setup; rules, roles, and a payoff scheme in points rather than money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; a note on what the facilitator may and may not say at each stage; a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the relevant ideas (self-reinforcing mechanisms, increasing returns, lock-in, efficiency-style explanations of why the surviving option survived, and the gap between showing that an outcome was contingent and showing that it was avoidable); the failure modes you would expect on a first run and how I should handle each one in the moment; and an honest paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce about the real historical situation — for instance, what the participants know about the payoff structure that historical actors would not have known, or why a second run is a luxury no historian is granted. You may lean on the familiar keyboard-standard example and on the counter-literature arguing that such lock-in stories are overstated, but the game itself should stand on its own.
