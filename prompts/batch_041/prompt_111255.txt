Last week a colleague and I stayed late after a planning meeting rehashing a call I had made earlier that day, and he kept coming back to a line I have now heard in a dozen podcasts and conference hallway conversations: my brain decided before I did. He treats it as the plain upshot of the classic experiments from the 1980s, in which people are asked to move a finger whenever they feel like it while their brain activity is recorded, and where a slow electrical buildup appears to begin a few hundred milliseconds before the movement and, on the usual gloss, noticeably before the moment the subject reports first becoming aware of an urge to move. He used the line to argue that the choice I defended in the meeting was not really mine, and from there that the praise and blame we hand out in incident postmortems are mostly theater. I have no formal training in philosophy — I studied computer science and have spent about ten years writing backend services — so I can tell that something is being smuggled in somewhere, but I cannot say what, and I would rather not answer with the usual but I still feel free move, which he quite reasonably finds unimpressive.

What I want from you is not an explanation of the experiments or a tour of the free will debate. I want the slogan itself taken apart. My brain decided before I did sounds like a single claim and is almost certainly several, and I would like them laid out as a menu, ordered roughly from the innocuous to the radical. The merely temporal reading would be something like: a neural event that precedes and is necessary for the action also precedes the reported awareness. The causal reading would be: that event fixes the action, so the conscious deliberation is a downstream report rather than an input. The authorship reading would be: the thing that decides is not the agent but something the brain produces, so either the pronoun in my brain is doing illicit homunculus work or the referent has quietly shifted from me to a mechanism inside me that is not me. Add whatever further readings you think are actually doing work in conversations of this kind, and for each one state the precise claim in a form where I can see what it would take to be true or false, give a verdict on whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false as stated, and then — the part I actually care about — say whether that reading would license his conclusion that the decision in the meeting was not mine and that blame is theater. If some readings leave the decision entirely mine, or are compatible with every position in the free will dispute, I want that said plainly rather than hedged.

A few loose threads keep coming up when I try to push back, and I would like them folded in. One colleague claims the slow buildup is just noise accumulating toward a threshold rather than a decision forming, which if true seems to matter for some of the readings and not others; tell me which. Another insists the whole literature concerns trivial finger movements and says nothing about the kind of deliberate, reason-weighing decisions we argue about at work, and I would like to know whether that is a genuine limitation or a dodge. Finally, give me a short closing paragraph I could actually deliver at the next postmortem: which single reading my colleague is most likely relying on, what is right and what is wrong about it, and what would have to be shown for his stronger conclusion to go through. Please do not cite papers or hand me a reading list — I want the disambiguation and your verdicts, in prose I can follow without a glossary.
