I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun attending a continuing-education seminar on history and memory. The assigned popular book summarizes Maurice Halbwachs, the early twentieth-century French sociologist who introduced the concept of collective memory, as follows: 'Halbwachs distinguished collective memory from history. Collective memory is lived, embodied in social frameworks, and always reconstructed from the standpoint of the present; history, by contrast, is the objective, disinterested record of what actually happened. So the distinction is essentially between myth and truth. It follows that when a community's memory conflicts with the historical record, the record should always win, and the historian's task is to correct the community's memory.' I am a scientist by training and I am inclined to think the summary is broadly right, but something about the sharp contrast between memory and history strikes me as too neat. Is the book's summary fair? Please go sentence by sentence and tell me what a scholar working on collective memory would accept, what they would qualify or wince at, and what is simply mistaken; and whether the book's normative conclusion follows from Halbwachs's actual position or from any defensible view of the relation between memory and history.
