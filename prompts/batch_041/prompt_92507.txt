My friend's in a seminar on testimony and he's built this argument himself, and now he can't decide whether its a proof or a trap. It goes:

(1) You're justified in believing something because someone told you only if you have evidence that the person is reliable about that sort of thing.
(2) Evidence about a person's reliability comes from one of two places: you've watched them get things right before, or someone else vouched for them.
(3) Watching for yourself is out for anyone you've just met, which is nearly everyone.
(4) So the vouching route is usually all you've got.
(5) But a voucher is only as good as his own record, so to take his word about the speaker you need evidence that he's reliable, which again means watching or vouching.
(6) So every testimonial belief sits on a chain of vouchers, and the only way the chain can reach the ground is if somebody at the bottom personally watched a speaker be reliable.
(7) For almost everything you've ever been told, nobody at the bottom watched anything — the chain just trails off into more say-so.
(8) A belief that never touches the ground isn't justified.
(9) So almost nothing you've been told is justified — not the history, not the science, not what your doctor tells you.

He's fond of this because it handles a case he likes: a village where nobody has ever left, and every villager's picture of the outside world comes from some other villager, all the way back to nobody in particular. He says the villagers know nothing about the outside world, and that feels obviously right to him, which is part of why he trusts the argument. He wants to keep (1), because he doesn't want to end up saying you should just believe whatever anyone tells you, and he wants to keep (8).

So which step should he give up? Why did it look fine to him? And what does each of the going positions on testimony have to pay to give it up? I want the price tag, not just the number of the step.
