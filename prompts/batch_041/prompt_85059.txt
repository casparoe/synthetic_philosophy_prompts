**Side effects without a value function: a feature-difference account**

*Thesis.* Proposals for making agents "low-impact" or "side-effect-averse" almost always define impact as a difference in expected value between a policy and a baseline: take some value function V, compare the expected return of the candidate policy against the return of a default or do-nothing policy, and penalize the gap. I want to argue that this construction is either underdetermined or otiose, and that a thinner object suffices — a feature ontology plus a default trajectory, with impact defined as a set-difference in features rather than a difference in value.

*1. The dilemma.* If V is incomplete, the measure is undefined or arbitrary on the unspecified margin: it will call a plan low-impact precisely where it has nothing to say. If V is complete, the measure is redundant, since a complete V already ranks trajectories and the impact penalty merely reweights an objective we already had. So a return-based impact measure cannot both be well-defined and do independent work. The work it appears to do — keeping the agent near the default — is either already implied by V or is being smuggled in from outside V.

*2. The proposal.* Fix a set F of thresholded features of the environment state, a discretization of each, and a reference policy: the agent's do-nothing or keep-the-lights-on default. Define the impact of a policy as the expected Hamming distance between the feature vector at the horizon under that policy and under the default. The claim is that F is not a value specification. A value specification says which differences matter and how much; F says only which differences are of a kind that can be tracked at all. An inventory of the furniture is not a ranking of the furniture's worth, and nothing in the inventory tells you what to do.

*3. The objection I cannot yet answer.* The Hamming measure weights every feature equally and treats every change as a cost. It therefore cannot distinguish rearranging dust from killing someone, and it treats any plan that improves anything as high-impact. Two repairs suggest themselves. The first counts a changed feature only if the change is in a direction not implied by the task. The second weights features by a lower bound on their importance — a partial order rather than a total one — and counts only changes to features not dominated by the task's objective. But the first reintroduces a value comparison, and the second has to say what "dominated by the objective" means when the objective is a scalar reward, which looks like exactly the value specification sneaking back in through the task.

*4. The defense I have.* Deflationary: the measure is not meant to be a complete account of what matters, only a presumption — a bias toward default-like plans that the objective can override. On this reading its incompleteness is a feature, since an incomplete impact measure is conservative, and conservatism is what you want when your value function is unreliable. I suspect this converts a definitional claim into a decision-theoretic one, and I cannot tell whether that is a concession or a clarification.
