Last week I was at my brother's flat when a heating engineer came to look at a boiler that kept cutting out. He made two remarks about an hour apart, and my brother's flatmate Rana and their friend Ivo have been arguing about them ever since. Pointing at the pressure gauge on the front panel, the engineer said: "If that gauge reads high, the boiler will cut out." Later, having discovered that the gauge had in fact been stuck for months and that the cut-outs had a different cause altogether, he said: "If that gauge had read high, the boiler wouldn't have cut out at all — it's just a dial." Rana and Ivo agree the engineer was right both times. They disagree about what follows from that. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Rana: There is only one kind of conditional in play. The first remark says that among the possible situations in which the gauge reads high, the boiler cuts out. The second says that among the possible situations in which the gauge reads high, the boiler does not cut out. Same antecedent, opposite consequents, the same range of situations — so as they stand the two remarks contradict each other, and the only way to save both is to notice that the second one is only intelligible against the discovery that the gauge was not in fact reading high. The second remark takes the gauge's being stuck for granted; that is a fact about the actual world, not part of what the "if" clause says. So the difference between the two remarks is a difference in what each presupposes, not a difference in what kind of conditional it is.

Ivo: That cannot be right, because the second remark is not about the situations the first one is about. When the engineer says what would have happened had the gauge read high, he means: leave the rest of the system alone — same water pressure, same thermostat, same scale in the pipework — and let the dial move. Then nothing happens to the boiler, because the dial sits downstream of everything that matters. The first remark is about what the reading tells you, and it tells you something only because the reading and the cut-out are both produced by the same thing, the water getting too hot. One remark is about evidence; the other is about what happens when you change one thing and hold the rest fixed. Both can be right, and they cannot be the same claim, because the same claim cannot be right in both directions.

Rana: If the first remark is a claim about evidence, it is not true or false at all; it is a report of how confident the engineer was, and reports of confidence have no truth conditions. But we used it as a premise. He made the first remark, looked at the gauge, saw that it was reading high, and concluded that the boiler would cut out. That reasoning — if the gauge reads high the boiler cuts out; it is reading high; so it will cut out — is valid, and its conditional premise was true. A fact about somebody's confidence cannot occupy the premise slot of a valid argument. So the first remark has truth conditions; and if it has truth conditions, it is about the same subject matter as the second, and we are back to a contradiction.

Ivo: The reasoning is valid whether or not the premise is true; validity concerns what follows from what, and a conditional can be perfectly in order to assert without being true, which is exactly what the probability facts suggest. And if conditionals were ordinary propositions they would behave like propositions. Try denying the first remark: "That's false" sounds like a complaint about the engineer's evidence, not a disagreement about the boiler. Try putting "probably" in front of it: "Probably, if the gauge reads high, the boiler will cut out" is not a sentence about a proposition being probable; it is the same conditional with a hedge attached. And from "Either the gauge is not reading high or the boiler cuts out" you can move to the conditional, but you cannot move back — which is very strange for two sentences with identical truth conditions. Content that refuses to be denied, hedged, and disjoined is not propositional content; it is a rule for adjusting what you expect.

Rana: Then say what the two remarks conflict about, if they conflict, or else explain why it so strongly sounds as though they do. And notice what your story costs. On your account the engineer's first remark was in good order while he was ignorant of the stuck gauge and defective the moment he learned of it: the same sentence, the same world, the same boiler, but its standing changes because his information changed. If a sentence can change its truth while nothing about the boiler changes, you are not describing the boiler at all. And that is not how "if" behaves elsewhere in the language, where conditionals get negated, reported, embedded, and argued with like anything else.

I have now heard this three times and I cannot referee it. Ivo keeps saying "intervene" and Rana keeps saying "conditional probability," so they are not merely talking past each other. Please adjudicate. Begin with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then rate each of the five moves in turn, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justifying each rating; finish by saying what both of them are missing — in particular, whether the engineer's two remarks really do conflict, and what any adequate account of the pair has to get right. No preamble, and do not restate the exchange back to me.
