I am a graduate student in a cognitive science program, and I keep running into a one-liner in seminars on computational theories of mind: the claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the human mind is not a machine. It gets deployed in different ways — sometimes to argue that no computer could ever be conscious, sometimes to insist that mathematical understanding cannot be algorithmic, sometimes just as a conversation-stopper when someone proposes that thought is computation. I have read enough to know the slogan compresses a tangle of distinct claims, but when I try to untangle it myself I lose track of which premise is doing the work, and I suspect different people mean very different things by it. What I want from you is a disambiguation: lay out the precise theses the slogan could be expressing, from the nearly trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false or at least unsupported. Then say which of those readings would actually license the uses I described, and which reading the people who repeat it most likely have in mind.

To make this useful rather than a summary of the familiar debate, please build the menu around the distinctions that actually matter. I am interested in, among other things: the difference between the claim that the mind is not a Turing machine and the claim that it is not any finite machine at all; the difference between showing that a particular formal system fails to capture some piece of mathematical knowledge and showing that no formal system could; the role of the idealization to an infinite or idealized mathematician versus the actual finite human; whether the argument requires the human to know, or merely believe, that the relevant formal system is consistent or sound, and what happens if that knowledge is itself not algorithmic; and the suggestion, which I have seen attributed to Gödel himself in a late lecture, that the theorem forces a disjunction between the mind exceeding every finite machine and there being arithmetic questions that are absolutely undecidable. I would also like you to distinguish the argument as Lucas originally gave it from the version Penrose developed, insofar as they differ in what they claim about insight, understanding, and the physical basis of thought, without turning this into a history lesson.

Please write the answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italicized labels, no tables. You may use paragraph breaks, but each reading should be introduced and assessed in the flow of the prose rather than as a separate labeled item. Write in American English. The point is a menu with verdicts, not a single verdict, so do not simply tell me which reading is correct; tell me what each reading commits one to, where it is plausible, where it collapses, and which reading the slogan's users need if their argument is to go through.
