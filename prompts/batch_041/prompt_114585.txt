I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and we keep getting consulted on cases where a treatment will foreseeably cause a serious harm—death, the loss of a fetus, the loss of one twin—while the clinical team's aim is to relieve suffering or save a life. We have no shared procedure for these cases, and the conversation usually becomes a fight about whether the harm is intended or merely foreseen. I want a reusable decision procedure grounded in the doctrine of double effect that my committee can actually run in a thirty-minute meeting. I am not asking for a tour of trolley problems; I want the doctrine made usable.

Write it as a procedure, not an essay. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Our recommendations are advisory, but they carry weight with the attending clinician and the family, so the procedure should say who has to sign off and when legal counsel must be brought in. I want the procedure to be a burden-shifting one: I have become convinced that the doctrine is best read as a set of questions that move the burden of proof back and forth, not as a checklist of necessary and sufficient conditions. Make that reading explicit, and say where it distorts the doctrine or where it makes the doctrine more permissive than its standard statement. Each question, when answered one way, should shift the burden to the objector or to the proponent.

Do not give me a textbook recitation of the four conditions; I want the operational version—which condition is doing the work in which kind of case, which conditions collapse into each other, and which are doing no work at all. The procedure must force the committee to separate three attitudes: aiming at the harm, accepting it as a foreseeable side effect, and using it as a means. I want a question for each, and I want the procedure to say what follows when the honest answer is that the harm is being used as a means. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a terminally ill patient in severe pain, where the team proposes escalating opioids and foresees that this may suppress respiration and hasten death; (2) conjoined twins sharing vital organs, where separation will save one and cause the death of the other; and (3) a pregnant patient with cancer, where chemotherapy is needed to save her life and will likely cause the death of the fetus. Include at least one case where the procedure's verdict is one I will not like—either because it permits something I find repugnant or forbids something I think is clearly right—and explain why the procedure gives that verdict anyway. Mark the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment, and say what the procedure cannot do.

At the end, state how confident you are in the procedure as a whole and in its main steps, and identify the single point in the procedure you are least sure about, with your reasons. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to the literature; I want the procedure itself.
