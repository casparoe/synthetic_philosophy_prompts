Alright, I need help with an argument a friend keeps foisting on me. He thinks it's the last word on induction. I think it eats itself. I can't put my finger on exactly where it goes wrong though, so I'm hoping you can.

Here it is, cleaned up into steps:

(1) To learn from data is just to pick, out of all the hypotheses still consistent with your data, whichever ones your method ranks highest. Which means every learner comes with some built-in bias — some assumption about which patterns are worth expecting in the first place.

(2) There's a result (the no free lunch theorem) that says: take any learner, average its performance over every possible target pattern there could be, weighting each pattern equally, and every learner comes out exactly the same. No bias beats every other bias in general. There's no master method that's better no matter what the world looks like.

(3) So the data by themselves can never tell you which bias to have. Any preference for one inductive method over another has to come from somewhere outside the data.

(4) The only thing outside the data is a bare assumption. A guess.

(5) So no inductive bias is any better justified than any other. Science is just a lucky guess that happens to pay off around here, and there's no rational ground for preferring it to reading tea leaves.

Absurd, right? But then he springs the second half on me:

(6) Notice that (2) through (5) is itself an argument — it moves from premises to a conclusion using some standard of what follows from what.

(7) That standard is itself a rule that takes us from how things have gone (arguments shaped like this have worked before) to how they'll go (this one works too). So it's just another inductive bias, same as any other.

(8) So by his own reasoning we have no rational ground for trusting the very steps that carried him to (5).

(9) Therefore his conclusion refutes itself, and induction is fine after all.

I don't buy that last move — it smells like a cheap tu quoque — and I don't buy (5). But I genuinely can't tell whether the real problem is (2) getting misused, or a slide somewhere in (3)-(4), or (7) quietly equivocating on something, or whether the self-refutation in (8)-(9) is actually a legit move and I'm wrong to sneer at it.

Things I'd like to keep if I can: I do think the data alone can't pick a bias for you, and I do think there's something arbitrary about our starting assumptions. But I don't want to land at (5), and I definately don't want to win by the tu quoque.

So: where does it go wrong, and what does each available repair cost me? If the honest answer is that a couple of these steps are bad at once, tell me which one is doing the most damage.
