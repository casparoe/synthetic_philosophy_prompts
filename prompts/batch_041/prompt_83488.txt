I'm a retired hydrologist — thirty-odd years on river basins and groundwater models, and since I packed it in I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars at the local university, mostly political philosophy, and I've gotten a bit obessed with what the people there call the boundary problem: before a demos can decide anything, somebody has to decide who the demos is, and that decision can't itself be made by the demos without going around in a circle. The way it's usually framed seems to be a fight between "everyone affected by a decision ought to get a say" and "everyone subject to a decision ought to get a say", and honestly both of those feel to me like the watershed-delineation arguments I used to have with engineers — you can draw the line at the topographic divide, or at the aquifer, or at the regulatory jurisdiction, and each one is defensible and each one hands you a seperate set of stakeholders. What struck me is that in my old field nobody ever settled the boundary in the abstract; we settled it by arguing about who had standing to contest a particular permit, at a particular hearing, with a particular clock running. So my half-formed idea is to stop asking "who should belong to the demos" and instead ask whether the boundary question gets more tractable if we go contestation-first: a boundary is legitimate to the extent that there's an institutional moment at which an outsider can challenge it, and the real philosophical work is in specifying what that moment has to look like — who can trigger it, on what grounds, with what remedy, and how it terminates, since an endlessly contestable boundary is just no boundary at all.

I'd like you to act as a political philosopher who works on democratic theory and has thought hard about the boundary problem, and turn this into a short research proposal — two pages, for a small internal workshop, nothing grand. Start by brainstorming three or four candidate ways to sharpen my vague "contestation-first" idea into a single question with a determinate answer (I'd much rather have a narrow question I can actually finish than a sweeping one), then pick the best of them and write the proposal around it. I want: the question stated precisely; an honest account of why the standard positions — all-affected, all-subjected, and whatever the third one is — don't already settle it; the method, described month by month, keeping in mind that I'm a philosopher by hobby with no archive and no lab, so it has to be something like constructing cases and testing them against explicit criteria rather than empirical work; why this approach rather than just another survey of the existing positions; what a genuine result would look like and what would count as the project failing or quietly dissolving into definitional housekeeping; and a realistic scope for three or four months of part-time work. Please think carefully about the self-reference issue rather than papering over it. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I'll dig up citations myself, and half of what you'd name I probably can't get hold of anyway, so refer to positions and arguments by description instead.
