I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past several months I've been reporting on the small industry that grades countries on how free, democratic, and liberal they are — the annual Freedom House scorecards, the Democracy Index, and the academic indices that hand every country a decimal score and then publish league tables. Like everyone else, I've been using them as though they settled questions of the form: is Sweden more liberal than Norway, or has Canada drifted further from the United States than the United States has from Canada? A political theorist I interviewed for the piece threw cold water on the whole exercise. She said these instruments don't measure an independent quantity called liberality so much as construct one, and that the "which neighbour is more liberal?" question may have no determinate answer, because the practice quietly smuggles in a theory of what liberality is.

I want to test that claim properly, so please take the practice itself — building a composite index of freedom or democracy by scoring a handful of components (competitive elections, press freedom, civil liberties, religious freedom, rule of law, and so on) and summing them into a single number — and tell me what philosophical commitments it presupposes. Does it require that "liberal" name one measurable thing, that the components be commensurable and mutually substitutable (so that a loss of press freedom can be offset by a gain in electoral competitiveness), that the resulting scale be cardinal rather than merely ordinal, and that there be a fact of the matter about one country's being more liberal than another? Which rival conceptions of freedom would the mere act of aggregating into one number rule out, and which would survive it? I'm especially interested in two pieces of apparatus the theorists I've been reading keep invoking and that I can't quite fit together: the psychometric distinction between "reflective" measures, where the components are manifestations of one underlying quantity, and "formative" measures, where the components jointly define the thing rather than reveal it; and the republican idea that freedom is the absence of domination rather than the absence of interference, which seems to cut across the components the indices actually use. Does the ranking practice need the strong reflective reading, or could a formative reading license the same league tables? And is the practice internally consistent, or does it presuppose a value-monist, additive picture of liberality while its defenders reach for pluralist justifications?

Finally, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined — whether several incompatible theories of liberality would each license the same rankings, so that the Sweden-versus-Norway question comes out determinate on some of them and not on others. Be explicit about your own confidence and about where your argument is weakest: tell me which consideration, if it turned out to be correct, would push you toward the opposite verdict — that these indices do after all track a real property that settles which of two neighbours is the more liberal.
