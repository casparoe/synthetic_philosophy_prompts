I have been reading an introductory book on metaethics, and its chapter on expressivism contains the following paragraph, which I would like audited rather than supplemented. I am not a metaethicist — I work in an adjacent area — and I cannot tell which of its sentences I should trust.

"Expressivism holds that moral judgements do not describe the world: to call an action wrong is not to state a fact about it but to express the speaker's attitude of disapproval, in something like the way that a cry of 'Boo!' expresses one. The Frege–Geach problem, sometimes called the embedding problem, is the standard objection to this view. If 'lying is wrong' merely expresses an attitude, then in the sentence 'If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong' the antecedent is not asserted and so expresses nothing; the conditional has no content for the expressivist to analyse, and the argument from 'Lying is wrong' and 'If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong' to 'Getting your brother to lie is wrong', though obviously valid, comes out invalid on expressivist terms. Since so much moral argument has this form, the view cannot account for it. The same difficulty shows up in the fact that two people who disagree about whether lying is wrong are contradicting one another, whereas two people who merely have opposed attitudes are not. The problem is named for Frege, whose observation that a sentence means what it does in virtue of its truth-conditions leaves the expressivist with nothing to say about the embedded occurrence, and for Geach, who pressed the point against the non-cognitivists of the 1960s. Expressivists have by and large conceded it, and now claim only that the inference is pragmatically acceptable rather than logically valid — that we are entitled to move from the attitudes to the conclusion even though no entailment holds. Because the point of moral reasoning is that it can be valid, the Frege–Geach problem is widely regarded as having refuted expressivism."

What I want is an accuracy audit, not a tutorial. Taking the paragraph clause by clause, please separate what a careful expressivist would accept, what would make them wince, and what is straightforwardly wrong — beginning with the opening characterization of the view, which I have some doubt any current expressivist would own. Where a clause is a simplification, say what it simplifies and whether the simplification is harmless. In particular, I would like the several complaints the paragraph runs together kept apart: the Frege–Geach problem itself, the embedding problem, the negation problem, and the problem of explaining moral disagreement. My suspicion is that the paragraph treats as one problem what are at least two, and I want to know whether the distinction is substantive or pedantic. I also want a verdict on three specific moves: the claim that an unasserted antecedent "expresses nothing" and so leaves the conditional without content; the claim that expressivists have conceded the objection and fallen back on pragmatic acceptability; and the closing claim that the problem has refuted the view. Does that conclusion follow from whatever in the paragraph is accurate? And if the view is not refuted, what is the strongest difficulty that remains, and what would count as a solution — I have heard quasi-realism and minimalism about truth offered as the expressivist's way out, but the paragraph never mentions them, and I do not know whether they change the audit.
