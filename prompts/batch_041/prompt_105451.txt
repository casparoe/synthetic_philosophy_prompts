I'm in a reading group going through some 19th-century political writing and we hit a passage I can't get straight. Here it is, from Constant's lecture on the liberty of the ancients compared with the moderns:

"First ask yourselves, Gentlemen, what an Englishman, a French-man, and a citizen of the United States of America understand today by the word 'liberty'. For each of them it is the right to be subjected only to the laws, and to be neither arrested, detained, put to death or maltreated in any way by the arbitrary will of one or more individuals."

And then, for the ancients:

"The latter consisted in exercising collectively, but directly, several parts of the complete sovereignty; in deliberating, in the public square, over war and peace; in forming alliances with foreign governments; in voting laws, in pronouncing judgments; in examining the accounts, the acts, the stewardship of the magistrates; in calling them to appear in front of the assembled people, in accusing, condemning or absolving them. But if this was what the ancients called liberty, they admitted as compatible with this collective freedom the complete subjection of the individual to the authority of the community."

My friend insists this is just Isaiah Berlin's two concepts of liberty in embryo — ancient = positive, modern = negative — and that there's nothing else to see. I don't think thats right, or at least not the whole story. Constant keeps saying modern liberty is the right not to be subject to "arbitrary will", and that sounds a lot more like the republican idea of non-domination — not being at anyone's mercy — than like Berlin's plain non-interference. So which is he actually doing here: distinguishing two *kinds* of freedom, or two *scopes* of it (who holds the sovereignty, the collective or the individual), or something else entirely? And does the "arbitrary will" line really commit him to non-domination, or is he just using a scary-sounding synonym for interference? Walk me through what the passage itself supports, not the textbook Constant.
