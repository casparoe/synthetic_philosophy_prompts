Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about whether he's allowed to say "the Swiss are orderly," and neither of us moved an inch, which bugs me, because we actually agree on the facts — he's a demographer, I did a philosophy degree, we both know within-country variance is usually bigger then between-country differences. So what is this disagreement really? My hypothesis is that it's three or four disagreements wearing one coat: (1) a verbal one about what "national character" even means — a statistical distribution over a population, or a shared essence that every member carries; (2) a normative one about when generalizing about people is permissible even when it's accurate — actuarial use at the level of policy vs. applying it to the individual in front of you; (3) a question about the reference class, since everyone belongs to dozens of them (nation, city, class, profession, generation) and nothing obviously privileges the national one; and maybe (4) whether a "people" is a bounded thing at all, or whether the boundaries are themselves the artifact. I don't want a verdict on whether generalizing is OK, I want a diagnosis: which of these is doing the actual work, is any part of it a real factual dispute that could in principle be settled, and what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, or nothing, because the two sides are just answering different questions under one name? And be straight with me about which parts of this are settled among people who study it and which are genuinely contested — don't hand me a contested view as the consensus, and tell me if my four-way split is itself a mess.
