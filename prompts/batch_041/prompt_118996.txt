I cover courts and criminal justice for a magazine, and I've been writing about the philosophy of punishment on the side for a few years now. I'm applying for a year-long fellowship at a journalism school, and they want a two-page research proposal: the question, why it's worth a year, and what I'd actually do with the time. I have a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into something a selection committee won't roll their eyes at.

Here's the idea. Everybody who argues about the death penalty runs the same three things: it's deserved, it deters (or doesn't), and we might kill somebody innocent. I've sat through enough clemency hearings to think the third one is doing something strange. The risk argument always gets presented as an empirical add-on — here's the exoneration count, here's the error rate, so here's the expected number of wrongful executions — and then it gets dropped onto the consequentialist side of the ledger as just another cost. But if you're a retributivist, that isn't where it belongs at all. Retributivism says the state should give each offender what he deserves and should not punish anyone who doesn't deserve it; that's not a cost to be weighed, it's the entire point of the theory. So a retributivist who supports capital punishment has to support a practice that, by design rather than by accident, will sometimes do precisely the thing the theory exists to forbid: treat a person who doesn't deserve death as if he did. The usual escape hatch is that a wrongful execution is a mistake — foreseen, maybe, but not intended — so the state isn't really punishing an innocent, it's just getting it wrong. That looks to me like a double-effect move, and I'm not sure it works, because the intention here is to kill this defendant for what he did. If the premise is false, the whole act is aimed at the wrong person; it isn't a good act with a sad side effect.

So the question I want to pose is whether a retributivist can coherently accept a non-zero chance of executing the innocent at all, and if so, where the acceptable chance comes from. Either the retributivist refuses to trade anything off, in which case the error rate is irrelevant to them and that sounds monstrous, or they do trade off, in which case they're doing expected-value reasoning and the retributivism has quietly gone home. Is there a third option? Maybe something internal to retributivism — a threshold of deserved confidence, or a rule about how much evidence a desert claim needs before the state may act on it — and if there is, what makes it retributive rather than just a dressed-up cost-benefit rule?

Two things I'd want the proposal to make room for, because they're what keeps me up at night about this. First, the probabilistic injustice gets all the attention, but the certain injustice doesn't: plenty of people on death row get more than they deserve, on any plausible account of desert, and that happens every time, not with some probability. If a retributivist is willing to live with a wrongful execution every so often to get the guilty what they're owed, what's the principle that says the certain over-punishment of the guilty-ish is tolerable while the occasional execution of the innocent is not? Second, I want to know how the institutions actually handle this, because I suspect they've already invented an answer. Clemency petitions lean hard on residual doubt. And as I understand it, the military runs a whole apparatus where an estimate of expected civilian deaths gets graded into a level and the higher levels have to be signed off further and further up the chain of command. That's an institution that kills people it doesn't intend to kill, on purpose, with a threshold rule. Is that rule a desert threshold or an expected-value cutoff? Whatever it is, I'd like to know whether the practice I'd be reporting on is doing something the philosophers haven't written down.

What I need from you: state the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than trailing off into musing; say why the existing positions — the philosophical ones and the way these arguments get made in public, in op-eds and court opinions and clemency statements — leave it open; lay out the method month by month, keeping in mind that I can't run a study and my evidence is going to be arguments plus the way institutions justify themselves in their own words; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give me a realistic scope for a year, including whether this should be one long reported essay or a seperate shorter academic-ish piece bolted on. Don't do a literature review or a bibliography, I'll fill in the citations myself, and don't feel you have to name names — I'm more interested in the shape of the argument than in who said it. Also I'm definately not a philosopher by training, so tell me where I'm likely to get the concepts wrong.
