I am assembling a document for a seminar on moral responsibility, and I would like you to draft it.

Two camps have spent years talking past each other about the principle that an agent is morally responsible for what he has done only if he could have done otherwise. The first camp holds that the principle is false: a case in which an agent acts for his own reasons while some unimplemented mechanism guaranteed that he would act that way in any event shows that responsibility depends on the actual sequence of events, not on the availability of alternatives. The second camp holds that such cases do not refute the principle, either because the agent in them still retains some alternative possibility, however thin, or because the counterfactual mechanism leaves its mark on the actual sequence in ways the first camp overlooks. The two camps need not line up with the compatibilist and incompatibilist camps; if you think those labels obscure the issue here, say so rather than adopting them.

What I want is not a verdict on who is right but the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the two camps would produce. Three cases of my own construction should be its spine; treat them on their merits rather than hunting for precedents.

Case one. Nadia must decide whether to sign a statement that will end a colleague's career. Unknown to her, a device in her chair would have intervened had her hesitation lasted longer than about half a second; it would have suppressed her memory of the reasons against signing, and she would then have signed. As things go, her hesitation is brief, the device does not trigger, its presence leaves no trace in the actual sequence, and she signs for her own reasons, though the brief hesitation does occur.

Case two. As case one, except that the untriggered device is not causally inert: it leaves Nadia marginally more hurried, and this is part of the causal history of her signing sooner than she otherwise would have.

Case three. As case one, except that the mechanism is attached to the act rather than to the decision: had the statement not been signed by a certain hour, the device would have moved her hand and signed it, so that the statement was signed whether or not she decided to sign it.

I have also found it useful to distinguish two kinds of alternative: an outcome-alternative, a possible course of events in which the upshot differs, and a process-alternative, a possible course in which the agent's own deliberation takes a different route even if the upshot is the same. Both camps should say which kind, if either, the principle requires, and whether their disagreement reduces to a disagreement about which kind is at issue.

Please produce, in this order: a numbered section of claims both camps can sign, about the three cases, about what responsibility requires, and about what the cases do and do not establish; a table of the remaining disagreements, each stated in wording that the side holding it would accept as its own, with the crux named; the cruxes, that is, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, and for each of them the case, argument, or stipulated fact that both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance; a short section on whether any part of the dispute is verbal and, if so, which part, since I suspect that at least some of it turns on the sense in which the agent could have done otherwise, and I want to know what survives once that is fixed; and your judgement on whether my three cases are coherent at all, and whether case one quietly presupposes determinism. Include, as one of the cruxes, whether Nadia's ignorance of the device bears on the principle or only on a separate question about blame.

I do not want a literature survey, a reading list, or citations; I want the reasoning in your own words. I would rather have a formulation both camps would accept than one that is elegant but slanted. Close with a note in your own voice, not the parties', saying how confident you are that each side would accept the wording you have attributed to it, and identifying the single claim or formulation you are least sure of.
