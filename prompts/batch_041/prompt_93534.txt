I'm a first-year PhD student and my reading group has gotten itself stuck on the problem of logical omniscience — you know, the fact that in the standard possible-worlds treatments of knowledge and belief, an agent automatically knows every logical truth and every logical consequence of what she knows. I get why that's embarrassing if you want to model actual thinkers. But my office-mate keeps brushing the whole complaint off and I can't tell whether he's right or just being lazy.

His line goes like this. It's like frictionless planes in physics, or ideal gases. Nobody thinks a real plane has zero drag; that's not what the model is for. The idealization strips the messy stuff away so you can see the structure, and then you add the mess back in as a correction. So, he says, complaining that your epistemic logic is logically omniscient is like complaining that Galilean mechanics ignores air resistence. And the people who go on about resource-bounded agents and awareness and so forth are, in his words, mistaking the map for the territory.

I want you to take the role of a philosopher of logic / formal epistemologist who knows this debate inside out, and walk me through whether that comparison can actually carry the weight he puts on it. Think carefully before you commit to anything, and start by just brainstorming the candidate correspondences — what plays the role of the plane, the friction, the model's predictions, the act of adding friction back — plus the places where the mapping seems to strain. I don't need a reading list or citations, I just want the arguement.

Then give me:

1. The correspondence table, spelled out, so I can see exactly what is supposed to map onto what.
2. Where the mapping breaks — and then, separately, which of those breaks actually matter for the use he's putting it to (dismissing the objection as naive) versus which are just harmless imprecision.
3. What the frictionless-plane picture quietly smuggles in about what a good idealization is — and whether logical omniscience really has the features it's smuggling in.
4. Whether some version of his defence survives. I don't want to end up at "idealizations are bad," which is a bad objection, so tell me what a defensible version would have to concede.
5. What analogy you'd reach for instead, if you wanted one that was honest about what the idealization does and doesn't preserve.

I beleive the key is somewhere in the difference between idealizing away a nuisance and idealizing away the thing you're trying to study, but I can't make that sharp on my own, so please make it sharp for me.
