I am putting together a long-form interview for a graduate workshop in formal epistemology, and my guest is a philosopher-mathematician who has spent a decade defending the logical induction program as the correct account of logical uncertainty. Her position, as I understand it, runs like this: an agent who is uncertain about what follows from what should be modeled as assigning prices to the sentences of a formal language, where those prices are set by a market of computationally bounded traders who buy and sell sentences as proofs and evidence accumulate over discrete stages; the standard the prices must meet is that no efficiently computable trader can make unbounded profit against them; and out of that single constraint come the results the program advertises, namely prices that converge to 1 on theorems and 0 on contradictions, coherence that holds in the limit rather than at any stage, and a limited form of self-trust in the agent's own future reasoning.

I want ten questions for her that go straight at the content of that view rather than at its motivation, and none that could be asked of any formal epistemologist. At least one should press on whether logical uncertainty is uncertainty in the same sense that empirical uncertainty is: whether the market's prices really are her credences, or whether calling them credences is a way of sidestepping the deflationary reply that a rational agent is simply certain of every logical truth and should be modeled as such. At least one should press on the choice of adversary: why efficiently computable traders are the right class against which prices must be defensible, whether the polynomial-time bound is doing real work or is an artifact of the proof, and what remains of the Dutch-book argument's normative force once the traders are resource-bounded rather than logically omniscient. At least one should press on self-reference and self-trust: what exactly the framework's limited self-trust result delivers, how it survives the Gödelian limits on an agent reasoning about its own conclusions, and whether the residue of untrustworthiness is enough to spoil the practical payoff. At least one should press on the gap between limit properties and usable advice: the fact that coherence and convergence are asymptotic, that the framework licenses no rule of inference the agent can actually apply, and that the agent must sometimes hold prices it knows to be wrong in order to learn. At least one should press on whether the account repairs the Bayesian picture or replaces it, and what it says about the idealization of logical omniscience that it rejects. At least one should press on scope: which formal language and which logic the account fixes, what becomes of an agent that is uncertain about its own inferential rules, and whether the account can say anything at all about a reasoner who does not yet know which formal system is right. At least one should press on failure conditions: what would have to be true for the program to count as refuted, or as an interesting dead end.

For each question, supply the follow-up that assumes her most likely answer, especially where that answer is the kind that relocates the difficulty rather than resolving it, and make each follow-up harder to absorb than the one before. Close with a single question she cannot answer by widening the scope of the program.

Do not hand me a menu. Commit to one arc and one ordering, and tell me briefly why you chose the questions you did over the ones you rejected; if you think one of the emphases I have listed is misplaced, substitute your own and say why, but make the call yourself rather than leaving it to me. Write in American English.
