Ok I need help nailing down what I actually think about money pumps, because my gut is all over the place here and I suspect my verdicts don't hang together.

The setup. Someone has cyclic preferences over three options. Concretely: three apartments, and her pairwise preferences run A over B, B over C, but C over A. This isn't as crazy as it sounds -- it's the kind of thing that happens when a choice has several attributes (rent, commute, size) and a different attribute dominates in each pairwise comparison. No single attribute ordering; the comparisons just dont add up.

Now a trader can walk her around the loop. She holds A. The trader offers to swap her into C for a small fee, and since she prefers C she takes it. Then offers B for C plus a fee; she prefers B, takes it. Then A for B plus a fee; she prefers A, takes it. She's back in A with less money, and the whole thing repeats.

Here are my verdicts, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one:

1. Nobody ever offers her a trade. She lives her whole life with these preferences and never makes a single swap. Is she irrational? My gut: her preferences are defective somehow, but I can't find one act of hers that's irrational. Then again I'd say someone with contradictory beliefs is irrational even if she never acts on them, so maybe that's not a real defense.

2. The trader shows up and she goes around the loop once. Every individual swap, taken alone, is exactly what her preferences tell her to do -- she prefers the thing she's swapping into. My gut: she's being irrational, but I can't point at which swap is the mistake, since by her own lights none of them is.

3. She sees the whole thing coming and turns down the first swap -- keeps A instead of getting C, which she prefers. My gut: obviously rational, even a little admirable. But notice she's now acting against her preferences on purpose. So "does what she most prefers" can't be the standard of rationality, or this case is a counterexample to it.

4. She hands her wallet to a friend and says never let me make these trades. She'll regret it later. My gut: rational. Feels like the same move as (3), just outsourced.

5. Different person, perfectly transitive preferences, but time-inconsistent: at every moment she prefers the immediate small reward, and from a distance she prefers the big later one, and a staircase of moment-by-moment choices walks her into an outcome she'd have rejected up front. My gut: this feels different from cases 1-4, but I can't say why. Is it that she's weak rather than incoherent? Or is that a distinction without a difference?

6. New one that's bugging me. Two people, each perfectly transitive on their own. They share decisions -- they alternate who holds the household budget -- and the pair's effective choices cycle, so an outsider can pump the household. Neither person's preferences cycle. My gut: neither one is irrational, and yet the pair is pumpable. So pumpability and intransitivity come apart, and neither one seems to be the thing that's actually wrong. Or maybe the pair is irrational as a unit and I should just say so.

7. And this one: the trader is a charity that spends the fees on bed nets, she knows it, and she cycles on purpose, "donating" through the pump. Gut: still irrational. The money just happens to land somewhere good. Which suggests rationality is about the structure of the preferences and not the outcome -- but then what does case (3) show, where acting against the structure is the rational move?

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? I keep cycling between: (a) you're irrational iff your preferences are cyclic, (b) you're irrational iff you're pumpable / take the bait, (c) you're irrational iff you fail to stick to a plan. Each one seems to save some verdicts and trash others. Which do I have to give up, and what does your preferred principle say about a case I haven't listed -- someone with cyclic preferences who owns nothing and has no money, so she literally can't ever be pumped? Irrational or not?

And be straight with me about what's actually settled here versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. I don't want a confident answer that's secretly just one camp's line dressed up as the consensus.
