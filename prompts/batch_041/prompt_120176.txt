I have agreed to referee a draft chapter on the interpretation of probability for an undergraduate philosophy-of-science textbook, and before I file my report I want a second opinion on the chapter's opening survey of the field. Its substance is this:

"There are three main interpretations of probability. The frequentist holds that a probability is the long-run relative frequency of an outcome in a sequence of trials — the probability that a coin lands heads is the limiting proportion of heads as the number of tosses grows without bound, and the law of large numbers guarantees that this limit exists. The subjectivist holds that a probability is simply a degree of belief; because beliefs differ from person to person, any assignment of probabilities is as legitimate as any other, so long as the believer is internally consistent. The propensity theorist holds that probability is a real physical disposition of a chance setup to produce a given outcome, and that propensity theory, alone among the three, treats probabilities as objective features of the world. The first two interpretations each face a fatal objection: the frequentist cannot make sense of single-case probabilities, and the subjectivist cannot explain why observed frequencies converge on the values that scientists report. Propensity theory escapes both objections and is for that reason the interpretation most philosophers of science now accept."

Parts of this I would sign my name to, and I do not want to write a report that treats it as uniformly careless. Please audit it claim by claim and sort the claims into three piles: those a philosopher of science working on foundations would accept as stated; those that are defensible simplifications of the sort a textbook may legitimately make, together with what each simplification costs; and those that are false. I would particularly like your judgment on whether the two "fatal" objections are in fact fatal, and if they are, to which member of each family they are fatal — the three labels the chapter uses are not monolithic, and I want my report to be clear about which version of a view is under attack at each step. Tell me, too, whether the chapter's closing recommendation follows from the parts of the summary that survive, and whether a course built on this three-way division would leave students unable to place interpretations that fit none of the three. Finish by giving me the cleanest accurate version of the contrast the chapter is reaching for, phrased so that I can quote it back to the author.
