I keep reading about hinge epistemology and I'm not convinced it's doing any real work, so I want to see it run on an actual case instead of the usual Moore's-hands stuff. Use continental drift. Not Wegener's own case, which I already know (the coastlines fitting, the same fossils and rock sequences turning up on both sides of the South Atlantic), but the long stretch afterwards, roughly from the 1920s to the mid 1960s, when the establishment treated the permanence of the ocean basins as the kind of thing you don't even argue about, and when the standard move was to explain the fit of the continents with land bridges and sunken continents rather than admit they had ever moved.

A few things I want out of this.

Start with the mapping, and be strict about it. What were the actual hinge candidates here -- the permanence of the basins, the rigidity of the mantle, the idea that physics gets to say what geology is allowed to posit -- and which of them was functioning as a hinge rather than as an ordinary well-supported belief? Who held it, and in what way? I want the hinge-versus-entrenched-theory distinction made as sharply as the case allows, not waved at.

Then the complication, because I dont want a morality play. The fixists' objection to Wegener was not stupid: the forces he appealed to really were far too small to move continents, and everyone could see that. So the interesting move is the one from his mechanism being inadequate to drift not happening at all. Is that move a hinge being held fast, or just an ordinary bad inference that a better physicist would have avoided? Say which, and say what hangs on it.

Then the other end. What actually broke it open in the sixties was seafloor spreading and the magnetic stripes on the ocean floor -- new instruments, a new subfield, people with no stake in the older synthesis -- and acceptance was quick once that happened. Argument had failed for forty years and then a magnetometer did the job. Does the hinge picture predict that, or does it just redescribe it? And why did the drifters' arguments fail if the evidence was sitting there the whole time?

Now the part I actually care about, which is where the theory breaks. Hinges are supposed to be held fast, exempt from doubt, not the sort of thing you can be argued out of, and on one reading not even truth-apt. Here is a hinge-shaped commitment that got revised, and that was, as it turned out, false. The riverbed-and-river image seems to anticipate exactly this, so tell me whether the case is a counterexample or just an illustration -- and if the riverbed can shift, what is left of the claim that hinges are held fast? I also want the normative question answered rather than dodged: were the fixist geologists unreasonable, or merely wrong? If the view can't say, does it end up licensing dogmatism, and is the word hinge just a sociological label for whatever the powerful members of a practice happen to assume?

Related, and be honest: what does hinge epistemology say here that Kuhn, Lakatos or Quine doesn't? If the answer is nothing, say so, and say which of them the case actually fits better. And run it the other way too -- what does the episode show about the theory? A confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? Its home examples are first-person and everyday, and its doubters are madmen and missionaries; a research community defending a background assumption for fifty years may be a different kind of thing entirely, in which case maybe the theory has no jurisdiction here and the honest verdict is that it is silent.

Finish by taking a side: either fixism was never a hinge, in which case say why and what that costs the view, or it was a hinge and the theory needs amending, in which case say how. I dont need citations, I know roughly where the literature sits; I want the argument.
