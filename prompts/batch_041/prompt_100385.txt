I'm putting together a handout for the second meeting of a reading group on the "wrong kind of reasons" problem -- the puzzle about why a promise of money can be a reason to say you believe something but not a reason to believe it, and why the same seems to go for desires, admiration, anger and the rest. What I want from you is a decision tree that starts from a fairly innocent commitment and ends at a specific position about what makes a reason the right kind, so people can find out which view they actually hold instead of trading labels. Start the tree at the question everyone has an opinion about -- whether there's a genuine, principled divide between reasons that bear on an attitude 'from the inside' and reasons that only bear on having the attitude -- and then branch on the real disagreements: is the divide fixed by the content of the attitude or by the state of holding it; if by the state, is it because of the attitude's constitutive correctness conditions, because of the role the consideration can play in reasoning that concludes in the attitude, because of the causal history that would make the attitude track the truth, or because of the value of having the attitude at all; and if by the content, whether that means reasons for attitudes are always reasons for the object to have some property (so the value of the attitude is derivative) or whether some attitudes are answerable to reasons in their own right. Each branch should be phrased as a question a person could answer without knowing where it leads, and each leaf should name the position and give one sentence on the commitment that costs it the most.

Couple of constraints, since I don't want a generic map. I need at least six leaves, and I want the state-based views seperated from each other, because in the group last time everything collapsed into 'object-given vs state-given' and that's exactly the distinction I find unhelpful on its own. Also please build it from the logical space first -- the combinations of answers the questions allow -- and then tell me which combinations nobody actually occupies, and whether that's because the view is incoherent or just nobody's bothered. And there's one case I want the tree to handle explicitly: a reason to be grateful to someone that comes from the fact that being grateful would make you a nicer person, versus a reason to be grateful that comes from what they actually did for you. I have a hunch the 'deliberative role' criterion and the 'object' criterion give different verdicts there, so if your tree can't tell those two apart at that leaf, flag it rather than fudging it. The people using this are grad students who've read some but not all of the literature, so no citations needed -- describe each position by what it's committed to, not by who holds it.
