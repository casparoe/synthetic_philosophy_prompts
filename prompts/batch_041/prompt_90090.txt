My two roomates got into a fight at the kitchen table last night and I honestly couldn't tell who was making sense, so I want you to sort it out move by move.

Background: Dan has gotten deep into panpsychism — the view that experience, or "something it's like to be," goes all the way down, so even an electron has some sliver of inner life. Maya thinks that's either false or empty, but that's not even what they were fighting about. They were fighting about what follows for ethics.

(1) Dan said: if panpsychism is true, then physics is stuffed with tiny subjects, and any experience has *some* feel to it — so some of it must feel good or bad, at least a little. Which means a rock weathering, a river running, me stirring my coffee, are all events in which vast numbers of little subjects are being helped or hurt. He said this makes ethics unrecognizably bigger — the moral stakes of literally any physical action are astronomical — and we're all monsters without knowing it.

(2) Maya said that skips a step. There's a difference between having experience and having *interests* — between a state that's merely felt and a state that's good or bad *for* someone. Suffering isn't just a flavor of experience; it needs a subject that can be better or worse off, a feeler whose condition can be thwarted. A micro-experience might be a bare glow with no preference attached, in which case there's no one there to harm, and panpsychism about experience is simply silent on whether anything matters morally.

(3) Dan came back: but that just relocates the mystery. If micro-experiences are all valence-free, where does *our* pain come from? You can't get a bad-feeling whole by adding up neutral parts. So either valence is a basic feature that shows up at every level, or Maya owes an account of how it pops into existence only up here — which sounds like exactly the "emergence" move materialists get mocked for.

(4) Maya: that's the combination problem, and it cuts her way, not his. Nobody knows how micro-experiences would combine into one unified subject in the first place; maybe what makes a state count as pleasure or pain is precisely that it gets represented by a system that models its own condition and can treat some states as things to seek or avoid. On that picture, valence needs a certain kind of organization, not just experience, and an electron doesn't have it. Dan said fine, but we don't *know* that, and if there's even a small chance that fundamental physics is full of suffering, the expected amount of it is so vast it should dominate everything we do. Maya said you can't multiply a probability by a harm whose very existence is the thing under dispute, and that if you're allowed to do that there's no action you could ever justify.

Then they both looked at me and I said I'd think about it. Can you go through it step by step and tell me who's right at each move — maybe rate each of the four from clearly mistaken to clearly right — and what each of them is missing? I don't want a verdict on panpsychism itself, just on the argument as it actually went.
