I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on war and the ethics of political violence — roughly sixteen students, a mix of philosophy and international-relations backgrounds — and I have one 90-minute session to spend on nuclear deterrence. I would rather the students play their way into the subject's central paradox than hear me state it, so please design an in-class game for me.

The paradox I want them to feel has two halves. The first is a credibility problem: a threat to escalate that stays entirely under the threatener's rational control is hollow, because at the moment of decision carrying it out will not be worth the cost, both sides can foresee this, and so the threat deters no one. The second half is the cure and its price: the only way to make such a threat credible without simply lying is to hand part of the decision to something outside one's own control — a randomiser, a subordinate who might act on their own, an automatic trigger, an ally who might run — and this is precisely what makes the situation dangerous, because the same surrender of control that gives the threat its force can also deliver the catastrophe that neither side wanted. If it emerges naturally, I would also like them to notice a corollary: once the top of the escalation ladder is made unthinkable for both sides, the contest simply migrates downward, so the lower rungs grow less stable exactly as the top grows safer.

Please provide the full design, with the following:

- The setting is the 90-minute session described above; assume only a whiteboard, a table, and cheap materials.
- Roles for at least two opposed teams plus a third role for whoever administers chance and accidents, so that the randomness is visibly not under either team's thumb.
- An escalation ladder of four to six rungs with a payoff scheme in chips or points (no real money) whose numbers actually generate the intended dynamics. Show the payoff table and check the arithmetic, including that conceding and matching are each sometimes attractive, so the threat is genuinely doubtful rather than transparently empty.
- A first phase in which the defender's announced threat to match fails to deter and the challenger climbs the ladder.
- A second phase in which the defender is given some concrete way to cede control — a die, a delegate, a tripwire, an automatic response rule — and the students watch deterrence return, at the price of a real and unowned probability of disaster.
- Either a third phase or a debrief move that brings out the corollary about the top of the ladder becoming safe while the bottom becomes dangerous.
- A timeline for the 90 minutes covering set-up, the phase or phases, and a debrief long enough to do the work.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the two halves of the paradox and the corollary, with the questions to put to the room, and that withholds the names of the ideas until the end.
- An honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students playing the person rather than the position, teams colluding, the arithmetic throwing up a dominant strategy, the chance mechanism feeling arbitrary — and how to handle each.
- A frank statement of what this format cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself: for instance the epistemic fog of a real crisis, the moral weight of the actual weapons, or the difference between a threat made credible by genuine irrationality and one made credible by chance.

Do not simply dress up the standard game of chicken; I want the mechanics themselves to carry the idea, so that the students run into the credibility problem and its remedy through the rules rather than through anything I say. No citations or reading list needed — I will add those myself.
