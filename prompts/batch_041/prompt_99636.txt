For a few years in my twenties I tried to improve myself by the method Franklin set out for himself: draw up a short list of virtues, keep a daily record, and mark each evening the occasions on which one had fallen short of each. I have no training in philosophy and am not asking for reading, only for help in identifying something. The exercise left me with a puzzle I have never been able to put a name to, and I would like to know whether there is an established term or literature for it, or whether I must invent one.

What I noticed was not simply that I failed, which I expected. It was that aiming directly at certain items on the list made them worse rather than better. Humility is the clearest case: to ask myself each evening whether I had been humble that day is already a kind of preening, so I would close the book humble on paper and vain in fact. Contentment behaved the same way — resolving to be content is the surest route to noticing what one lacks. Other items, such as spontaneity, or simply enjoying things, seemed impossible to pursue at all, since the moment I made them a target they ceased to be what they were.

A second thing, which I think is distinct, and which is the part I most want a name for, is that the successes generated faults that were not on the list, so the list could never be finished. Noticing my own humility bred pride, and pride had no column, so I added one; keeping score of pride made me self-absorbed, which then demanded a column of its own; and so on, each round of improvement producing a fresh defect of a kind the previous list had no room for. I could have gone on adding lines indefinitely without ever reaching a state in which the book was closed. There was a quieter version of the same trouble: the items interfered with one another, so that scrupulous attention to one — keeping the record honestly, say — consumed the time and attention another required.

I suspect I am circling several different things at once, and that some of them have names while the one I care about does not. The paradox of hedonism seems nearby, and so does the general observation that certain goods can be had only as by-products of pursuing something else; but neither seems to capture the part about the list having to grow, and about the very act of recording an improvement producing a new item of a kind the original scheme could not have anticipated. I am also unsure whether the whole project was self-defeating from the start or merely unfinished, and whether that distinction is itself a known one.

Please tell me what the nearest established concepts are, how precisely the thing I have described differs from each of them, and, if nothing fits, coin a term and define it — including its edges: which virtues or projects it applies to, which it does not, and what would count as a borderline case. A bare label would not help me; I am trying to work out exactly what the phenomenon is and what it is not. Please also be explicit about how much of this is settled and how much is genuinely disputed among people who work on it — I would rather see the disagreements than be handed a single tidy answer, and please do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
