The examination question was the following:

*Explain the distinction between rigid and non-rigid designators. Using one identity case and one natural-kind case, set out what a necessary a posteriori truth is supposed to be and why these examples are thought to support the existence of such truths. Finally: does the existence of necessary a posteriori truths refute the analytic/synthetic distinction? Defend your answer.*

The student's answer, reproduced in full, was:

"A rigid designator is a term that picks out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists; a non-rigid designator picks out different objects in different worlds. Proper names are the paradigm case of rigid designators, whereas definite descriptions such as 'the teacher of Alexander' are typically non-rigid, since someone other than the actual teacher could have taught Alexander.

The necessary a posteriori is a truth that could not have been otherwise but that we can only come to know through experience. The standard example is the identity 'Hesperus is Phosphorus.' Both names are rigid: each picks out the same object, Venus, in every world. Since they designate the same object, and an identity between two rigid designators of the same object holds in every world, the statement is necessary. Yet we could only discover that the evening star and the morning star are one and the same heavenly body by astronomical observation, so the statement is known a posteriori. The general moral is that necessity and a priority come apart: there are truths that are necessary but not knowable independently of experience.

The natural-kind case is Putnam's. Putnam, who introduced the notion of the rigid designator, extends it to natural-kind terms: 'water' is rigid, picking out the same substance in every world, namely whatever substance actually has the relevant underlying nature. Since that nature turned out to be H2O, the statement 'water is H2O' is necessary, even though it was discovered empirically. And because 'water' is rigid, statements such as 'water boils at 100 degrees Celsius' are necessary as well, since they concern the very substance water and could not have concerned anything else.

Does all this refute the analytic/synthetic distinction? I do not think so. What the examples show is that the traditional equation of the necessary with the a priori, and of the a priori with the analytic, cannot be maintained. An analytic truth is one that is true in virtue of meaning alone, and the necessary a posteriori truths are not of that sort -- they are true in virtue of how the world is. So the analytic/synthetic distinction survives, although we must abandon the idea that analyticity and necessity always coincide. What does fall is the thought that all identities are trivial: 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is an identity statement, and since all true identity statements are necessary, its necessity is unremarkable -- the only surprising thing is that we needed telescopes to find it out."
