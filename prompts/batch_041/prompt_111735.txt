ok, so I'm a fourth-year phil of science student and I've been keeping a running list of cases where I think the principle of indifference gives a right answer, and cases where I think it just hands you a convention, and the list has stopped looking like it belongs to one person. Can you tell me what principle, if any, generates my verdicts — and then tell me which verdicts I have to give up? Here's the list.

1. Box of dice from a factory known to make fair dice to tight tolerances. I pull one out, haven't rolled it. 1/6 on each face feels forced, not stipulated — it's in the manufacturing.
2. Different box, from a batch known to be weighted, but I know nothing about how. I don't think there's any fact about the right credence for the 6, and I'm suspicious of anyone who just hands me one.
3. A point marked "at random" on a sphere, no other info. I think uniform over the surface is forced. The thing is perfectly symmetric, there's no other structure in the problem, and I can't see what else there is to go on.
4. The chord in a circle (you know the one — 1/3, 1/4, 1/2 depending how you do it). No right answer, and I get annoyed at "the circle is symmetric, so it's settled."
5. Cube factory: I'm told every cube has side between 1 and 2 cm and nothing else. No fact of the matter about P(side < 1.5).
6. But if I'm told the cubes are cut to a target side length with error uniform on the target, then P(side < 1.5) is settled. Fine.
7. Location-scale: unknown quantity = fixed center plus error, whole setup invariant under shifting the center and rescaling everything, zero mechanism info. I think that does pin the prior down to the 1/sigma-type thing, and that it's pinned down by the invariance rather than by a choice I'm making.
8. Coin known to be biased, three heads in a row. I do not think there's a determinate credence for the next toss — Laplace's 4/5 and the other standard answers all look like choices to me, not discoveries.

So 1 and 6 are mechanism cases and I'm fine with those. 2, 5, 8 are no-fact-of-the-matter for me. The interesting ones are 3 and 7, where I think pure symmetry forces a prior with no mechanism at all. My toy version of a principle was: indifference is licensed exactly when the unknown is a coordinate on a space that some group acts on transitively, and the prior is the unique measure that group leaves alone; downstream quantities that are functions of that unknown just inherit whatever they inherit. I like it because it makes 3 and 7 forced (sphere is homogeneous; the center-and-scale pair is homogeneous under shifts and rescalings) and 4 and 5 unforced (the space of chords isn't homogeneous — rotations fix the distance from the center, so the unknown is really a pair and only one factor is symmetric; and the interval (1,2) has no nontrivial group of transformations preserving it). But it also feels like I designed the principle around the verdicts.

A friend says I should drop all of that and go with something like: indifference is only ever licensed by local facts about the actual setup — the mint's tolerances, the cutting machine, the flips — and never by symmetry in the abstract, since a symmetry argument is only ever shorthand for an unsaid fact about a mechanism. On that view 3 and 7 are me fooling myself, and the sphere case is really just me having tacitly assumed the marking procedure was rotationally symmetric. I dont want to belive this, but I can't articulate why not, other than that 3 feels different from 2.

What I want:

- One principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't thought of.
- For each clause, the case in my list that forces it.
- What it predicts for a case that's not on the list: a rigid body whose orientation in space is "random" — is uniform over the rotations forced or not, and does your answer come from the same clause as the sphere, or from a different one?
- And an honest verdict on which of my eight has to go. If the only principle that saves 3 and 7 is so baroque that it also makes 4 and 5 come out forced, say so — that's a reductio for me and I'd rather hear it.
