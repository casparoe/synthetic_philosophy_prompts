I'm in a small interpretability reading group and I've fallen down a rabbit hole on mechanistic anomaly detection — the setup where you've got a mechanistic story about what a model does on the inputs you've checked, and you want to flag the inputs where it's probably running some *other* mechanism you don't understand, so you stop trusting the story there. What's eating me is that the whole thing seems to presuppose an answer to a question I can't find stated anywhere: when are two mechanisms the *same* mechanism? One person in the group says just define it operationally and move on, but I want the concept sorted first, so I've been jotting down my gut verdicts on cases and they won't line up. I'd like you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, is actually behind them, and where I have to give something up. Tentatively I've been assuming two mechanisms are the same iff there's a structure-preserving map between their computational graphs (some kind of isomorphism), but I already know that's too strict in one place and maybe too loose in another.

Here are the cases. (1) A circuit in a tiny transformer that does a greater-than comparison, and the "same" circuit in another model trained from a different random seed — same wiring, same algorithm, different numbers in the weights. Gut: same mechanism. (2) That comparison implemented by attention heads in one model and by an MLP block in another — identical input-output behavior on everything I test, completely different computation. Gut: not the same mechanism. But this bugs me, because in both (1) and (2) I've got "same behavior, different low-level details" and I'm calling one same and one different. (3) The same circuit appearing twice, in parallel, in one model, not interacting — gut says that's the same mechanism as the single copy, just instantiated twice; but if the two copies feed into each other in a little loop, gut says that's a *new* mechanism, not the old one twice. So multiplicity seems free but interaction isn't, and I can't state that cleanly. (4) A circuit with one neuron that provably never fires on any input I've tried — same mechanism as the version without it? Gut: yes, same. (5) A "cleaned up" sparse version of a circuit that matches the original on the whole training distribution but goes its own way off it. Gut: different mechanism — and this is exactly the thing I want to detect, so a principle that calls it "same" is useless to me. (6) A mechanism in a mouse brain and the analogous one in a silicon model. Gut: not the same, but I notice I can only say that by appealing to substrate, and I'm suspicious of substrate answers. (7) The same computation run in reverse — a decoder versus the encoder it inverts. Gut: different mechanisms that happen to be inverses. (8) And here's the one I genuinely can't decide: a component replaced by a functionally equivalent one computed totally differently, say a ReLU swapped for a smooth curve that agrees on every input inside some bounded box. On-distribution I can't tell them apart at all, so by my case-5 reasoning maybe same, but by my case-2 reasoning different.

So: what principle gets these, or close? If none does, which verdict do I drop, and what does the surviving principle say about a case I haven't listed — a mechanism identical to another except it sits in a model where the rest of the network compensates for a small local bug, so the overall behavior is identical but the local computation differs? And is there a version of this that's actually checkable, something I could hand to someone to run, or does "same mechanism" just bottom out in my taste? Don't worry about citing papers or pointing me to a reading list, I'm after the argument itself, not a bibliography.
