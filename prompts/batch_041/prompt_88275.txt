My flatmate and I have been going round in circles about American exceptionalism for weeks now and I want someone to actually do the work instead of the two of us just trading vibes at each other over the kitchen table. The thing I keep coming back to is a pair of episodes that sit right next to each other in the same region and the same decade. In 1953 the CIA and MI6 helped push Mossadegh out of power in Iran, after he'd nationalised the oil business that the British had been running there, and the Shah ended up properly in charge instead of nominally in charge. Three years later Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal, Britain and France and Israel invaded Egypt, and it was Eisenhower who made them pull out — America telling its two oldest allies that the days of just taking other peoples stuff were over. Then the Americans moved into the gap themselves with the Eisenhower Doctrine, promising to defend the region from communism. Same region, same ten years, opposite behaviour. My flatmate says that's exceptionalism working exactly as advertised: America is different because it doesn't want colonies, it wants a free world with American leadership, and 1956 proves it. I say 1953 proves the opposite and 1956 only proves they're better at it than we were.

What I actually want from you is to take the thing apart into its separate claims rather than treating it as one blob. I've been treating it as (a) the historical thesis that America's origins and development genuinely were unlike anyone else's, (b) the normative claim that America has a mission to spread liberty, (c) the self-image, the story Americans tell about themselves, and (d) what I've started calling the exemption reading — the rules bind everyone else and America decides when they don't apply to it. Map both episodes onto those four, one at a time. Which reading explains what, which reading is quietly doing the work when someone says but we're different, and can one single reading hold both episodes together, or do you have to swap readings halfway through, which I'd say is cheating. Tell me where exceptionalism is just silent. I don't think it has anything to say about the difference between a colony and a client state, and I want to know whether that's a real gap in the view or whether I'm missing a version of it that handles the distinction — and if the honest answer is that no version does, say so instead of inventing one.

And then the part I actually care about. Is there anything that could count as evidence against this thing? Because both of us can absorb both episodes with our version of it, and that makes me suspicious. Is that a defect in the theory, or does it just mean exceptionalism was never an empirical thesis at all and is closer to a creed you either sign up to or don't — and if it's the latter, does it even make sense to ask whether it's true, or is the only real question what the creed does for the people who hold it?
