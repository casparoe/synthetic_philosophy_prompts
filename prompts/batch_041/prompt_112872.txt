So my flatmate's in a philosophy reading group and she keeps banging on about hedonism — the view that pleasure is the only thing that's good in itself and pain the only thing that's bad. I'm a backend engineer, so my first thought is always "grand, but whats the metric?". That's the story I want you to write.

Setup (change names, product names, whatever, just keep the bones): a small startup has shipped a wristband plus phone app that gives you a daily "pleasure score" out of 100 — like a step counter but for how good your day felt. Tell the story from inside the team, from the point of view of one of the engineers who has to write the scoring pipeline. That's where the trouble is. Because the stuff the band is meant to be adding up is wildly different stuff. The relief when a migraine finally lets go. The first cold beer on a hot afternoon. The tiny buzz of a unit test going green after three hours. A hot bath. Gossip you enjoy and then feel bad about. Finishing a hard run. A joke landing. Getting into a bed with fresh sheets. Winning an argument. The team has to decide what all of these have in common — what the number is actually *of* — and every option they try turns out to be a different product. Score the dopamine-ish spike and the app starts rewarding novelty and doomscrolling. Score self-reports and you're measuring what people think they ought to say about their day. Score relief from pain and you've built a pain band, not a pleasure band. And at least one person on the team definately thinks there's no fact of the matter at all: the band just picks a proxy, calls it pleasure, and that's a business decision rather than a discovery.

What I want out of it: the story should push hard on that question — is there one thing all these pleasures share, or is "pleasure" just a word we slap on a heap of very different experiences — without any character reading out a philosophy lecture or quoting anyone. Let the philosophy come through the product meetings, the bug reports, and a support ticket that makes everyone go quiet (someone whose best-scoring day by miles was the day their chronic pain finally stopped; someone else's was their wedding). Don't let it resolve too neatly, but do let it land somewhere. No real companies, no real people, no quotes or citations.

Keep it under about 1,200 words, and finish with one final line in exactly this form: TL;DR: <one sentence saying what the story ended up claiming about pleasure>.
