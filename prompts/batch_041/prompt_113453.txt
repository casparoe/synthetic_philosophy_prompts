I am a third-year undergraduate currently enrolled in a metaphysics course, and I have been asked to run one seventy-five-minute session of its peer-led tutorial on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition. I would like you to act as someone who has taught this material to undergraduates many times and has watched it misfire in predictable ways, and to plan the session for me. Think it through carefully before committing to anything: begin by working out what the session is actually trying to get the students to see, and only then decide what order the pieces have to come in. Do not explain the regress to me; I can read. What I want is the teaching plan — the sequence and why it has to be that sequence, the one example that carries the whole thing, the misconceptions the students will bring or form, and the point in the sequence at which each one has to be headed off.

The audience: eight students, all of whom have taken a first course in formal logic and have met Frege's distinction between objects and concepts, none of whom has read any metaphysics of relations, and most of whom assume that a proposition is simply a sentence together with a truth value. No readings are assigned, so they will arrive cold; I have a whiteboard and seventy-five minutes. Two habits of the standard presentation I want you to avoid. First, do not carry the session with the familiar examples — the two-place relation whose relata need a further relation to be attached to them, or the constituents of a sentence that need something to hold them together. Students can feel that regress arriving about four moves before it arrives, and they leave thinking the whole thing is an artefact of notation. Second, do not let the session become a survey of the replies — the Fregean, the Tractarian, tropes, the view that the regress is benign — before the students have felt the problem. I would rather they leave with one problem they cannot shake than five positions they can recite.

What I want instead is the example my seminar leader used to open the topic, which I have not seen anywhere else. She asks the students to consider a chess position from a middlegame. The position is not the pieces: the same pieces on the same squares in a different arrangement are a different position, and the pieces are unchanged. But neither is it the pieces together with their arrangement, because the arrangement is one more item that would itself have to be arranged with the pieces — if the arranging is an item in the position, then something must arrange the arranging with the pieces, and so on. She calls the position, understood this way, a "slipping object", and claims that this is Bradley's regress in a form no student can dismiss as a fact about language. Tell me, with reasons, whether the chess case does the work the linguistic cases do, or whether it imports a distraction; and if it does both, where exactly in the sequence it belongs and what has to be added to it.

I have guesses about what they will get wrong, and I want your ordering to absorb them: that the regress shows relations do not exist; that Bradley was a sceptic about relations; that a relation is just a set of ordered pairs and the regress therefore stops; and that an infinite regress of analysis means the proposition is infinitely complex. Tell me which of these arrives first, which are really the same mistake in different clothing, and where each should be met — and say plainly if one of my guesses is wrong. I also want your view on two dependencies in particular: whether the students need the notion of a proposition as a non-linguistic bearer of truth before the regress can even be stated, and whether the distinction between a vicious regress and a merely infinite one has to be in place before or after they feel the problem.

Give me a single plan rather than a menu of options: one order, one carrying example, one closing question that will show whether they have grasped the problem rather than learned the moves. Tell me what to leave out at this level, and to say out loud that I am leaving it out. Where you think my seminar leader's example fails, say so directly and tell me what to do in its place. I do not need further reading or references.
