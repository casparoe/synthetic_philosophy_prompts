I'm a novelist — third book, and the first one where the main character is a moral philosopher, which I'm already regretting — and I need help building one specific scene. The scene is her talking herself out of one of her own moral beliefs, in real time, on the page, without the chapter turning into a lecture. I don't want a trolley, I don't want a tidy hypothetical about a runaway train of any kind, and I dont want the standard "and then she realized evolution made her believe it" beat, because everyone's seen that. What I need is the hard direction: one evolutionary debunking argument, one agent, and it lands on one of her moral beliefs but not on the rest of her moral thinking. She drops the first belief — really drops it, it stops doing any work in how she lives — and she does not slide into "so I can't trust any of my moral faculties," and she does not undercut the very reasoning she just used to run the argument on herself. Local without global. The easy version, where the debunking sweeps away everything or where the only belief that survives is something so thin and formal ("pain is bad," "suffering is bad") that no realist ever cared about it, is boring to me; I want the surviving belief to be substantive and contested too, and of roughly the same kind as the one that falls — both about how we treat people, both things a real person could argue about, neither one more abstract than the other. Pick the pair yourself.

Constraints, because I'm reading this aloud at a workshop and there are two philosophers in the room who will pounce: no divine revelation, no non-natural causal contact between moral facts and our ancestors' brains, no group-selection hand-waving, and no assuming the agent is a saint or a monster — she should be a normal reflective person who can run this argument on herself. The selection story has to be the sort of thing a working biologist wouldn't laugh at. And if you're tempted to save the second belief by having the moral facts themselves do some of the selecting — a "third factor," the direction of the pressure being sensitive to the truth — you can use that, but only if you make it a real causal story rather than a patch, and flag it when you do. One page of prose for the case itself, no bullet points. And if you genuinely think the spec is unsatisfiable as stated — that any debunking that bites one belief this hard can't stay local — say so up front and give me the closest satisfiable thing instead, rather than fudging it.

Then walk me through it for the workshop. Give me, in this order, with these headings: "The case" (about a page, prose); "Why it lands on the first belief" (a paragraph); "Why the second one survives" (a paragraph); "The load-bearing feature" (two or three sentences, and be ruthless — I want the one thing the whole case hangs on); "Pull the feature out" (a short paragraph on what happens when I remove that thing, and specifically whether the case collapses back into the standard dilemma everyone already knows); "The best objection" (a paragraph, from whoever thinks the argument, if it works at all, works globally — the moral-fictionalist type, who'd say the belief that falls was never true in the first place, so what exactly has she lost? — plus what a realist defender would say about the belief that survives); and finally "What would change my mind" (two or three sentences, and I mean it — the specific consideration that would flip your verdict, or the step in your own reasoning you'd bet is weakest).
