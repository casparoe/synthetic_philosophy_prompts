For a 75-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar on distributive justice, with about 18 students, design a trading activity that leads them to endorse, and then to doubt, the justice of an unequal outcome they have produced themselves. Participants should begin with equal endowments and then exchange tokens under simple rules; every individual transfer must look voluntary and unobjectionable, while the aggregate distribution drifts away from equality, so that any later attempt to restore equality appears as an interference with someone's liberty rather than as a correction of an injustice. Arrange the roles so that one participant's gains come from being genuinely valued by the others and another's from a coin toss, and let the difference between being entitled to a holding and deserving it surface in play. At a point of your choosing, disclose that the initial endowments were themselves generated by an earlier, rigged round, and have the group decide what, if anything, follows for the present holdings. Provide the rules, roles, a payoff scheme that requires no money, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the contrast between historical and patterned principles of justice, identifies where the group's own reasoning reproduced the entitlement view, and states plainly which element of that view — its treatment of past injustice — the activity cannot settle and the facilitator must supply. Do not name the argument or its author until the debrief. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
