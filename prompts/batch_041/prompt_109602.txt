I chair the intangible-heritage panel of my country's national heritage council. The panel meets on 14 November to settle this year's listings; the decisions are published in the first week of December, and the next nomination window does not open until March. I need a written opinion I can circulate to the six other members in advance and read aloud at the start of the meeting. It should give one clear recommendation with a deadline and a fallback if the panel will not adopt it, and it should state the strongest case against the recommendation as its proponents would state it, not as a straw man.

The case before us is the Longnight Procession, a lantern walk through the harbour of Aldermere, a town of about nine thousand, held on the eve of the winter solstice and nominated by the town council for national listing. The nomination calls the procession immemorial, describes it as one of the last surviving examples of a national custom, and asks us to list it under the statutory test, which requires that a practice be transmitted across at least three generations and rooted in the customs of the community that keeps it.

Our researcher's report contains three findings. First, the procession in its present form dates from 1971 to 1974: a town development committee organised it to draw visitors in the off season, a local schoolteacher composed the chant that the nomination calls ancient, and the lantern design was standardised in 1979. Second, an antecedent procession of the same name is documented from about 1860 and appears to have been discontinued around 1911; nothing indicates it was performed in the sixty years between. Third, the description of the practice as a national custom first appears in a 1936 folklore survey that gathered regional practices into a single national canon; the antecedent was a local and regional affair.

Two complications. The schoolteacher is ninety-one and still living; she has told our researcher that she wrote the chant, that she is not ashamed of it, and that she would rather be named in the listing than have the town pretend the words are of unknown origin. The town council replies that amending the nomination now would embarrass the town and endanger the festival's modest grant. Separately, in the same town, a practice nobody has nominated — the blessing of the boats on the first Sunday after the herring season, documented continuously since 1888 — is now maintained by eleven people whose average age is seventy-eight, and the statute permits the panel to open a file on its own motion, though in its history the panel has never done so.

The memo should do the following, in this order. Give the recommendation in a single sentence, with the date by which the panel should act. Apply the statutory test to a practice with a sixty-year gap and a documented founding moment, saying what work the word rooted is doing and whether the test requires continuity of practice or continuity of the community's own sense of continuity. Decide what weight to give the town's belief in its own antiquity when the archival record contradicts it, and whether listing a practice whose origin is documented makes the panel a party to a false claim or merely recognises something the community has genuinely made its own. Say how the living composer's preference should figure, if at all. Address the nomination's claim that the procession is a national custom, and whether a national register should be in the business of certifying customs as national at all. Finally, give the best argument against your recommendation, in the voice of the member who will make it. One member has already said that a register which certifies a development committee's invention as national heritage discredits itself; another says the register exists to support living communities rather than to award pedigrees, and that a panel which starts auditing origins will end up protecting nothing but archives.

No literature review, no citations, no bibliography — I want a document the panel can act on, under 1,200 words. Do not spend the memo explaining that traditions are invented in general; assume the panel knows that, and show what follows from it here.
