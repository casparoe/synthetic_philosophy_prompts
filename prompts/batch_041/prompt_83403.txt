I recently read a short popular piece on value theory — I no longer have it, so I'm reconstructing the argument from memory, and what I want from you is an audit of the reconstruction rather than an answer to the question the piece poses. As best I can recall, it ran roughly like this.

'Contemporary philosophers often hold that for something to be valuable is just for it to be fitting to value it: to be worth admiring, or desiring, or caring about. This view runs straight into what is known as the wrong kind of reasons problem. Suppose an evil demon tells you that unless you admire him, he will destroy the world. You now have a reason to admire him. But the demon is not admirable — he is contemptible — so the reason you have cannot be the right sort of reason for admiration. Call these state-given reasons: they concern the consequences of having the attitude rather than features of the object. The trouble is that the analysis seems to count the demon's threat among the reasons to admire him, and therefore to imply that he is admirable, which is absurd. Since the view returns the obviously wrong answer in a case like this, it has been refuted, and most value theorists have moved on to other accounts of value. The real lesson is that value cannot be reduced to reasons, and that the puzzle is a formal version of the familiar thought that people should be admired for the right reasons — for their character rather than their wealth or their threats. The obvious repair is to restrict the analysis to object-given reasons and set the state-given ones aside, which disposes of the demon without further ado.'

I'd like a careful, sentence-by-sentence audit. Which claims would a philosopher working on this problem accept as stated, which would they wince at, and which are simply wrong? Three things I'd especially like separated out. First, whether the demon case really refutes the analysis, or whether the analysis can accommodate it — and if it can, at what cost. Second, whether the piece is quietly running together two different formulations of the view, one in terms of an attitude's fittingness and one in terms of the existence of reasons for an attitude, and whether the demon case bears equally on both. Third, whether the proposed repair is as cheap as the piece makes it sound, or whether it presupposes exactly the distinction it is supposed to establish.

I'm also unsure how much of the argument survives changes to the case, and I'd like you to think through the variations rather than gesture at them. Suppose the demon threatens to destroy the world unless you want to admire him, or unless you believe he is admirable, or unless you swallow a pill that makes you admire him. Does the piece's reasoning apply uniformly across these, or does it matter which attitude is at stake and which causal route to it is on offer — and does the answer to that tell against the piece's tidy contrast between reasons that concern the object and reasons that concern the state of having the attitude? I'm inclined to think the piece is basically right, since the demon case does look like a knockout, but I'd rather find out where the soft spots are before I repeat any of this to anyone.
