Write an essay on a controversy about iterated dominance reasoning in games, taking the following case as the fixed point of reference and arguing from the case itself rather than surveying what has been said about it.

        C1      C2      C3
R1    (3, 2)  (2, 2)  (2, 1)
R2    (3, 1)  (2, 2)  (1, 2)
R3    (1, 2)  (2, 1)  (3, 1)

The row player chooses a row, the column player a column, and in each cell the row player's payoff is given first.

Four features of the case matter. First, no strategy of either player is strictly dominated by any pure or mixed strategy of that player. Read dominance strictly and the procedure eliminates nothing, leaving the three pure-strategy equilibria standing: (R1,C1) at payoffs (3,2), (R1,C2) at (2,2), and (R2,C2) at (2,2). Second, there is exactly one weak dominance on each side: R2 is weakly dominated by R1, and C3 is weakly dominated by C2. Third, both rest on a tie. R1 matches R2 against C1 and C2 and beats it only against C3; C2 matches C3 against R2 and R3 and beats it only against R1. Fourth, the two deletions interact: R2 is weakly dominated by R1 only because of what R1 does at C3, and C3 is weakly dominated by C2 only because of what C2 does at R1.

Now iterate, in two orders. Delete R2 first: in the reduced game C3 is strictly dominated by C1, so delete C3; R3 is then weakly dominated by R1, so delete R3; what remains is R1 against C1 and C2, which the column player cannot tell apart, both giving it 2, and which the row player ranks 3 against 2, so the outcome is (R1,C1) at (3,2), with (R1,C2) surviving only through the final tie. Delete C3 first: R3 is then weakly dominated by R2, so delete R3; C1 is then weakly dominated by C2, so delete C1; what remains is C2 against R1 and R2, which the row player cannot tell apart, so the outcome is the set of two cells (R1,C2) and (R2,C2), at (2,2) each. The two orders share only the column player's payoff. The first deletes (R1,C2) and (R2,C2); the second deletes (R1,C1). Deleting R2 first turns C3's weak dominance into a strict one and drives the procedure to a single cell. Deleting C3 first removes the only column that made R2 dominated, so R2 survives, and a fresh dominance, C2 over C1, appears in its place.

Weigh the case for weak-dominance reasoning against the case against it, and reach a verdict. Take a definite position on each of the following, and say how your positions fit together.

1. Whether the order-dependence is fatal. If it is, say what should replace the procedure and what your replacement yields here; a verdict that nothing is eliminated, leaving all three equilibria standing, is a substantive answer rather than an evasion. If it is not fatal, either defend one order as privileged, saying what privileges it, or propose a repair and defend it as more than a stipulation. Repairs worth considering, though not necessarily adopting: delete a strategy only if every admissible order deletes it; delete a strategy only if it is never a best response to a belief confined to the strategies that survive; replace weak dominance by dominance with a margin, so that a strategy is deleted only when it is beaten by some fixed amount.

2. What notion of rationality the procedure presupposes, and whether the iteration is legitimate. The pressure point is whether the second round can be justified at all when the comparisons it uses are manufactured by the first round's deletions. If a player is rational only in the sense of never playing a weakly dominated strategy, what does that tell anyone about the second round? A verdict is wanted on whether iterated weak dominance is a fixed point of the reasoning it attributes to the players, or a procedure whose later steps are smuggled in.

3. Whether a dominance that rests on a tie can be a reason to delete. Both first-round deletions rest on ties, and so does the last step of the first order. If ties do not count, say what the procedure yields here. If they do count, say whether exact equality and an arbitrarily small strict advantage must be treated alike, and what becomes of the case under a perturbation of the payoffs smaller than any plausible unit of measurement: raise the row player's payoff at (R2,C3) from 1 to 2, and R2 is no longer dominated at all, only the second order remains available, and the procedure's answer flips to (2,2).

4. Whether the dominance test should range over mixed strategies. In this case mixtures add nothing, since nothing is strictly dominated even by a mixture, but in general a strategy can be strictly dominated by a mixture that no pure strategy strictly dominates. Say whether rationality requires avoiding what a mixture strictly dominates, and whether that widens or narrows the work the elimination procedure has to do.

End with a single verdict — the procedure as it stands, a named repair, or its abandonment in favour of strict dominance — together with what it implies about the rational outcome of this game, and one sentence on what would have to be true for the verdict to be mistaken.
