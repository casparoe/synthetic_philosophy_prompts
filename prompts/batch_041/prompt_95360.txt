I am a software engineer with no formal training in economics or philosophy. I have been helping run a small internal seminar on measurement and decision-making at my company, and I have been asked to grade the written take-home answers. The question was set by the seminar organizer, not by me, and it concerns a proposal that came out of one of our product teams. I would like you to assess the one answer reproduced below, and to give me a grade together with the specific comments that justify it.

The take-home question was:

"Suppose a product team wants to make welfare claims about its users. For each user, the team logs the options that were available on each occasion and the option that the user selected. The team now proposes the following: define the user's utility function to be the real-valued function recovered from that log by the standard revealed-preference construction; treat differences in the values of that function as differences in how well off the user is; and, where two users' functions are on different scales, normalize each of them to the unit interval so that the two can be compared. Assess this proposal, addressing in particular:

(a) What do logs of this kind constrain, and what do they leave open?

(b) The logs also contain patterns of this kind: for one user, the option 'small' is selected when the menu is {small, medium}, while 'medium' is selected when the menu is {small, medium, large}. Does this pattern undermine the idea that the user's behaviour can be represented by a utility function at all? Explain.

(c) Can two users' recovered functions be compared by normalizing them to a common range?

(d) What is the phrase 'the user's utility' doing in the team's proposal, and does it do that work?"

The answer submitted was:

"Separating what the logs measure from what they mean is the right first move. (a) The logs record selections, and selections alone fix only an ordering of the options that were actually offered; they fix no intensity, and they say nothing at all about options that were never offered. The recovered function is therefore ordinal, and any strictly increasing transformation of it represents the same choices — which is why the team should not read its numerical output as an amount of satisfaction. (b) The menu-dependence is more serious than it first appears. Since the user selects small when medium is available, small is revealed preferred to medium; since the user then selects medium when small is available, medium is revealed preferred to small. Both verdicts cannot hold, so the user's behaviour is not represented by any utility function, and welfare claims about that user should be dropped, or made only from the subset of logs that satisfies the consistency axiom. (c) Two users' functions can be compared once they are on a common scale, and normalizing each to the unit interval puts them on one: the arbitrary scale is removed while the ordering is preserved. A user whose normalized function rises further has gained more. (d) 'The user's utility' in the proposal names what the user genuinely values; the construction is a way of measuring that underlying quantity. Where the choices are coherent, the measurement is reliable; where they are not, the measurement fails, and the failure is in the user rather than in the instrument."
