I'm a third-year biology student with a minor in philosophy, and this term I'm taking a course on the philosophy of biology while interning two days a week at our state wildlife agency. My job is to draft the status assessments that go to the review board when we propose putting a population on the protected list: I pull together the survey counts, the genetic work, the museum records and the field notes, and write the document that is supposed to say what the thing is, whether it is distinct from its nearest relatives, and whether it is in trouble. It is the first question that keeps defeating me. I have drafted four of these this quarter and there are three more in the queue before the board meets, and every one of them has run into the same wall: sometimes the field crew treats two populations as one thing because they interbreed where they meet, sometimes as two because they look different, or live differently, or have been apart long enough that nobody expects them to meet again; sometimes the geneticist says the two are plainly separate while the person who has watched them for twenty years says it is the same animal with local variation. There is a case in front of me now where population A interbreeds with B along one river, B interbreeds with C further east, and A and C never meet and by every measure we have would not breed; everyone agrees about the facts and nobody agrees about how many things we are looking at. I also have a parthenogenetic snail in another file, where half the criteria I would normally reach for do not even apply. Our director is eager to see the snail listed, and the county commissioners are just as eager that the river populations not be, which does not make the question any easier but does make it matter.

Please write me a procedure I can actually run on the next file. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops and returns "one thing" or "two things," and the points at which it hands the decision upward. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of biology has genuinely worked out about what these groupings are and are not, but keep it usable by someone with one week, an intro course, and no taxonomic training beyond that; the review board has to sign off on the document, so it also has to be defensible to people who will not read the philosophy. I would rather you commit to specific rules and defend the line you draw, telling me where it is and why there rather than why not an inch either way, than hand me a menu of schools of thought and leave the choice to me.

Then run the procedure on the two files I described, the A–B–C river case and the parthenogenetic snail. In the write-up, be explicit about the places where the procedure will force an answer I will not like, and the places where it cannot decide and I will have to argue the case myself.
