I'm a teaching assistant for an introductory social theory course, and a running argument with the friend I co-teach it with has gotten out of hand. She works on early modern Europe and treats the base–superstructure picture as a nineteenth-century curiosity, useful for shocking undergraduates and not much else; I have come around to a fairly strong version of it, though not the caricature in which every sermon and sonnet is a reflex of class interest. The version I hold is roughly this: a society's relations of production — who controls what, on what terms, and how the surplus is extracted from those who produce it — explain in the first instance why that society has the legal forms, political institutions, religious doctrines, and dominant ideas it has. The rest follows, with lag, friction, and feedback loops, and over long stretches the explanatory traffic runs mostly one way; when the relations of production change, the superstructure eventually catches up. I have read defenses that recast the claim as a functional explanation — the relations persist because they enable the development of productive power — and I have read the softer formulations, but I want to know what the position is actually exposed to, not which of its friends can restate it most elegantly.

So: give me a map of this view's exposure. If I hold it, what should worry me? I want the candidate defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, from ones that ought to make me give the view up to ones that are merely awkward, and I want them drawn from wherever they genuinely live — historical cases, conceptual objections about what "explains" and "determines" can mean here, and results from adjacent debates that bear on it. Distinguish along the way between the objections that defenders of the view would themselves concede and those they would dismiss as question-begging, and say plainly which of my concessions a critic would claim has already given the game away. I also want to know what my position predicts that its rivals do not — say, a Weberian account in which a religious ethic drives economic transformation, or the pluralist line that there is no general theory of historical change to be had. Finally, I want an honest verdict on the suspicion that the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: once we say the base determines the superstructure "in the last instance," or grant the superstructure "relative autonomy," what remains that could falsify it? If the answer is that almost nothing could, tell me whether that is a fatal defect or simply the condition of any large-scale social theory.

Two kinds of case would move me more than any amount of conceptual tidying. The first is a case in which law, religion, or ideology changed first and plausibly caused a change in the relations of production; I have heard the abolition of slavery in the British Empire offered as exactly this, and I cannot tell whether to believe it or whether defenders of my view have a story that absorbs it without strain. The second is the divergence of Western and Eastern Europe after the fourteenth-century demographic collapse: the same catastrophe, and yet in one place the peasantry ends up with more leverage and in the other it ends up bound more tightly to the land, which looks as though what the shock did depended on the class structure it struck rather than the other way round. Tell me how much weight cases of these kinds can bear, and whether they cut for my position, against it, or somewhere unhelpful in between.
