I was at a reading group on Marx last week and we ended up on the picture everyone reaches for: that a society's economy is the base, and that the law, the state, religion, art and the rest are a superstructure raised upon it — the proof text being the 1859 preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, where the economic structure of society is called the real foundation and a legal and political superstructure is said to arise on it. One of the people there pushed the image much further than I had heard it pushed before. He said the relation is like that of a building's foundation to the building itself: change the foundation and you change what can be built on it; try to remodel the upper floors while the foundation stays where it is and you are doing something either futile or merely cosmetic. He drew the obvious conclusion — that legal and political reform, moral persuasion and cultural work, pursued on their own, are at best rearranging furniture in a house whose cracks are structural — and I found it both persuasive and somehow too easy, without being able to say exactly where it goes wrong.

Could you audit the analogy for me? I would like the correspondence laid out explicitly — what is supposed to play the part of the foundation, of the ground the foundation rests in, of the load-bearing walls, the roof, the architect, the tenants — and then the places where the mapping gives out. I am particularly interested in the features the image quietly borrows from buildings that a society does not have: that a building is designed and put up from the bottom by someone working to a plan; that a foundation does not respond to what happens on the floors above it; that foundations can be underpinned and buildings retrofitted; that the same foundation can carry very different buildings; and that a building is not continuously made and remade by the activity of the people inside it. Which of those failures actually bear on the conclusion he drew, and which are merely decorative? I am also unsure where the metaphor wants the line drawn: it makes the division between base and superstructure look as plain as a floor, but is the law of contract part of the base or part of the superstructure, and what of the state's enforcement of property? And what becomes of the picture in the cases where legal and political arrangements seem to have come first and made a market possible, or where the economy is said to determine the rest only in the long run, so that everything else is left room to push back?

Finally, I would like you to say what work the metaphor is actually doing that a plain statement of the thesis could not do, whether the escape hatch of a merely last-instance or long-run determination rescues the image or quietly concedes that it was never a building at all, and whether some rival image — a living organism, a language, or a set of levels each enjoying a degree of independence — would serve better, or whether the honest move is to drop the architecture altogether and state the claim in its own terms.
