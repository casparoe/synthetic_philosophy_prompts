I work as a reliability engineer — condition monitoring, root-cause analysis, and fitness-for-service assessments on industrial machinery — so the vocabulary I actually think in is the one my trade hands you: design specifications and tolerances, nominal versus off-nominal operating envelopes, the fault–error–failure chain, degradation as distinct from failure, mission profiles and duty cycles, and, at the end of every investigation, a judgment about fitness for continued service, which everyone in my line of work agrees is a judgment about downtime, cost, and acceptable risk rather than a discovery about the machine. I have recently been sitting in on a philosophy of medicine seminar, where the dispute between naturalism and normativism keeps being staged as a fight about whether disease is a matter of biological dysfunction assessed against a species design, or instead a matter of harm and the question of whether something ought to be treated — a matter of values all the way down. My suspicion, which I cannot evaluate on my own, is that I already run a working version of this distinction on the shop floor, and that it may be either the same distinction or a cheaper one wearing similar clothes. So I would like you to restate both positions, and the standard objections they trade against one another, as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, taking them one at a time. For the naturalist: tell me what is supposed to play the role of the specification, who or what is supposed to play the role of the designer, and whether species-typical can do the work that a documented tolerance does in a maintenance manual. For the normativist: tell me whether my category of fitness for continued service — value-laden by construction — is really the same category as their claim that the concept of disease is value-laden, or whether they are making a stronger claim about the concept itself that my vocabulary cannot state at all.

Then give me the residue report, and run it through three specific things. First, my team once spent a month arguing about a compressor that had sat outside every published tolerance for years without ever once failing to deliver its function, and about a second machine that failed while comfortably inside every tolerance, because the specification had never covered the load case it actually saw. In my vocabulary those are a maintenance-policy question and a requirements question respectively, and I suspect that the philosophers' contested cases — aging, pregnancy, the conditions that were struck from the diagnostic manual once the surrounding social values changed — are not the same shape as either, so I want you to say exactly what breaks. Second, a clinical colleague once told me that a laboratory reference interval is just the middle ninety-five percent of a reference population that somebody chose, and that it is not the same thing as a decision limit derived from outcomes; I would like to know what that does to the naturalist's appeal to species-typical functioning, because it looks to me like a nominal value read off a sample of a population that I get to pick, which would make the naturalist's specification look suspiciously stipulated after all. Third — and this is the thing I notice I cannot say — I have no way of saying that a machine has a good of its own. I can say a part is out of spec, and I can say the plant would be better off replacing it, but the better off attaches to the owners and the operators, never to the machine, and I have come to suspect that the entire naturalist/normativist fight is about whether an organism is more like the part or more like the plant. Tell me whether that suspicion is a genuine translation or a distortion, and then give me your verdict on where the residue actually sits: is my framework missing something the philosophers can say, or is their distinction doing less work than it appears to, and which of the two is the more interesting failure?
