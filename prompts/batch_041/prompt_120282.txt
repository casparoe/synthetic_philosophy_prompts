The paper is basically called No Formal Fix for Simpsons Paradox, and here's the argument I'm defending:

- A Simpson reversal — the aggregate table says one thing, every sub-group says the opposite — can be produced by at least three structurally different situations: (a) classic confounding, i.e. a common cause of both the grouping variable and the outcome; (b) conditioning on a collider, a common effect of two other variables, which manufactures an association that wasn't there; and (c) plain non-collapsibility of the effect measure, where an odds ratio flips between its marginal and conditional versions even with no confounding at all.
- Those three want three different responses. Confounding: adjust for it. Collider: stop conditioning on it, you induced the thing yourself. Non-collapsibility: there's no bias to remove — the marginal and conditional parameters are just different quantities and you have to say which one you actually want.
- Since one and the same table is compatible with all three, no rule that looks only at the tables can be correct. Analyse at the finest partition; trust whichever table is stable under refinement of the grouping; use sure-thing/dominance reasoning — every one of them either quietly imports a causal assumption or I can counterexample it.
- So the punchline is that resolving a reversal is never a statistical operation. It's a causal commitment plus a decision about which parameter you care about. The maths is bookkeeping.

Two constructions do a lot of work in the talk. (i) A 2x2x2 where the reversal flips back if you collapse the third variable the other way, so no level of grouping is privileged as the finest. (ii) A table where my stability-under-refinement rule and the dominance rule point in opposite directions on the same data.
