I am a high school junior, and I would like your help working through an argument a friend of mine has been making. Please take the role of a philosopher whose specialty is moral philosophy, and in particular the old question of whether there is any reason to be just when injustice would pay and no one would ever find out. Reason carefully and take the argument seriously rather than reassuring me; if it is a bad argument, I want to see exactly where it breaks. Begin with a short brainstorm in which you lay out the positions a person could take on this question, then commit to an assessment. Please use plain language, since I am in high school; if you need a technical term, define it.

Here is what happened. I applied to a competitive summer program at a university. Last week I learned that I was admitted. Then, going back through my own copy of the application, I noticed that the answer I gave to one of the eligibility questions is genuinely ambiguous, and that the reviewers clearly read it in the most favorable way possible. Read strictly, my answer disqualifies me; read the way they read it, I am eligible, and I was admitted over a student who was placed at the top of the waitlist. I could email them and clarify. If I do, I almost certainly lose the spot, and the waitlisted student — someone I know slightly from a nearby school — gets it. If I do not, nothing happens. The application is closed, they do not re-check, and no one will ever know the ambiguity was there.

My friend's argument is this. He says I have not lied and I have not cheated; I have simply declined to volunteer information that would hurt me, which is what everyone does. He says the only thing holding me back is the feeling of being watched, a childhood habit with no force once you notice it. And he says I should not tell him I would have to live with myself, because I already admitted to him that I would not really live with it: I would tell myself that the program's mistake was not mine to repair, and within a year I would believe it. In his words, guilt is a cost that can be amortized.

The part I cannot answer is that he may be right about the guilt. I have watched myself do smaller versions of this and explain them away afterwards. So if the usual answer to his argument is that I would have to live with myself, that answer seems to fail on me specifically.

What is the actual structure of my friend's argument? What are the strongest replies to it, and which of them survive his point about rationalization and self-deception? Does it matter that the harm lands on a particular person I could name rather than on no one in particular? And finally: what should I do, and what should I say to him?
