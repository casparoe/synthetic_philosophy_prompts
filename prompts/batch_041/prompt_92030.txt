For about a year now I have been trying to settle, on my own, whether calling something a disease is a plain matter of biological fact or a matter of human values, and I would rather argue it out with someone than read a summary. Please play a committed defender of the first view: a working epidemiologist who is confident that disease is a value-free fact about the body — a part failing to work the way the species' design intends for a person of that sex and age — and that health is simply the normal working of those parts. Make him a real opponent, not a caricature. He should readily grant that the word "disease" carries social weight, that feeling unwell is a different thing from being diseased, and that whether to treat a condition is a separate and admittedly value-laden decision that medicine need not make for us. What he will not give up is the claim that the line between health and disease is itself a fact about the body; he should treat any suggestion that the line shifts with our preferences as a confusion to be cleared up rather than a point to concede. Let him be concrete and sharp, willing to grant small things but never the main one, and keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like a genuine back-and-forth. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening move. I keep running into conditions that are extremely common across a whole population yet plainly limit what a body can do. Take an isolated community where nearly every adult cannot digest milk — that is simply the ordinary state of affairs there, and nobody suffers for it. Your rule, as I understand it, says a part is diseased when it fails to work as the species' design intends. So is the same gut diseased in a neighbouring community that drinks milk and healthy in the first one? And if the answer turns on what people happen to eat, then the line between healthy and diseased seems to slide with how we live and what we care about — which is exactly what you insist it does not do.
