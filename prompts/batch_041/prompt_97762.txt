So I'm getting married next spring — to a woman, which is the relevant part — and over the holidays my uncle, who did a philosophy degree back in the day and never lets anyone forget it, held forth on what the marriage debate is "really" about, and now I can't stop turning it over. He said, roughly:

Look, it all comes down to one disagreement. One side says marriage, by its nature, is the union of a man and a woman, ordered toward having and raising kids together. The other side says marriage is a loving, committed relationship between two adults, full stop. The first view has to say same-sex couples can't marry because they can't have children together — and since it's stuck with that logic, it also has to say a sterile or elderly straight couple isn't really married either, which is why even the people who defend it admit it's a hard bullet to bite. The second view says marriage is just about love, so the state should recognize any two people who love each other and want to commit. Really it's religious versus secular — the first view comes out of church teaching, the second is the modern secular one — and the whole fight is about whether the government should be enforcing a religious definition of a word.

I suspect he's mostly right but I also have a feeling he's smuggling things in. I'm obviously on the side of getting married, but I want to know what's actually true here, not what's convenient for me. Can you go claim by claim: what would someone who holds that first view happily sign their name to, what would they say is a distortion of their own position, and what's just wrong? And that last bit — that it's religious versus secular, and about the government enforcing a definition — does it actually follow from the parts of his summary that are accurate, or is it doing its own seperate work?

Two things specifically bug me. First, the infertility point: if it isn't actually forced on the first view, what do its defenders say instead? Second, he made the second view sound like it's simply "two people who love each other," which would seem to include me and my best friend, or three people, and that can't be the real position — so what is it actually claiming marriage is for? I'd also like to know which parts of his summary are the standard talking points on each side (so I can stop being surprised when I hear them) versus where he's quietly taken a side. And if I had to say which parts I'm inclined to believe: I buy the infertility problem being a real cost, and I'm suspicious of the religious/secular framing — but tell me if that instinct is off too.
