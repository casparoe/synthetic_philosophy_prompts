I'm putting together a session for my grad seminar on the ethics of AI, and I keep running into the same muddle: my students treat intent alignment and value alignment as if the first were just the tractable core of the second — get the system to do what the operator asks, and the value question is a separate, later problem. I want a case that puts pressure on that, and I want you to build it rather than describe it.

Here's the spec. Construct one realistic scenario — present-day capability, no superintelligence, no fast takeoff, no rogue system, no deceptive AI, and no reward hacking or spec gaming; I want everything to go "right" — in which two systems differ along a single structural dimension. System A is more intent-aligned: it does what its operator instructs, including in the cases where the operator is mistaken. System B is less so: it has some slack, deferring to its own read of what the people affected actually value rather than to the instruction. Hold capability, information, and training data fixed between them; the only difference is that slack. Your case has to make A come out worse on the broad measure. Two constraints, and I think they're where the difficulty lives. First, the operator has to be a good-faith professional who shares the values of the people affected and is simply wrong about how to achieve them — not a villain, not someone issuing a legally or morally outrageous instruction, and not someone whose instruction is obviously bad; the harm has to flow through the operator's false belief, which A faithfully transmits and B doesn't. Second, the harm has to be a genuine value failure and not just a mismatch between what the operator wanted and what the stakeholders wanted, which would be too easy.

Then do the work. Say exactly how you're pinning down intent alignment and value alignment, since both are loose enough that the verdicts could be stipulated rather than derived, and give each system's verdict. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the case if I delete it — I suspect it collapses back into an ordinary just-do-what-you're-told story, and I want to know if thats right. Finish with the strongest reply available to someone who thinks the narrow problem is the real one. I expect they'll say your operator's instruction was never the right object of alignment in the first place, and if that's their move, say what it costs them. And make clear whether the case shows intent alignment to be insufficient or actually harmful, because those are different claims and I dont want them run together.

Also, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables please — continuous prose, like you'd write in an email. I'll be reading it aloud to the seminar.
