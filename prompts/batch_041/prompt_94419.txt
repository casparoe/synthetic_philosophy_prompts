I sit on the Novel Entities Ethics Panel at my university, and at Thursday's meeting we have to vote on a specific proposal. Our current welfare policy is a historical accident: full protection for vertebrates, a lesser precautionary status for cephalopods, and nothing at all for insects or for the simple adaptive machines in the robotics lab. The proposal comes from a bioengineering group that wants to run a twelve-month study using a neuroorganoid robot: a small mobile platform controlled by a three-dimensional culture of human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons. The protocol exposes the platform to a simulated noxious stimulus, a signal the neural culture can learn to avoid by steering toward a safe zone, and then discards the culture at the end. The company argues the system is morally inert because it has no body, no nociceptors, and no evidence of valenced experience; a neuroethics advocacy group that has been following our deliberations argues that the culture's avoidance learning and its capacity for motivational trade-offs put it somewhere above insects and below decapods, and that we should not license its destruction.

The panel is split, and our subcommittee has drafted a four-tier Graded Sentience Protocol that we might adopt as a general framework. Tier 0 covers entities with no plausible welfare interests, such as thermostats and simple reactive agents. Tier 1 covers entities with nociceptive circuitry but no evidence of integrated pain, such as many insects. Tier 2 covers entities with strong behavioral and physiological indicators of valenced experience, such as cephalopods and perhaps decapods. Tier 3 covers vertebrates and anything else we treat as a full subject of welfare. The draft also contains a novelty clause: a system that exhibits motivational trade-offs or self-administered analgesia can be upgraded one tier by a two-thirds vote. The question before us is whether to approve the neuroorganoid study, and if so, under which tier and with what conditions. I want the memo to be usable: it should state a clear recommendation for Thursday's vote, approve, approve with conditions, or reject, with any conditions and a review date, and give the panel a defensible rationale. It should also confront the hardest objection to that recommendation, not just dismiss it.

Because the whole point of the Graded Sentience Protocol is to make the line-drawing explicit, the memo needs to say where the neuroorganoid robot falls on the continuum and why. Does the absence of a body or nociceptors settle the matter, or does learning and avoidance behavior carry independent moral weight? If we upgrade it under the novelty clause, what stops us from upgrading a sufficiently clever thermostat, and is that a reductio or just the cost of taking gradations seriously? Please keep the memo under 850 words, lead with a one-sentence recommendation, and make sure the reasoning is something my neuroscientist and lawyer colleagues can both sign onto, even if they disagree about the outcome.
