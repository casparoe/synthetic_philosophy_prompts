Hi, I'm a second-year student on a module about convention, normativity and social morality, and I've got a tangle I want to sort out before a presentation next week. It started with my flatmate Priya, who's from a country where you don't tip, refusing to leave anything at a restaurant in town while the rest of us were splitting the bill, and my other flatmate Sam insisting she'd done something morally wrong, not just something awkward. Then the same week a stranger told me off for crossing against the lights on an empty road at 2am, and a visiting lecturer mentioned that in his hometown queueing doesn't really exist, you just press towards the counter, and it made me realise I don't actually know where etiquette stops and morality starts, or whether a visitor is bound by local conventions they never agreed to and might not even know about. Our lecturer keeps gesturing at Hume's idea that some of our virtues are artificial, invented to solve coordination problems rather than discovered, but she never says what that's meant to imply for the poor visitor standing on the wrong side of the escalator.

What I want is a taxonomy, like a decision tree, of the actual positions here, built so that someone can work out where they sit by answering questions about concrete cases rather than by picking a label they already like. Please structure it in two stages: first the branching tree, where every branch point is labelled with the question that seperates it from its siblings (whether a convention needs to be backed by an independent moral reason to bind at all; whether the obligation attaches to the local rule itself or to some deeper duty like fairness, reciprocity, gratitude, or not free-riding on a practice others uphold; whether the visitor's ignorance or lack of consent defeats the obligation; whether it matters that the convention is harmless versus one whose breach imposes real costs on others); then, at the leaves, the positions themselves, each with a name if it has one and 'unnamed' if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I'd like the tree to cover at least these leaves: the view that it's all just etiquette and breach is rudeness rather than wrongdoing; the view that whatever the local practice is, visitors are bound by it just because it's local; the view that only conventions tracking independent moral duties bind anyone, visitor or not; a view where the obligation is conditional on something like reciprocity, fair play or tacit consent; and a pluralist view that treats queueing and jaywalking as different kinds of case rather than instances of one kind. I also want you to flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't separate and say why, and to note the answer-combinations nobody occupies, with a brief diagnosis of whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Use these cases as the material for the branch questions, and phrase every question so that someone who's never read any philosophy can still answer it: jaywalking across an empty road at night; not tipping in a restaurant where the staff are paid on the assumption of tips; barging to the front of a queue at a busy post office; standing on the wrong side of an escalator or blocking the left; a visitor who doesn't know a local mourning custom and so behaves wrongly at a funeral without meaning to; someone who keeps their seat on a crowded train while an elderly person stands; and a visitor from a place where haggling is normal refusing to haggle in a shop where the price is fixed. Aim for six to nine branch questions and no more than about eight leaves, and keep the whole thing usable as a one-page handout for first-years.
