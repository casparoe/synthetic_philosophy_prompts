I teach a third-year seminar in philosophy of medicine — 16 students, one 90-minute session — and I want an activity where they *feel* the pull of both naturalism and normativism about disease, instead of me standing up and listing the two positions at them. Heres the skeleton I've got; turn it into something I can actually run: rules, materials, timings, scoring, debrief, and an honest list of what'll go wrong the first time.

The skeleton. I build a deck of about 15 cards. Each card is one case, described in plain bodily terms with no diagnostic label — the second I put a name on a card they just retrieve the textbook verdict instead of judging. Every card asks two questions, which students rate separately and, importantly, privately first, before any group discussion: (a) is something in this body malfunctioning — not doing what that kind of body is supposed to do? (b) is this person harmed, and would medicine be doing them a favour by stepping in? Then the group becomes a nosology committee and has to commit in writing to one definition: a condition counts as a disease iff ___. One criterion only, no 'or', and it has to classify every card in the deck.

The cases are rigged so (a) and (b) pull apart: someone with mirrored internal organs, healthy and unaware of it; a person who feels no pain at all from birth; lactose intolerance in a herding community that lives on milk versus a community that never touches it — identical biology, opposite verdicts; sickle-cell trait, protective in one place and risky in another; ordinary grief after a death; pregnancy; a thirty-year-old with high blood pressure who feels fine; a benign mole; six fully working fingers; someone who hears voices, isn't distressed by them, and whose community has a place for it; a twenty-year-old carrying a gene that will certainly wreck them at fifty but who's fine now; an asymptomatic carrier who's perfectly healthy and infects other people (whose harm counts?); a dog with a torn ligament; a maple with a fungus. The last two matter — they stop the group from settling on 'a disease is whatever the person says hurts'.

I want a game element, but I dont want winning to line up with being right. So after they've committed, groups swap definitions and try to break each other's with a sealed deck I've kept back — five cards engineered as traps, one per lazy definition (purely statistical: catches the mirrored organs and the six fingers; purely harm-based: catches the grief; purely malfunction-based: catches the asymptomatic carrier; purely 'what doctors treat': catches the blood pressure; purely cultural: catches the maple). Points for landing a hit. The debrief should then say out loud that the group that won won by appealing to the values everybody already shared — which is the normativist's point, not a bug.

What they should walk out seeing: that their own verdicts were never read off the biology, and that a definition of disease does real work — it decides who pays, who gets treated, who counts as normal. What I need from you: the exact materials (how to word the cards so the two questions stay independent and don't leak into each other), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the scoring, and a debrief that maps particular moments back onto the naturalist/normativist split.

Two things to be straight about. First, what predictably goes wrong and how I handle it — my guesses: groups will write a two-pronged definition to cover everything ('malfunction or harmful'), someone will settle arguments by saying a doctor would call it a disease, and one person will announce that its all socially constructed in the first five minutes and the room will relax. Second, tell me what the activity can't do. My worry is that it can make them feel facts and values are tangled in their judgments, but it can't touch the naturalist who simply says the committee is ignorant — that all these cases really do have a determinate malfunction-or-not answer and we just haven't found it. If thats right, say so and tell me what I have to supply myself.
