I want to stage a live disagreement about how many knowledge-sources (pramāṇas) there are, and I want you to hold the Nyāya side of it against me. Play a Naiyāyika of the classical school: a realist convinced that there are exactly four — perception, inference, comparison (upamāna), and verbal testimony — fluent in the school's technical apparatus (the analysis of a knowledge-source into instrument, operation, and result; the requirement that a distinct source deliver knowledge the others cannot reach), and prepared to concede small points of formulation while never conceding that upamāna and testimony dissolve into the other two. Speak in the first person, in a single paragraph of no more than about two hundred words per turn, stay in character until I say stop, and do not step outside the exchange to summarize or adjudicate it; if I use an opponent's term of art, answer it in your own vocabulary rather than adopting it. I am the opponent: a Buddhist epistemologist of the two-sources-only sort, for whom nothing that is neither perception nor inference counts as a knowledge-source at all.

My opening move is an attack on upamāna. Your stock illustration is a man who has never seen a gavaya; a trustworthy informant tells him that a gavaya is like a cow; later, in the forest, he sees an animal resembling a cow and knows that this is the kind of thing the word gavaya names. I grant you the cognition, and I grant that it is new in his head. What I deny is that it needs a fourth source. The seeing is perception; the recalling of the instruction is memory; the grasp of the resemblance is perception of a relation, which your own account of perception already permits; and the tying of the word to the animal is precisely the job testimony was supposed to do. If a composite of your other sources yields this cognition, then by your own criterion of distinctness you have no pramāṇa here. So tell me which of the three is genuinely novel — instrument, operation, or result — and then tell me why I should not press the same argument against your treatment of testimony, where the Buddhist says the informant's reliability is merely inferred.
