I've fallen down a rabbit hole on cliodynamics after a podcast episode about it and I genuinely can't tell whether the fight over it is a real fight. Here's the setup as I understand it. On one side you've got the people who say history is at least partly quantifiable and maybe even forecastable (Turchin is the name everyone cites): societies run through long 'secular cycles' of integration and disintegration, and the engine is a structural-demographic package — popular immiseration (wages and rents moving the wrong way for ordinary people), elite overproduction (too many aspirants competing for too few elite positions, producing a mass of frustrated, well-connected losers), and fiscal distress of the state — which together push up measurable rates of political violence. They build indices that put numbers on all of this, they claim the numbers for the US and a bunch of other countries line up with the actual waves of unrest, and they claim to have flagged in the 2010s that the 2020s were going to be rough. On the other side, the historians I read (and a couple of people in my dept) basically say it's numerology: the categories get chosen after you already know the outcome, 'elite overproduction' gets defined whatever way preserves the fit, the proxies are junk, decade-long 'cycles' are imposed on noise, and even if you could predict that a society will get violent you haven't explained anything anyone wanted to know, because you've said nothing about why this particular thing happened in this particular way. And yet both sides seem to agree on alot of the descriptive facts, which is what makes me think the fight isnt really about the facts.

My hunch, and I'd like you to treat it as just a hunch, is that it's two or three different disagreements tangled up under one word. Maybe one is verbal: 'prediction' meaning a forecast registered before the fact versus a model that fits the past after the fact, and 'science' meaning 'uses numbers and general laws' versus something else entirely, so they end up talking past each other. Maybe another is a real but unstated disagreement about the standard of evidence — out-of-sample forecasting error versus whether a narrative makes sense of the sources. And maybe a third is about the object of explanation: aggregate rates of events versus the particular events and choices historians actually care about. Two things friends threw at me that I keep chewing on. One: the tell is whether the theory has ever made a claim that could have come out false, so does anyone register forecasts in advance, or is it always retrodiction? Two: nobody says meteorology isn't a science because it can't tell you what you'll be doing next Tuesday, so why isn't a coarse probabilistic macro-history just the same kind of enterprise — and is that analogy helpful, or does it smuggle in the assumption that societies are more like weather systems than like whatever they actually are? Somebody else asked whether 'a society' or 'an elite' is even a stable enough unit to be measured the same way across five hundred years, or whether the theory quietly assumes that.
