I'm a novelist about halfway through a book where one of the main characters, a locksmith named Delia who's slowly going blind, has talked herself into a version of Berkeley's idealism — she thinks the whole to-be-is-to-be-perceived thing is just obviously true and that everyone else is too embarrassed to say it out loud. Problem is I want her to be smart, not a strawman, so I need to practice arguing against the strongest version of her position and I keep getting my ass handed to me. So: play her. Play a committed Berkeleyan idealist — sharp, patient, a little smug in the way people get when they think they've seen through something, willing to grant me a point here and there but never the big one — and stay in character until I say "cut." Keep your replies to a paragraph or so, like we're actually talking. One rule you have to live with: no appeals to God or to any infinite mind. You think that move is a cop-out anyway, that it just relabels the problem instead of solving it, so you have to defend the core of the master argument using only what finite experience gives you — regularity, expectations, counterfactuals about what you'd perceive if you looked, habits of the imagination, whatever you've got. If that means you have to throw out some of what Berkeley actually wrote, then say so, in character.

My opening move: you claim we can't conceive of anything existing unconceived, that whenever I try I'm secretly just entertaining another one of my own ideas. But this morning I imagined a valley on a planet with no minds on it, ever — no people, no animals, no God, nothing anywhere that perceives anything — and the imagining was perfectly coherent, as vivid as the room I'm sitting in. So either I really did conceive of an unperceived valley, in which case your premise is false, or you tell me I was only ever conceiving my own idea, in which case your argument tells me nothing about the valley and everything about my head. And dont just tell me the valley is an idea of imagination while the room is an idea of sense — I know that distinction, and if you lean on it I want to know which kind of idea the argument was ever about in the first place. Which horn are you taking, and if it's the second, how is that not the cheapest kind of retreat?
