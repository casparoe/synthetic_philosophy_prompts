I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and I am writing a feature on the quiet spread of lottocratic institutions. For a sidebar, I need a forecast and a consistency check on a regional oversight body I have been covering, the Sortition Standards Board. Its published rulings, stripped of rhetoric, are these. It approved a one-off Transit Citizens' Jury: twenty-four members chosen by stratified lottery from the transit-tax register, six days of professional facilitation, a stipend, and only advisory recommendations; the ruling emphasized that the jury mirrored the affected public and that facilitation kept the loudest voices from dominating. It rejected a Neighborhood Zoning Assembly: eighteen members drawn by lottery from people who had attended open meetings, no stipend, unlimited terms, and a binding veto; the ruling cited self-selection and entrenchment. It approved a Standing Housing Lottery Panel: thirty members chosen by stratified lottery from all residents, paid a stipend, two-year non-renewable terms, and binding recommendations on rent caps; the ruling cited rotation and representativeness. It rejected a Youth Budget Parliament: fifty members chosen by lottery from volunteers in school civics clubs, one-year renewable terms, no stipend, no professional facilitation, and binding budget authority; the ruling cited the absence of support and the self-selected pool. The board's opinions never say which feature is doing the legitimating work.

The new case is a proposal for a permanent Hospital Closure Review Panel with binding power to block hospital closures. Members would be chosen by stratified lottery from a combined patient and resident register, so the panel would be descriptively representative of the region. They would serve renewable five-year terms, receive no stipend beyond travel, and deliberate on a chat platform without professional facilitation. The chair has already said she likes how representative it is.

I need a prediction, not a bibliography. Please write the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: Bottom line (about 100 words): your predicted verdict and your confidence. The precedents and the candidate rules (about 250 words): state at least two rules that fit all four earlier rulings and explain why each fits. What each rule predicts for the new case (about 150 words). Which rule the board is probably running (about 150 words): your best guess and the reasons for it. The closest precedent (about 100 words): which earlier ruling is the nearest analog, and where the analogy breaks. The decisive case (about 100 words): a further proposal that would tell the candidate rules apart. If consistency is the aim (about 150 words): what the board should decide to cohere with its own record, and what principle it would be adopting by deciding either way.
