I am a senior at my high school and the captain of our robotics team, and on Saturday evening I have to give the captain's address at our end-of-season banquet. I would like you to write that speech for me, built on Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù rather than on the usual banquet sentiments about teamwork and believing in ourselves.

The occasion and the room: roughly fifty-five people in the school cafeteria. Fourteen teammates, most of their parents, a scattering of younger siblings, the principal, two alumni mentors who still come to build sessions, and our faculty advisor, who teaches AP European History and spends a full week on Machiavelli every year. Two of my teammates read The Prince for his class this fall. Everyone else in the room has never read a word of it. I have about four minutes.

What actually happened this season, so you have material. Last year we finished second at the state qualifier with a slow, patient, precision-scoring machine. This year's game rewarded speed and risk, and the teams that tore up their designs in January did well; we kept refining the thing that had always worked for us. We did prepare carefully in the ways we could foresee: our spare-parts system and our pre-match checklist saved us twice, once when a motor controller died and once when a wheel hub cracked. At the qualifier, in the quarterfinal, the venue's field-control system dropped out for most of our best match; the referees could not replay it, and we were out because of something no one on our side of the glass could have touched. And on the first morning of the tournament we lent our spare drive gearbox to a team from another school whose own gearbox had stripped, and they eliminated us the next afternoon.

What I need the speech to do, philosophically. Machiavelli treats fortune as genuinely powerful, on the order of half of what happens to us, and virtù as prowess and capacity rather than goodness, so the speech cannot promise that effort or excellence will win. It also cannot tell us to stay true to who we are; his point is that the man ruined is the one who cannot change his ways when the times change. And it should not dress up the gearbox as virtù, since that is not what the word means for him. If the speech can honor that moment, I would rather it do so on other grounds, or openly step outside Machiavelli to do it, than quietly pretend he would have approved.

Practical constraints. Do not lecture: no explaining who Machiavelli was, no definitions up front, no chapter numbers, no direct quotations from The Prince, and nothing that sounds like a reading list. The two teammates who know the text should be able to enjoy it, and everyone else should be able to follow it without feeling taught. Please stay away from the passage where he compares fortune to a woman who yields to the forceful, and from anything that would let the parents hear the ends justify the means.

Give me the speech, written to be spoken aloud in my voice, at roughly six hundred words. After it, add at most five bullets naming the places where you had to soften, dodge, or step outside Machiavelli, and which of them our advisor is most likely to object to, so I can decide before Saturday.
