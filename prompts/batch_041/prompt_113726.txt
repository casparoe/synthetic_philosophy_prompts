I've been reading alot about the free will debate lately, specifically the Dennett side vs the Caruso side, the two who did that book together where Dennett defends desert and Caruso argues nobody ever really deserves anything. Caruso's big move, and I think it comes from Pereboom too, is that punishment should be thought of like quarantine. You don't blame someone for carrying a contagious disease, you don't say they deserve to be locked away for it, you isolate them because it protects everyone else. Same supposedly goes for people who commit crimes: no desert required, just containment. Then Dennett fires back with something like, criminals aren't pathogens.

I want to know how far this quarantine analogy actually holds. Give me the correspondence table first -- what's the disease, what's the carrier, what's the quarantine, who plays the public health officer, what's the incubation period, all of it. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Some of the breaks I can already guess: quarantine ends when you're no longer contagious but a prison sentence is fixed in advance; you can reason with a person and not a virus; a disease carrier didn't choose to be dangerous while the whole point of the analogy is that it's supposed to work even when the criminal did choose. What I can't tell is which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion Caruso wants and which are just noise.

The specific conclusion I want stress-tested: that we can keep everything worth keeping in the criminal justice system -- locking up dangerous people, deterring others, maybe even the expressive/denunciatory stuff -- without ever saying anyone deserves it. Does the quarantine picture actually support that, or is the analogy carrying the conclusion by quietly smuggling in assumptions? For instance, does quarantine work as a model only because we've already decided the carrier isn't a member of the moral community we're addressing, and can that be seperate from the desert question or is it the same question in disguise? And if the analogy is bad, is there a better one for the no-desert view, or does the no-desert view not need an analogy at all?

One more thing I keep going back and forth on. Dennett's reply that you can reason with a person and not a virus -- is that a genuine disanalogy that sinks the model, or is it just him insisting on the very thing that's under dispute?
