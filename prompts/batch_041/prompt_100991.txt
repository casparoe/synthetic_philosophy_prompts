You are an expert in democratic theory and in writing for the spoken word; think carefully before you begin, and start by brainstorming a few candidate lines of argument.

I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last five years reading political philosophy, and my town has asked me to give the opening address at the first session of a citizens' assembly that will spend six months deciding how to replace its aging water system. The room will contain two very different audiences: a few deliberative-democracy scholars who helped design the assembly, and a large number of ordinary residents, many of whom doubt that a randomly selected body has any business deciding something this technical. I want the address to argue for selection by lot — not as a grudging fallback for an ignorant electorate but as a positive requirement of political equality — while taking the competence objection seriously. The assembly's charter also gives an appointed panel of engineers a feasibility veto over whatever it proposes, and I want the speech to confront honestly whether that arrangement hollows out the assembly's authority or legitimately constrains it. Let the argument do the structural work: the shape of the case should determine what I can and cannot say, and it should resolve, or candidly fail to resolve, the tension between equality and competence in a way both halves of the room can follow. Contrast sortition with term limits and with the career-politician model, and be concrete about how rotation by lot differs from rotation by rule. Assume no philosophical background; explain any idea you introduce. Aim for roughly eight minutes of speech, then give the final text.
