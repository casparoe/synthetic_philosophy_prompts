I retired from a career in soil chemistry a couple years back and have been working my way through philosophy since, mostly philosophy of language lately. Last term I sat in on a grad seminar on vagueness and came away thinking half the room wasn't even answering the same question, so I want to run a session where people sort themselves instead of arguing past each other.

Please build me a decision tree that starts from a few plain commitments — is there a fact of the matter about exactly where 'heap' or 'bald' or 'red' gives out, is the vagueness in our words or in the world, do borderline cases get no truth value, both truth values, or a definite one we just can't know — and leads to a named position at each leaf. I want epistemicism, supervaluationism, and the main rivals on the tree: degree/fuzzy, contextualist, the 'no such property' line, and ontic vagueness.

Two things I care about. Every branch node has to be phrased so a student can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to. And each leaf should say which premise of the sorites it ends up denying, plus whether it can live with higher-order vagueness (vague boundaries to the boundary).

Use at least one case that isn't the sorites — the problem of the many, say, or a predicate whose borderline clearly shifts with context. For the supervaluationist branch especially, make the split turn on where truth comes from (what all the admissible sharpenings agree on) and whether those sharpenings are constrained by the connections between borderline cases.

Then tell me which answer-combinations are unoccupied and whether that's because the view is incoherent, ugly, or just unexplored, and flag any two leaves the tree can't really separate. Twelve to fifteen nodes, aimed at people who've read a bit but aren't specialists.
