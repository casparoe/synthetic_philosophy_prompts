I am the convener of the logic and philosophy of language paper in our department's PhD qualifying examination, and I am drafting this year's long question on the Liar paradox. The difficulty I keep running into is that my candidates arrive having been drilled in the same three set pieces — Tarski's T-schema and the hierarchy of languages, Kripke's fixed-point construction over Strong Kleene valuations, and Priest's paraconsistent alternative — with the result that any question inviting them to "explain the Liar and assess the leading solutions" is answered by recitation alone and tells me nothing about whether they can actually operate the machinery. What I want instead is a question that cannot be answered without doing some calculation, and which nevertheless remains fair to a candidate who has had one term of the material. Please act as an expert in philosophical logic with a particular specialism in the semantics of truth predicates, and think the matter through carefully before writing: begin by brainstorming, in a few lines, the various ways a candidate might go wrong, so that the marking scheme can be built to catch them, and then give me the finished materials.

The device I want the question to turn on is a sentence I have been using in tutorials and have taken to calling the hedging liar: a sentence H which says of itself that it is either false or else lacks a truth value. Set the exercise in a language containing its own truth predicate, evaluated by Kripke's minimal fixed point over Strong Kleene valuations, with falsity treated as truth of the negation and "lacks a truth value" treated as "neither true nor false"; candidates may be assumed to know the Kleene tables and the shape of the fixed-point construction, but they will not have seen this sentence before. The question should require them to work out what the construction assigns to H, to say whether the second disjunct buys the Kripkean anything, and then to press the general issue: whether a theory of this kind can express its own verdict on the sentences it leaves unsettled, what that shows about the revenge problem, and how the answer bears on the choice between paracomplete and paraconsistent treatments of truth. I want the question to be answerable in ninety minutes and to be worth 25 marks.

Alongside the question, please supply a model answer of roughly 700 to 800 words, and a marking scheme that names the five or six moves a distinction-level answer must make, the marks each of them carries, and, for each move, the specific error that typically costs candidates the mark — I am thinking here of errors such as confusing an unsettled sentence with a false one, or assuming that the disjunction must take a classical value. Close with a short note distinguishing a solid upper-second answer from a first-class one. Please write continuous prose wherever prose is called for, and use displayed formulae only where they genuinely earn their place.
