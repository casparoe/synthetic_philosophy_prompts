Several nations organize public commemoration around a defeat rather than a victory: the American South's monuments to the Confederacy, the last stand at Masada, the memory of Kosovo, the evacuation at Dunkirk. Treat this as a standing practice and ask what it presupposes. For a rout, a last stand, or a retreat to function as founding glory, what must be true of the relation between an event's moral significance and its outcome? Does the practice require that the worth of a sacrifice be intrinsic and independent of what it achieved, that collective identity be constituted by shared suffering rather than by success, or merely that a defeat is more pliable material for later myth-making? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined among these options. Then address the apparent inconsistency: the same publics that sanctify these defeats judge policy, careers, and blame by results elsewhere, so explain whether the commemorative practice commits them to a view of moral worth that their ordinary judgments deny. Finally, say what would have to change in how such commemorations are conducted if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforwardly consequentialist one.
