I was appointed last month to the Commonwealth's Commission on the Marsh River Removal, the mid-century program in which state agencies took children from the valley's families and placed them in institutions run by what is now the Department of Children's Services, and I have been asked to draft the covering memo that will accompany our report to the legislature in March. My own grandparents were among the families affected, which is part of why I want the reasoning to be right rather than merely decent-sounding. The Commission is already agreed on a material remedy and a memorial; what is unresolved is the apology, and the memo has to resolve it.

Three positions are on the table. One commissioner holds that the Commonwealth cannot apologize at all, because an apology is a first-person act that presupposes an apologizer who did the thing: the officials are dead, the agencies that ran the program were dissolved and later reconstituted under other names, and so what we can honestly offer is acknowledgment plus remedy, with anything further amounting to a comfortable fiction that lets the present legislature collect the moral credit without paying the cost. A second member accepts the apology but proposes wording that apologizes for the harms suffered rather than for the program itself; a third calls that an apology with the wrong left out. And some descendant groups have told us they want the remedy and no apology, which they expect will be used to close the file, while others say an apology is the only thing that treats the removal as a wrong rather than as an outstanding debt.

Draft the memo I will circulate: a concrete recommendation with a time frame, specifying in what form and by whom the apology should or should not be issued, and engage the authorship and addressee objections seriously enough that their holders could respond to it.
