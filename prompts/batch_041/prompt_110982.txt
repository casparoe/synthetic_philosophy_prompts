I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm trying to get clear on a puzzle in the way copyright offices treat AI-assisted art. The practice, as I understand it, is roughly this: when someone applies to register a work that was made with a generative AI system, the office will register the human-authored elements—selection, arrangement, modification, and the like—but requires the applicant to disclaim the AI-generated material itself, on the ground that it lacks human authorship. Meanwhile, a photographer who chooses a subject, frames a shot, and presses the shutter gets a copyright in the whole image, even though the camera does most of the optical and electronic work. And a painter who uses a custom brush, a filter, or a generative texture tool can still own the resulting painting.

I'd like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. What theory of creativity, authorship, and originality does it assume? Does it require that the human be the efficient cause of the expressive elements, or only that the human exercise a certain kind of control or intentional selection? Does it presuppose a distinction between conception and execution, and if so, where does it draw the line? What does it quietly rule out—for instance, the view that creativity can be distributed across a human-machine system, or that originality is a property of the process rather than the output? And is the practice internally consistent, or does the photographer analogy expose a tension?

I'd also like you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Could several different theories of creativity license the same registration decisions? If so, which ones? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we adopted a rival view—say, that authorship should track causal responsibility for the expressive features of the work, or that it should track the locus of creative decision-making, however distributed.

Please be candid about the limits of this exercise. If the question rests on a confused premise—if, for example, copyright law is not in the business of metaphysics and the practice is better explained by administrative convenience or statutory history than by any theory of creativity—say so plainly rather than forcing a philosophical reconstruction. I'd rather have a sharp "this framing is wrong" than a polite answer that pretends the practice is more theoretically unified than it is. And if you think the practice is philosophically underdetermined in ways that make the question partly unanswerable, tell me where and why.
