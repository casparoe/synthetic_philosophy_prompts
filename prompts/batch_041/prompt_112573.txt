Background first: I'm a software engineer, no real philosophy training (one ethics elective I mostly slept through), but I've lived in a few countries and worked with people from a lot more, and I keep noticing that my gut reactions to "corruption" type situations don't line up with each other. I want you to tell me what principle, if any, actually captures my reactions, and then test it to destruction.

Here are the cases and my honest gut verdicts. I'm being as concrete as I can.

- A customs officer at the border takes $50 from me "for the paperwork" and moves my shipment up the queue. He was going to process it anyway, just slower. I think this is corruption, full stop, not fine.
- After a friend at a company puts in a good word that helps me land a contract there (he wasn't the one deciding), I give him a nice bottle of wine. I think this is completely fine, just thanking someone.
- A company hires the son of the minister who regulates that company. The son is genuinely qualified and went through the normal process. I can't shake the feeling that this is corrupt, even though nothing improper happened that I can point to.
- My uncle gets me an internship at his firm. I'm qualified. This feels fine, obviously just family.
- A politician steers a highway to her own district when two districts needed it equally badly. Sleazy, wasteful, I'd vote against her, but I don't actually feel it's corruption — more like bad politics.
- A nurse in a public hospital gets slipped some money to bring my mother in to be seen sooner. It's officially free; without the payment she'd be seen, just later in the week; the medical decision is identical either way. This feels wrong to me, and I'd still do it, and I'd call it corruption.
- A company donates to a politician's campaign, and later the politician takes a meeting with them. Fine. That's just how it works.
- Same donation, but now the meeting is followed by a contract award that the company was about to lose on the merits. That's corrupt, clearly.

The tentative principle I'd write down if you forced me: it's corruption when someone in a position of trust receives a private benefit that's tied to how they exercise that trust. But I can already tell it's either too loose or too tight in places — e.g. it seems to nail the nurse and the customs officer, but then why is the campaign donation fine, and why is the minister's qualified son dirty while my qualified uncle's-nephew arrangement isn't? And the wine to my friend — a private benefit tied to how he exercised... something? — should be corrupt by that rule and obviously isn't.

What I want:

1. A principle, or a small set of competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. For each clause, tell me which of my cases it exists to handle.
2. Its verdicts on a few cases I haven't listed. In particular: a purely private-sector version of the minister's-son case (a startup hires the CEO's college roommate, who's qualified); a gift to a public school teacher; a case where the official is elected rather than appointed; and a case where the "bribe" is a favor in kind rather than cash.
3. An honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate. I'd rather drop a verdict than keep some Frankenstein rule that saves all of them — tell me which verdict is the one to give up.
4. And settle the thing I actually keep going back and forth on: a "facilitation payment" for a permit that I'm legally entitled to, which will be granted either way, but takes six months without the payment and two weeks with it. Some days I feel certain it's just case #1 with extra steps; other days it feels more like tipping the nurse, or maybe like nothing at all. Where does your principle put it, and does my wavering itself tell you something about the principle?

One more thing, and don't dodge it: am I just imposing some Western or American standard on arrangements that are normal and even expected elsewhere, or does the principle you land on say something that holds regardless of the local norms? If the honest answer is "it's relative," say so and tell me what that does to the rest of your answer.
