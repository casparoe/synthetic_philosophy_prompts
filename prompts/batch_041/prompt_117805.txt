I am writing a short essay for a general readership on the idea that creativity is essentially variation on a theme, and that the interesting cognitive act is the twiddling of knobs on an inherited conceptual instrument. Before I send it to my editor I would like a critical reader's comments. I am not looking for references and would prefer that you not supply any: the draft is deliberately citation-free, and what I need is an assessment of whether the argument holds up. Two things especially: whether I have characterized Hofstadter fairly, and whether the one idea I add to his is a genuine addition or only a redescription of what he already says.

Draft follows.

**Where the Knobs Come From**

Hofstadter's picture of creativity is a picture of variation. A creative act, on this view, is rarely an invention ex nihilo; it is the exploration of a space of possibilities that a tradition has already laid out, performed by someone who has internalized the space well enough to feel where it can be pushed. He uses the image of a bank of knobs: the maker of a variation reaches for a parameter — tempo, register, dynamics, proportion — and turns it, and the result is at once surprising and recognizable, because it is a displacement of something already there. The engine that makes the turning possible is analogy: to know which knob to reach for is to see the new case as a version of an old one.

The charm of the picture is that it explains why creative work is so reliably kin to what came before it. Its cost is that it presupposes the bank of knobs and does not say where the knobs come from. A knob is only twiddlable if it has positions — if some settings on the underlying continuum count as settings. I will call those positions detents. A dimension with no detents is not a knob but a smear. A melody is twiddlable because pitches and durations and intervals have already been carved into recognizable units; a stream of undifferentiated noise offers nothing to vary, not because it is formless but because there is nothing in it that could be recognized as the same thing again.

Detents, I want to claim, are made rather than found, and making them is the harder and more interesting creative act. Some detents are hard: the fourteen lines of a sonnet, the twelve bars of a blues, the standard grammar of shot and reverse shot. Others are soft and provisional: the house style of a scene, the working conventions of a band in a particular year. What detent manufacture consists in is the act of making one point on a continuum into a natural stopping place — treating a certain duration as the length a song should be, a certain timbre as the sound a guitar should have, a certain pace of cutting as the rhythm a scene should move at. Once a detent has been made, it is available to everyone else, and it does not go away: it ratchets. Later makers twiddle inside a space that earlier makers stocked.

Two consequences follow. The first is that the canonical creative act is not twiddling within a detented space but manufacturing a detent, and this is not straightforwardly an individual cognitive achievement. A detent only works if other people treat it as a stopping place; a private detent is just a habit. This, I suspect, is why so much innovation looks like a scene rather than a genius — not because solitary minds are incapable of novelty, but because novelty that no one else can hear as a position is not yet a position.

The second consequence is a limit case. Consider Calvinball, the game whose rules may be rewritten at will. It looks like pure twiddling with no detents at all, and therefore like the purest possible creativity. But the joke depends on the players' shared knowledge of how games work: the improvised rules borrow their intelligibility from the detents of baseball, tag, and capture the flag. Take those away and nothing can be invented, because there is no difference between a rule and a whim — every move is equally arbitrary, so no move can be a variation. Detents, then, are not a constraint on creativity that a sufficiently creative act transcends. They are its condition.

The obvious objection is that this makes creativity conservative: on my account the creative person is someone who follows conventions and then nudges them. I think the objection misses the point, but I would like to know whether it does. Detent manufacture is the production of new space, not movement within old space; what is conservative in my account is only the requirement that a detent be legible to somebody else. Whether that requirement is a fact about creativity or merely a fact about audiences is, I concede, exactly where the argument is most exposed.

What I would like from you:

1. **Fairness to Hofstadter.** I worry that I have attributed to him a purely inside-the-space model of creativity and then congratulated myself for stepping outside it. He may already hold that creative acts reshape the space rather than merely moving within it — he talks about concepts as fluid, and about slippage between them, in a way that may already contain my detents. If so, tell me: is my notion an addition, a relabeling, or a distinction he draws under other names? If it is a relabeling, say which of his terms does the work, and say whether my version is still worth keeping for expository reasons.

2. **The limit case.** Does the Calvinball argument work, or does it prove too much? I can imagine a reading on which the improvised rules are detents being made in real time rather than borrowed from other games, and that reading might either sink the section or turn it into a better one. Tell me which, and what the section would have to say to survive.

3. **The social turn.** Is the move from individual cognition to coordination a change of subject rather than a consequence of the account? If it is a consequence, name the premise that licenses it and tell me whether you would defend it. If it is a change of subject, tell me whether the essay can survive keeping both halves.

4. **Load-bearing moves.** I make four claims: knobs need detents; detents are manufactured rather than found; manufacture is a coordination achievement; detents ratchet. Which of these is doing the real work, and which could be dropped without loss to the argument? I would rather cut a limb than pad one.

5. **Prose and proportion.** Flag any sentence that asserts something a careful reader would dispute, and any place where I lean on the examples — the sonnet, the blues, the cutting rhythm — to do argumentative work that ought to be done by the argument itself. I am not asking for a rewrite; a diagnosis of where the essay overreaches, and a suggestion about which paragraph should carry a counterexample rather than an illustration, would be more useful.
