Imagine a device, call it a chance mirror. At noon it scans a particular agent, A, and reads her credence that a designated future event E will occur; the reading is a physical state of A's brain. The device then sets the objective chance of E at that moment to a fixed function f of the reading. The whole arrangement — the scan, the function f, the fact that the chance equals f applied to A's credence — is common knowledge to A. Take two versions of the case. In the first, f is the continuous map sending a credence x to 0.5 + x/2. In the second, f is discontinuous: it returns 1 for every reading strictly below 1 and 0 for a reading of exactly 1.

The Principal Principle, in the broad form in which it is usually stated, says that a rational agent's credence in an event, given evidence consisting entirely of matters of fact up to the present, should equal the objective chance of that event. Its conditional form says that her credence in E, given that the chance of E is p, should be p. In the case above A knows f and knows her own credence, so she knows what the chance is; applying the principle yields the equation x = f(x).

Argue for exactly one of two positions. Position A: no arrangement of the kind described can be a genuine chance setup at all, so the Principal Principle emerges untouched. If you take this line, say what a genuine chance setup requires that this one lacks, and defend that requirement against the obvious reply that on a Humean, best-system picture of chance the credences of agents are themselves part of the physical mosaic, and so may legitimately figure in the laws and chances. Position B: arrangements of this kind can be genuine chance setups, so the Principal Principle as usually stated is false or incomplete. If you take this line, state the restriction that repairs it — for instance, a restriction to chances that do not depend on the credences of the agents subject to them, or a retreat to the conditional form — and say precisely what the restriction costs.

Whichever position you take:

- State the principle you are working with in your own terms, in both its unconditional and its conditional form, before you use it.
- Say what happens in the first version of the case and in the second, and do not assume that both are best handled by the same move.
- Address the strongest version of the position you reject, not a caricature of it.
- If your argument turns on a claim about what credences are — normative states, physical states, or both — make that claim explicit and defend it, since it is doing the load-bearing work.
- Note any point at which the puzzle depends on idealizations (exact self-knowledge of one's own credence, a perfectly reliable scanner, a chance fixed at the same instant as the credence) and say whether your argument survives relaxing them.
- Keep the reasoning self-contained: no appeals to what anyone has written, no citations, no claims about the literature.
- Close by naming the objection to your conclusion that you find hardest to answer, and either answer it or say what it would take to do so.
