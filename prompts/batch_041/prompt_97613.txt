Background: a friend and I have been going back and forth on reduction/emergence for like two years and we finally tried to make it precise instead of gesturing. Here's the criterion we landed on — call it the nudge test.

Fix a micro theory T (whatever the deepest currently-accepted dynamics is) and a macro law L stated in coarse variables (second law, Navier-Stokes, whatever). L is *derivational* iff every micro-state in the set S of micro-states that the coarse description picks out evolves under T into macro-behavior matching L. L is *emergent* iff S contains micro-states whose T-evolution violates L. Note this isnt about computability, or chaos, or our ignorance — its about whether the macro regularity is even true of everything the coarse description admits.

My credence that at least one actual macro law in physics is emergent in that sense — genuinely, not just derivational-with-a-sloppily-drawn-line — is about 0.15.

Give me your number, decomposed. I'd guess the terms are roughly:

- P(the micro/macro split is even well-defined, i.e. that "the set S" can be specified without smuggling in a typicality measure that quietly does all the real work)
- P(no deeper level of description exists beneath the one we're calling micro, which would just re-run the whole question one floor down)
- P(the counterexample micro-states, if they exist, are physically possible rather than measure-zero artifacts of how we coarse-grained)
- P(the whole thing is verbal — the gerrymander move, where you can always redefine the coarse variables so L comes out derivational by construction)

then the combination. Tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what would actually move it — a theorem, a simulation, a conceptual distinction, or nothing at all.
