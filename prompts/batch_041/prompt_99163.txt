I am a third-year student in a philosophy of mind course, and for a term paper I have been working through the clinical literature on patients with parietal damage who can feel pain — they localize it, rate its intensity, tell sharp from burning — yet report that it does not bother them and show none of the usual withdrawal, guarding, or distress, sometimes smiling as they describe it. The textbook gloss is that the sensory-discriminative and the affective-motivational components of pain have come apart. Before I write anything, I want the specification rather than another summary of that dissociation. Set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of the badness of pain — the badness specifically, not the nature of pain as a sensation — must meet.

What I need is the full list: the cases the theory has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the clinical or conceptual results it cannot contradict, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and with a note on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp. Two cases I want handled explicitly, because they are where I expect the standard theories to break. The first is the one above — a pain that is felt, even intense, but is not bad for the person having it. The second is the reverse: states in which someone suffers or is distressed without any identifiable pain sensation at all. I would also like you to take seriously a case I have constructed and cannot place. A subject reports that the pain is present and unpleasant, that it hurts, and yet denies that it is bad for her — she is not distressed by it, would not pay to be rid of it, and avoids the stimulus only out of habit or curiosity. If that description is coherent, say what it forces on a theory; if it is not, say which requirement it violates. Throughout, keep separate the things that are too easily run together — badness for the subject, being disliked, being the object of a desire to stop, being experienced as to-be-stopped, and representing a bodily disturbance as bad — and treat their non-collapse as a requirement in its own right.

Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since that is the part I most want, and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular theories. Then, briefly, score the leading contenders — the view that the badness is intrinsic to the sensory quality of pain, the desire or attitudinal views, the imperative and representationalist views, and the evaluativist view that pain represents its object as bad — against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than on the scoring; I can work out the scorecard myself once the specification is right.
