I chaired the truth commission that this country created under the agreement that ended its civil war, and ten years ago this month we handed our final report to the president. There is a ceremony in the old parliament chamber to mark the anniversary — the agreement obliges every signatory to be invited — and I have been asked to give the closing address. Write it.

The room will hold four audiences at once: the families of the dead and the disappeared, some of whom worked with the commission and some of whom refused to; the men we granted amnesty to, a few of whom are now local officials; the government, whose reparations commitment is still largely unmet; and the foreign delegations that paid for the whole exercise.

What we were, for the record. The commission was created by the negotiated agreement rather than by ordinary legislation, and it had no power to prosecute. Amnesty was individual, never blanket: an applicant had to apply, disclose his acts in full, and show that he had acted for political ends; the grant was revocable if he lied. We took statements from some twenty thousand people and heard a fraction of them in public. About four thousand men applied for amnesty; we granted roughly eight hundred and revoked six. Everyone we refused stayed liable to prosecution, and that liability was the other half of the bargain. Our report said so in terms: those refused amnesty were to be prosecuted, and the state was to explain itself where it did not prosecute. It has never explained. In ten years, one senior perpetrator has been convicted — and he was convicted because we revoked his amnesty, not because we refused it. The report named names; two of the men it names are at liberty, never amnestied and never tried, and one of them has accepted his invitation and will be in the room.

I want the address built on the following, and I would rather you let the constraints bite than smooth them over.

1. The vocabulary we used at the time is mostly unavailable. Healing, closure, moving forward, journey: none of these may appear except where the address turns on them and marks them as words we got wrong. The register that asks victims for patience or grace is out as well.

2. Keep three things distinct throughout, and keep them distinct in the wording, not only in intent. Amnesty is a legal act — conditional, revocable, and ours to grant. Forgiveness was never ours: not the commission's, not mine, and we never claimed to deliver it. Acknowledgment is what we actually produced — the state putting its own name to what had happened, so that a victim's account stopped being one party's claim and became the record. Say what acknowledgment gives that information alone does not, and be equally clear about what it does not give: it does not undo, and it does not punish.

3. Confront the enforcement record rather than gesture at it. The condition was the whole of the exchange: amnesty counted as a form of accountability only if non-disclosure was actually sanctioned, and we sanctioned it six times out of four thousand applications. I have spent ten years telling audiences that what we did was a form of justice and not merely the price of peace. The address must either make that claim survive that number or give the claim up; it must not soften the claim into something unfalsifiable.

4. Take a position on the question the families keep asking: did the state owe them punishment, and if it did, were they wronged by the bargain? I have come to think the only honest formula is that what we did was justified without being just — permissible, given what the alternative would have been, and not what anyone was owed. Put that distinction into ordinary language, for an audience that will hear it as a lawyer's evasion, and make it land as a concession rather than a defense. If the address also wants to say that we did justice, it has to show how the two claims are compatible; it does not get to have both without an argument.

5. Address each part of the room directly. To the amnesty recipients, say what the grant did and did not purchase, and what six revocations say to eight hundred grants. To the government, say something about the unpaid reparations that is neither a plea nor an excuse. To the foreign delegations, say something that is not thanks for their money. To the man the report named who is sitting in this room untried, say something that does not pretend he is not there.

6. Somewhere in the address there must be one thing the speaker explicitly declines to say, with the reason for declining it. The candidates I have in mind are an apology, a promise that the reparations will come, and a claim about where the dead are now. Choose, and make the refusal do work rather than perform modesty.

7. The family that refused us from the first week — whose son's body was never found, who has said in public that we gave the murderers their lives back and gave her a document — must be able to hear the whole address without being told that her grievance is irrational. If the position the address takes implies that she has no grievance, change the position, not the tone.

The design is recognizable — it is the conditional-amnesty model, and a few real commissions have used something like it — but do not import the history, personnel, or vocabulary of any actual country, and do not put words into the mouth of anyone but the speaker. Do not name the country or any real party, person, or document; where the address needs a specific, use the roles and facts above. Write it as delivered: no headings, no stage directions, no bracketed notes. Eight to nine minutes, roughly twelve hundred words.
