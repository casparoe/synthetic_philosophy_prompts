For the past several months I've been reporting on the small research community working on what they call eliciting latent knowledge — getting at what a language model actually represents, independently of what it says. Nearly everyone I interview reaches for the same analogy: what we need is a lie detector. A polygraph, on this telling, doesn't listen to the subject's words; it reads the involuntary channel running underneath them. That, they say, is precisely our situation with a model whose outputs have been shaped by training incentives while its internal states still carry the truth. One researcher put it to me plainly: build the equivalent of a polygraph for activations, and the honesty problem is solved — everything after that is engineering.

I'm wary of analogies that end up doing load-bearing work in an argument, so I'd like an audit of this one rather than a verdict on whether models can lie.

Start with the correspondence: in the analogy as my sources use it, what plays the role of the suspect, the interrogation, the question with a known answer, the involuntary physiological channel, the polygraph operator, and the operator's independent knowledge of what actually happened? A table is fine. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I'm most interested in three joints. First, whether the analogy quietly presupposes that there is a fact of the matter about what the model believes, independent of its dispositions to say and do things — a private inner state that the instrument merely reports on — and what becomes of the analogy if that presupposition fails. Second, that a real polygraph operator already knows what happened (the theft, the alibi, the missing money) and the machine only registers a discrepancy, whereas in the AI case the equivalent of ground truth is exactly what is in dispute. Third, that a lie detector tests a specific claim on a specific occasion, which is a long way from the further claim my sources want it to support: that we could catch a model disposed to deceive us in circumstances it has never been tested on.

Finally: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If the latter, what comparison would do better — and separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the intended use from the ones that are merely decorative.
