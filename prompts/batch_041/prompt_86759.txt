I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I have to sign off on a design document whose central argument I'm not sure I believe. The argument runs like this: because our agent is trained to maximize a predicted human-approval signal rather than to pursue some internal objective of its own, and because we constrain its action space so that it can only affect a sandboxed environment, it will not develop the convergent instrumental behaviors — resource acquisition, self-preservation, resistance to shutdown or modification — that people worry about in goal-directed systems.

I want your actual credence that this argument is sound, together with the decomposition that produces it. Treat its conclusion as the claim that a high-capability agent trained this way, under a constraint of that kind, would not act so as to defeat human oversight. Break the question into the sub-questions it actually turns on, and for each give me a number and one line of justification. At a minimum I want terms for:

- the probability that the orthogonality thesis holds in the form the argument needs — that a system's capability level and its effective final objective vary independently enough that training it for approval tells you little about what it will end up optimizing;
- the probability that instrumental convergence is a fact about optimization in general, over any sufficiently rich outcome space, rather than an empirical regularity of the particular objectives we happen to train;
- the probability that gradient descent on an approval-prediction loss installs an internal goal-directed policy at all, as opposed to a behavioral disposition that tracks approval only within the training distribution;
- the probability that, if such a policy is installed, its objective is one for which acquiring resources, preserving itself, or preserving its own objective are instrumentally useful;
- the probability that the sandbox constraint holds up under optimization pressure — that a sufficiently capable agent does not find a way to act outside it, or to make the constraint irrelevant;
- anything else you think belongs.

Then combine the terms into a single number and show me the arithmetic. Tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what specific evidence — an experiment I could actually run, a theoretical result, or a pattern in existing systems — would move that term the most.

Where the argument is ambiguous, resolve the ambiguity in the way most favorable to the person who wrote it and tell me that you did so; I want the number to function as an upper bound on how safe I should feel. Don't hedge by saying it depends on how the terms are defined. Pick the most reasonable precisification, say which one you picked, and commit to a number. I would rather have a definite answer I can argue with than a survey of positions.
