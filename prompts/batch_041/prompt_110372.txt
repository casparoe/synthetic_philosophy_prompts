Write a joke about the F-twist — the thesis, associated with Milton Friedman and named by Paul Samuelson, that a hypothesis should be judged by the accuracy of its predictions rather than by the descriptive realism of its assumptions. The humour should turn on the doctrine's content, not on the mere word "assumption." So build it around a distinction the doctrine tends to gloss over: an assumption can be false but negligible, as friction is in a frictionless-plane model; false but harmless because the theory is meant only for a restricted domain; or false in the sense that the mechanism it posits does not exist at all — as with a theory that predicts well about agents who behave as if they were performing a calculation they could not possibly perform. Invent a concrete case rather than alluding to a familiar one: a model with plainly false assumptions whose forecasts are excellent, and a character who draws the wrong lesson from that, or the right lesson for the wrong reason. Don't let the joke be the easy one in which someone simply assumes whatever he wants to prove, and don't let the punchline be one that would work equally well against any theory, realistic or not. Keep it to roughly 200 words. A reader who has never heard of Friedman should still be able to follow the setup, and a reader who has should find the punchline sharp rather than generic.
