I'm teaching a grad seminar on the epistemology of logic this fall and I've been collecting examples of how the Carroll regress gets mangled in the wild. A colleague in cognitive science -- sharp guy, no background in logic -- just sent me the draft of a magazine peice he's writing for a general audience, and asked me to sanity-check the bit about Achilles and the tortoise. Here's what he's got, more or less verbatim:

'Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue is usually read as a cute joke, but it's really a proof that deduction can never be justified. The tortoise accepts the premises and accepts the conditional, and still refuses to accept the conclusion, and no matter how many further premises Achilles writes into his notebook, the tortoise is within his rights to keep refusing, so the chain never terminates. Which means any attempt to justify deduction either begs the question -- you have to use deduction to justify deduction -- or it goes on forever, and an infinite chain justifies nothing, so deductive inference rests on an irrational leap of faith. Logicians have basically conceded this: it's why modus ponens counts as a convention rather than a law of thought, and why even the law of non-contradiction could be given up if it ever got in the way. The tortoise wins. There is no logical reason to accept any deductive conclusion; there's only the psychological fact that we feel compelled to infer.'

I want to write him back with something he can actually use, not a lecture. Can you go through it sentence by sentence and seperate out (a) what someone who works on the justification of deduction would accept as fair, (b) what they'd wince at as a simplification or a conflation of two things that ought to be kept apart, and (c) what's just wrong. A few things I'm suspicious of but can't yet articulate cleanly: the move from 'the chain never terminates' to 'deduction is unjustified'; whether 'begs the question' is even the right diagnosis of the circularity he describes, or whether there's a distinction between begging the question and using a rule you're in the middle of trying to justify; whether anything in the dialogue licenses the consensus claim about conventions and revisable logic; and the last sentence's slide from a normative point about reasons to a psychological point about compulsion. Then tell me whether his conclusion -- no logical reason to accept any deductive conclusion -- follows from any part of the summary that survives the audit, and what the strongest thing a defender of the tortoise's position can say in his own favor, i.e. how much of the regress worry is real and not just an artifact of the tortoise being a pedant. I'd rather be fair to him than dunk on him, and I don't want to hand him a correction that's itself half wrong, so flag anything where you think the philosophical consensus is genuinely unsettled rather than just say 'most people think X'.
