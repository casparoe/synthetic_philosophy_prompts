For the past few weeks I've been trying to write up, for a graduate seminar on practical reason, a puzzle I keep running into under the heading of transformative experience — and I've ended up with an argument I can't decide whether to trust, because it seems to prove far too much. I'd like you to diagnose it rather than solve the underlying topic.

The setup I'm working with is this. Some experiences are epistemically transformative: before you have one, there is a kind of knowledge of what it is like that you simply cannot possess, and this isn't a matter of missing information you could in principle gather — it's a difference in the sort of knowledge at issue. Some of these experiences are also personally transformative: having them changes what you care about, so that your preferences before and after cannot be ranked on a single scale. With that in hand, the argument runs:

(1) To choose rationally between options is to select the option that, by the lights of the reasons available to you, you have most reason to expect is at least as valuable as the alternatives.
(2) You have reason to expect an outcome is valuable only if you can represent its value — you must be able to grasp, at least in outline, what you would be getting.
(3) For an epistemically transformative outcome, you cannot grasp what you would be getting before you have it.
(4) Where the experience is also personally transformative, you cannot even rank the outcome against your present options, because your present values supply no common currency with the ones you would have afterward.
(5) Therefore, for any choice whose outcome is transformative in both these ways, there is no fact about what you have most reason to do, and the choice cannot be made rationally.
(6) But the decisions that matter most in a life — whether to become a parent, whether to take up a vocation, whether to leave a country for good — are precisely of this kind.
(7) A theory of practical rationality on which the weightiest decisions of a life fall outside the space of reasons has proved too much; whatever is wrong must be wrong somewhere above.

A friend who looked at this says the argument is fine as it stands and that the honest response is to accept that our biggest choices are simply arational, and I find that unbearable, because the entire interest of the phenomenon is that it feels unlike ordinary ignorance — I don't think I'm merely choosing under uncertainty when I imagine these cases, and I don't think the friend is either. I'd most like to keep (1) and (6), since those are the observations that started me off, and I'd surrender them only under protest. So please tell me which step to reject, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what it costs to give it up. In particular, I want to know whether the damage is confined to a single premise, or whether it spreads to (1), to (4), or to the distinction between the two kinds of transformation that the argument leans on — and if it spreads, which of those casualties I should be least willing to accept.
