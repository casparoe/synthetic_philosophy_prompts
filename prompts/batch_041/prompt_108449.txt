I retired about four years ago after 35 years in control engineering (flight control first, then a long stretch on software for medical devices), and since then I've been doing philosophy part-time, mostly ethics of technology. Next month I'm giving a talk at a two-day workshop on law and automated systems, and I want to be ready for the question period.

Heres my thesis, roughly. The responsibility gap for autonomous systems isn't a metaphysical hole, it's an artifact of what I've started calling the single-operator assumption — the tacit idea that for a harm to be properly attributable it has to land on one agent who could have done otherwise at the moment the harm occurred. I want to argue that we should drop that and trace instead: every harm traces back to some role in the pipeline — whoever specified the objective, whoever chose and cleaned the training data, whoever deployed the thing, the operator on shift, the maintainer, the certifier — and each of those roles carries its own obligations. For any given harm, ask which role's obligations went unmet and whether the role-holder had both the information and the authority to meet them. If they had both, they're answerable; if not, the unmet obligation belongs one rung up the ladder. I also keep a three-way split in my pocket: being the author of a harm, being the one who owes an explanation for it, and being the one who ends up paying for it. I think a lot of the gap discussion collapses those three and that most of the confusion lives there.

The audience is mostly not philosophers. Two torts and insurance people who think everything reduces to who pays and whether it's insurable (they'll be thinking strict liability versus negligence the whole time). One moral-responsibility philosopher who works on the control condition and will not let go of it. A criminal law person who cares about mens rea and about how we treat corporate defendants. Someone from a safety regulator. An engineer from rail signalling who thinks the whole debate is navel-gazing. And a grad student who will ask about deskilling.

Before you write anything, brainstorm for a bit about what each of these people actually cares about and what they take to be obvious — the objections I get will be downstream of that. Then, writing as someone who knows both this literature and how academic question periods actually go, give me my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll really get, ordered by how much damage they do to me rather than just how likely they are, each phrased the way that particular person would put it, each with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and a call on whether I should just concede and move on. Flag any of the eight that are really the same objection wearing different hats, tell me what to concede in my opening so I dont have to fight it later, and include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. I want a sheet I can reread in the cab, not an essay.

Last thing: be upfront about how confident you are in each of these calls, especially your read on this audience, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
