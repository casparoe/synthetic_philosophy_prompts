I write about design and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I am at work on a feature about the strange stability of the letter a: how a shape brushed by a medieval scribe, a shape cut in neon tubing, and a shape squeezed out of a five-by-seven pixel grid can all be the same letter, while a mirror image of any of them is a different letter entirely. In the piece I want to argue against what I have come to call the typographer's Platonism, and I would rather test that argument against its strongest defender than against a straw man.

So play the defender. You are a type designer of long experience and considerable reputation, and you hold as a settled conviction that each letter has an essence — a set of structural commitments that any shape must honor in order to be that letter — and that style is freedom only within those commitments. You are sharp, not a caricature: concede real points when they are real, never the main one, and never take refuge in the claim that it is all convention. You may ask me questions, but answer mine first. Speak like someone who has spent forty years looking at shapes and has limited patience for philosophy. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, and stay in character until I say stop.

My opening. Imagine a chain of designers. The first draws an a and hands it to the second; the second looks at it, sets it aside, and draws an a from memory; the third does the same with the second's drawing, and so on. No drawing is ever seen by anyone but the next person in the line, and each step is a small, unremarkable deformation. Two hundred steps later, the last shape is a hook and a loop that I would not call a letter at all — and yet each of the two hundred designers, looking at the shape in front of them, judged it an a. Here is my problem for your essence. If the essence is a property of the drawings, it ought to be the thing that survives all two hundred steps, and by your own account it does not. If instead it lives in the judgments — each designer calling the next shape an a because it is close enough to the last — then the essence is a habit of the eye, manufactured one step at a time, and there is nothing left for your Platonism to be about. Notice also what your essence has to cover: the two-storey a of a book face, with its bowl and its arched hood, and the one-storey a of a geometric sans, which is a circle with a stem. Tell me where the essence lives, and tell me what you would accept as a refutation.
