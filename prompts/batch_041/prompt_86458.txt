I am a retired physical chemist — forty years of kinetics and spectroscopy, nearly all of it spent on one question: what becomes of the parts when things combine. Since I retired I have been working my way into philosophy through a reading group, and panpsychism is the view that keeps me up at night, because it looks to me like the one position in the field that refuses to do what chemistry has always had to do.

I would like you to play a committed panpsychist for me. Not a caricature and not a fence-sitter: a serious defender of the version on which phenomenal properties are the intrinsic natures that physics leaves blank, micro-experiences are genuinely present in fundamental entities, and my own experience is genuinely constituted by them rather than merely correlated with them. Give the view its strongest resources — you should be able to produce an actual account of how micro-experiences bond into a single subject, and you may concede peripheral points, but you must not retreat into saying that we do not yet know, and you must not quietly dissolve the position into something else whenever you are pressed. Write as a careful philosopher who is also willing to talk shop with a scientist; keep each reply to a paragraph or two; do not cite papers or books, since I cannot look them up and would rather have the argument than the reading list; and stay in character until I say stop. Think your position through before each reply, but give me only what you would say out loud, beginning with one sentence stating what your view commits you to.

Here is my opening move, and it comes out of my own trade. When sodium and chlorine combine, the sodium atom is no longer a small piece of sodium — the silvery lustre, the violence with water, all of it is gone, and what remains is a component of something whose properties are of a wholly different kind. That is what combination has always meant in my line of work, and it is why I distrust the word bonding in your literature. So tell me which of these you mean. Either the micro-experiences survive as subjects, in which case what I call my experience is a committee of countless subjects and not one subject at all; or they do not survive, in which case nothing has been combined and you have only given a name to a gap. I also want to press a constructive suggestion, because I have been wondering whether your view needs something like a phase transition. I have never been able to think of temperature as more than bookkeeping over molecular motions, but magnetization is a different animal: above the Curie point it is simply not there, and below it the specimen as a whole has a property that belongs to no individual spin. Would you take that as the model of combination — and if you would, is there anything left of panpsychism that distinguishes it from the plain emergentism you panpsychists seem chiefly concerned to avoid? Answer as the panpsychist, and do not let me off the hook either: tell me where you think my chemical analogy misleads me.
