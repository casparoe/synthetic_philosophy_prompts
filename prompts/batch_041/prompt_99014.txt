Right, so I run a third-year metaphysics seminar and the last session is on individuation — we close on the identity of indiscernibles and Black's two spheres, the pair of identical iron spheres in an otherwise empty universe that's meant to be the counterexample to the principle. I'd like to open that session with a short poem rather than a mini-lecture, something we can read out and then argue about for an hour.

Here's the shape I want. The poem should be a mirror: eleven lines, where the last five repeat the first five in reverse order, so a single line is left standing at the centre as the axis of symmetry. Read it top-down or bottom-up and it should be the same poem — which is what the universe in the case is like, perfectly symmetric, nothing anywhere to break the tie. But the reader still counts two halves. That's the whole point of it: one poem, and also two halves that nothing distinguishes, the only difference between them being where they sit. If you can manage it, make the mirroring *near*-exact rather than exact — one word that won't quite fold — and let the poem notice that, because that's where the honest bit lives. Either the halves come out word-for-word the same, in which case you've simply repeated yourself and we're back at the triviality worry, or the difference has to be imported from somewhere: position, or a thisness you can only point at, or the property of being the left-hand one, which is the cheating move. (If a nod to the modern twist fits — the one where 'identical' particles are supposed to be told apart only by relations that belong to neither of them on its own — take it, but dont force it in.)

Don't name-drop. Nobody in the poem should be saying 'Leibniz' or 'indiscernibility'. Someone who knows the literature should recognise the case and find the poem right; someone who doesn't should just find it a decent poem. Twenty lines absolute maximum, and it mustn't turn into an essay in verse.

Then give me a short note at the end, three or four sentences, on where the poem takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading of the case, and what would change your mind about how it has to be written: if you count 'being this sphere' as a genuine property, or if you think absolute position is real after all, does the poem come out differently? And say where you're least confident you've got the philosophy right.
