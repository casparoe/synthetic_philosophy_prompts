I read Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five years ago, and a friend and I have been arguing about it ever since, so this is really a request to settle an argument. I should say at the outset that I have no philosophical training; everything I know about the question below I have picked up on my own, so forgive me if I state it clumsily.

The question is what it is for a thing to last through time, and as I understand it there are two camps. One says that a thing is wholly present at every moment of its life — that Billy Pilgrim is entirely there in the prison car and entirely there again in the hospital, the same single thing each time, with no part of him left behind in 1944. The other says that lasting things are stretched out in time the way they are stretched out in space, so that what really exists is a long four-dimensional worm, and the Billy you meet at any given moment is only a slice of him, the way one cross-section of a sausage is not the sausage. I believe these camps are called endurantists and perdurantists.

What I keep noticing is that people treat the novel as a poster child for the second camp, and I am not convinced. The Tralfamadorians never tell Billy that he is a worm made of slices. They tell him that all the moments of his life are equally real and already laid out, so that his death is not the end of him but one moment among the others, and that he can be unstuck from one moment and set down in another. That seems to me to be a claim about time itself — about whether the past and the future are as real as the present — rather than a claim about what it is to be the kind of thing that persists. My suspicion is that the book, and most of its readers, run those two questions together, and I would like you to tell me whether the charge is fair.

So please read the novel through this debate and say which side, if either, it actually lands on — or whether it is really making the other, separate point and quietly using it as though it had settled this one. Work from the scenes rather than from my summary: the war film the Tralfamadorians show Billy run backwards, with the bombs drawn back up out of the ruins and into the planes; the glass dome in the zoo, where he is displayed and where the view is explained to him; the refrain the book repeats every time somebody dies, including Billy's own death, which he already knows the date of; and the narrator himself, who is not a Tralfamadorian and who insists on Dresden as something that really happened. Be honest about where the novel refuses the reading, too, and where the view that consoles Billy would be a scandal if you took it seriously.

I do not want a reading list or citations; I will not check them and I would rather you just argue. What I am after in the end is what the novel shows about this question that the philosophical argument on its own cannot.
