ok so everyone keeps asserting "instrumental convergence" like it's settled — power-seeking is convergent, self-preservation is convergent, any agent smart enough will want these things. before I buy any of it I want to know what an actual theory of this would have to deliver. so write me the desiderata: what does any adequate account of instrumental convergence have to get right? assume I want it good enough for one job — telling me whether I should be worried about a particular trained model ending up power-seeking.

Here's what I think has to be on the list, with the case behind each, but tell me if I'm wrong about what belongs:

- it has to be a claim about a *class* of goals and environments, not one goal. So what's the quantifier structure, and why does "for most goals in most situations" do real work — and where can it fail?
- it has to survive the obvious counterexamples. An agent whose final goal is already fixed no matter what it does — I've heard the example of one that only cares about a particular digit of pi, or only about whether some event in the past happened. An account that predicts power-seeking for those is dead on arrival.
- goals where more power is actively *bad* — a fragile goal, one that requires not disturbing something, or one that needs the agent to stay weak or ignorant. The theory can't just call those exotic and move on.
- does it hold for optimal agents only, or for realistic / learned / suboptimal ones too? I suspect the confident claims quietly assume optimality and then get applied to actual models. If that assumption is load-bearing, the theory has to admit it.
- it has to be seperate from the orthogonality thesis. Orthogonality is about final goals being independent of capability; convergence is about instrumental goals. I don't want a theory that only works if you already bought orthogonality, or one that secretly assumes some particular final goal.
- what counts as 'power'? The account needs a notion it can actually reason with (optionality over outcomes? influence? resources?) or 'power-seeking' is unfalsifiable.
- what observation would count as evidence either way? Not citations — just what would make you lower your credence.
- it must keep convergent, universal, and logically necessary apart. People conflate these constantly.

Then: which of these are genuine requirements and which are just preferences of one camp (say, people who want the strong doom-y version)? Which single requirement, enforced strictly, knocks out the most popular versions of the thesis? And flag any two requirements you think can't both be met — that's the part I most want to see. A table is fine.

and please don't just recite the usual list of convergent instrumental goals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, whatever. I've seen that. I want the spec, not the roster.
