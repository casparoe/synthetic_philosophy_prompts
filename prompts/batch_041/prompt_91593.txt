Novelist here, and I keep bumping into something in my own trade that I can't make sense of philosphically, so I'm hoping you can tell me what my profession actually believes.

Here's the cluster of things. Everyone in publishing says you can't own an idea, only the way it's written down. If I hear a great premise at a party and write a novel on it, that's legal and, most people would say, fine. But: my agent won't read unsolicitted manuscripts, and the reason given is always that if she reads someone's unpublished novel and then a similar book comes out, she'll be accused of stealing. Editors I know won't open an envelope that might contain a story idea. If I read a friend's draft and two years later publish something with a similar twist, I get quietly frozen out of that friendship even though I never used a word of hers. So we all say ideas aren't property, and then we behave, day to day, exactly as if they were, as if the causal history of where I got something had a bearing on whether it's mine.

Second wrinkle. What actually gets you in trouble, legally and socially, is sequence of words: a copied paragraph, a lifted sentence. Lift an entire plot, the structure, the whole emotional architecture, rewrite every sentence, and you're mostly in the clear. But surely the plot was the harder, more original thing? So the rule is grabbing hold of something much narrower than the thing we intuitively think of as the creation.

My partner teaches history and has to cite where every idea came from, and my brother is a chemist and in his world you can patent a molecule. Nobody in mine can patent a plot. So it's not as if this is just how ownership of the intangible always works. It seems to work differently in each shop.

What I want from you: what does this practice presuppose about what a creation is, and about why anything can be owned at all? Is there a real theory underneath it, about labor or incentives or personality or fixability or boundaries, or is it a pile of historical accidents wearing a theory as a costume? Which parts does the practice strictly need, and which does it just happen to sit comfortably with? And if you swapped the underlying view out, say for one on which ideas genuinely were ownable and influence counted as theft, what would actually have to change in how we work?

Also, tell me how confident you are in what you say, and flag the one point you're least sure about.
