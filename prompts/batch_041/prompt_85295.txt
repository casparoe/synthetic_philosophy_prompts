I am a retired immunologist — thirty years in vaccine development — who has spent the last few years reading moral philosophy with more enthusiasm than discipline, and I now sit on my state health department's public communications review panel, which must approve any message sent to patients before release. On Thursday we vote on a letter to parents whose children are overdue for a routine childhood vaccination. As drafted, it reports only the relative reduction in infection seen in the licensing trials and omits the absolute risks, the natural history of the infection, and the number needed to vaccinate; every sentence in it is true. In our pilot across roughly four thousand households, that version outperformed our current materials by about nine percentage points, with the gain concentrated among parents who had previously declined or delayed, while a version adding the absolute figures and the natural history performed no better than what we already send. Our charter requires only that messages be "accurate and not misleading" — the second term is left undefined — and separately forbids "exploiting known cognitive limitations," a clause added after a complaint about an earlier smoking-cessation campaign. The panel is split: one faction holds that true statements cannot mislead, the other that a letter leaving parents with a false picture of the benefit is misleading whatever its individual sentences say; both sides have noticed that our already-approved materials lean on patient testimonials and a photograph of a child who died, which is not evidence either. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote: recommend a concrete disposition of the letter, give reasons my colleagues can actually argue with, and state the strongest case for the position you reject, including what your recommendation implies for the material we have already approved.
