You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory and the design of sortition-based institutions. Before answering, spend some time brainstorming the main normative and epistemic functions that selection by lot is meant to serve—legitimacy, descriptive representation, deliberation, resistance to capture, accountability—and the standard objections to lottocratic bodies. Then consider a proposal I will call recursive lottocracy. Under it, a permanent citizens' chamber of 200 is chosen by lot, but members do not have fixed terms: each is rotated out when a randomly drawn pool of 50 citizens votes to replace them. The chamber also controls the selection frame—the categories used to stratify the next lottery—and may revise those categories each cycle in light of testimony about who is affected by its decisions. Please describe the most important implications of this design for the legitimacy, accountability, and deliberative quality of a lottocratic system. Be concrete about the trade-offs it creates, the ways it might solve or worsen familiar problems such as amateurism, staff capture, and the tension between descriptive and deliberative representation, and any unintended consequences that follow specifically from making the selection frame revisable by the chamber itself.
