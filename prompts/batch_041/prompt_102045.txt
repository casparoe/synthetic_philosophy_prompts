I'm a retired entomologist -- thirty-odd years on ant colonies, never once called myself a philosopher -- but since I retired a friend handed me one of those popular evolution books and I've found myself arguing with the page more than reading it. The chapter on the units-of-selection debate summarizes things roughly like this: "For a long time biologists assumed selection could work for the good of the species or the group, until Williams, and then Dawkins, showed that this is a mistake -- selection only ever acts on genes. The organism is just a vehicle the genes build to haul themselves around, and groups are even less real than that. Kin selection isn't really an alternative to this picture; it's just the selfish gene doing its sums, since a gene that helps copies of itself inside a relative is still acting selfishly. And group selection was finally laid to rest when Maynard Smith showed it could only ever get off the ground in contrived setups like the haystack model. These days all that's left of the old arguement is a quarrel about words -- whether you describe the same mathematics as gene selection or as group selection."

I'd like you to audit this for me sentence by sentence. What would someone who actually works on this -- a philosopher of biology, or a theoretical population geneticist -- happily accept, what would make them wince, and what is just wrong? I'm especially unsure about two things. First, I keep hearing that kin selection and group selection are somehow "the same math," which dosen't square with the chapter treating one as the real story and the other as dead; is the book conflating a bookkeeping equivalence with a substantive disagreement, or am I missing something? Second, I came away thinking the debate is basically over and only semantics remains, and I'm inclined to believe that -- but I have a nagging feeling the chapter got there by overstating a modest thesis into a strong one. Tell me which sentences a defender of each position would object to and why, whether the "it's all just words" conclusion survives once the errors are fixed, and where I've been seeded with a framing I should be suspicious of.
