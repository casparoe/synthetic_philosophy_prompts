I'm trying to put together a proposal for a little internal grant our department runs — six months, a bit of money, and the deliverable is basically a 15–20 page working paper — and I've got a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into something presentable. The literature on civil disobedience always seems to get stuck on the same two questions: when is it justified, and what makes it 'civil' in the first place — public, nonviolent, willing to take the punishment, addressed to the majority's sense of justice. I keep thinking the second question is where the action is, and that it's been framed wrong. Everyone treats the point of the disobedient act as persuading the political community: appealing to its sense of justice, bringing an injustice to its attention. But a lot of the disobedience that actually mattered wasn't trying to persuade anyone in power at all — it was trying to change the facts on the ground so the unjust arrangement couldn't keep running. Sit-ins that made segregation unenforceable, blockades that made a project too expensive to finish, strikes that made a workplace unusable. I've been calling this the difference between persuasion and prefiguration, or between talking to the powerful and acting on the world. If you take that seriously, the civility criteria start to look like they were built to protect the wrong thing: publicity and nonviolence make sense if your aim is to move a majority, but if your aim is to change the facts, anonymity and disruption can be features rather than bugs — and even the prior question of whether there's a general duty to obey the law shifts, because the answer might depend on which of the two games you're playing.

So: write me the proposal. I want (a) the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — my stab is something like 'does the justification of civil disobedience depend on its being addressed to the political community, or can it rest entirely on what the act does to the world?', but sharpen it or replace it if you can do better; (b) an account of why the going positions — the appeal-to-justice line, the fair-play line, the deliberative-democracy line — leave this open instead of settling it; (c) what I'd actually do over six months, in some roughly month-by-month form, and why that method rather than just sitting down and writing a conceptual essay; (d) what would count as the project failing, or as the question turning out to be ill-posed; and (e) an honest verdict on whether this is six months of work or the seed of a dissertation I'm pretending is small.

Don't give me a reading list or citations — I'll add those myself, and honestly I care more about whether the question is worth the money than about who's said what. What I do want is for you to push back: if the persuasion/prefiguration distinction collapses (maybe changing the facts is just a slow, indirect way of persuading), tell me so, and tell me what the better cut would be. And keep in mind this is going to a departmental audience that's allergic to anything that smells like old arguements re-described in new words.
