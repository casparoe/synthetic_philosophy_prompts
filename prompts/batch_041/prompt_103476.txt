I'm working through Aristotle's De Anima for a seminar on the history of philosophy of mind, and I've gotten stuck on a passage in Book III, chapter 2 that my classmates and I keep reading backwards through contemporary higher-order theories of consciousness. The translation I'm working from renders the opening of the chapter this way: "Since it is through sense that we are aware that we are seeing or hearing, it must be either by sight that we are aware of seeing, or by some sense other than sight. But the sense that gives us this new sensation must perceive both sight and its object, viz. colour: so that either (1) there will be two senses both percipient of the same sensible object, or (2) the sense must be percipient of itself." He then adds that even if the sense perceiving sight were different from sight, we would either fall into an infinite regress or have to posit somewhere a sense aware of itself, and he concludes that "to perceive by sight" has more than one meaning. What I want is a patient reconstruction of the argument as Aristotle actually gives it, before any modern gloss is laid over it, and then an honest accounting of how much of it survives the transfer.

In particular, I need help with four things. First, what exactly is the regress, and why does Aristotle treat it as intolerable rather than merely inconvenient — is it vicious in the strong sense, or is he simply refusing an infinite demand on other grounds, and what would a defender of a separate higher-order sense have to say in reply? Second, what does it mean for a sense to be "percipient of itself," and how does that self-perception differ from a distinct higher-order state that takes a first-order perception as its object? I keep sliding between two readings — one on which the awareness of seeing is somehow intrinsic to the seeing itself, and one on which it is a numerically distinct act — and I can't tell from the text which Aristotle holds, or whether he thinks the question is well posed. Third, how does this chapter hang together with the earlier distinction between special and common sensibles and with incidental perception (the example about perceiving the white thing and only incidentally perceiving it as someone's son), since the common-sensibles discussion seems to do some of the same work by a different route. Fourth, and this is the one my seminar keeps fighting about: if we do read Aristotle as an ancestor of higher-order perception or higher-order thought theories, does he inherit their worst problem, namely that a higher-order state can misrepresent the first-order state it is about, so that it cannot be what makes that first-order state conscious in the first place? Does anything in his hylomorphic psychology — the claim that the sense faculty and its activity are one in being, or the way he treats actualization — block the misrepresentation worry, or does the worry simply not arise for him because he is asking a different question?

I'd also like to be told where my translation may be leading me astray: whether the English "aware" is smuggling in a cognitive or judgmental flavor that the Greek does not carry, and whether the verb rendered as "perceive" in the regress argument is doing the same work as the verb rendered "discriminate" a few lines later, or whether the shift in vocabulary is itself a clue. Please keep the answer to the text and the concepts — I don't need reading lists or citations, and I'd rather have the difficulty left visible than smoothed over with a tidy summary.
