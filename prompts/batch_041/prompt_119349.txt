I write backend software for a living and never took a philosophy course, so apologies in advance for the vocabulary. A friend recently lent me Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979), and the part that has been rattling around in my head is the dialogue about Aunt Hillary, the ant colony: every ant in it is doing something small and mindless, and yet the colony as a whole is presented as a single creature that thinks, remembers, and talks. What stuck with me wasn't the argument so much as a structural wrinkle — a colony is an entity whose every component gets replaced while its identity survives the replacement, which is uncomfortably close to the six-year-old service I maintain whose original machines are all long gone from the rack. Four of us argued about it over dinner until midnight, got nowhere, and I've wanted to see somebody do something with the idea ever since.

So here is what I'd like: a mock obituary, about 600 words, for an ant colony of the Aunt Hillary kind — individually mindless insects, collectively a person — written in the deadpan register of a small-town newspaper's death notice, with a boxed summary at the top (Name, Age, Residence, Occupation, Cause of death, Survived by) followed by the standard furniture: a dateline, a life story, a paragraph of survivors, a line about memorial donations, and one quotation from a colleague who is not entirely convinced the deceased ever existed. Set it in a world where the paper has been running notices like this for years and nobody on the copy desk finds anything odd about it; the comedy should come from the form quietly failing, never from a character remarking that the situation is strange.

A few things the piece has to get right, because they are the whole point. The age in the box should be the colony's, even though not one ant alive at its death was alive at its founding, and the founding queen died years before the colony did. The cause of death should be a backhoe, with the great majority of the ants surviving and wandering off, so that the person dies while nearly all of its parts live. The survivors should be at once her daughters, her body, and strangers she never met, and the notice should not be able to say how many of them there are or whether any of them lost anything. The mind should never be located anywhere, and no surviving ant should have noticed a thing. Let the colleague's quote leave the question of her existence genuinely open rather than settling it either way, and let a reader finish the piece able to see, without being told, that the same obituary would work for a city, a company, or a person. Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking, no explaining, no mention of philosophy, and no real books, scholars, or sources inside the piece; invent the town, the paper, and the people. It also has to stand on its own for someone who has never heard of Hofstadter.
