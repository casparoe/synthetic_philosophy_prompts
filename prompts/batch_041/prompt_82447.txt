For about a year now I've been trying to settle, for myself, where the line falls between what I owe other people and what I merely may do for them, and I've reached the point where I'd like an outside opinion, because the way I've been thinking about it lately may be doing more harm than good. The practical question is this. I'm thirty-four, no children, a job I'm competent at and indifferent to, and enough savings that I could take a year off without much damage. My aunt, who lives two hours away, is in the early stages of dementia and has nobody else nearby; my cousins have scattered. I could take unpaid leave, move in with her for a year, and manage her care until something more permanent is arranged. My sister says that settles it: if I'm the one who can do it, and the alternative is that my aunt goes into a home she'll hate, then the year is mine to give, and calling it a sacrifice is just vanity. My partner says the opposite — that my sister has turned my life into a debt I never agreed to, and that there is a real difference between what is demanded of me and what is merely admirable. I don't trust either of them, partly because I suspect each is defending the answer that suits them.

What I'd like help with is the framework I've been using, which I'll lay out because I can't tell whether it's an insight or a rationalization. I picture morality as having a floor and a stretch of open ground above it. The floor is what I have to do: keep my promises, don't lie, don't let someone go hungry when feeding them would cost me little. Above the floor, I've been assuming, nothing is required, but some things are still better than others, and when I do them I'm building up something like a balance. So when I spent four weekends last spring helping a friend renovate a house I had no stake in, I told myself I'd banked something; and when I skipped my best friend's wedding in the fall for no good reason, I told myself the banked surplus covered it. My sister says this is just indulgences — that I've invented a currency in which to buy exemptions from obligations I actually have.

My question is whether she's right. If an act really is optional, can it be spent like that, or does the very fact that I can spend it show it was never optional, only a different kind of debt? And if the floor-and-surplus picture can't survive that objection, is there a version of it that can — something that tells me where the floor sits without collapsing into the claim that everything I could possibly do is owed? Finally, and most practically: given whatever you conclude, what should I do about my aunt?
