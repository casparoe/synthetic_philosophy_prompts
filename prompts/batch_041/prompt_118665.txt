Next month I am to give the toast at the retirement dinner of the woman who hired me onto a small trading desk twenty-three years ago and who has run the firm ever since. Roughly half the room spent their working lives on that desk and will catch every term of art; the other half are her family, who have known for thirty years that she was in finance and have long since stopped asking follow-up questions. I would like you to write the toast.

So that you have the ideas in full — they are not the ideas most of the room will assume, and I would rather you work from what I set out here than from whatever the phrase 'risk management' brings with it — here is what she and I believed together. She held that the correct size of any bet is the one that maximizes the expected logarithm of the stake, which is the same as the one that maximizes the rate at which the stake compounds, and that this is emphatically not the same thing as maximizing expected value. Her favorite observation, produced reliably at the least appropriate moments, was that across a long sequence of multiplicative bets the average over many imaginary copies of a bettor can rise steadily while the single path that any actual bettor walks does not, and that everything worth knowing about prudence lives in that gap. She treated ruin as absolute: there is no logarithm of zero, so no edge, however large, licenses a stake that can take you to zero, and a strategy that can reach zero has no growth rate at all. She held that when you are guessing at the edge rather than reading it off you should stake only a fraction of the growth-maximizing amount, because the cost of overbetting is steeper than the cost of underbetting, and because she wanted to be able to say of every bet she ever made that it had been a little too small. She gave the whole stance a name and used it to embarrass me in front of clients — geometric rationality, by which she meant that the question is always about the rate at which something compounds and the avoidance of zero, and that where the calculation is uncertain a deliberately mixed or modest position beats the calculated optimum. And she took the further step that made her impossible to argue with after the second glass of wine: that none of this is really about money. A life, a reputation, a marriage, a firm and a friendship are also things that compound, and the question to ask of any plan is not what it is worth on average but what it does to the thing being compounded, and whether it can take you to zero.

Two complications. First, since announcing her retirement she has been telling anyone who will listen that she intends to make one last large bet on a single idea, and everything above is precisely an argument against doing that. I want the toast to register this without scolding her, so that her family hear affection and the desk hear the argument. Second, the obvious objection to all of it is that the logarithm of wealth is just one utility function among many and the mathematics singles nothing out. I think that objection is correct, and she never had an answer to it beyond a shrug — she was not trying to derive a universal rationality, only to describe the arithmetic of a life that has to be lived once and in order. The toast must not overclaim on her behalf and must not say anything either of us would regard as false; in particular I do not want the consolation of the words 'on average', or 'in the long run it worked out', which she would not accept and I do not believe.

Write the toast itself: about five minutes spoken, warm, built around one concrete story rather than a survey of her career. It should not define or explain any of the ideas above to the audience, and it should give no investment advice. It should be intelligible to the family, and it should end in a way that someone who believes what is set out above could actually mean rather than merely applaud.
