A friend in my program is writing on Augustine and lying and dropped this on our reading group last week; we argued for two hours and got nowhere. He thinks it shows Augustine has to allow some deception, which he really doesnt want, and i want to keep the no-exceptions rule if i possibly can. Here's his argument, tidied up:

(1) For Augustine a lie is a false signification joined with the will to deceive: you lie when you utter (or otherwise signify) one thing while holding another in your mind, intending your hearer to take the signification as true. Two corollaries he's explicit about — if you assert a falsehood you take to be true you don't lie, and if you assert a truth you take to be false you do lie. So a lie is judged "from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves."

(2) He also says the fault of the liar just is "the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind," and he permits no exceptions whatever — not to save a life, not to protect someone's chastity, not even to catch heretics (he wrote a whole book against lying to trap the Priscillianists).

(3) Now the murderer at the door. You can't say "he's not here" — that's a lie. So you say something true: "I haven't seen him all day." True; you saw him last night. You say it because you know the murderer will take it that your friend isn't around.

(4) You wanted the murderer to believe something false about your friend's whereabouts. That's the desire to deceive — the very thing (2) names as the fault. (He has a second case too: you say nothing at all, you just lift your friend's coat off the hook and put it in the closet so the murderer draws the conclusion for himself. No signification at all, so not a lie by (1) — but you still willed his false belief.)

(5) Yet you didn't lie. You said what you believed, and in the coat case you signified nothing.

(6) So either (a) the desire to deceive is not what makes lying wrong — in which case (2)'s account of the fault is wrong — or (b) it is, in which case the true-but-misleading sentence and the coat trick are as bad as the outright lie, and Augustine's insistence that you may conceal the truth but never lie is empty: you can never protect anyone, and tact, surprise parties, bluffing at cards, and half of ordinary conversation turn into sins. Either way Augustine loses. Since he won't give up (2) and can't swallow (b), he says (1) has to be the guilty step — the will to deceive can't be part of what makes a lie a lie. (He tried escaping through Augustine's exemption for jokes — "jokes, which have never been accounted lies" — but that plainly doesn't reach the murderer, so it didn't help him.)

Which step do i reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather keep (2), the no-exceptions rule, than keep (1) intact if it comes to that — but tell me what i'm buying, and what it does to the coat case. Lead with the step you'd reject in your first sentence, then argue for it.
