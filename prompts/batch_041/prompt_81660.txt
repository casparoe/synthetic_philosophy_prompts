Here's the question:

Our handout said that when people argue about lying they usually mean the sentence said something false. But a lot of real conversation is spent not saying anything false while still getting someone to believe something untrue. The handout split indirect speech into two jobs it can do:

1. Deniability — hedges, vagueness, non-answers, of the kind that leave the speaker a way out, so that they never actually claimed anything. ("I'm not sure that would be possible for us.")
2. Cushioning — softening that protects the hearer from the sting of what's being said. ("That's a really interesting direction.")

Using this distinction, compare Dutch directness with Japanese indirectness. Then say whether a white lie is ever morally ok, and which of the two styles you would call "more honest", if either. Finally, say who you think ends up responsible when someone is misled by something that was never actually stated.

And here's what Maya wrote:

Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness look like opposites but I think they're doing the same job with different tools. Dutch speakers tend to see bluntness as respect: if I tell you your plan has a problem, I'm treating you as someone who can handle it and I'm not wasting your time. In Japanese the same information is often carried by what isn't said — honne and tatemae, a refusal that sounds like a maybe, a pause that goes on a beat too long. The hearer is expected to infer it, and the speaker is expected to leave room for the inference.

The handout's two jobs map onto this pretty neatly. Japanese indirectness is mostly cushioning: the point is to protect the hearer's face. "That might be a little difficult" is gentler than "no". Dutch directness is the opposite — it refuses to cushion, because cushioning treats the other person as fragile. Grice's positive and negative face fits here: negative face is the want to be unimpeded, and Dutch bluntness actually respects that by not making you guess.

Deniability is where it gets interesting. When a Japanese speaker flouts a maxim, say the maxim of relevance, by answering a question with something adjacent to it, they're breaking the rule on purpose. That's what flouting is: you hide your real meaning so that later you can say you never said it. The hearer fills in the gap and the speaker keeps their hands clean.

So is a white lie ok? Mostly yes, I think. If the only effect is that someone feels better and nothing important turns on it, the cost of the truth outweighs the benefit, and refusing to lie starts to look like making your own conscience the point rather than the other person's feelings. And I'd say the Japanese style comes out more honest than the Dutch one, because indirect speech never actually asserts anything false, so strictly speaking no lie gets told. The Dutch person who says your soup is bad has said something true and hurt you. The Japanese person who says it's an interesting flavour has said nothing false and hurt nobody. On the last part of the question, who's responsible when someone is misled by something that was never stated — I'd say the hearer is. If you infer something the speaker didn't say, that inference is yours, so the responsibility is yours too.
