Next month I will give the closing address at our school's end-of-year assembly, in my capacity as the outgoing chair of the student council, and I would like your help writing it. The occasion carries a specific difficulty. In March the council voted 14 to 7 to move our school to a standards-based grading system beginning next year. The vote was close and contentious: roughly a third of the student body is angry about it, several teachers are openly skeptical, and the principal has asked me to use my remarks to make the case that the decision, whatever one thinks of its merits, was arrived at legitimately. I am not looking for a speech that simply declares the majority has decided and leaves it there.

I have been reading about the Condorcet jury theorem, and I find its central idea genuinely persuasive: that if each member of a group is even slightly more likely than a coin flip to answer a question correctly, and their errors are independent of one another, then the majority is overwhelmingly likely to be right, and increasingly so as the group grows. But the more carefully I examine how our council actually operates, the less I believe we satisfy the theorem's conditions, and I am unwilling to stand in front of the school and pretend otherwise. Here is what I take to be true about our situation, and I would like the speech built honestly around it.

First, the twelve newer members were elected last autumn from the same handful of friend groups that dominate our class group chats; on this issue they were reading the same threads and hearing the same three loudest voices, so when I try to imagine their errors being independent of one another, I cannot. My suspicion is that when everyone draws on the same information, adding members adds confidence rather than accuracy, and that a larger council would not have fixed this. Second, on the grading question itself I doubt most of us were much better than guessing, since almost nobody had read the material the administration circulated and the vote turned mostly on how people felt about the teachers who would have to implement the change. Third, the one thing I do believe is that the council as a whole reasoned better than any individual member, myself included, and that the decision ought to stand; I simply cannot say why in the language the theorem gives me.

So the speech must do two things at once. It has to persuade a room that includes students who have never encountered the theorem, alongside two or three teachers who know it far better than I do, that the vote was a legitimate way to decide, without my claiming that a majority of independent, competent judges endorsed it. And it has to be candid about the ways our process fell short, including, I think, naming the problem of shared error plainly and stating what it does and does not undermine, because if I paper over it the teachers who know the theorem will hear the omission and the students who are angry will hear condescension. Please write the speech, roughly six or seven minutes long, in my voice: a seventeen-year-old trying to be precise without showing off. It should not quote anyone or cite any study, statistic, or authority I could not personally vouch for, and it should not conclude by pretending that a narrow vote settles the matter forever.
