Write a pantoum spoken by one of the Hamburg reservists of Reserve Police Battalion 101 — the middle-aged policemen of Browning's Ordinary Men. The poem should hold two explanations of what he did in a single grip: the situational one (no conviction to speak of, a uniform, five hundred men in the same clearing) and the characterological one Goldhagen offered in reply (a willing executioner, made by a culture that had taught him to want this). Let the form carry the argument. Because a pantoum returns its lines in new positions and new light, a line that reads as an excuse in one quatrain should read as a confession when it comes back, and the closing return of the opening lines should fuse the two readings rather than settle on one. Build the turn on one concrete thing rather than on the atrocity itself: the commander's offer, made once at the first mass shooting, that any man who did not feel up to it could step out — an offer fewer than a dozen of the five hundred took, and one that, on Browning's account, cost those who took it nothing. A reader who knows both books should be able to locate the whole disagreement inside the poem; a reader who knows neither should still find it a good poem.
