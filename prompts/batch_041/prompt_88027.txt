I am in a reading group that has spent a year failing to settle a question about forgeries, and I have become less interested in the answer than in why it will not settle. The case we keep returning to is a painting that hung for decades as the work of a seventeenth-century master, was reassigned to a twentieth-century forger, and now hangs labelled as a forgery; the surface is unchanged. One camp holds that aesthetic value tracks the perceptible properties of the object: if no suitably equipped observer can tell two objects apart, no aesthetic difference can ride on their provenance, and the price gap between original and forgery, the museum's acquisition budget, and the connoisseur's disappointment must all be explained in non-aesthetic terms. The other camp holds that not everything we appreciate is perceptible — that being by a particular hand, or being the product of a particular achievement at a particular moment, is itself an aesthetic property — so the reattributed painting really did lose value, and a forgery is defective as a forgery rather than merely disappointing. Each side has a ready reply to every argument the other offers, and the replies have not changed in a decade.

We have tried to break the stalemate with cases, and the cases have not broken it. A painting restored so thoroughly that none of its original material remains: the first camp says nothing was lost that a viewer could ever have had; the second says either that a work survives the replacement of its material or that it does not, and cannot keep itself consistent about which. A forgery of an artist who never existed, so that there is no original to be false to and no achievement to fall short of: the first camp finds nothing wrong with it, the second finds a defect that cannot be comparative. An object that began as a forgery and ended as an original, the forger's own work now exhibited under his own name, where both camps can account for the change in value and so neither is tested by it.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that two questions are wearing one phrase. One side is asking what makes an encounter with an object worth having; the other is asking what makes something a work of art and what our appreciation is answerable to. Both call the answer aesthetic value, and so each side's arguments arrive at the other as non sequiturs. I would like that hypothesis tested rather than assumed.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about the extension of a term, in which case say precisely what each side would have to concede for the dispute to become substantive, and whether either side could coherently concede it? Is it a substantive normative disagreement about what aesthetic value is for, in which case say what an argument for either side would even look like? Or is some hidden question doing the work — perhaps an empirical one about what perceivers can in fact discriminate, or a formal one about whether aesthetic properties can supervene on non-perceptible facts? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a redescription of the cases, or nothing at all? If the honest answer is that each side rests on a commitment it cannot argue for, say so, and explain why that conclusion is not simply a way of ending the conversation.
