I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about self-deception, and he left me holding an argument that I can neither refute nor accept. Its conclusion — that self-deception is impossible — seems to prove far too much. It would equally rule out wishful thinking, motivated reasoning, and any influence of desire on belief; it would make every person who sincerely reports having deceived themselves either a liar or a stranger to their own mind. Yet each step, taken on its own, looks almost definitional. He set it out as follows, and I have kept his numbering.

(1) To deceive someone about whether p is to bring it about that they believe p while you take not-p to be the case.
(2) Self-deception is deception in which the deceiver and the deceived are one and the same person.
(3) So in any case of self-deception, a single person must both take not-p to be the case (as deceiver) and believe p (as deceived).
(4) No one can both take not-p to be the case and believe p; belief is a single truth-directed state and cannot contain its own negation.
(5) Therefore self-deception is impossible.

When I objected that the deceiver and the deceived might be separated in time — that one might suppress the evidence on Monday and hold the comfortable belief by Friday — he added two steps.

(6) Even granting a gap in time, the deceiver must retain the capacity, at the moment of the deception, to recognize the suppressed evidence as evidence; otherwise she has merely forgotten, and forgetting is not deceiving.
(7) But to recognize considerations as evidence for not-p is already, in the relevant sense, to take not-p to be the case.
(8) So the contradiction in (4) is reinstated, and self-deception remains impossible.

He then pressed a dilemma. Either the self-deceiver intends to produce the belief, in which case (3) and (4) apply and the contradiction is unavoidable; or she does not intend it, in which case what she has is at most motivated error — and a process no one intends is not deception at all. Either way, he says, self-deception names nothing.

The case we kept returning to was a man who by every outward measure believes his marriage is intact, though by his own later account he had known for years that it was not. My friend insists that the phrase had known is doing all the work: spelled out as a belief it collides with (4), and left vague it changes the subject.

I would most like to keep (4); I am not prepared to say that a person can simply hold contradictory beliefs at once and call that self-deception. I would also like to keep something in the spirit of (1), since deception has to have something to do with taking the truth to be otherwise. So which step should I give up, why did it look so plausible, and what does giving it up cost me? In particular: is his dilemma exhaustive, and what must I say about the man in the example?
