I'm two years into retirement after about thirty years in behavioural ecology — long-term field work on cooperative breeding in birds, so I've spent an unhealthy ammount of time thinking about how selection could build a tendency to do things that cost the doer and benefit somebody else — and lately I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. Last term we did evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics: the family of arguments that runs roughly, our moral beliefs are in us because holding them made our ancestors behave in ways that got their genes into the next generation, not because they're true, so they aren't justified, or anyway not knowledge. The version everyone cites goes: either the selective pressure tracked the moral truth or it didn't — if it didn't, our moral beliefs are off-track, and if it did, that's a coincidence we've got no reason to believe in. Hence the dilemma. What's driving me up the wall is that this thing has been running for something like twenty years and nobody has moved an inch, and I can't tell what kind of fight it actually is. The realist side has a stock of replies that all look structurally similar to me: either the debunker is using a principle of defeat so strong it would also gut our beliefs in mathematics, logic and induction; or there's a third factor that's both what did the selecting and part of what makes the belief true — survival is good, say, so selection pushing us toward believing that survival is good is itself a connection to the truth — so the coincidence dissolves; or the empirical premise is too crude, because selection shaped pro-social dispositions and emotions rather than the content of beliefs, so it can't touch moral facts either way. The debunking side then says all of that either begs the question (the third-factor reply has to help itself to the truth of the very beliefs under attack) or is a companion-in-guilt move that doesn't rescue the moral case specifically. And then it just... repeats. I've read maybe a dozen papers now and I genuinely cant tell whether the same moves are being re-run because nobody has an answer, or because the two sides aren't even asking the same thing.

My own hunch — please treat it as a hunch and try to kill it — is that nothing here is really about evolution, or even about whether moral facts exist. I think it's a disagreement about the correct theory of epistemic defeat, and specifically about whether justification requires a certain connection between the cause of your belief and the truth of your belief (something modal in the neighbourhood: you wouldn't have believed it if it hadn't been true) or only requires that the belief-producing process be reliable in fact, in which case the whole etiological story is beside the point. If that's the fault line, then the debunking argument and the third-factor reply are each decisive against the other depending on which side of it you stand on, and neither side can argue for its standard without assuming it. But I'm a bird person and I may well be conflating a real epistemological fault line with two quite different things — an unresolved empirical question about what selection actually shaped, and a pair of sides quietly answering different questions under one heading (the causes of moral belief versus the existence of moral truths).

So here's what I want: you're a philosopher who knows both the metaethics of debunking and the philosophy of biology, so give me the diagnosis, not a survey, and tie it to what the two sides actually say rather than to the labels. Start by brainstorming the candidate diagnoses before you commit to one, and say which of them my hunch collapses into if it turns out to be right. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive but underdetermined, or resting on starting intuitions neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? And tell me honestly what would count as progress here — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or whether it's the kind of dispute where no progress should be expected, and why. One more thing while you're at it: the same shape shows up in my old field (does an adaptationist explanation of helping behaviour debunk the claim that the helper is genuinely altruistic?) and in the cognitive-science-of-religion arguments about whether religious belief is debunked by its origins — so if there's a general template, say so, and say what, if anything, the moral case adds. And don't be diplomatic about it: if my framing is confused, or the debate isn't as stalled as I think, or the question as posed is malformed, say so straight out instead of massaging it into something answerable.
