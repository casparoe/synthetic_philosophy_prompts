Write a dialogue between two economists stuck at an airport bar, arguing about information cascades. One is a card-carrying cascade guy: herding is just rational Bayesian updating on other people's actions, information stops aggregating once the first couple of actions agree, and cascades are a big deal — the urn experiments are his home turf. The other thinks the whole thing is a knife-edge artifact of how the model wires people up: in the standard setup each person's information set literally contains the previous person's, so after two agreeing actions nobody's private signal can ever be decisive again, and thats a fact about the wiring, not about human psychology. Let him push his alternative wiring — everyone observes two randomly chosen earlier people instead of the whole prefix — and make the cascade guy actually fight back rather than cave. Should they also argue about whether the result needs coarse two-valued signals with fixed precision, and whether the fragility of cascades (one tiny public announcement and the whole thing collapses) is a weakness of the theory or the most interesting thing about it? And do 'herding' and 'cascade' even pick out the same phenomenon — everyone doing the same thing versus information having stopped flowing? Neither guy should be a strawman; I want real back-and-forth, point and counterpoint, no tidy resolution required.
