I'm not a philosopher — I studied engineering and I came to this sideways, through a book about the history of seismology — but I've been reading about the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 and I keep getting stuck on the philosophical fight it started. The earthquake struck on the morning of All Saints' Day, destroyed most of the city, and within the hour a tsunami came in and then a firestorm burned for days; tens of thousands died. Voltaire wrote a poem about it and then, a few years later, Candide, where he mocks the idea that this is the best of all possible worlds. Rousseau wrote back to him, and Rousseau's reply has always seemed to me the more interesting one, because he argued that the earthquake itself was not the main culprit. People had built tall houses in a crowded city, they had gone back into the burning buildings to save their belongings, they had lit candles for the feast day, and the fire did the rest. A great deal of the death, on his account, was human-made rather than God-made. I want you to take this whole episode — the earthquake, the pamphlet exchange, the sense that it marked a turning point in how Europeans thought about providence — and analyze it through the lens of the problem of evil and the classic theodicies. Not the history, which I know well enough; the philosophical mapping. Which theodicies were actually on offer in 1755, and which ones did the event put the most pressure on? The best-of-all-possible-worlds line, the free-will defense, the reading of disaster as punishment for sin, the soul-making idea that suffering is what builds character, the skeptical-theist move that we simply cannot see God's reasons — how does each handle an event like this, and where does each one strain or break? I'm also curious about the distinction between a defense, which only has to show that God and evil can coexist, and a theodicy, which claims to tell you why God actually permits it: does the Lisbon case fall on one side of that line or the other, and does Voltaire's response even engage the logical version of the problem, or is it doing something else?

And I'd like the analysis to run in both directions. Given the episode, what should each of the main parties have concluded — the optimists, Voltaire, Rousseau, and the practical men like Pombal who simply buried the dead and rebuilt? Where is the theory silent rather than wrong? Two things seem to complicate the standard story, and I'd like you to take them seriously. First, Rousseau's move looks like a proto-soul-making or human-responsibility theodicy, but it also seems to dissolve natural evil as a category — if nature has no intentions, is calling an earthquake evil a mistake, and if so, does that help or hurt the theodicist? Second, the earthquake was eventually explained by plate tectonics, and it arguably helped found modern seismology, so there is a strange sense in which the disaster was redeemed by the knowledge it produced — is that a theodicy, a substitute for one, or a category mistake? Tell me which features of the episode the theodicies explain, which they miss or misdescribe, and what the episode shows about the whole project of theodicy — whether it confirms it, refutes it, or reveals that it was never really about events like this.
