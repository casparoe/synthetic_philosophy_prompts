I've been re-reading GEB for the first time since college and the Ant Fugue is the bit that won't leave me alone — Aunt Hillary, the idea that you can talk to the colony rather than to the ants and find someone there to talk to, and Hofstadter's insistence that the colony's first-person perspective isn't located in any single ant and isn't just a summary of ant behavior either. So I've spent two weeks asking everyone I know whether an ant colony can have a mind, and my partner finally made me stop and watch a movie with her, and we put on Antz. Which was definately a happy accident, because the film is basically an argument against my question before the question is even asked: the colony is a conformist mass, the workers are interchangeable and treated as numbers, individuality is what makes you a person, and General Mandible's vision of a perfect colony is a horror of dissolving into the whole. The movie knows exactly what it thinks about colony minds, and what it thinks is that there isn't one — just a general and a queen at the top and replaceable parts below.

What I want is a real reading of Antz through the Aunt Hillary lens, not a plot summary. So: does the film's implicit theory of mind hold up, or does it beg the question by assuming any collective would have to be either a tyranny or a swarm of drones, so that a genuinely distributed mind with no central controller never even gets considered? Where does it accidentally dramatize the thing it's arguing against — the synchronized digging scenes where thousands of ants move as one body, the way the soldiers' deaths register as a loss of materiel rather than as the loss of anyone, the colony reacting as a unit to threats — and say honestly whether those read as a colony-level agent or just as alot of ants following local rules (this is the crux for me, and I don't think the film settles it either way; I keep wanting to just apply the intentional stance to the whole colony and see what breaks). Then the reverse direction: does Hofstadter's cheerful plural subject survive contact with the film's politics, or is the film right that a colony with a mind would be a colony with a ruler? Bring the actual biology in where it helps (real colonies are superorganisms, no individual ant has a clue what the colony is doing, which is sort of the whole point), and don't just tell me the movie got the science wrong — the reading I want is fair in both directions, takes the film's own stakes seriously, and is willing to say where the film resists the philosophy and where the philosophy can't quite meet the film. End by telling me what, if anything, the movie adds to the colony-mind debate that GEB doesn't. My film club is doing Antz and A Bug's Life back to back, so if the second one sharpens or complicates any of this, fold it in.
