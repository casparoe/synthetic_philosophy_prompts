Question 2 (20 marks).

Clark and Chalmers argue that Otto's notebook is part of Otto's cognitive system. In presenting the case they identify three features of the notebook: it is reliably available to Otto and typically invoked when he needs the information; what it contains is directly available to him without difficulty; and he automatically endorses what he finds there rather than treating it as one source of testimony to be weighed against others. They also discuss cases in which a person solves a puzzle by physically rotating a shape on a screen with a button rather than rotating it mentally, and argue that the external operation should be counted as part of the cognitive process in the same way.

Consider two further cases.

Case A, Rosa. Rosa is a radiologist who reads chest films in a reading room. A laminated card of diagnostic criteria hangs on the wall at eye level. She consults it for essentially every film she reads, without deliberating about whether to consult it; the entries are legible at a glance; and she does not treat them as a second opinion to be weighed against her own judgement. She reads films by their lights. She has never worked in a room without one, and she would find reading without it a different and much harder task.

Case B, Dev. Dev is a radiologist who uses a phone app that proposes differential diagnoses from an image. He consults it for most, though not all, films. The proposals appear quickly. He treats them as prompts: he frequently rejects or overrides them on the basis of his own reading, and he says he would not sign off on a report whose conclusion he could not independently justify.

(a) Apply the three features to Rosa and to Dev and say, for each, whether the resource counts as part of the person's cognitive system on Clark and Chalmers's account. Be explicit about whether you take the three features to be necessary and sufficient conditions for an external resource to count as part of a cognitive system, or something weaker, and say what follows for the rotation-button case. (8 marks)

(b) State the coupling–constitution objection to the extended mind thesis, and assess whether it defeats the claim about Rosa. (6 marks)

(c) A classmate objects as follows: if Rosa's card is part of her cognitive system, then so is the wall it is mounted on, and then the reading room, and then the hospital, so the thesis proves far too much. Is this a good objection? Is it the same objection as the one in (b)? (6 marks)

Student script.

(a) Rosa's card satisfies all three features. It is reliably available and typically invoked, since a card is on the wall of every reading room and she consults it for essentially every film. Its contents are directly available without difficulty, since they are legible at a glance. And she automatically endorses them, since she does not weigh them against her own judgement but reads films by their lights. So on Clark and Chalmers's account the card is part of Rosa's cognitive system, and we should say that her memory for diagnostic criteria extends onto the wall. Dev's app fails the first feature. He consults it only for most films, not all, so it is not reliably available in the relevant sense; and an app's suggestions are not reliable in the sense that matters, since they are statistical guesses that are sometimes wrong. Since the first feature is not satisfied, the app is not part of Dev's cognitive system, and Dev is in the position of someone who occasionally asks a colleague for advice rather than someone whose memory is extended. This is the right result, because Dev's own reasoning is doing the work. I take the three features to be necessary and sufficient for an external resource to count as part of a cognitive system: they are necessary because without them we would have no principled way of distinguishing cognition from mere causal influence, and they are sufficient because once a resource is reliably available, directly readable and automatically endorsed, there is nothing left for it to do in order to qualify. It follows that the rotation-button case is a genuine case of extended cognition as well, since the external operation plays the same role in the task that a mental rotation would, and the two cases are on a par in the way Clark and Chalmers say they are.

(b) The coupling–constitution objection is that Clark and Chalmers infer constitution from coupling. A resource's being causally coupled to a cognitive process, so that the process goes worse when the resource is removed, shows only that the resource is part of the causal story; it does not show that the resource is part of the cognitive system, any more than the fact that a calculator is coupled to my arithmetic shows that the calculator is part of my mind. The objection does not defeat the claim about Rosa. Rosa's card is not merely coupled to her reading: it satisfies all three features, including automatic endorsement, and it is therefore integrated into her cognitive system rather than being an external aid she consults. The objection has force against a view on which any causal contribution counts, but that is not the view under discussion.

(c) The classmate's objection is the coupling–constitution objection in a different form, and it fails for the reason given in (b). If we imagine the card's contents stored in Rosa's head rather than on the wall, we would have no hesitation in calling her use of them cognitive, and that is the test Clark and Chalmers use: what would we say if the process were internal? Applying that test, the card counts as part of her cognitive system. The wall does not, because walls are not reliably available to her in the relevant sense, are not directly readable as diagnostic criteria, and are not automatically endorsed; the same goes for the room and the hospital. So the thesis does not prove too much: the three features draw the line exactly where it should be drawn.
