My gut calls on who gets to walk out, and I want the rule behind them, if there is one. Scotland 2014: fine, both governments agreed and the question was honest. Catalonia 2017: not fine — the court had ruled it out and nobody was keeping them from ordinary politics. Quebec 1995: I think a clear Yes would have been a mandate to negotiate, even though Ottawa never agreed to hold the thing. Kosovo 2008: fine. Crimea 2014: not fine, soldiers in the street and no option to just stay put. South Sudan 2011: fine, it was negotiated with Khartoum. Bougainville 2019: fine, it came out of a peace deal and was near-unanimous. Northern Cyprus 1983: not fine. Somaliland: honestly fine by me, thirty years of a working state. The Falklands: the islanders decide, and they've decided. Texas: not fine, whatever the bumper stickers say. Nevis: they voted to leave St Kitts and their own two-thirds bar kept them in, and I think a bare majority should have been enough there. Confederacy, Rhodesia: obviously not, but I want a rule that says so for the right reason and not just 'they were the bad guys'.

The pair I cant square is Quebec and Catalonia. Same shape as far as I can see — rich, distinct, own language, democratic state, a vote the state calls illegal — and I come out differently. The Falklands makes it worse, since if the islanders count as a people then the Catalans sure do. What principle gets the most of these, which of my calls does it force me to drop, and what does it say about two I haven't decided: (a) a chunk of the departing region voted No and wants to stay with the old country — do they get to stay, or does the region go as a whole, and (b) a rich suburb votes to leave a poor city that doesn't want to lose it. Also: what would have to change about the Catalonia vote for me to say Yes, because I suspect my real objection isn't about the ballot box at all.
