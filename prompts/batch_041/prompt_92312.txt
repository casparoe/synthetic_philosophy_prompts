My flatmate and I have been going back and forth about whether he should tell his mum she has this slow condition she'll never actually notice. He won't, because 'ignorance is bliss', and he says that definately settles it — she's better off not knowing, so there's nothing left to discuss. I'm pretty sure that one phrase is doing about four different jobs in his sentence and he keeps sliding between them, but I can't lay it out cleanly.

Can you give me a menu of the precise claims 'ignorance is bliss' could be making — from the boringly-true ones up to the ones that are actually carrying weight — and for each say (a) whether it's really true, (b) what theory of well-being it's smuggling in (hedonism, desire-satisfaction, objective-list, whatever), and (c) whether it's the reading he actually needs to get to "so there's nothing to discuss". Some of the readings are probably claims about how people feel, some are claims about what's genuinely good for someone, and some jump straight to what we ought to do — I want those seperated out.

One thing I threw at him and he just brushed off: I asked him to imagine a machine that leaves every experience exactly as it is but quietly makes a pile of your beliefs false — you feel just as good, you just think your partner's faithful, your book sold well, your kid's happy, and none of it's true. He said that's still 'ignorance is bliss' and still fine. Is he right that that case is covered by the slogan, or does it split the slogan down the middle? I want to know which half of it the phrase is really about, and whether that split changes which theory of well-being you'd have to be holding to keep using it. Lay it out as a list I can actually argue from, not a survey of what Nozick said.
