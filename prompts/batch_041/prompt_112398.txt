I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and I am applying for a small internal grant that would fund six months of work on a prospectus chapter. My half-formed idea concerns the familiar claim that information can never be bad for a rational agent: the expected value of acquiring free information is nonnegative because the agent can always ignore it. I have come to suspect that this argument, commonly associated with I. J. Good's work on the value of information and the principle of total evidence, trades on an ambiguity between two different things. On one reading, the agent can choose an action equivalent to the action they would have taken without the information; on another, the agent can choose to exclude the information from the body of evidence on which they deliberate. The first is a claim about the act-set; the second is a claim about the norms governing belief and evidence. I want to turn this into a precise research question: under what conditions, and in what sense of availability, is the expected value of information nonnegative, and what happens to the result when the agent cannot commit to ignoring the information — either because the information is already part of their evidence, or because ignoring it would violate a norm they accept, or because the act of ignoring is itself costly or impossible to perform once the information is received?

Write me a short research proposal, about two pages, for a six-month project. I need a precise statement of the question that has a determinate answer; an explanation of why the standard formulations of the value-of-information result leave the question open (I am not interested in a literature review by citation — I will add those myself — but in the conceptual gap); the method I would actually use, which I imagine involves constructing small formal models and a few carefully chosen cases where the nonnegativity result either fails or becomes vacuous; and a clear statement of what would count as a successful outcome and what would count as failure. I am especially interested in whether the distinction between information one can ignore and information one cannot ignore maps onto anything independent, or whether it collapses into the distinction between acts that are and are not available. Please also say something about scope: six months is not long, and I would rather have a narrow question answered well than a grand theory. If you think the project is too big, say so and propose a smaller version.

One more thing: I want the proposal to be honest about the possibility that the whole puzzle is an artifact of a bad formalization. If the nonnegativity result is a theorem about a well-defined decision problem, then perhaps the cases where it seems to fail are just cases where the decision problem has been mis-specified — for example, where the act-set has been restricted, or where the agent's evidence has been treated as fixed when it is not. A good proposal should say how to tell the difference between a genuine counterexample and a mis-specification, and what would settle it. Do not include a bibliography or reading list; I will handle citations. The tone should be that of a serious prospectus, not a blog post.
