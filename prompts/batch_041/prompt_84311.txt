I ran into the absent-minded driver problem the other day and it's been bugging me since, because it describes my life a little too well. The way I understand it: your driving home on a road with two turnoffs. You want to take one of them and stay on the highway at the other, but the two spots look identical to you when you reach them, and you don't remember passing the first one by the time you get to the second. The punchline is that you can't fix it by just working out whats right at each turnoff — the best you can do is randomize at each one, with a bias, and accept that from inside any single turnoff your choice will look either obviously right or obviously stupid.

Thats me with a project I've been half-doing for four years. Every few weeks I sit down and decide whether to keep going or kill it. The two states I could be in — early, where pushing through pays off, and late, where I should cut my losses — genuinely look the same from in here. I can't reconstruct what I decided last time either; I'll find a note I wrote saying keep going and feel zero conviction reading it. Two wrinkles the textbook version doesn't have. The states aren't perfectly identical: when I'm sleeping well I can sort of tell them apart, maybe 60/40. And I can leave myself notes, except the note is written by a guy who also couldn't tell which state he was in.

What I want is the most important implications of this for someone actually living inside it — not the formal solution. What does it imply about how I should decide, how I should judge my past and future selves, and what am I getting wrong if I assume a rational person would just know what to do? And be honest about which implications survive once I'm only partly able to tell the states apart, or once I can leave myself messages, and which ones are just the theory being cute.
