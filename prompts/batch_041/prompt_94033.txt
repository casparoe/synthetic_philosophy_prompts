I keep returning to the meta-problem of consciousness — the thought that the fruitful question isn't "how does the brain produce experience?" but "why do we have the intuitions, and make the reports, that make experience look like such a special problem?": why we insist there is something it is like to taste coffee, why we treat experience as ineffable, why the topic feels uniquely resistant to the standard toolkit of cognitive science. It seems to me this reframes everything. The meta-problem is, after all, just one more case of explaining a class of judgments and behaviors — exactly the kind of thing cognitive science is built to handle — so once we have that account, together with the rest of the functional story, there is simply nothing left for a "hard problem" to be about. And any alleged fact of phenomenal consciousness that never surfaced in a judgment, report, or disposition would be causally and epistemically inert, and we would have no reason to posit it. So doesn't the very tractability of the meta-problem show that consciousness is at bottom an illusion generated by our cognitive architecture, and that the hard problem will turn out to have been a confusion from the start? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
