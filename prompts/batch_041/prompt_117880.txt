I serve as rapporteur to the Chamber of Competence, the tribunal of a federal union whose sole jurisdiction is the question whether the union or a constituent state was entitled to enact a given measure. The Chamber does not weigh the wisdom of legislation, except where entitlement turns on it. The union is hypothetical: treat its founding instrument as containing a subsidiarity clause of the familiar kind, but do not import the doctrine or case law of any actual court, and do not supply citations to statutes, decisions, or secondary literature. The Chamber's record on this question consists of five decisions.

1. A union regulation laid down a single safety specification for a good traded throughout the union, displacing the stricter specification of one state, under which the good had lawfully been sold for a decade. The Chamber upheld the regulation. Its stated reasons were that no single state can secure the conditions on which a union-wide market in the good depends, and that the specification had been adopted after consultation with all the states.

2. A union directive required every state to assess, document, and publish the environmental effects of permits for installations whose discharges, noise, and dust were confined to the territory of the permitting state. Its preamble justified the requirement as ensuring a uniform methodology. The Chamber struck the directive down, observing that the states were entirely capable of devising procedures of their own and that uniformity of method is not an objective the states are unable to attain.

3. Two states share a river. The upstream state declined to negotiate any limit on the discharge of a local industry; the downstream state asked the union to act. The union imposed a discharge cap binding on the upstream state, and the Chamber upheld it, reasoning that the states had not been able to reach an agreement and that the harm in question was suffered outside the territory of the state that caused it.

4. A state adopted a costly technical standard for a practice carried on within its borders. Firms based elsewhere, which compete with the state's firms in the union market, bore most of the cost; the practice itself and its physical effects were local. The Chamber upheld the standard, observing that the burden on the outside firms was a competitive disadvantage rather than an injury to person or property, that those firms could comply with the standard and go on trading in the state, and that the state had not sought to regulate anything beyond its own borders.

5. A state paid subsidies to firms in a sector, funded from its own revenues by a levy on its own residents. Rival firms in other states complained that the subsidies distorted the union market. The Chamber upheld the scheme, observing that the cost fell on the state's own taxpayers and that no one outside the state bore any part of it.

The case now pending. A state has enacted a scheme requiring installations in a sector concentrated in its territory to purchase offsets for their emissions. The scheme is administered by the state, applies to installations within the state, and has plainly achieved the objective the state set for it: the sector's emissions in the state have fallen by about a third, and no party disputes the figure or that the state's own instruments produced it. The sector's output is sold throughout the union. The scheme raises the state's producers' costs by roughly two per cent, and their competitors in other states bear no comparable cost. A further feature has emerged in the pleadings: about a fifth of the sector's remaining emissions are deposited downwind, in a neighbouring state, whose own limit for the relevant pollutant is looser than the cap the union now proposes. The two states have not attempted to negotiate. The union has now enacted a regulation establishing a union-wide market in offsets, which would displace the state's scheme. Its stated justifications are, first, the cost disadvantage the state's scheme imposes on firms that compete union-wide and, second, the deposition of pollutants in the neighbouring state. The regulation contains no finding that the states could not, individually or together, have achieved the reduction the union's regulation is meant to secure.

What should the Chamber decide if it is to remain consistent with its own record, and what do you predict it will decide? Identify the closest analog among the five decisions and say what makes it the closest. Set out the rules that are jointly consistent with the record — there is more than one — and for each say what verdict it requires in the pending case. Say which rule you take to be doing the real work in the record, and why the others fit less well. If the record genuinely underdetermines the case, say what principle the Chamber would commit itself to by each verdict available to it. Finally, name a further case, one the Chamber has not yet faced, that would most cleanly separate the rules you identified, and say what each rule would decide in it.
