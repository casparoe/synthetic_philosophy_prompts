Some years ago a colleague and I began arguing about the Sleeping Beauty problem and we have not moved an inch since. The case as we both accept it: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, a fair coin is tossed, and she is woken once on Monday if it lands heads and on both Monday and Tuesday if it lands tails, with her memory of the earlier awakening erased between the two; on waking she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads, and she knows the whole protocol. My colleague says one half; I say one third; a third member of our group insists the question is malformed until one says what a credence is supposed to be — a degree of belief in a truth-evaluable proposition, a betting disposition, or a piece of advice about how to act — and that once that is settled only one of the other two answers can even be meant. A fourth has tried separating her credence in heads from her credence in heads given that it is Monday, holding both at one half. We agree about the mechanics of the case, about the frequencies the protocol generates, and — my colleague concedes this too — about how she should bet if a bet is offered and settled at each awakening. The credence itself is the only thing we cannot agree on.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about self-locating belief, a verbal one about the word 'credence', two camps answering different questions under a single heading — one about what is true, one about what to do — or something else again? Consider in particular whether the impasse rests on a hidden disagreement about the principle of indifference, about whether an indexical fact can be part of one's evidence, or about whether 'I' and 'now' enter the content of a proposition at all. Here is my hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume: that the disagreement is not about probability but about the job description of a credence, and that the halves-versus-thirds split is downstream of an unexamined difference over whether a credence answers to truth or to decision. If that is right, say why; if not, say what is actually doing the work.

I would also like a probe I can run on the two of us. Strip out the first-person element: an experimenter who knows the protocol is told only that one awakening has been selected at random from the awakenings that actually occur, and is asked for the probability that the coin landed heads; then restore the first-person case. Does our disagreement survive the change, and if so, does it survive in the same form? Use that to say whether my colleague and I are answering the same question or merely using one word for two. Finally, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a revised question — or whether nothing should be expected to move us, and why.

Please write your answer in British English.
