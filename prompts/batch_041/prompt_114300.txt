The colleague who runs our cultural-evolution reading group is a committed Darwinian about culture, and we have spent the term keeping a scorecard of her verdicts on which items count as memes in the strict sense. Her rulings so far: the tune of a jingle that a whole city can hum — a meme; a vowel shift spreading through a regional accent — a meme; a chain letter that costs its recipients money and time and spreads anyway — a meme, and one whose success she insists is explained by the letter's own propagation rather than by any good it does the people who pass it on; and a surgical technique that spread because the surgeons who adopted it lost fewer patients — a meme. She has been equally firm that a behavior everyone in a village picks up merely by seeing others do it, such as fishing at the same bend in the river, is culture but not a meme, since nothing is copied from anybody.

Next week's item is a folk remedy that has been in use in a region for centuries. Nobody copies it exactly; each user reconstructs it from a rough idea and improvises the details, and most improvisations are forgotten within a generation. Yet the versions in circulation are not scattered at random: the recipes that get remembered keep being reinvented in nearly the same form, and the ones that stray too far are never reinvented at all, so the practice stays recognizably itself without anyone ever transmitting it faithfully.

What will she say? I would like (a) her likely verdict on this case; (b) the two or three rules that fit her earlier rulings equally well, and what each of them predicts here; (c) which of those rules I should take her to be running, with your reasons for preferring it; and (d) the single further case that would separate whichever rules survive your analysis.
