I am helping a colleague revise an essay on personal identity, and I want to pressure-test the analogy at its center rather than the conclusion it is meant to support.

Her evidence comes from the split-brain literature: after the commissures are cut, the two hemispheres can be shown to carry different information, to favor different options, and occasionally to work at cross-purposes, while the patient continues to report and behave like a single person. Her analogy is that such a patient is two subjects sharing one body — two tenants in a single apartment who cannot speak to each other but can leave notes and rearrange each other's furniture. From this she concludes that the unity of consciousness is an illusion, that there is no single subject behind the eyes, and that personal identity admits of degrees.

Audit the analogy rather than the conclusion. Begin with a correspondence table: what plays the role of the tenants, the apartment, the wall between them, the notes, the furniture, and the fact that the tenants never meet? Then locate where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in three pressure points. First, unity of consciousness is doing at least three distinct jobs in her essay — a single phenomenal field, a single bearer of experience, and a single agent — and the analogy may license a conclusion about one of these while she draws it about another. Second, the tenants are antecedently subjects with their own points of view and interests, whereas that is precisely what is in dispute for the hemispheres; if so, the analogy may be assuming what it is meant to establish. Third, the surgical division is less clean than the apartment suggests: the hemispheres continue to share subcortical machinery, arousal, and a body, and patients often achieve coordination by cross-cueing, so the wall between the tenants may be porous in ways that matter.

Finally, separate the disanalogies that bear on her conclusion from those that are merely picturesque, say whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table, and indicate whether a better analogy is available. If the analogy is carrying the argument rather than illustrating it, state that plainly.
