Write me a short story built on a version of the gradual-replacement idea that I dont think gets used much. Skip the usual neuron-by-neuron swap where each new part is a perfect functional duplicate — I want this instead: the clinic replaces the brain in chunks, and each new chunk is a *predictive model* of the chunk it replaces, trained on that chunk's recent activity. So the new hardware is never a copy of you, it's a forecast of you, and it's a little lossy and a little biased. The patient is awake the whole time and swears there's no seam, no flicker, nothing.

Heres the thing I want the story to chew on: because each replacement model gets trained partly on the patient's own self-reports and self-image, the forecast drifts — it predicts the person the patient *thinks* they are rather than the person they are. So by the end, after say fourteen sessions, the person is a cleaner, more coherent version of themselves, kind of a caricature. The clinic sells this as "continuity guaranteed," and meanwhile there's a relative who wants to halt the process at 73% and have the remaining biological tissue declared a seperate person.

I want the story to leave the central question genuinely open — not resolved by a closing speech — whether there was ever a fact of the matter about when the original stopped being around, or whether that question is just empty once all the behavioral facts are fixed. Give me an actual scene with people in it, not an essay. Casual tone is fine, this is for a reading group.
