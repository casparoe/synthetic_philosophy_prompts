I'm a graduate teaching assistant for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar, and I've been handed the discussion section for the unit on deception. The problem is that every conversation on the topic collapses into one of two slogans — either lying is obviously worse than merely misleading, or there is no real moral difference because deception is deception — and nobody can say precisely what turns on the choice. I was arguing about this with two friends over the weekend, and we kept discovering that we agreed on every concrete case yet disagreed on the labels, which is exactly the failure mode I want a worksheet to expose. So please build me a taxonomy, laid out as a decision tree, of the positions one might take on whether lying and misleading differ in their moral status.

Construct the tree from the logical space first and place the actual positions into it afterward, rather than starting from a list of labels. Each branch node should be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to, and I want the following distinctions to do real work: whether the object of moral assessment is the kind of act performed (asserting something false versus implying something false versus merely omitting) or the outcome (whether a false belief was actually produced in the hearer); whether the view treats falsity of the utterance as necessary for a lie at all; whether an intention to deceive is required or whether foreseeable deception suffices; and whether the hearer's entitlement — what the conversational context gives them a right to assume — figures in the verdict, and if so whether violating that entitlement counts as the same kind of wrong in the lying and the misleading cases. I also want the tree to reach a case type that the standard textbook examples do not. The usual examples are true statements carrying false implicatures, but consider a speaker who asserts something true whose implicatures are also true, and who deceives purely by selecting which true thing to say, knowing the hearer will draw a false conclusion on their own. An advisor asked whether an investment is safe replies that the fund is audited annually by a major firm — true, and the audited part is true too, but the client hears safe, which is false, and the advisor chose that fact precisely to trigger the inference. I want a branch that separates this selection-style deception from ordinary misleading-by-implicature, and I want at least one leaf to be the position that it is worse than either lying or implicating, on the ground that it leaves the victim with no false statement to point to.

Aim for six to nine leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of the position — or be marked unnamed if nobody actually defends it — plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. At a minimum the leaves should include: no moral difference between lying and misleading; lying always worse; misleading always worse; the difference is wholly context-dependent with no general ranking; and whatever further positions the logical space forces that I have not listed. For any combination of answers your tree permits but that no position occupies, say so and diagnose in a sentence whether the gap is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate rather than papering over it. Please do not include citations, reading lists, or the names of philosophers — the diagnostic questions should do all the work, and the students can supply their own labels. Write the whole thing in American English.
