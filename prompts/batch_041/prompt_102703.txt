I am moderating a session of our graduate decision-theory seminar, and our guest argues that a rational agent should defend itself against acausal threats — threats from a party that cannot causally affect it and that bite only through a correlation between the agent's choices and the threatener's predictions — by adopting a surrogate goal: a proxy objective it treats as terminal, chosen so that blackmail aimed at its real ends loses its purchase. She also extends the argument to ordinary causal blackmail, holding that we should expect human and artificial agents alike to adopt such standing defenses. Draft eight to ten interview questions that force her to argue rather than to restate the proposal. Include at least one question on why a purely causal decision-maker would treat the threat as inert and what the evidential or updateless reasoner is supposed to see that the causal one misses; at least one on the relocation objection — if she now genuinely cares about the surrogate, why is that not simply the new hostage, and what would distinguish a well-chosen surrogate from a badly chosen one; and at least one on whether the strategy collapses once the threatener knows the agent has adopted it. For each of those three, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Close with a question on what would count as evidence that the maneuver is rational rather than a rationalization of an agent's inability to commit.
