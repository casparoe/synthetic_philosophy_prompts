I am a novelist at work on a book whose central scene is a legislative hearing on which artificial systems deserve legal protection, and I want the philosophical spine of that scene to hold up. In my draft, a bioethicist proposes what she calls the recurrence criterion: a system's computations count morally only if some of them are about the system's own states, are revisable in light of those states, and can be used by the system to steer what it does next — so a device that merely detects tissue damage and withdraws would not qualify, while a device that registers its own detection as a state of its own, and can act on that registration, would. Take the criterion exactly as stated, and describe its most important implications. Please organize your answer in the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths: "The criterion, restated" (about 150 words), in which you put the criterion into your own precise terms and say what it does and does not require; "What follows immediately" (about 400 words), for the consequences that the criterion forces on anyone who accepts it; "What it leaves open" (about 250 words), for the further questions it does not settle and the additional choices one would have to make; "The hardest cases" (about 300 words), for the two or three instances that strain the criterion most and what each strain reveals; and "Bottom line" (about 100 words), stating plainly what the criterion buys and what it costs.
