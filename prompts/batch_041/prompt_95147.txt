I serve as a trustee of a small charitable trust that administers a row of almshouses in the town where I live. I am the only member with any philosophical training, which means I am the person the other trustees expect to say something principled and no one expects to be practical. Our meeting on Thursday must settle a dispute that has been rumbling for the better part of a year. The trust has, for as long as anyone can remember, reserved its rooms for applicants with a connection to one particular parish; nobody can tell me when the practice began or what it was for, and the trust's own minutes simply assume it, as one assumes the weather. A woman with no such connection applied last year, was turned down without much explanation, and has now written to us to say that the rule is arbitrary, that our charitable objects say nothing about parishes, and that we are dressing up a preference as a rule. The rooms are the last affordable housing in the town, so she has rather more at stake than we do. Reading the correspondence again last night, I noticed that my own attachment to the practice has no better ground than the fact that it is our practice, and that when the argument runs out the trustees can simply vote, and she can do nothing about it. The thought struck me as either scandalous or clarifying — I cannot decide which — and I am no longer sure whether I am defending the trust's purposes or merely our power over someone who has less of it.

One of our number, a retired solicitor, tells me that if the practice were a custom in the strict legal sense it would have to satisfy a whole list of conditions — long and continuous use, peaceable enjoyment, certainty, consistency with statute — of which the most troublesome is that it must be reasonable. She says the rest are matters of evidence, but that this one is a matter of judgement, and that the courts have never managed to say clearly what it means. I mention this because it is the sort of thing that keeps me awake: I suspect that the reasonableness of a long practice cannot be demonstrated from outside the practice, and that if I am honest at the meeting the only thing I can say is something no trustee wishes to hear — that the practice holds because we hold to it, and because we can.

I should therefore be grateful for your advice on two questions. First, what should I actually argue on Thursday, given that I would rather not be either a hypocrite or a wrecker? Second, is my unease about the ground of the practice a genuine objection to the practice, or merely a scruple that will dissolve once I stop brooding on it? You should assume that I will be speaking to colleagues who are kind but impatient, and to a woman who has already been made to feel like a nuisance for asking. Please answer in British English.
