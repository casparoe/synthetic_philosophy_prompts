The Condorcet jury theorem is usually stated as a claim about competence: if each voter is individually more likely than not to be right, then a majority is more likely to be right than any single voter, and the probability that the majority is right approaches certainty as the group grows. I want an explanation of the theorem that identifies which of its assumptions is actually load-bearing, by contrasting two electorates of equal size whose members have identical individual competence but whose errors differ in structure. In the first electorate, errors are independent: whether one voter errs carries no information about whether another does. In the second, errors share a common cause: on some questions a single influence — a shared rumor, a shared framing, a shared authority — leads every voter astray together, while on the remaining questions the errors are again independent. Explain, without leaning on the theorem's formula, why the majority is reliable in the first electorate but can be no better than a lone voter, and even worse, in the second, even though no individual in either electorate is any less competent at the question. Then draw out the moral: why the theorem is better read as a claim about the independence of errors than about the competence of crowds, and what it must assume about the joint distribution of votes — not merely each voter's marginal competence — in order to retain its conclusion. Finally, say whether you think any real deliberative body could satisfy that assumption, and what follows for the claim that majority rule tends to track the truth.
