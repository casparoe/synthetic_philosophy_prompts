I teach a third-year seminar in decision theory, and next week's session is on satisficing. Don't explain the idea to me; plan how I should explain it in eighty minutes to students who have had one term of expected-utility theory and nothing else. The crux I want the session built around is a distinction my students collapsed last year: between a rule that stops at the first option clearing a fixed bar and a rule that stops at the first option better than whatever you already hold. These are different rules with different failure modes — a bar set too high can leave you searching indefinitely, whereas an improvement rule can settle you into something you would never have accepted from scratch — and until they see the two come apart in a case, they will read every version of satisficing as the first. So: give me the order in which the pieces must go for bounded rationality to look like a constraint on what maximising can even mean, rather than a concession to human weakness; the one case to carry the session, which should not be the secretary problem (it presupposes the maximising frame I am trying to dislodge) and must let the two rules give different verdicts; the misconceptions to expect in the order they will surface — my guesses are that satisficing is settling for less, that it is just constrained optimisation, that the aspiration level is an arbitrary fudge, and that it is a claim about psychology rather than about rationality — with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and one closing question that would show they can tell the two rules apart in a case they haven't seen. Tell me too what I should refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud in the session. If the crux I have built the session around is confused, or the framing is a bad way in, say so rather than working around it. Write in American English.
