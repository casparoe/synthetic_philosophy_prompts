So I'm in a philosophy of science reading group — mostly grad students, plus this one chemistry postdoc who's read a ton of the history of the field — and he keeps shutting down every realist in the room with the same line: 'the history of science is a graveyard of theories.' I've heard two other people use it since, so I guess it's a thing now. His move is always identical: phlogiston was a serious, succesful theory; caloric was a serious, successful theory; the luminiferous ether was a serious, successful theory; every one of them is dead now, so why would you believe in electrons, or dark matter, or whatever the current fashionable unobservable is? He thinks this 'settles' scientific realism. I've got a nagging feeling the slogan is doing four or five different jobs at once and that some of them don't get him anywhere near his conclusion, but every time I try to pin it down I get tangled up in what 'successful' is even supposed to mean and what it is for a theory to be 'false.'

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims 'the history of science is a graveyard of theories' could be making, as a menu, with a verdict on each — trivial, defensible-but-contentious, or just false? I especially want the readings kept apart where the induction is run over whole theories versus over the theoretical terms or entities those theories posited (is the claim 'the theory was false,' or 'phlogiston didn't refer,' or 'the ether isn't real'? — these seem different to me). And the gap between the version where 'successful' means 'was widely accepted at the time' and the version where it means 'made novel predictions that later came true.' Also: does it matter that a lot of the theories he lists were retained in a stripped-down form — the equations or the structural relations kept, the posited stuff thrown out? Because it feels like one of the readings quietly supports 'keep the structure, drop the entities' rather than his flat 'believe in nothing you can't see.' And somewhere in the pile is a base-rate argument (most past theories died, so probably most current ones will too) — I want to know whether that's the reading he's actually leaning on, and wether it survives once you ask what the sample of 'past theories' actually is and whether it's biased toward the dead ones.

Give it to me as a menu with verdicts I can bring back to the group, and tell me which reading he's most likely really relying on and whether that reading actually supports 'don't believe in electrons.' Bonus if you flag where 'approximate truth' changes the picture, because I suspect 'the theory turned out to be false' is doing way more work than it can bear when what he really means is 'not exactly right.'
