So the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials basically invented a bunch of law on the spot — crimes against peace, plus the rules that being a head of state or just following orders doesn't get you off the hook. I get why people celebrate them. But theres something about the whole setup I can't square, and I'd like you to dig into what it actually presupposes philosophically.

Heres the part that bugs me. They were happy to convict people for waging aggressive war even though, by most accounts, you couldn't actually be charged with that when they did it — the tribunal more or less said yeah it wasn't on the books, but it was obviously wrong, so tough luck. Fine, I can live with that. But then the same outfit never went near the Allied area bombing of German cities, or the firebombing of Tokyo, or the atomic bombs, and the standard answer is that those just weren't recognized war crimes yet. And when Doenitz got done for the unrestricted U-boat campaign, the judges said his submarines had broken the rules but they wouldn't punish him over it, because the British admiralty had issued much the same orders. So ex post facto is apparently fine when its the losers in the dock, and a bit of "you did it too" is a perfectly good answer when its the winners.

I'm not asking you to just call them hypocrites — obviously the victors ran the whole thing, that part is easy. I want the philosophy underneath it. What does a tribunal like that have to believe about law and morality for it to work at all? It feels like it has to assume there are standards of right that bind people whether or not any legislature ever wrote them down, otherwise "it wasn't illegal when I did it" is a knockout argument and the whole trial collapses. But then, if it's committed to that, why doesn't the same commitment force it to go after the bombers — or is there some honest way to hold that aggression is wrong in itself while area bombing is merely not-yet-illegal? I want you to separate three things: which parts of what they did the institution genuinely has to presuppose, which parts it could drop tomorrow without losing anything, and which parts are just as well explained by a cynical "the winners write the rules" story with no real theory behind them at all. Then the individual-responsibility bit — throwing out the head-of-state and superior-orders defenses — what does that presuppose about when a person's actions count as thier own even though they were acting as an official carrying out orders? Does that need a particular picture of practical reason and autonomy, or would several different pictures license it just as well?
