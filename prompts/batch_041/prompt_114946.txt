I cover science and medicine for a general-interest magazine, and I've reached the point in a long feature on the boundary of disease where I need a philosopher rather than another interview. I've been keeping a list of my own gut verdicts on hard cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Please take the role of a philosopher of medicine — someone equally at home in the naturalist tradition, which treats disease as a value-free matter of biological malfunction in the spirit of Boorse's biostatistical theory, and in the normativist and hybrid alternatives, from Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis to the more thoroughly value-laden accounts on which disease depends on social norms and individual goals. Think carefully before you commit to anything. Before you answer, spend some space brainstorming the candidate principles and noting where each one strains.

My verdicts, as I'd defend them over a drink:

- A tibia snapped on a ski slope. Obviously a disorder.
- A man whose internal organs are all mirrored left to right — situs inversus — found by chance on a scan, no symptoms, an ordinary life. Not a disease. Nothing in him is failing; his parts are simply arranged the other way round.
- A woman born with no sense of smell, who has never missed it, cooks well, eats happily, and tells me she would refuse a cure. Not a disease. I can't see what has gone wrong for her.
- A man who carries a single sickle-cell gene: his red cells are not quite normal, he has no symptoms, and the variant protects him against malaria. Not a disease; if anything, an advantage.
- A forty-two-year-old with familial high cholesterol. No symptoms, no plaque anyone has found, but his father died at fifty. A disease — or at least the near edge of one. Telling him to come back in ten years feels like negligence.
- A thirty-five-year-old who cannot conceive and wants a child: a disease. Her sister, with the same reproductive physiology, was sterilized by choice at thirty: not a disease. Same biology, different verdicts.
- An eighty-eight-year-old with brittle bones, stiff arteries and failing hearing. Not a disease — that is what being eighty-eight is.
- A widow unable to work, sleep or take pleasure two years after her husband's death: a disorder. A colleague widowed the same week, weeping at six weeks: not a disorder, just grief.

My working guess is that it takes two things — something in the body failing to do its job, and that failure making the person's life go worse. Test it, repair it, or throw it out as you see fit.

What I need from you:

1. The principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh case without asking you again.
2. For each clause, the verdict above that forces it — and where two of my verdicts pull in opposite directions, say plainly which one has to give.
3. Your verdicts on cases I haven't listed. Invent at least three, aimed deliberately at your own principle's weak spots.
4. An honest accounting of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should abandon them or accept a more complicated principle instead.

No reading list and no citations, please — I want the argument, not the literature, and I'll check the sources myself. Write it as a self-contained analysis I can read straight through without any other material at hand.
