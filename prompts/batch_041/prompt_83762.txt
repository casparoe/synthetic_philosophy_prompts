I'm a backend engineer at a forty-person startup and through a series of bad luck I now own the little bot that tags our post-incident write-ups. The CTO wants every incident tagged either "individual slip" or "team failure", because the fix is different: an individual slip means coaching or a checklist for one person, a team failure means we change the process, the interface, or the on-call rota. I have to put the tag in within 24 hours of the incident call, and the incident lead can overrule me but has to write down why. It comes up maybe two or three times a month. Stakes are moderate — nobody dies, but people get pretty upset when the tag lands on them personally.

My problem is that I can't actually tell when something was genuinly done together by a group, versus several people each doing their own thing that happened to combine into a mess. I've read bits of the philosophy on this — shared intentions, each person doing their bit, everyone knowing everyone else is in on it, that sort of thing — but I can't remember the names and half of it I don't understand. One of our PMs keeps calling anything where the whole team voted the same way a "group decision", which I'm fairly sure isn't the same thing at all.

Can you turn the actual philosophy of joint action into a decision procedure I can run? I need the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops and says individual, where it stops and says joint, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than a fact. Then run it on three cases and tell me honestly where it'll misfire:

1. Two engineers each independently edit the same shared config file on the same afternoon, neither knows the other is touching it, and together they take down checkout.
2. An on-call team follows the written runbook exactly, everyone does their bit, and the runbook itself is wrong so the outage gets worse.
3. I deploy to prod on a Friday without telling anyone and it breaks. I'd quite like the procedure to call this a team failure, since the company decided anyone can deploy to prod, but I suspect it won't.

Keep it something an engineer with no philosophy background can actually run in fifteen minutes, and be upfront about the bits that are just me making a judgement call dressed up as a test.
