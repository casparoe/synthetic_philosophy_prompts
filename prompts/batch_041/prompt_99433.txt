I am assembling a small anthology of satirical institutional documents, each of which takes some real position in the theory of art and follows it to a place the reader did not expect, and I would like you to draft one of them. The premise is this: a fictional museum, which you should name, has adopted as its binding acquisitions and curatorial policy the account of art that Tolstoy develops in What Is Art? — the view that art is above all a means by which one person deliberately transmits a feeling to others and thereby infects them with it; that the marks of genuine art are the sincerity of the maker, the clarity and individuality of the expression, and its accessibility to ordinary people rather than to a cultivated elite; that the decisive question is not whether a work is beautiful or technically masterful but whether it unites people in a shared feeling, with the works that do this most universally and most in the spirit of human brotherhood ranked highest; and that whatever fails these tests — however celebrated, however historically canonical — is counterfeit. The museum has accordingly reviewed its holdings, and the exhibition you will be announcing consists of everything it has decided to remove from the collection.

Please write, in the deadpan and slightly self-congratulatory register of a real institutional press release — a headline, a dateline, a few short sections, a boilerplate paragraph at the end — the announcement of that deaccession exhibition. Give the show a title. Let the humour come entirely from the policy being applied honestly and consistently to works and practices the reader recognizes, rather than from any joke the document lets itself notice: the communications office should sound genuinely pleased with itself, and the absurdity should be left for the reader to assemble. The theory must be represented accurately, so that a reader who knows the book finds the satire fair and does not catch the document in an error — but the piece must also be intelligible to someone who has never heard of it, since the release itself should explain the standards plainly, as a press office would. Include, among the sections, a short quoted remark from an invented trustee or curator who objects to the policy on grounds that the release treats as merely administrative, and a brief note on the verdict of the museum's advisory panel of ordinary visitors, whose judgment the policy treats as decisive; both quotes are to be invented, not drawn from any real person or text. Aim for roughly six hundred words.

The point of the exercise is not to catalogue verdicts but to dramatize the consequences of the policy for the institution that sincerely adopts it — what it must give up, what it must say in public, how it must explain itself to donors, critics, and the public it claims to serve. The standard objections should be made legible in the course of the document without ever being asserted: let the reader feel them arriving. Do not break the deadpan, do not have the narrator wink, and do not let the release quietly soften the policy into something more moderate at the end.
