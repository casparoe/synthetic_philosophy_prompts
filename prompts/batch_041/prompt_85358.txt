I'm a science journalist — I mostly cover physics and cognitive science, but I've been trying to write something about the Great Man theory of history and I keep getting stuck, so I'm crowdsourcing my confusion. Here's the position I've basically landed on and want stress-tested. When you explain something as large as Nazi Germany, the structural conditions do almost all of the causal work and the person at the top is close to fungible. Not that Hitler was irrelevant to how things went — obviously he shaped alot of the texture — but that a Germany with the Depression, the hollowed-out Weimar institutions, the paramilitary street politics, the resentment over Versailles, the fear of Bolshevism, the collapse of the liberal center, was going to produce something functionally like the Third Reich no matter which of the plausible nationalist-right figures ended up in the driver's seat. The interesting variance, on my view, is in the details, not in the outcome. I'd like a map of this position's exposure: which observations, arguments, or pieces of the historical record should move me off it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and which of those would a committed structuralist concede actually counts against them versus wave away. And be honest with me about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — because I notice that whenever someone points at something that looks like the product of one man's particular fixations rather than of structural pressure, the reply is that his psychology was itself part of the structure, and at that point I can't tell what would falsify the thing.

Two things I'd like you to work with, since I think they're where my real uncertainty lives. First, I've been running a crude thought experiment in my head: imagine the 1920s and 30s re-run a hundred times, with the leadership slot filled each time by a random draw from the pool of figures who were actually plausible at the time, and ask what fraction of runs end in something we'd recognize as a fascist regime, a European war, and a genocide. I call the loose version of this the structural slack question — how much room is there in the system before individual variation changes the macro-outcome — but I don't know if the question is even well-posed, since I can't tell you what the correct sampling frame for that pool is, and if the frame is arbitrary then so is my answer. Second, I keep sliding between two claims that I suspect are very different: that the outcome was overdetermined (something like it was coming) and that the path was overdetermined (it would have been reached by recognizably similar steps). A regime that emerges from a different leader, a different tempo, no pact with the conservatives, no invasion of the Soviet Union, might still be a Nazi-ish Germany while being a totally different object — and I don't know whether the structuralist is entitled to count that as a win, or whether it's a quiet concession that the individual is doing real work in everything except the headline. So tell me what the strongest version of the anti-structuralist case looks like that doesn't just cherry-pick dramatic decisions, and what it would take for you to say the Great Man people have it right after all. I'm most persuadable by the argument that there's some single trait or fixation of his that was load-bearing — flip it and the whole thing comes apart — so if that kind of claim is available, tell me what it would need to show.
