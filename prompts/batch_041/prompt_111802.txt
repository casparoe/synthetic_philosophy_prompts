I belong to a small monthly reading group — a type designer, a phonetician, a philosopher of language, and two cognitive scientists — that has spent its last three sessions on Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project, and we keep circling the same question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? We would like to leave next month's meeting with an actual instrument rather than another round of anecdotes, so what I am asking for is a decision tree of the positions on that question: a sequence of branch nodes, each phrased as something one of us could answer about a concrete case without already knowing which position the answer leads to, terminating in leaves that name a position (or are marked "unnamed" where the combination is unoccupied) and carry one line identifying the commitment that sits least comfortably with the rest of the view. Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of -isms: the distinctions first, the positions located among them second, unoccupied combinations diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate flagged rather than papered over.

Please make the branch nodes do real work on the cases we keep getting stuck on. The two-storey 'a' of most print and the one-storey 'a' of most handwriting are treated by every reader as the same letter, yet the IPA's inventory keeps its 'a' and its 'ɑ' apart as two symbols rather than two drawings of one. 'A' and 'a' differ in shape far more than those two 'a's do, and are unambiguously one letter. A Cyrillic 'а' and a Latin 'a' can be, in a given face, the same shape and two different letters. In most faces 'n' and 'u' — and 'b' and 'q' — are 180-degree rotations of each other, so any account that identifies a letter with a shape owes us an account of what orientation is doing. We have all met a glyph so ambiguous between 'a' and 'e' that we assign it only from the word it sits in, and lately we have been collecting glyphs produced by generative models that are 'a'-ish without being any typeface's 'a'. And last month we demonstrated to ourselves that most of us cannot say which way the tail of a printed two-storey 'g' curves, though we read it instantly; whatever the tree says about tacit recognition should survive that.

Because our interests pull in different directions, please let the tree separate at least the following, in whatever order the logic demands: whether 'a'-hood is a property of the mark itself or of something outside it — the other letters, the reader, the community of users; whether the account is an essentialism about a shared feature, a family-resemblance or prototype account, or a holist account on which a letter is what it is by contrast with its neighbours in the alphabet; whether letter identity is separable from style at all, or whether, as Letter Spirit seems to suggest, an 'a' is only ever an 'a' as realized in a style; whether the standard is a fact about the world, a fact about our concepts, a convention, or a sentiment corrigible by the judgments of the practised (one member insists the whole thing is Hume's standard of taste with serifs, and I would like to know which leaf he lands on); and where the deflationary options sit, including that there are only inscriptions and no letter-types, and that the question has no fact of the matter. Aim for eight to ten leaves, and close with a short paragraph on which single node you would expect two people who agree about every case to split over — that is what we will be arguing about next month.
