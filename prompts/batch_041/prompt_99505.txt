Not sure this is even a real pattern, so tell me if it falls apart.

I've been going to a Stoicism-and-CBT group at the community centre, and the whole point of it is telling each other to feel differently about stuff. What I've noticed is that two completely different kinds of advice get said in the same tone of voice, and everyone acts like their the same kind.

My mate Priya is furious at her ex. One person tells her "he didn't actually do anything to you, he just moved on faster" — that's about whether the anger is pointing at anything real. Another person tells her "the anger is wrecking your sleep and your job, let it go" — that has nothing to do with whether he did anything, it's about what the anger does to her. Two different things, fine, everyone gets that much.

What I can't get past is that the second kind only seems to work in one direction. Nobody blinks at "drop it, it's poisoning you." That reads as a genuine reason to stop being angry, even to people who agree he did nothing wrong. Flip it, though: "hang onto the grudge, you'll get a better settlement" — everyone hears that as a reason to act angry, or to keep acting angry, but not as a reason to actually be angry. Milder version: "keep up the gratitude practice, it's good for your heart" sounds fine; "start feeling grateful to your boss, grateful staff get promoted" sounds like a joke, or a threat. So a consideration about what the attitude does seems able to get you out of an attitude but never into one.

Second thing. Sometimes these effect-y reasons seem to work by changing the world rather than by changing you. A friend of mine started being warmer to her manager, the manager warmed up back, and now she says she has reasons to be warm to him. I can't tell if she changed the subject or if the world shifted underneath her and the thing became true.

Third thing, might be the same or might not. "You'd have to be a monster not to feel anything about that." That gets used like a verdict on whether the feeling fits, but it's really about what kind of person you'd be, which smells like the effect-y kind. And it's the only one that ever lands — nobody folds because something's bad for them, but "what kind of person doesnt feel anything" makes people fold.

Someone there told me this is just the wrong kind of reasons problem. I looked it up and I don't think that's what I'm asking. That seems to be about whether that kind of consideration can count as a reason for an attitude at all — there's some example about someone offering you something so you'll feel a certain way — whereas I'm asking about a lopsidedness: easy to get out, impossible to get in, plus whatever's going on with the monster line. Happy to be told they're the same thing and I've got the wrong end of it.

So: does the lopsidedness have a name? Is it real, or does it collapse when you push on it — e.g. is the actual line just "reasons to have" versus "reasons to keep," in which case all my cases are keeping cases and I've got nothing? Where does the monster line go? And does the manager case break it, or is it a different animal?

I don't want a reading list, I'll never get hold of the papers. I want the carving — what the thing is, what it isn't, which nearby things it keeps getting mistaken for, and where its edges are. If there's no name, make one up and tell me exactly what it covers and what it leaves out.
