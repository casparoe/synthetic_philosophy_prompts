I am drafting a proposal for a one-year fellowship and would like help turning a half-formed idea into something with a spine. The idea concerns the intuition that history turns on trivial accidents — the horseshoe-nail picture, in which a kingdom is lost because a nail was missing. I think that intuition is quietly doing the work of three independent claims, and that most disputes about historical contingency go astray by sliding between them. The first is causal sensitivity: a small difference in initial conditions makes a large difference later, as in a chaotic deterministic system. The second is metaphysical openness: the outcome was not fixed by the prior state, so a genuine alternative was available. The third is epistemic: we could not have predicted the outcome, whatever its modal status. These come apart in both directions — a process can be deterministic and exquisitely sensitive, or undetermined yet robust to small perturbations — and the persuasive force of contingency talk in history usually rests on the third while borrowing the authority of the first. The wrinkle I want the project built around is sharper still: the technical notion of chaos is defined for well-posed deterministic systems, and social and political processes are arguably not of that kind, so appeals to sensitivity-to-initial-conditions in history may be a category mistake — and yet the sense that history is contingent in some non-epistemic way is very hard to abandon. What I want is a principled account of when a claim of the form this outcome could have gone otherwise, and its going as it did turned on such-and-such, is warranted, and of what kind of contingency such a claim attributes.

Please turn this into a proposal. I need: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the standing positions — determinism versus indeterminism about human affairs, and the structural-versus-accidental-cause debate — leave that question open; the approach I would actually take, and why it beats the obvious alternatives (I imagine conceptual analysis plus a small taxonomy of senses, tested against one or two historical episodes I will supply, and perhaps a toy model, but tell me if something else would carry more weight); what a real result would look like and what would count as the project failing; an argument that the work is worth doing rather than a verbal quibble; and an honest verdict on whether this fits a year or is secretly a career. Assume roughly 2,000 words. Do not supply citations or a literature review — refer to positions by description and I will attach the references myself.
