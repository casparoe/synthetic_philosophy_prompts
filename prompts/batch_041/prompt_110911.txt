Two of my oldest friends, Ana and Ravi, spent most of a long dinner last week locked in an argument, and a third friend and I have been going back and forth about it ever since without settling who had the better of it. I would like you to sort it out for us.

The argument was about a scenario Ana had posed. In the scenario, you have to spend tomorrow morning in one of two places — call them the north office and the south office. You cannot skip, and you cannot hedge: you will be in one of them. There is a man who has spent years studying you and who, as far as anyone can tell, has never once been wrong about what you will do. He made his prediction yesterday, and he has already gone to the place he predicted. He does not care what happens to you; his arrangement is simply that he goes where he predicted, and he does not change course. If you end up where he is, you lose, and the loss is serious. If you end up where he is not, you are fine. Nothing you do tonight can reach him: he is not watching you, he will not change his mind, and he is already where he is going to be.

Here is roughly how it went.

Ana: The prediction is already made and he is already in place, so nothing I do now changes anything about him. I should simply go where he is less likely to be. Given how he works, the place he predicted is the place I would naturally head for, so I go to the other one. That way I have a real chance, and the obvious choice gives me none.

Ravi: You have no chance either way, and that is the whole point. If you go to the north office, that is very strong evidence that he predicted the north office, which means he is in the north office, which means you lose. If you go south, the same reasoning runs. Whichever way you go, the going is the proof that he knew. You are caught before you start.

Ana: You are treating evidence as though it were a cause. My choosing the north office does not put him in the north office. He is either there or he is not, and that was settled yesterday. You sound like someone who will not open the weather app, because learning that it will rain feels like making it rain. My credence about where he is cannot move him an inch.

Ravi: I am not saying it moves him. I am saying your advice has nothing underneath it. Your only reason to think he is in the south office is that you would go north, and your only reason to think he is in the north office is that you would go south. Whatever chance you think you have is borrowed from the prediction. Take the prediction away and there is no reason to prefer either place; put it back and it cancels whichever place you choose.

Ana: Then tell me what to do. When I ask you, you say it does not matter, I am caught either way. But it does matter that I go somewhere, and there is a way the world could be in which I go south and I am fine. A view that shrugs in a case like this is worse than one that at least points me somewhere.

Ravi: It does not shrug. It tells you the truth, which is that this is a case where nothing you do helps. And notice what happens to your advice the moment I take it. Suppose I decide to go south. Now, having decided that, I should think: so he predicted south, so he is in the south office, so going south is the worst thing I could do. Your recommendation dies as soon as it is adopted, and a recommendation that cannot survive being taken is not a recommendation.

Ana: Then the trouble is in your psychology, not in the world. If deliberating makes you revise your credences, that is a fact about you. And in any case I need not choose at all: I will flip a coin in my pocket tonight, in the dark, and go wherever it lands. He cannot have predicted a coin.

Ravi: He predicted the coin. He knows your pocket, your thumb, and the coin.

Ana: Then I settle it now, out loud, in front of everyone: whatever happens, I go north. Now the thing he predicted was my rule, and the rule is mine.

Ravi: The prediction was made yesterday, before you settled anything. Settling it now is just one more thing he saw coming; the rule may be yours, but so was the deciding.

Ana also insisted that this was some famous puzzle, and Ravi insisted it was a dressed-up version of something obvious; neither of them could say what they meant.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each of Ana's and Ravi's turns, say who has the better of that particular point and why, rating each move on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and flag any move that is right for the wrong reason or wrong for a good reason. Then say what both of them are missing, and whether there is an assumption the two of them are quietly disagreeing about without realizing it. Finally, tell me what the person in the scenario should actually do, or explain why there is nothing to do. I am not after a reading list or a history of who said what first; I want the arguments themselves sorted out.

End with a single final line of the form

Verdict: <Ana | Ravi | neither | both> — <a short phrase>

where the label names whoever has the better of the argument overall.
