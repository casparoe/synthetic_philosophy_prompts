I teach a first-year seminar that uses Hofstadter's MU puzzle to get students to feel the difference between working inside a formal system and reasoning about that system from outside — the move he christens JOOTSing, and which he illustrates with the invariant that makes MU underivable — and I want to close the term with Abbott's *Flatland*, which I have taught twice and still cannot decide how to frame. What I want from you is a critical reading of the novella through the JOOTSing idea, one that takes both sides seriously: not a summary of *Flatland* as a parable about higher dimensions, and not Gödel-for-beginners with a Sphere in it. The students will already know that MU cannot be produced, and that the reason is an invariant no amount of clever rule-pushing reveals from within; the question I care about is what A Square's situation adds to, or quietly subtracts from, that picture. I should say that I have been arguing about this for weeks with a colleague who insists the Sphere is the villain of the book, and I am not sure he is wrong, but I am not sure the book is on his side either.

Two things in particular seem to me to carry the philosophical weight, and I would like you to spend real time on both. First, the Sphere: he has done the thing the MU-puzzle solver cannot do unaided — he has been lifted out, he has seen the third dimension — and yet when A Square, standing in Spaceland, infers a fourth dimension by exactly the analogy the Sphere used on him, the Sphere flatly denies that anything of the kind could exist. Is that the novella's sharpest observation, namely that the escapee is simply the next system's prisoner, so that JOOTSing is always available one level up and never at the top? Or is it a failure of nerve on Abbott's part, an unwillingness to let his own logic run? And is there a difference, philosophically, between the invariant argument that dispatches MU — a proof, transferable to any competent reader, which is why it counts as knowledge — and A Square's insight, which arrives by vision and cannot be argued into anyone, which is why he ends in prison writing a book addressed to the inhabitants of Space? I want to know whether the novella is dramatizing a distinction that the logic textbooks leave out, or whether it is trading on a confusion between proof and revelation. Second, the social material: the persecution of irregular figures, the suppression of the color movement, the treatment of women as mere lines. Is the political and religious conformity in Flatland the same phenomenon as the epistemic confinement, such that one kind of JOOTSing would free you from all of it, or does the book conflate two quite different things — a formal system's inability to certify its own consistency and a society's refusal to tolerate deviation — and if it conflates them, is that a flaw or the point?

Where the analogy strains, I would rather you say so than paper over it: dimensions are a geometric matter and consistency is a metatheoretic one, and the MU-puzzle escape works by ascending to a stronger system rather than by leaving systems behind altogether, so there may be less here than the parallel invites. Tell me which scenes I should put in front of the class, what I should say the novella shows that the puzzle cannot, and what misconceptions I need to head off before students walk away thinking that Gödel licenses mysticism or that any system can be escaped by sufficient cleverness.
