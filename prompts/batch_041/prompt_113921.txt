My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about the ladder that runs from Darwinian to Skinnerian to Popperian to Gregorian creatures, and the argument stalled on an analogy he kept returning to: that the four rungs are one and the same process — blind variation with selective retention — performed at different speeds and in different places. On his reading, natural selection is trial-and-error learning in which the trial is an organism and the error is death; a Skinnerian creature runs the same loop with the trial moved inside the skin, where the error costs it a reward rather than its life; a Popperian creature runs the loop faster and further in, eliminating candidate actions and hypotheses before any of them is performed; and a Gregorian creature runs it once more, except that the variations it entertains are supplied by tools of thought borrowed from its culture rather than by its own nervous system. From this he drew a fairly strong conclusion: that the four labels mark positions on a single continuum of speed and internalisation, that the differences between them are differences of degree rather than of kind, and that there is consequently nothing categorically special about the Popperian rung — no difference in kind between a creature that must try things out in the world and one that can try them out in its head, only a difference in how much of the trying has been brought indoors. I don't think the argument survives its own analogy, but I could not say precisely where it fails, and I would rather have it audited than rebutted in the abstract; I don't need the taxonomy restated. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of variation, of selection, of retention or heredity, of the environment, of the trial, and of the standard against which a trial counts as failed — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am particularly interested in three things. First, whether the analogy has any place for the vehicle of retention: if a creature's candidates are eliminated internally, what corresponds to the inheritance that carries a successful variation forward on the other rungs, and does the continuum story require the lessons of a single lifetime to be transmitted? Second, who or what does the eliminating when the selection is internal, and whether that internal critic can be taken as given or must itself be a product of the earlier rungs — and if the latter, whether that strengthens his continuum claim or quietly reinstates the distinctions he wants to dissolve. Third, whether speed and internalisation are really the ordering principle, given that some Darwinian processes are extremely fast and some Skinnerian ones extremely slow; if they are not, tell me what the ordering principle actually is. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from those that do not, say whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table, and tell me what a better analogy would be, if there is one, for what is distinctive about being able to let a hypothesis fail in the head rather than in the world. Please answer in British English.
