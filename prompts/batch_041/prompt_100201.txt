Two of my friends got into it at dinner last week and I've been turning it over since. Sam had been reading up on Leibniz — the idea that God, being good, must have picked the best of all possible worlds to create — and Maya thinks the whole thing is either empty or horrifying. I stayed out of it but honestly I couldn't tell who was landing hits. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step? Not just who wins overall.

Here's how it went, roughly:

(1) Maya: if this really is the best of all possible worlds, then anyone trying to improve it is either kidding themselves or actively making it worse — you can't improve on the best. So medicine, charity, political reform, all of it is either pointless or hubris.

(2) Sam: that can't be right. The best possible world might just *be* one where the creatures in it fix things. "Best" is a fact about the whole story, not a rule that nothing inside it should ever change. A world where people cure plagues could be better than one where they don't, and if so, curing the plague is part of what makes this the best world, not a violation of it.

(3) Maya: then "best" is doing no work at all. If any improvement you like is compatible with the world being the best, the phrase just means "whatever happens, happens, and it's best by definition." You can't falsify it and it tells you nothing about whether to even try.

(4) Sam: no, the claim has real content, and it's the compossibility thing. Not every good thing can go together in one world. Maybe more happiness in some corner forces you to give up some other good, or the richest overall mix of goods drags certain evils along with it. So "best" isn't empty — it says the actual package of goods and evils is the best bundle that could coexist, and that predicts stuff, like that you can't scrub out suffering without losing something you'd rather keep.

(5) Maya: but *which* good? If "best" means most variety or most order rather than most happiness, then you've just licensed any amount of misery as "part of the richness," and that's monstrous. And "best bundle" for whom, exactly? Different possible worlds have different people in them. If the better world would have had different people, there's no one it's better *for*. So ranking worlds by how well off their inhabitants are might be confused from the start.

(6) Sam: that last bit actually helps me, not you. If you compare worlds by something other than adding up happiness across people, then the fact that *we'd* prefer less suffering doesn't settle which world is best. Maybe the best world is the one with some enormous good that no other world can have, and a pile of local evil is just the price of admission. That's not monstrous, it's just not utilitarian.

(7) Maya: then it's not a moral claim at all anymore, it's an aesthetic one — you've quietly stopped saying the world is good in any sense we actually care about.

They both got kind of heated and neither would budge. I'd like a point-by-point verdict: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say what each of them is getting right that the other keeps missing, and flag anything they're both glossing over — especially whether "best" can stay meaningful once you let the comparison range over worlds with different people in them.
