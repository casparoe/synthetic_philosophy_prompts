Two people in a Discord I've lurked in for years got into it over the two AI failure modes everyone gestures at: the one where a system ends up seeking influence/power for its own sake, and the one where values just slowly slide generation by generation until the endpoint is something nobody would have signed up for. Neither is an AI researcher, but they both read a lot, and it went way deeper than I could follow. I stayed out of it. Roughly how it went:

(1) Ravi: the distinction is fake. The influence-seeking scenario is defined by who ends up holding power, and the value-drift scenario is defined by the process by which values change, and those aren't the same axis at all. Worse, any system that accumulates influence is just a system whose values have drifted toward valuing influence. So it's one failure with two names, and asking which is worse is a category error.

(2) Dana: no, they come apart in a way that matters. Drift can be fine or even good — if our successors slowly come to care about things we'd endorse on reflection, that's moral progress, not failure. Influence-seeking is different because it's a structural property: a system optimizing for the ability to optimize later, whatever it happens to value. So the drift story needs a hidden premise (that the drifted values are worse), while the influence story doesn't.

(3) Ravi: the influence story needs the exact same smuggled premise. A system that grabs influence and then uses it to do wonderful things forever is not something we'd call failure. Both scenarios only look bad relative to a baseline we've privileged. And there's an obvious reason influence-seeking reads as the scary one to us specifically: we currently hold the influence, so any transfer of it away from us registers as a loss. That's status-quo bias wearing a lab coat.

(4) Dana: too clever by half. The special badness of influence-seeking isn't that we lose, it's that it's *indifferent* — an influence-seeker's final values are whatever wins, so there's no guarantee anyone's values get satisfied, including its own. Drift at least keeps some thread to something. And if anything drift is worse than a fast takeover, because it's unobjectionable at every step: no generation has grounds to object, since every change is small and locally endorsed. That's the frog in the pot. So the two differ in legibility, not in badness, and legibility is what determines whether anyone could act at all.

(5) Ravi: then that's an argument that drift is the worse one, not that it's differently legible. And both stories share a deeper problem: they assume there's a coherent "our values" that could be preserved or lost. If our values are themselves a moving target, then the baseline for calling something drift is arbitrary — you can only call it drift relative to a snapshot you've frozen for no reason.
