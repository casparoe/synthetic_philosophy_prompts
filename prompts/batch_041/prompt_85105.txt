I run a small grad reading group on formal epitemology, and for the last session of term I've got a guest who's been defending, in a book manuscript she's circulating, a view she calls the partition-first reading of Jeffrey conditionalization. Quick version, in case it matters for what you write: the rule says that when your evidence doesn't tell you which cell of some partition you're in but does tell you new probabilities for the cells, you should adopt those new cell probabilities and leave your conditional probabilities inside each cell alone. The standard gripes are that the rule is order-dependent (update on A-then-B and you can land somewhere different than B-then-A, even when the final partition and the total evidence are the same), that the posterior it hands you usually isn't anything you could have gotten by conditioning on a proposition you learned, and that the rule presupposes you already know which partition your evidence is 'about'. Her line is that all three complaints come from treating the partition as an input the rule has to be handed from outside; the partition is chosen, that's a virtue rather than a hole, and what looks like underdetermination is really the rule's way of encoding the fact that uncertain evidence is exactly the evidence that doesn't arrive with its own partition attached. I buy maybe 60% of this and I want the Q&A to do some real work.

I'm moderating next week and I need eight to ten questions that go past having her restate the view. At least one question each on: (a) what is supposed to constrain the choice of partition, if not the evidence itself, and whether 'chosen' quietly turns the rule into a whole family of rules with no way to pick between the members; (b) the order-dependence -- give me something that forces her to say whether two agents with the same final partition and the same evidence, arriving in different orders, can both be rational, plus a follow-up for whichever way she goes; (c) why the conditional probabilities inside the cells have to stay put, i.e. the rigidity assumption, since that looks like the one place where she isn't allowed to say the agent chose it; and (d) the story that the rule is just what you get when you look for the distribution closest to your old one, in the information-theoretic sense, subject to matching the new cell probabilities -- I want to know whether she thinks that's a justification or a restatement. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Then, for the last couple: one question that uses her own partition-is-chosen move against her (roughly, if partitions are chosen, what stops an agent from choosing a partition that makes the update trivially easy, or one whose cells carve up the evidence in a way that looks gerrymandered from outside), and one closing question about what would actually change her mind -- what a partition-first theorist would count as evidence against the view.

Please approach this as someone who works on Bayesian confirmation theory and has refereed in the area. Think carefully before drafting, and start with a short scratch list of the two or three replies she's most likely to give to the partition and order questions, since the follow-ups depend on those.
