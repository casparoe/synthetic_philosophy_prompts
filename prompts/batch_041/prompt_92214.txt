A friend and I have been circling one argument for about two years now, and I would like a number out of you rather than another round of us talking past each other. She is an epiphenomenalist about phenomenal properties: experiences are real, are not identical with anything physical, and never figure as causes of any physical event — including the events that constitute her saying that she is having one. I keep pressing the obvious objection, the one usually called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If the experience is doing no causal work, then her reliable production of the judgment that she is having an experience cannot be explained by the experience; a judgment merely correlated with its supposed subject through a shared neural cause looks like a lucky guess, and lucky guesses are not knowledge. So her epiphenomenalism appears to cost her the one datum she started from. Her reply, which I find more interesting than I expected, is that this only looks damaging if you assume that knowing something requires the thing known to have caused the belief. She is content to be a reliabilist: her judgments about her experiences are produced by a mechanism nomologically locked to the presence of those experiences, since both are downstream of the same brain state, and reliability of that kind is all the warrant anyone ever had for anything. She adds that causally inert is not the same as explanatorily irrelevant, and that the objection quietly presupposes a theory of causation that the exclusion problem should already have made us suspicious of.

What I want is your credence that the paradox is genuinely fatal to epiphenomenalism, where fatal means that someone who accepts epiphenomenalism cannot, without either inconsistency or a change of subject, claim to know or even justifiably believe that she has experiences. I want it decomposed rather than asserted: your probability that phenomenal properties are causally inert in the first place; conditional on that, your probability that the judgments can nonetheless amount to knowledge or justified belief, broken down into whether warrant requires a causal connection to the object, whether some non-causal relation such as acquaintance or givenness can supply it, and whether common-cause reliability suffices; your probability that the objection is really a reductio of something in the neighborhood other than epiphenomenalism — of the causal theory of knowledge, say, or of phenomenal realism itself, in which case the honest move is illusionism rather than a patch; and your probability that causally inert is even a well-formed predicate for a phenomenal property to have. Give me a number and a line of justification for each, then the combination, then the term you are least confident in and what evidence or argument would move it most. Treat knowledge as requiring only true, reliably formed, undefeated belief; I do not want the verdict turning on a Gettier-adjacent technicality.

Two people's numbers are in play here, and I want to know where the disagreement actually lives. Mine is about 0.3 and hers is about 0.85. I would also like a separate number for a question I suspect is doing hidden work in both of us: your credence that the whole matter has a determinate answer at all, rather than being a verbal dispute about how to use causation and justification. I would rather have a decomposition with its weak joint marked than a confident verdict.
