Next month I'm giving a paper at the joint social-choice / political-theory session in my department and I want a Q&A prep sheet before I go in. My claim is that Sen's liberal paradox has been misdiagnosed for fifty-odd years: the standard reading treats it as a head-on collision between weak Pareto and minimal liberalism, and then everyone just picks a side -- weaken Pareto, water down the rights condition, let people waive their rights, move to game forms. I think all those escapes miss the real trouble, which is an assumption baked into the social-choice setup itself: that a pair of alternatives differing only in what happens to one person is still a pair the Pareto principle gets to rank. Once you treat an individual's decisive pair as partly constitutive of what the alternatives even are, minimal liberalism and weak Pareto stop colliding.

The audience: two people who do Arrow-style social choice and will hit me with formal stuff, a legal philosopher who'll think I'm quietly cheating on what a right is, a Kantian who thinks rights aren't the kind of thing you can trade off at all, and a few grad students who've only ever seen the Prude/Lewd Lady Chatterley version. So what will each of them actually object to, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how likely and how damaging? Two-minute reply each, plus a note on where the reply is soft, plus the one objection I'm praying nobody raises. And for each one, tell me whether it's something people in the field basically agree on or a genuinely live fight -- don't sell me a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
