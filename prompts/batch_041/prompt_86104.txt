So I got appointed to our state parole board in January. I'm the public member, which I'm pretty sure is the seat where they want somebody who isn't a cop, a prosecutor, or a public defender, and honestly the bar is low: I read a lot of true crime and I have opinions. Our state brought discretionary parole back two years ago after 25 years of truth-in-sentencing, so we're the first board anybody around here has seen in a generation and every vote we take ends up in the paper. Seven of us, one vote each, and the chair breaks ties.

The statute is no help. It says we have to consider five things — how serious the offense was, the person's risk, his conduct inside, his insight into what he did, and his release plan — and then it never says what to do when those point in different directions. No grid, no weights, no definitions. The chair's line is that the sentence is the punishment and everything else is supposed to be repair. Two members keep bringing up Norway in the meetings, the kind of place where the sentence is the punishment and prison is supposed to fix the guy up. And I'm fairly sure one member would be happy never releasing anybody, which is an opinion, but its not in the statute.

We've decided about sixty cases. Six of them are the ones people still argue about, and those six are the only ones that tell you anything about what we actually believe.

Marcus, 44, in for a string of armed robberies. He got 15-to-life under the old law, served 19; the range a court would give him today is 8 to 12. Score came back low. Finished every program we have, wrote the victim's family a letter they never answered, line-cook job and a room at his cousin's. Released 6–1; the no was basically "robbery is robbery."

Danny, 35, burglaries. Served 3 of a 2-to-5. Score high, hasn't done a single program, won't talk about the houses he hit, no address. Denied 7–0.

Tanya, 53, shot her ex-husband during an argument, second-degree. Served 24; the range today is 12 to 18. Low score, did everything, talks about it without excuses, moving in with her sister. His family spoke against it every time. Released 5–2, and both no votes were "she took a life, she doesn't get to leave."

Ray, 29, broke a guy's jaw outside a bar. Served 2 of a 2-to-4. High score, no programs, says the other guy started it and the whole thing is a joke, no plan. Denied 6–1.

Devon, 46, distribution, and a lot of it. Served 9 of a 4-to-7. Low score, did the programs, straight talk about it, job at his uncle's shop and an apartment. Released 6–1; the no was "nine years for dealing is already generous and he hasn't paid for what he sold."

Wes, 27, drove drunk and killed a kid. Served 4 of a 6-to-9. High score, refused the victim-impact class because he says he's being punished twice, no license, nowhere to live. Denied 5–2.

So: every case where we let somebody out, he scored low, he'd done every program and could talk about the offense without excuses, he had a job and an address, and he'd served past the top of the range a court would give him today. Every case where we kept somebody in, he scored high, he'd done nothing, he had no insight and nowhere to go, and he was under that range. I've been on this board five months and I still can't tell whether we're running on risk, on insight, or on time served, because the three have never once pointed in different directions. There's also a version of this where we just let out anybody over 40, which I hope isn't what's happening.

Thursday is the case that breaks it.

Dennis, 58. Convicted of second-degree murder at 31. A guy came at him with a bottle outside a bar, he says; the state said it was over a debt. He pleaded guilty but told the judge on the record it was self-defense, which I now know is called an Alford plea, and he's been officially saying he didn't do it for 27 years. He's served 27 years. His own minimum was 25-to-life, so he's two years past the earliest date he could ever have come out, and the range a court would give the same facts today is 12 to 18.

The score comes back high. It's driven by his first arrest at 17, two juvenile adjudications, and one serious fight in the yard in 2003. The analyst's memo says the tool was validated on people within three years of release, nobody has ever checked it on a guy who's been down 27 years, his last write-up was 22 years ago, and — I'm paraphrasing her — she'd score him low if she were eyeballing it, but the vendor says we're not supposed to override the number. So "he's high risk" is either true or an artifact, depending on what you think risk is.

Programs: he's done the HVAC certificate, anger management, parenting. He refuses the accountability class, because the last module is a letter to the victim's family admitting the killing and he won't write it. He did write them his own letter saying he's sorry for what they lost. His brother has shown up to every hearing for twenty years and the DA opposes every time. He has a job offer at the shop where he did his apprenticeship and his sister, who's a nurse, will take him in.

Two members have told me privately they're voting to release because 27 years is enough for anything; two are voting to hold him until he stops lying about what he did. I'm the fifth vote and I'd rather not be the fifth vote. What's also nagging at me is that if we hold him because the tool says he's dangerous, we're doing at the back end what those laws that keep sex offenders locked up after their sentences are up do at the end, and I have a feeling that's the whole ballgame.

So here's what I want, and please don't just tell me the statute says "consider" five things, I know. Start with a two-sentence Bottom line, then give me:

What the six cases commit us to — around 150 words on which theories of ours the record is actually consistent with, which ones it rules out, and which of the surviving ones are just the same theory in different clothes.

What each theory says about Dennis — around 300 words, and a small table is fine here if it helps: theory on the left, prediction and reasoning on the right. Include the risk-number-versus-risk-judgment thing, because I genuinely don't know if that's one theory or two.

The closest case so far — around 100 words on which of the six is the best analog for Dennis, and which difference between them is doing the real work.

My vote — around 150 words. What I should vote if I want to be consistent with ourselves, what principle we'd be adopting either way, and if the honest answer is that consistency means denying him even though I think he should go home, say that out loud and tell me what that says about the rule.

The case that would settle it — around 100 words on the next case that would separate the theories that are still standing, and if no single case can separate all of them, give me the two that together would.
