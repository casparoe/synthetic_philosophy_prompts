I'm putting together a little anthology of fiction that does philosophy by other means, and I want a short story that makes the gradations-of-sentience thing feel real without a single character ever explaining it. I've got a premise and I'd rather you build on it than swap in your own, since the whole point is that it's not the story you've definately read before: a national standards body has published a rubric that gives every entity a "welfare weight" between 0 and 1, scored on four subscales — does it have nociceptors, are those signals integrated anywhere, can it form valenced memories, does it carry anything like a self-model. The numbers are load-bearing in boring ways: how much suffering a farm practice is allowed to cause, how much a lab has to justify a procedure, what a pest-control contractor owes in mitigation. A thermostat comes out at 0.02. A bee at 0.31. Your protagonist wrote the firmware for a new domestic climate system, an adaptive homeostat that scores 0.34 on the official sheet.

What I want: tell it through ordinary institutional life — forms, an appeals window, a licensing hearing, someone's Tuesday — not through debate. Nobody says the spectrum point or the where-do-you-draw-the-line point out loud. Somewhere a character is genuinely kind to something scoring near zero, and somewhere a character is casually awful to something scoring high, and neither moment gets framed as the lesson. No twist where the rubric is a hoax, and no reveal that it was secretly about AI; if AI comes up, let it come up the way it would in a committee that's already bored. End on the decision the protagonist makes about the firmware, and leave me unable to say whether the rubric is monstrous or just the least-bad thing anyone's thought of yet. 2,000–3,000 words. I read alot of this stuff and what I can't stand is the version that resolves.
