I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas — books mostly, the occasional film piece when something gets its hooks in me — and I've been obssessed with Everything Everywhere All at Once since it came out. I've watched it three times and I still can't decide whether it's a great movie about the self or a great movie with a confused theory of the self, and I want to write the piece that decides it.

Here's the lens I want to use. Not the usual "strange loop" stuff that gets trotted out in every explainer about consciousness. I want the stranger claim in Douglas Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop: that a person's "I" is distributed over numerous brains rather than being confined to one. He got there partly through grief — his wife Carol died young, and he argues, not entirely as a metaphor, that a diminished version of the loop that was her kept running in his head and in the heads of people who knew her. He's happy to use the word soul for this, and he's willing to say souls come in sizes, depending on how much of a given person is actually represented, with any real fidelity, in other minds. So: no single seat of the self, no ghost in one machine, just a pattern that gets copied around, well or badly, and is more or less there depending on the fidelity of the copy. I know this sounds like it grades human worth by how many friends you have, and I'd like that dealt with rather than dodged.

The movie, in case it helps to have it laid out: laundromat, IRS audit, verse-jumping triggered by doing something statistically improbable, Evelyn as the multiverse's biggest failure and therefore its best hope, and her daughter Joy, who in the Alphaverse was pushed by Alpha-Evelyn to keep jumping until her mind splintered, and who now exists as Jobu Tupaki, experiencing all universes at once, able to rebuild reality out of nothing, and building the Everything Bagel out of sheer nihilism. The googly eyes, the hot dog fingers, the rocks on the cliff with the subtitles, the parking lot at the end.

My problem is that the film seems to run two pictures of the distributed self and I dont think either is Hofstadter's. Evelyn is one "I" that keeps moving house — the same someone, borrowing skills and memories from other bodies — which is migration, not distribution. Jobu is one "I" that got smashed, a soul shattered into a million shards, which is destruction, not distribution. Hofstadter's version is neither: nothing moves and nothing shatters, there's just a pattern replicated more or less faithfully in several places, and the interesting questions are where the fidelity threshold sits and who still counts as her below it. I think the film cheats a little — it wants the metaphysics of copies and the emotional payoff of a single continuous someone — and I want to know whether that's a cheat or whether it's Hofstadter's point arriving through the back door. I also want the Parfit question in there: if verse-jumping is really fission, with two Evelys who both remember being her, then maybe what the film dramatizes isn't identity at all but what matters instead of it. And Jobu raises the sharpest version of the question: does a self spread across a million universes have a million points of view, or one? The film talks like she's a single somebody who has seen everything, and I'm not sure Hofstadter is entitled to that, and I want to know whether the film is.

I also have a remix I want tested. Take the film's own rule — you jump into another you, borrow her skills, and she stays put — and change one thing: the jump copies you instead of moving you, so now there are two of you who both remember everything up to the moment of the jump and then diverge. Does the story survive? Does the ending still work? Second variant: what if the fidelity were partial and visible — say each jump leaves you with 70 percent of the other Evelyn and she gets 30 percent of you? What does the film's closing moral, that kindness is the right answer precisely when you can't know what's going on, turn into under that rule?

What I want back, in this order:

- The pitch: about 120 words, the version of my thesis I'd put at the top of the piece, verdict included.
- What the movie actually assumes about selves: about 300 words on the mechanics of verse-jumping and what they commit you to — migration, distribution, or fission — and which one the film is really running.
- Hofstadter, applied: about 400 words on where the film dramatizes his view better than any argument does, and which scenes I should point readers to. I'm thinking the rocks and the parking lot, but tell me if I'm wrong and why.
- Where the two part ways: about 350 words, including the point-of-view question about Jobu and whether the film's ending needs something Hofstadter can't give it.
- My remix, tested: about 250 words running the copy-instead-of-move variant and the 70/30 variant, and telling me what either one shows.
- The bottom line: about 120 words on what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone don't, and whether I should say it gets Hofstadter right.

Two house rules: don't quote dialogue from the film or passages from Hofstadter — I've seen it and read it, and I'll pull any quotes myself, so give me your reading in your own words. And no reading list or citations, I'll add those. Just don't hedge so much that there's no thesis; I need something I can argue with my editor about.
