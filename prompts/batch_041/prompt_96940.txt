Ok so I'm a 3rd year PhD student (philosophy, mostly moral responsibility) and I've got an argument that a friend in my reading group came up with that I cannot get out of my head. It proves way too much but every step looks fine and every fix I try costs me something I care about. Would love your help finding the bad step.

Here it is:

(1) A cause is something that happens. Causes are events.
(2) An omission is not something that happens. It's the absence of an event -- nothing at all, not a weird ghostly happening.
(3) So an omission is never a cause.
(4) You're only morally responsible for an outcome if you caused it.
(5) So you're only ever responsible for outcomes your actions cause, never for outcomes you merely failed to prevent.
(6) So I'm not responsible for the kid who drowned while I watched from the bank. I never touched him, never did anything to him at all.
(7) But (6) is monstrous, and so is every cousin of it: the parent who never feeds the baby, the nurse who never checks the drip, the lifeguard who just doesn't swim.

My friend's line is that (1)-(3) is airtight and the real casualty is (4), i.e. we should just stop pretending responsibility needs causation. My instinct is that (4) is about as safe as anything in this area gets, and that the damage is done earlier, but I can't say exactly where without hand-waving.

Two things I keep tripping on. First, (3) on its own already seems to prove too much for anyone who isn't a monster: it kills a huge range of ordinary causal claims -- the drought caused the famine, the absent guard caused the break-in, the missing bolt caused the crash. If the price of (3) is that all of those are strictly false, I want to know whether that's actually a price anyone serious pays or whether they quietly redefine "cause" somewhere. Second, if I bail out by letting absences be causes, my other friend Priya says the whole thing explodes: for any outcome there are infinitely many things that didn't happen that would have prevented it (no one watered, no butterfly sneezed, the sun didn't go nova), so every outcome has infinitely many causes and picking out "the" cause is pure pragmatics. Is that objection actually fatal, or is it just the ordinary problem of causal selection that positive cases have too, dressed up?

So: which step do I give up, why did it look so good in the first place, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd most like to keep (1) and (4) and obviously (6), and I'm fine being told I can't have all three, I just want to know what I'm paying and to whom.
