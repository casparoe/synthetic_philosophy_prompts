I write about science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and each spring I teach a graduate seminar for the journalism school on argument and evidence. I'm midway through grading the midterms and would like a second opinion on one script before I settle the marks. The question was this:

"In class we discussed two cases. Case A: Rita rents a car, glances at the fuel gauge, sees that it reads three-quarters full, and believes that the tank is three-quarters full. Over the following week she checks the gauge several more times, and on each occasion the tank turns out to be as full as the gauge said. She concludes that the gauge is reliable. Case B: Sam looks at a tomato in good light and believes that it is red. Reflecting on the fact that the tomato looks red to him, he concludes that his colour vision is reliable.

Question (30 points). What epistemic problem are these two cases meant to raise? What kind of view is the problem meant to embarrass, and why? Does the problem, as stated, succeed in embarrassing that view? Defend your answer."

The student wrote:

"Both cases illustrate the bootstrapping problem, sometimes called the problem of easy knowledge. In each case a subject tries to establish the reliability of a source — a fuel gauge, a perceptual faculty — by relying on the very source whose reliability is in question. Rita's evidence for the gauge's accuracy is simply what the gauge has told her; Sam's evidence for his colour vision is what his colour vision has shown him. Since neither performs any independent check, the reasoning is circular: Rita's conclusion that the gauge is accurate can be reached only if we already grant that the gauge is accurate. Her belief is therefore not knowledge, because it rests on the very thing that is at issue.

The problem is meant to embarrass externalist views, and reliabilism in particular. Reliabilism holds that a belief is justified when it is produced by a reliable process, and it deliberately drops the requirement that the subject be able to show that the process is reliable. The bootstrapping case turns that omission against the theory. If reliability is all that matters, then Rita's conclusion looks to be in good order: the gauge really is reliable, and her belief about its reliability is produced by perception plus a simple induction, both of which are reliable processes. That strikes most of us as an absurd result. Internalists, by contrast, have nothing to fear from the case, since they require the subject to have grounds for taking the source to be reliable, and Rita has none.

I conclude that the cases do embarrass reliabilism, since a theory that licenses knowledge this easily has lost contact with what justification is for."

I'd be grateful for a point-by-point assessment: a grade, and comments keyed to particular sentences, saying what deserves credit and where the answer goes wrong or falls short, including anything a full-credit answer would have needed to say that this one doesn't. You needn't cite the literature; I'm after your judgment of the script. Please commit to a single grade rather than a range — a mark out of 30 with the corresponding letter — and if two grades seem defensible, say which one you'd defend and why it beats the alternative.
