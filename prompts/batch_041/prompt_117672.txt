I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on collective responsibility, and what I need from you is a self-contained argument, not a survey of positions. Leave the literature out of it entirely: no names, no titles, no citations, and no sheltering behind the formula that other people have said otherwise.

Consider the following structure, which I use to isolate the inefficacy objection. A harm H occurs if and only if the number of unit contributions to a common undertaking reaches a threshold T. Contributions are binary, individually indistinguishable, uncoordinated, and each is worth one unit. In the case I want you to work with, exactly N > T contributions are made, so H occurs; and every contributor knows this in advance. Each of them therefore knows that her own contribution is not needed for H, is not sufficient for H, and makes no difference to whether H occurs — the outcome is the same whether she contributes or not.

I want you to defend the following thesis: the contributors in such a case are morally responsible for H even though none of them makes a difference to it, because the inefficacy objection presupposes a criterion — marginal causal difference — that is the wrong criterion for attributing responsibility for harms that are composed rather than produced; and each contributor's responsibility is proportional to her share of the minimal set of contributions that sufficed for H, not to the difference her contribution made to the world.

Defend that thesis; do not merely state it, and do not let the defence rest on the generalisation test (what if everyone did it), on rule-consequentialism, or on the claim that the harm is caused by the aggregate as a whole — I will not accept that last phrase as an answer. In the course of the argument:

(a) Say precisely what it is for an act to be a component of a harm, in a way that distinguishes composition from mere aggregation.

(b) Give and defend an answer to the question of how responsibility is divided when there is causal slack — when more contributions were made than the threshold required — and explain why your division is not arbitrary.

(c) Meet the objection that your view makes responsibility hostage to facts about other people's choices and to the size of the group, so that one and the same act becomes more or less wrongful depending on how many others happen to be contributing.

(d) Say what would count as a decisive refutation of the thesis, and whether any ordinary case — a single vote, a single purchase, a single flight — actually has this structure, or whether such cases differ in ways that matter.

Begin your reply with a single sentence stating the position you are defending, then argue for it: no preamble, no restating of my setup. State at the end how confident you are in the argument and identify the step you would defend with the least conviction.
