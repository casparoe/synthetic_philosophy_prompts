I'm a retired chemist, and I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group for about a year. We did Kavka's toxin puzzle last week — the setup where a billionaire offers you a million dollars if you intend tonight to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make you sick for a day, and you're free to change your mind after the money is locked in. A friend in the group says the puzzle actually shows something stronger than people think: that a rational agent can't get the money at all. His argument goes like this.

(1) You can intend to do X only if you believe you will do X.
(2) If you're rational, and you know the money is paid for the intention alone, then once you've got the money you'll see no reason to drink the toxin.
(3) So you'll believe you won't drink it.
(4) By (1), you can't intend to drink it.
(5) So you can't get the money.
(6) But it seems obvious that someone could form the intention, take the money, and skip the toxin — and that this is exactly what a rational agent would do.
(7) So rationality makes it impossible to do the rational thing.

I can't find the exit. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (1) if I can, because it seems true of intentions. There's definately alot riding on how the answer goes.
