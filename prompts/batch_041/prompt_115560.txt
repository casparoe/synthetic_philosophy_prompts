In 2012 the ENCODE consortium published a set of papers reporting that its data enabled it to "assign biochemical functions for 80% of the genome." The operational definition behind that figure was generous: a functional element was "a discrete genome segment that encodes a defined product (for example, protein or non-coding RNA) or displays a reproducible biochemical signature (for example, protein binding, or a specific chromatin structure)." The same paper reports that comparative genomic work puts only about 3–8% of bases under purifying selection, and the data behind the 80% figure come from assays across roughly 147 cell types. Critics replied that reproducible biochemical activity in at least one cell type is a very low bar — that defining function as any causal role makes nearly every base functional and drains the word of content — while the consortium answered that it was offering an operational catalog of elements, not a claim about what natural selection has preserved.

I work as a software engineer, and the reason this interests me is that it looks like a dispute about whether DNA is a code. I want you to treat the episode as a case study for the sender–receiver (teleosemantic) account of biological information: roughly, a state carries information about something when a sender produces it, a receiver is set up to be sensitive to it, and the receiver's response is what the state is for. Shannon-style correlation I already understand and it will not settle anything here; the account I want tested is the one that ties information to use. Run the analysis in both directions. First, map the episode onto the theory: what would the 80% figure have to show for the genome to count as code-like, what distinguishes a signal from incidental chemistry on this view, and how does the actual genetic code — an arbitrary triplet-to-amino-acid mapping with dedicated decoding machinery — compare to a transcription factor binding site or a stretch of noncoding RNA? Then say which parts of the episode the theory explains well (I suspect both the critics' intuition about noise and the defenders' operationalism fall out of it) and where it misdescribes the case or is simply silent: does it have anything to say about the resource value of the catalog, or about sequences that have a causal role but no sender and no consumer? Finally, turn it around: does ENCODE confirm the theory, count as an anomaly, or reveal a case the theory never anticipated — and does the episode show that "DNA is a code" is a thesis that has to be graded rather than answered yes or no?
