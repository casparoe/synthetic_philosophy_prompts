I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and I'm trying to settle on a dissertation area. The topic I keep circling is the precautionary principle -- the family of regulatory principles that license acting against a possible hazard before the science is settled. What strikes me is that the literature seems stuck in a loop between defenders and critics: one side treats the principle as a sensible response to uncertainty, the other as an incoherent licence for arbitrary regulation, and the two rarely meet. I suspect the real work now is not defending or attacking the principle wholesale but identifying which of its internal questions are upstream of the rest.

I'd like you to give me a defended agenda rather than a survey. Which open conceptual questions about the precautionary principle most need settling, in what order, and why? For each, tell me why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone trained in philosophy of science rather than law or economics, how neglected it is, and what argument or case over the next few years would move it up or down my list.

One framing I want you to engage with directly, and to tell me if I'm overstating it: I think the principle is usually stated as a first-order rule for choosing actions under uncertainty, but that most of the real disagreement is actually about an earlier, epistemic question -- namely when a hazard may be treated as a probabilistic risk to be traded off against benefits, and when it must instead be treated as a non-probabilistic threat that shifts the burden of proof onto the proponent of the activity. If that is right, then several of the familiar open questions may collapse into that one. Is it the genuine upstream question, or is it a redescription that hides the hard cases?

Please be explicit about how confident you are in the ranking you give, and single out the element of it you're least sure about.
