I'm a third-year undergraduate currently taking a course on rings and modules, and I've been reading ahead into the set-theoretic machinery the course keeps invoking without proof. In a problem session last week a friend of mine — who is taking the parallel logic and set theory course, and who is already suspicious of the axiom of choice on the usual grounds — produced the argument below, which he says shows that Zorn's lemma quietly smuggles in more than it advertises. I can follow every line on its own and I cannot find the seam, but I refuse to accept the conclusion.

The setting, so there is no ambiguity: let R be a commutative ring with identity and M an R-module; call a subset S ⊆ M linearly independent if every finite subset of it is, in the usual sense (if r₁s₁ + … + rₙsₙ = 0 with the sᵢ distinct elements of S, then r₁ = … = rₙ = 0), and call S a basis if it is linearly independent and spans M. His argument: (1) Let P be the set of linearly independent subsets of M, ordered by inclusion. (2) P is nonempty, since the empty set is linearly independent. (3) Every chain in P has an upper bound in P: if C is a chain, its union is linearly independent, because any finite relation among elements of that union involves only finitely many elements, each lying in some member of C, and since C is totally ordered those finitely many members have a largest, which is itself linearly independent. (4) So by Zorn's lemma P has a maximal element S. (5) Suppose S does not span M, and choose v ∈ M not in the span of S. (6) Any finite relation r₁s₁ + … + rₙsₙ + rv = 0 among elements of S ∪ {v} is either a relation among elements of S alone, when r = 0, which forces all the coefficients to vanish, or else it can be divided through by the nonzero r to exhibit v as an R-linear combination of elements of S, contrary to the choice of v. (7) Hence S ∪ {v} is linearly independent, contradicting the maximality of S. (8) Therefore S spans M, so S is a basis and every R-module is free. (9) In particular Z/2, regarded as a module over Z, has a basis, and so does every abelian group.

Step (9) is false — Z/2 is plainly not a free abelian group — and my friend treats this as a reductio of Zorn's lemma itself, which he was happy to discard anyway.
