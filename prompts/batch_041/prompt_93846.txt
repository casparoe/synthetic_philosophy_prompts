I teach a 90-minute session of an upper-level seminar in decision theory and rational choice — about twenty students, one room, no computers, and they are already bored of the prisoner's dilemma and of Nash equilibrium. I want them to feel, rather than hear about, the force of this idea: when the person across from you is reasoning exactly as you are, your deliberation is no longer about what you will do but about what everyone in your position does, and the right move can flip as a result. I also want them to feel its boundary — to leave having found for themselves that it works only inside a "we" that has been stipulated rather than derived, and that it goes silent the moment the stipulation fails. Do not let the name of the position be spoken in the room until the debrief, and no lecture before the first round.

Build the activity on this spine, which I have sketched and want you to make runnable. Change it if you can do better, but keep the three-way contrast.

Payoffs throughout: mutual cooperation, 3 each; mutual defection, 1 each; unilateral defection, 5 to the defector and 0 to the cooperator.

Round 1, about 8 minutes. Half the students draw a red card at the door and half blue, for no stated reason. Each writes C or D on a slip, for an anonymous one-shot partner. I collect, pair at random, score, and post results by slip number.

Round 2, about 12 minutes. I announce that the pairing rule has changed: every slip will now be scored against itself. Nobody may revise. Those who wrote D collect 1 and those who wrote C collect 3, from slips already filled in. I then let them write again under the new rule, and we spend a few minutes with two volunteers putting the best case for D and the best case for C on the board — still unnamed.

Round 3, about 15 minutes, two variants that must be felt as separate steps. (a) The copy is written by someone who drew the other colour: same payoffs, same information, different label. Most will hold that the round-2 argument still runs. (b) I hand out a variant table in which mutual cooperation pays you 3 and your counterpart 1, while mutual defection pays you 1 and your counterpart 3. Now they must write something, and every justification available to them will be a prediction or a bargaining claim rather than the round-2 argument.

Round 4, if time, about 10 minutes. Treat the room as one public-good game with contribution cost c and per-contributor benefit b. The answer on the diagonal is to contribute just in case nb exceeds c, where n is the number contributing — so the more people there are, the stronger the case for contributing, the reverse of the not-pivotal instinct. Put the threshold on the board, then ask them how likely it is that all twenty of them are the same reasoner, and what happens to those who act as though they are.

Materials: index cards, slips, a whiteboard, both payoff tables pre-printed, and one small non-monetary prize so the scores matter a little but not enough to make them strategize.

Then the debrief, about 20 minutes, mapping each round onto a specific claim: choosing a move versus choosing a cell; the difference between the other's sameness as a stipulation and as a prediction; the causal objection that my writing C does not make my copy write C; the reading on which the deliberating subject is the pair rather than the individual, and the standard objection to it; and the reading on which the argument is only an equilibrium refinement that selects a cell on the diagonal and so has nothing to say off it. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time — at minimum, that someone writes a conditional in round 1; that the round-2 reveal lands as a gotcha unless I handle it; that a faction will dig in on the causal objection and I must not settle it for them; that the round-3b discussion will overrun; and that if nobody defects in round 2 there is no tension to work with and I will need a way to seed the case for defection. Be frank about at least one feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply myself. My own candidate is that the activity hands them a guaranteed duplicate, whereas the argument has to work in the world with no such guarantee; say whether that is the most serious gap or whether something else is.

At the end, name the position and its origin (the term is Hofstadter's) and say what it does and does not cover. No citations or reading list — I don't want the students sent to the literature, and I would rather you not gesture at sources you cannot check.

Open with one sentence naming the single design feature the whole activity turns on, then give the design straight through, with no preamble and no restatement of my request.
