I am a PhD student in political theory, and I have been asked to run one session of our departmental political theory workshop. The group is eighteen people, a mix of graduate students and faculty, and I have ninety minutes, with no preparation expected from anyone beforehand. My topic is the boundary problem in democratic theory: the difficulty that any appeal to democratic legitimacy seems already to presuppose a bounded demos, so that the demos looks as though it must constitute itself, and the question of who belongs can never be settled by the very procedure whose authority depends on it.

I want to run this as an activity rather than a discussion. When I have simply explained the circularity, people nod and then go on reasoning as if the demos were given. I need the rules of an exercise to put them inside the circle so that they feel it before I name it.

Please design the activity, including its rules or roles, its materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and the debrief.

Here is what I want them to arrive at through play, not through my summary:

1. That the currently enfranchised decide the boundary, and therefore have a standing incentive to draw it in their own favor, which makes any founding decision look like a seizure of power rather than a legitimate authorization.
2. That there are at least two candidate principles for drawing the boundary, and that they diverge. One enfranchises everyone whose interests are at stake in the decision; the other enfranchises everyone subject to the coercive enforcement of the decision. I want the exercise to force participants to choose between them and then to discover that each principle yields a different membership, so that neither can be treated as obviously correct.
3. That the first principle, followed honestly, threatens to make the demos unbounded, because interests overlap and propagate outward, while the second secures a bounded demos only by excluding people who are manifestly affected.
4. That the regress is genuine: any attempt to fix the boundary by appeal to a higher rule — a constitution, a principle, an impartial chair — immediately raises the question of who was entitled to adopt that rule, and the answer reopens the same problem.

The structure I have in mind, which you should feel free to improve: several constituencies that differ in population and in how much each member stands to gain or lose from a single pending decision; an initial electorate that is not the whole room; a sequence of rounds in which the current electorate votes both on the substantive decision and on whether to amend who may vote; and the option to adopt either boundary principle, with the exercise arranged so that each adoption produces its characteristic failure. Payoffs should be in tokens or points rather than money.

Two features I especially want. First, at some point a participant must challenge whether a given person or constituency belongs, and the challenge must land on me, the facilitator. I want a designed moment in which my authority to rule on that challenge is itself shown to be the thing in question, so that the regress is enacted rather than described. Second, the moment when someone proposes simply including everyone should be allowed to happen and should then be examined rather than applauded, since it either enlarges the demos without limit or quietly reintroduces a criterion for membership.

For the debrief, map each round onto the corresponding part of the problem, and be explicit about which participant behavior illustrates which point. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where the group will reach for fairness instead of noticing the structural issue, where a dominant participant will capture the franchise, where they will conflate the substantive fight over the decision with the boundary question — and how I should handle each. Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce. I already suspect that a lottery or a founding draw sanitizes the historical violence and contingency that actually fix boundaries; that a room of eighteen makes the demos trivially bounded, so the unboundedness of the first principle will have to be argued rather than felt; and that the international and future-generations dimensions do not fit inside the room. Tell me which of these gaps I must supply verbally and which I can leave unmentioned.
