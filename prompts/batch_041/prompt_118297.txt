Construct a pair of cases for the extended mind debate, engineered so that the standard criteria for cognitive extension deliver opposite verdicts while every functional feature of the situation is held fixed.

The background the cases may assume (set it out in your own words if you need to, and do not depend on the reader's recognising any particular paper): on the parity principle, an external resource that plays a role which we would unhesitatingly count as cognitive if it were played inside the head is to be counted as part of the cognitive process; the conditions usually added alongside it require that the resource be constantly available, easily accessible and automatically endorsed by the subject, and that it be integrated into the subject's activity much as a familiar tool is. The stock illustration is a memory-impaired subject who consults a notebook.

Requirements:

1. Two subjects, or one subject in two situations, whose relation to the external resource is functionally identical: same content, same reliability, same effortless consultation, same automatic endorsement, same role in guiding what they do. Exactly one further feature differs between the two cases, and that feature is not functional.

2. In one case the verdict is that the resource is part of the subject's mind; in the other it is not. Describe each case in a single paragraph, realistic and contemporary: no science fiction, no imagined neurotechnology, no difference that consists merely in one resource being biological or implanted, and no reuse of the familiar notebook.

3. Derive each verdict rather than asserting it. State the parity-principle argument, then the conditions, then the conclusion.

4. Then report honestly on the construction. Name the single feature doing the work; remove it and say whether the verdict flips; and if the feature can be redescribed as a difference in availability, accessibility, endorsement or reliability, say so and rebuild the pair. Candidate features of the sort that might do the work include who owns or controls the resource, whether it is shared with others, and whether the subject understands how it operates; treat that list as neither exhaustive nor reliable. If every pair you can construct smuggles in a functional difference, report that as the result and say what it shows.

The pair is for an exercise testing whether the advertised criteria for extension are jointly sufficient, so a pair that stipulates its verdicts rather than generating them from the criteria is worthless. Close with the strongest reply available to a defender of extended cognition and say whether that reply adds a condition to the criteria or denies the intuition; then say which verdict the cases force and which they merely invite. Keep the whole reply to roughly eight hundred words.
