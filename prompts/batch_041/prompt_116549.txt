I would like you to attack a position I hold, as hard as it deserves.

The position: an AI system can be constrained to avoid harmful side effects without anyone first writing down a full specification of what is valuable. The constraint is usually called low impact. The claim is that low impact is a genuinely thinner notion than acting well, and that it can be defined precisely enough to serve as a training signal or a hard constraint even while the value specification problem remains unsolved.

Here is the definition I have in mind, spelled out so that you can see exactly what is being attacked. Fix a counterfactual default policy — roughly, the system continues on its current trajectory and does nothing out of the ordinary. For any future state, define its option value as some measure of what agents could still achieve from that state: the size of the set of futures still reachable from it, or the range of payoffs attainable from it. The impact of a system action is the expected reduction, relative to the default policy, in option value. Low impact means that expected reduction is small. Nothing in this definition refers to which futures are good; it refers only to how many of them remain open. That is supposed to be the reason the measure does not smuggle in a full theory of value.

Three choices in that definition are load-bearing, and I suspect the objections will concentrate on them: (i) the default policy that supplies the baseline, (ii) the option-value quantity that gets reduced, and (iii) the claim that both are value-neutral.

What I want:

1. What would count against the position? Give me the arguments, thought experiments, formal results, and empirical findings that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. Include anything that has arguably already been demonstrated in the literature on side-effect avoidance, safe exploration, and corrigibility.

2. For each objection, say which of the three load-bearing choices it attacks, and whether it is fatal to the whole program or only to my particular version of it.

3. Separate the objections that a defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against it from those a defender would dismiss as question-begging, and say whether each dismissal is legitimate.

4. Address the insulation worry directly. There is a tempting move in which every counterexample is met with the reply that impact is only ever relative to a model, a baseline, or a set of features, and the position becomes unfalsifiable. Is that what happens here? If it is, is unfalsifiability fatal for a position of this kind, or is there a version that stays substantive and still does the work I want?

5. State the strongest version of the opposing position — that avoiding side effects cannot be defined without a full specification of value, because any impact measure is relative to a choice of state variables and dynamics, which is itself a value-laden choice, or because it presupposes that leaving options open is good. Then tell me whether that argument succeeds or whether it conflates being value-laden with requiring a complete value specification.

Two things would move me most: a case in which the measure is fully satisfied and we would all agree the outcome is a disaster, and a case in which the measure can only be satisfied by an agent that does nothing at all. Tell me if either exists.

I do not want a reading list. I want the structure of the objections and an honest verdict on how much of the position survives.
