I am preparing teaching materials on the Frege–Geach problem and have been working from a rough rendering, in my own words, of an argument that I take to be Fregean in spirit. I do not have the original text in front of me, so what follows is a paraphrase rather than a quotation, and what I want is help understanding the argument, not verification of its wording.

The argument runs roughly as follows. Take the sentence: If lying is wrong, then getting one's little brother to lie is wrong. Here the clause lying is wrong occurs inside a larger sentence. Whoever utters the whole conditional does not assert that clause; she may assert the conditional while denying outright that lying is wrong, and she may be entirely indifferent to the matter. The same holds for other embeddings: in It is possible that lying is wrong, in She believes that lying is wrong, and in Lying is not wrong, the words lying is wrong appear without being put forward as true. Yet the clause must mean the same thing in these occurrences as it does when it stands alone and is asserted. If it did not, the conditional would trade on a pun, the inference from the conditional plus the antecedent to the consequent would be an equivocation rather than a valid application of modus ponens, and the negated sentence would not be the contradictory of the original. So whatever the meaning of the term wrong is, it must be something the term carries into every context, asserted or unasserted, and it must be capable of forming part of the content of something that can be true or false. But if the meaning of wrong consisted in the fact that sentences containing it express an attitude — disapproval, say — then in the embedded occurrences there would be no attitude expressed at all, and the required sameness of meaning would fail.

What I am struggling with is how to state precisely what this argument establishes and how much it establishes. In particular:

1. What exactly does the sameness-of-meaning requirement demand? Is it a requirement on the meaning of the term, on the content of the clause, or on the identity of the thought? What is the smallest version of it that still yields the objection?

2. At which step does an expressivist about moral terms come under pressure? I can see the objection as targeting the claim that the meaning of wrong is exhausted by the attitude expressed by sentences containing it. But I am not sure whether the objection also targets the weaker claim that the attitude is expressed in the primary or central use of the term, with the embedded occurrences handled by some secondary mechanism. Which of these two claims does the argument actually refute?

3. There is a response in the literature, as I understand it, according to which the embedded clause does not express an attitude but rather attributes one, or prescribes the attitude, or uses the attitude in a different mode. I would like this response unpacked as a piece of semantic machinery rather than a slogan. Does it preserve the sameness of meaning, or does it concede the point by giving wrong a descriptive content that then does the logical work?

4. A related response, which I have seen described as a two-component or descriptive-plus-evaluative analysis, treats an evaluative predicate as combining a purely descriptive element with an evaluative one, so that the descriptive element secures the logic and the evaluative element secures the attitude. Does this solve the problem or merely relocate it? If the evaluative element is inert in the logic, in what sense is the resulting view still expressivist?

5. Finally, I am confused about the relation between two problems that are sometimes run together. One concerns the term in the antecedent of a conditional; another concerns the term under negation, in a sentence such as Lying is not wrong. Are these the same difficulty for the expressivist, or are they two difficulties with different shapes and different possible solutions? I have a vague sense that the negation case is harder, because the expressivist must say what attitude the negated sentence expresses without identifying it with the mere absence or withholding of the attitude expressed by the unnegated sentence. If that sense is right, I would like it explained; if it is wrong, I would like to be told where the asymmetry I am imagining breaks down.

6. Last, if the expressivist offers an account of validity itself — so that calling an inference valid is not the assertion of a proposition but the expression of an attitude toward a pattern of inference — does the original problem simply recur at the level of the expressivist's own metalanguage? What, if anything, blocks that regress?

Please explain the argument step by step, flagging at each stage what is being assumed, and then answer the numbered questions in order. For each proposed escape route, tell me what it buys and what it costs, and say plainly whether it is a solution or a relocation of the difficulty. I am interested in the structure of the problem, not in who said what where; no reading list or citations are needed.
