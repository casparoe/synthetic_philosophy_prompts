Hey — I host a small podcast about the history of economic ideas (audience is basically a dozen grad students and my brother), and next week's guest is a philosopher of science who's built a reputation on defending Malthus. Her line, roughly: the famous prediction, that any gain in food production gets eaten up by population growth so average living standards can't rise for long, was never actually falsified. It got *outrun*. The world changed for reasons the theory never claimed to cover — first fossil fuels, then the demographic transition, where richer people started having fewer kids instead of more. On her view Malthus was right but early, and that's a third category, not a polite way of saying wrong.

I buy maybe 60% of it and I want to actually push her rather then let her do a victory lap. Can you draft 8 to 10 questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? I want at least one question each on:

- what "right" is even supposed to mean here — right about the mechanism (diminishing returns to land), right about the magnitudes, or just right about a conditional?
- the difference between a theory being outrun and a theory being rescued ad hoc — how do you tell those apart without just rewarding whatever the defender says next?
- whether right-but-early is falsifiable at all, and what observation, even in principle, would count against it
- whether the demographic transition refutes the behavioral core of the theory (that fertility climbs with income) rather than just the timing — because that looks like a real refutation, not a delay
- the conditional trap: if the claim is that absent any other changes population will outrun food, and something always changes, has the theory bought immunity from ever being wrong? Is it true but empty?
- how her position differs from just saying theories are tools, or from the flat claim that no long-run prediction can ever be refuted
- whether the category travels — does the same move work for peak oil or resource-depletion forecasts, or does it only flatter Malthus?

Also, I vaguely recall Malthus himself kept tinkering with what counts as a "check" as the editions went by, so maybe work in a question about whether widening the theory counts as the author rescuing it himself.

Keep it in American English, skip the reading lists and citations, and cap the whole thing at 900 words.
