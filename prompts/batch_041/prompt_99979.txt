I'm a novelist — three books in, the last one was a historical thing about a lighthouse keeper, so this is a bit of a departure — and I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading about sortition, the idea of filling political offices by lottery instead of by election. The more I read, the more I think it's criminally underused in fiction: everyone writes dystopias about elections going wrong or AI taking over, but almost nobody writes about what actually happens to a person when they're told, congratulations, you're governing now, a random number generator picked you. I want to write a short story that's genuinely *about* the idea rather than set in a world where the idea is just wallpaper. Not a civics lecture with a plot stapled on — I want the concept itself to be the thing that breaks or saves a character.

Here's what I've got so far, and feel free to push back on any of it. The setting is a fictional mid-sized river town — call it Marrow — that, after a bribery scandal, dissolves its city council and replaces it with a Citizens' Assembly of forty people drawn by lot from the voter rolls, with two wrinkles I made up: (a) every seat also has a "reserve" drawn at the same time, who takes over if the primary drops out or dies, and (b) every decision the Assembly makes expires after a year and has to be re-lotted and re-argued, so nothing is ever final. My protagonist is a reserve — she only got in because the woman whose name came out of the drum had a stroke, so she's been governing for eight months on what she's pretty sure is a clerical error, and the town is about to vote (sorry, lot) on whether to let a chemical plant reopen on the north bank. I want the story to interrogate something specific: whether the legitimacy of sortition lives in the randomness, or the deliberation, or the fact that the person can't be bought because they never campaigned — and whether a substitute, a second-choice pick, carries any of that or is just a ghost in a chair. Maybe there's a fourth angle I haven't seen, about what it does to a person to hold power they didn't ask for and can't keep.

So: write me the story. Make definite choices — pick a stance on what her seat actually means and let it drive the plot, don't hedge, and don't end on "well, who can say." And at the end, give me a short note (a paragraph or two, plain prose) on where you've taken liberties with sortition as it's actually theorized, or leaned on a reading of it that people argue about, so I know what I'd be defending if a political theorist read the draft.
