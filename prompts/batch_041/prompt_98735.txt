Exam question:
Explain the wrong kind of reasons problem for fitting-attitude analyses of value. Your answer should (i) state the fitting-attitude analysis and the demon-style objection; (ii) explain what distinguishes reasons of the right kind from reasons of the wrong kind; and (iii) assess whether the object-given/state-given distinction is an adequate solution. Be explicit about whether the distinction solves the problem or merely restates it.

Student answer:
The fitting-attitude analysis (FA) holds that for something to be valuable is for a pro-attitude toward it to be fitting. On this view, value is not itself a reason for the attitude; rather, the value property consists in the existence of reasons for the attitude. This is the buck-passing account, and it is the standard way of developing FA.

The wrong kind of reasons problem is usually introduced with a demon case. Suppose an evil demon threatens to torture you unless you admire him. The threat gives you a reason to admire the demon. But intuitively the demon is not admirable or valuable just because he threatens you. If FA is correct, however, the threat makes the admiration fitting, and so makes the demon valuable. This is a reason of the wrong kind: it counts in favor of having the attitude, but it does not bear on the fittingness of the attitude.

The standard solution is the object-given/state-given distinction. Object-given reasons concern the object of the attitude. For example, the demon's cruelty is an object-given reason not to admire him. State-given reasons concern the state of having the attitude. The threat of torture is a state-given reason: it is a reason to be in the state of admiring the demon. Only object-given reasons bear on fittingness. Since the demon's threat is state-given, it does not make the admiration fitting and does not confer value on the demon. So FA survives the demon case.

I think this distinction does solve the problem, but it is somewhat stipulative. We are defining fittingness in terms of object-given reasons, and then saying that wrong-kind reasons are not object-given. Still, it captures a real difference. The demon case shows that we can have reasons to bring about an attitude that are not reasons for the attitude. Strictly speaking, a state-given reason is not a reason at all; it is a cause of the attitude, and causes are not reasons. So the distinction successfully eliminates wrong-kind reasons from the domain of reasons.

One limitation is that the problem only arises for attitudes that can be caused by threats, like admiration, desire, and belief. It does not arise for actions, since actions are not fitting or unfitting in the same way. So the problem is narrower than it first appears. But within that domain, the object-given/state-given distinction is the right response, and it shows that the wrong kind of reasons problem is not a fatal objection to FA.
