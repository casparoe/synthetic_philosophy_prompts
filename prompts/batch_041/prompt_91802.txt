I am a novelist, and I am writing a book in which a picture conservator and a philosopher of time share a studio and a long quarrel. I need a case for them to turn over together, and I want it built to order rather than borrowed.

Construct a thought experiment on the difference between endurantism and perdurantism. The familiar exhibits are no use to me: no ship with its planks replaced one by one, no divided person, no lump of clay that becomes a statue, nothing out of science fiction. I want something small, contemporary and domestic — an object in a room, a garden or a workshop, of a kind a reader can hold in mind without a diagram.

Constraints. The scenario itself must run to a single paragraph. Between the opening and the close of the story, something must become true of the object that was not true of it before, and time alone, acting on the object itself, must be what brings that about: no part of it is added, removed or exchanged, and no shift in other people's opinions, tastes or markets may be what changes it. The property in dispute must be one that no momentary state of the object could have on its own — it must reach back into the object's past or forward into its future. And the case must resolve into one sharp question with a sentence attached to it: does the thing now present itself have the property, or does the property belong only to a longer entity of which what is now present is a part? Both views must agree on every plain fact, and on whether the sentence is true; they must part company over what bears the property, and so over what makes the sentence true. Neither side may escape by declaring the sentence loose talk, and neither may be required to abandon its own theory in order to answer — the case should be one each can handle, though not comfortably.

Then, in this order: the case; the verdict of each view and the reasoning, given in that view's own terms; the single feature of the case that does the work, and what becomes of the case once that feature is removed — does it collapse into something familiar, or dissolve entirely; how a committed defender of whichever view comes off worse would attack the case, and whether the attack lands; and whether any part of the disagreement is merely verbal, and if so, which part.

I want no names of philosophers, no citations and no reading lists — nothing that asks me to take an authority's word for it. Invent the case; do not adapt one. And let the divergence be earned by the two theories rather than written into the scenario.

Write the whole reply in British English.
