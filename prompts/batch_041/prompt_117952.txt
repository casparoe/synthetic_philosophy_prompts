I want to do something with the Clever Hans case that I dont think gets done properly, and I want you to do it through a particular pair of ideas about identifying what a system is actually made of.

Setup, briefly, since I'd rather you work from facts than from memory of the story: Hans was a horse in Germany around 1900 whose owner, a gymnasium maths teacher named Wilhelm von Osten, exhibited him doing arithmetic, telling the time, spelling and so on, all by tapping his hoof. In 1904 a commission of experts -- a vet, a circus manager, a cavalry officer, schoolteachers, the director of the Berlin zoo -- looked into it and concluded there was no trickery. Then Oskar Pfungst, working under Carl Stumpf, ran proper trials and found that Hans answered correctly only when the questioner knew the answer and Hans could see the questioner: about 89% correct when he could see them, about 6% when he couldn't. What was actually happening is that the questioner would unconsciously tense and then release at the moment of the answer, and Hans read that. Pfungst also ran the role-reversed version in the lab, playing the horse himself, and found his subjects gave off a small involuntary head-jerk at the answer. Von Osten never accepted any of it. And there's one loose end I want you to press on: Pfungst never showed that he could produce the cue deliberately and thereby get Hans to answer wrongly, so the sufficiency half of the account -- this cue, produced at will, is enough -- was never nailed down.

The two ideas I want to run it through. Call the first ontology identification: given a system's behaviour, work out what it is actually computing over -- which variables, which internal states, which causal structure -- as opposed to what it is convenient or flattering to describe it as doing. Call the second inaccessible information: the facts that would settle which ontology is right are, from the observer's vantage point, not available -- they sit inside the system, or behind a channel you can't run backwards, or they're spread around in a way that no participant can report on.

What I want:

1) Map the episode onto the identification problem. At the outset, what were the competing ontologies on the table, and what was the actual hypothesis space? My suspicion is that the sharpest point here is the 1904 commission working with a dichotomy -- genuine arithmetic competence versus deliberate fraud -- when the true answer was neither, so no amount of further looking could have identified it. Tell me if that's right, and what it implies about the identification problem generally.

2) Then the part I actually care about: what did the identifying work -- more observation, or interventions? Blinders, changing who asks, changing whether the asker knows the answer, isolating horse and questioner from the crowd. Say what each manipulation rules out and which ontologies it leaves standing, and then say whether the identification is ever unique or only unique relative to a hypothesis space you brought with you. If it's the latter, is ontology identification a well-posed problem at all, or is it really a claim about which interventions are available?

3) The inaccessible-information side. Here the information was in the room the whole time and in nobody's belief box -- the carrier was von Osten's own posture, which he had no access to. Say what that does to the standard picture on which the observer's problem is just a shortage of data. Then the regress: identifying Hans's ontology required making von Osten's micro-behaviour observable, which required knowing to look for it, which required already having most of the answer. Is that general, or peculiar to this case?

4) Where the case tests the theory. Press on Pfungst's unfinished half: he found a cue that was necessary, but never demonstrated sufficiency by producing it at will. Was the ontology identified anyway? What does your answer say about what the standard for identification should be -- is necessary-condition-plus-plausible-mechanism enough, and does that standard let too much through? And does von Osten's refusal to be persuaded tell us anything about identification, or is that just a fact about people?

5) One short bridge, keep it short: same problem, current version. Behavioural benchmarks as the questioner, model as Hans. What's the analogue of the blinders manipulation, and of the knows-the-answer manipulation, when the thing being tested can't be blinded to the fact that a human wrote the question? And is the lesson of Hans that you should be deflationary about benchmark-passing, or that the deflationary move is itself underdetermined?

Be concrete about the mapping -- who plays what role, what counts as evidence, what would count as having identified the ontology -- and where the theory has nothing to say, say so rather than forcing it to speak.

Last line: a line starting Verdict: with one sentence on whether Clever Hans is a case that supports the intervention-based picture of ontology identification or one that breaks it.
