I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about a decade, so my working vocabulary is Bayesian and statistical: priors and posteriors, calibration, proper scoring rules, consistency and convergence in probability, identifiability, and — since everything I fit is misspecified — the fact that a well-behaved estimator converges to the best approximation available inside its model class rather than to the truth itself. I want the pragmatist dispute over truth restated in that vocabulary, and I want the residue report.

As I understand it, Peirce's picture is that truth is the limit of inquiry: the opinion investigators would be fated to settle on if they carried the process far enough. James's picture is roughly that an idea is true insofar as it pays off in the course of experience, that truth is made rather than found ready-made. Translate each into my terms as faithfully as you can. What exactly is the Peircean limit, stated as a claim about estimators, model classes, and the distributions they converge to? What is the Jamesian criterion, stated as a claim about loss functions, decisions, and expected utility? Be careful not to quietly rewrite one of them in the other's terms and call it translation: if a step requires a fixed loss function, a decision problem, a well-specified model class, or a common prior that the original view does not supply, say so and mark it.

Then the residue. First, which parts become actual results — does the convergence-of-inquiry picture correspond to a consistency or posterior-merging theorem, and what do the hypotheses of that theorem presuppose that Peirce's story leaves open? Relatedly, is there anything in my vocabulary that delivers convergence of opinion about unobservable structure, or only convergence of predictions, and if the latter, what does that gap cost Peirce? Second, which parts I can get only by smuggling something in — a loss function that fixes what counts as paying off, a decision problem that makes a belief useful, a reference to the true distribution just to state the convergence conditions? Third, what has no counterpart at all. I am especially interested in normativity: calibration and expected score are properties a forecaster has, not obligations it is under, and I do not see how any statement about convergence or about expected utility yields an ought on belief. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it is a defect of my vocabulary — something about truth that statistics cannot express — or evidence that the original claim was doing less work than its slogans suggest. I am not after a survey of the literature or an inventory of who said what where; I want the translation itself and an honest account of what fails to come through.
