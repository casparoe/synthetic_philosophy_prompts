I do cost-benefit analysis for a state regulator -- mostly transport and environmental rules, but I keep getting pulled into the moral ones (gambling, sex work, drug laws), and last month we had a real fight about whether to count, in the monetised benefits column, the offence that residents report at the idea of a brothel opening near them. My training is standard welfare economics: externalities of both the pecuniary and non-pecuniary kind, compensating and equivalent variation, willingness to pay vs willingness to accept, Kaldor-Hicks, public goods, and the usual move where you launder meddlesome or antisocial preferences out of the calculus rather than count them. I've been reading about the old argument between Hart and Devlin over whether the criminal law may enforce morality as such, and the more I read the more I think its really a fight about my machinery wearing a wig. So: restate the whole thing in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what didn't survive the translation. No reading list or citations, please -- I'm not going to chase the literature down this week.

As I have the two of them -- and tell me where I'm flattening them -- Devlin says a society is constituted partly by a shared morality, that this morality is one woven fabric where you can't pull a thread without the rest coming loose, and that the community may therefore use the criminal law against conduct its ordinary members find intolerable rather than merely distasteful (with a decent margin of tolerated private vice left over; he isn't a totalitarian about it). Hart says that's just legal moralism: immorality by itself is not a reason for coercion, you need harm to others, or to the agent within limits; Devlin's disintegration thesis is an untested empirical claim about social cohesion; and he slides from 'most people around here think X is wrong' to 'X is wrong', and from 'morality matters to the law' to 'the criminal law should enforce it'. A colleague who does game theory keeps telling me Devlin is really talking about a coordination equilibrium with multiple equilibria -- the norm's value is that everyone else follows it -- which would make the disintegration thesis a claim about the cost of switching, not about harm. Is that a translation of Devlin or a replacement of him? Same question for Hart's insistence that the law and social disapproval are two different instruments with different costs.
