Ok, so for this one I want a satirical piece for a dataset I'm building. Here's the brief.

Write a post-incident report — the kind a software company publishes after a public screwup. Give it the whole apparatus: a case reference number, a timestamped timeline, a Root Cause section, a Corrective Actions section, a sign-off from someone with a made-up title like Head of Linguistic Quality. It's from a fictional machine-translation company (invent them — don't use a real company, and no citations, no references to real papers or products). Voice is deadpan corporate-technical, in-universe, never winking at the reader, never admitting it's a joke.

What the report is quietly about: the company's product sells "ambiguity resolution" as a flagship feature, and the marketing calls that understanding. The incident was not a grammar error. It's that the source text left something open, the target language forced a choice, and the system chose silently and confidently on the author's behalf. I want the report to be technically correct about the real cases it names, so here are the ones I want in there:

- Translating into Hebrew: adjectives and present-tense verbs agree with the gender of the speaker, so a plain English "I'm tired" or "I'm writing" makes the system pick a gender for the author that the original never gave anyone. (Same story in reverse going out of a language with no grammatical gender — Turkish "o" has to come into English as he or she.)
- Translating into Turkish: the past tense has to say whether the speaker witnessed the thing or only heard/inferred it (-di versus -miş), so an English sentence that was neutral about evidence comes out as a positive claim about how the speaker knows.
- Japanese and Korean: you have to fix a politeness level, which means fixing a social relationship the original didn't specify.
- Verse is the worst case: meter, rhyme and wordplay can't come across as content, so they get dropped or "compensated" for somewhere else, which is a decision about what the poem was for.

And the report should notice, at least once, that a human translator faces the exact same forced choices — the difference is the human knows that a choice was made. That's the nerve I want hit: the system's fluency and its ignorance of its own guessing are the same mechanism.

Beats I want:

- the Root Cause section blames the evaluation data — the reference translation was one human's arbitrary resolution, the model got penalized for a different but defensible one, and the fix is to go correct the reference;
- a section called something like "What the system understood," listing things it demonstrably could not have known: who was speaking, whether they were guessing, how polite they meant to be, whether the line was a joke;
- corrective actions that all amount to making the system commit harder, plus a new confidence score, plus a new ambiguity-flagging feature — which is, of course, exactly what understanding would have been;
- and a closing assurance, straight-faced, that the product has never claimed to understand anything, sitting directly under the tagline at the top that claims it does.

Around 700 words. A linguist should laugh and also wince a bit; someone who's never thought about translation should still come away feeling that something is being covered up. Don't invoke the Chinese Room or the Turing test, and don't let the report moralize about AI — the shallowness should show up entirely in the specifics, not in the commentary. (This is basically the Hofstadter complaint, that it's all surface, so keep it at that level of concreteness.)
