The setting is the one that self-amending games such as Nomic turn into a sport: a code whose own amendment is governed by the code. An association adopts a code of three articles and nothing else. Article A: any article of this code may be repealed or amended by a resolution carried by two thirds of those voting. Article B: articles A and B may be repealed or amended only by a resolution carried unanimously. Article C: the officers of the association are elected annually by a simple majority of those voting. The code says nothing about when a resolution takes effect, and nothing about whether a resolution may replace the code wholesale rather than amend it.

Here are my verdicts on resolutions moved from that starting point. In each case the resolution is carried by the margin stated, and each is considered as though the others had not happened.

1. Carried unanimously: "Articles A and B are repealed; amendments henceforth require only a simple majority." Valid.

2. Carried by 60 per cent of those voting: "Article B is repealed," the mover arguing that the resolution takes effect the moment it is carried, at which moment B is no longer in force and so imposes nothing on the vote that carried it. Invalid.

3. Carried by two thirds: "A new article D is added: every amendment adopted after this resolution requires unanimity, and D may be amended only unanimously." Valid. Nothing prevents the association from making its rules harder to change, and when the vote was taken only A applied, since D was not yet part of the code.

4. Carried unanimously: "Articles A and B are repealed; the code shall contain no provision for its own amendment," C remaining in force. Invalid, even though everyone agreed. Unanimity is a procedure the code supplies, and a resolution that abolishes the code's procedures cannot take its authority from them.

5. Carried by 60 per cent of those voting: "This code is repealed in its entirety and replaced by the following code," the text that follows being identical to the old code except that A's threshold is lowered to a simple majority and B is omitted. Valid, I think — and this is the one I keep going back and forth on. The old code regulated amendments to itself and said nothing about being replaced, and a new code draws its authority from the association's decision to adopt one rather than from the code it supersedes. But if that is right, the sixty per cent who were refused in case 2 got their way by the expedient of re-enacting the code, and on this reasoning a single voter could have done it.

The principle I have been trying out is this: a resolution takes effect if and only if it complies with the code in force at the moment the vote is taken, and it does not leave the code without any means of its own amendment. That covers 1 through 4. It is silent about 5, which is precisely where I need help, since 2 and 5 look like one manoeuvre performed twice and I cannot see what separates them except a fresh sheet of paper: any amendment can be redrafted as a wholesale replacement, and any replacement can be redrafted as a single large amendment.

Please give me the principle, or the smallest set of candidates, that best fits these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it. I want to know in particular how it draws the line between amending a code and replacing it, whether it can save both 2 and 5 or must sacrifice one, and what it implies for two cases I have not listed: a resolution carried by the required majority whose own text declares that it is valid whatever the code says about its adoption, and a resolution adding a clause that no future resolution may lower the requirement for its own adoption. Where one of my verdicts has to go, say which and why, rather than adding clauses until the principle fits everything.
