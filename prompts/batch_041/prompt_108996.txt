A familiar argument in drug-policy debates runs that drug prohibition is alcohol Prohibition all over again: a ban on a substance a great many people were going to use anyway, which failed to suppress use, handed the market to organized crime, filled the prisons, and was abandoned once the public reckoned with the costs — after which the sky did not fall. The conclusion it is used to license is that we should repeal drug prohibition much as the United States repealed the Eighteenth Amendment, and that Portugal's move to decriminalize personal possession is simply the first stretch of the same arc. Audit the analogy. Lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the banned substance, the drinking public, the bootlegger, the enforcement apparatus, the repeal — and then say where the mapping breaks: in how far the substance was already culturally entrenched, in who its users were, in what the ban actually did to consumption, in the mechanism by which the ban was undone (a constitutional amendment reversed, versus a body of statutes and treaties eroded piecemeal), and in what legal market was waiting on the other side. Which of these disanalogies bear on the conclusion being drawn and which are mere decoration? Tell me whether the conclusion survives the audit, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole way; and if it is being asked to carry too much, say what comparison would do the work better.
