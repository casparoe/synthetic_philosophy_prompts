Hey, I'm in a philosophy class and we've been doing this exercise where you take some real decision and try to figure out what it's assuming without anyone ever saying it out loud. Heres the one I want to do. Germany shut down all of its nuclear plants, France kept its reactors and is building more, and both countries say the whole point is the climate. Meanwhile Germany turned some old coal plants back on for a while, so the choice wasn't "nuclear or nothing," it was kind of "nuclear or coal for a bit." So what does each policy actually presuppose? Not the economics, the philosophy. Does the German side assume that even a tiny chance of a giant disaster beats a guaranteed medium-sized amount of harm, while the French side just does the expected-deaths math and takes the answer? What about the waste, which stays dangerous way longer than anyone alive will be around — does that mean one side thinks we owe basically nothing to people thousands of years from now and the other thinks we owe them a lot? Also both sides call what theyre doing "clean energy," which feels like a lot to hang on one word, so what do they have to believe about what counts as pollution for that to work? Is there one consistent set of beliefs behind each country, or do these policies just happen to fit a bunch of different views so that nobody's really committed to anything? And if you did swap the assumptions out, would the policies have to look different, or would the same plants and the same shutdowns still make sense?
