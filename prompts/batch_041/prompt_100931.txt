I've been going back and forth with a couple of mates about sortition — replacing elected legislatures, or at least checking them, with randomly selected citizens' assemblies — and I've got to write up where I've landed before a workshop on democratic innovation next month. The trouble is I suspect some of my commitments don't actually fit together, and I'd like someone to tell me where the cracks are rather than find out in the Q&A. So here's the pile, as honestly as I can state it. (1) What makes a political decision legitimate is that the people under it have a standing opportunity to contest it — not that they authorised it in advance, just that they can push back and the push-back actually registers somewhere. (2) A randomly selected chamber beats an elected one mainly because a decent sample looks like the country: same mix of class, region, gender, age, so the room ends up wanting roughly what the country would want if it had the time to think it through. (3) Political equality just is an equal chance of holding power; equal influence over outcomes is a mirage and I don't think we should be chasing it. (4) When an assembly has deliberated properly and reached a verdict, that verdict should bind — no referendum, no parliamentary override, no second-guessing by the wider electorate. (5) Members should get good briefing material from people who know the subject, but nobody's vote should count for more because they happen to be an expert; the whole point is that the room reasons from lived experience. And a friend keeps pitching what he calls a 'sunset lottery': any assembly decision lapses after three years unless a fresh, independently drawn panel re-affirms it, on the grounds that a randomly selected body has no other way of being held to account at all.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict and are in fact perfectly compatible, and for the real ones, which commitment is doing the damage and what the cheapest repair would be. Rank the genuine tensions by how bad they are — I'd much rather you told me that one thing has to go than handed me a tidy list where everything is equally worrying — and say what each repair costs me, since I don't want to quietly drop something I actually care about just to make the set look neat. Also tell me whether the sunset lottery actually rescues anything or just relabels the problem. Don't tell me to go and read somebody; work with what's here.
