Two criteria for death coexist in the same hospitals. A patient whose brain has irreversibly stopped functioning can be declared dead while still warm and still on a ventilator, and their organs — the heart included — may then be removed. But a patient who is not brain-dead can also be declared dead once circulation stops after life support is withdrawn, provided a short stand-off period passes and no one restarts it; there, the reason circulation stays stopped is partly that everyone has agreed not to restart it. In some programs, once that circulatory death has been certified, the donor's organs are re-perfused with oxygenated blood before removal — circulation is restored to a body that has just been pronounced dead. And throughout, everyone insists that we may not bring about a patient's death in order to obtain their organs.

What conception of death do these practices presuppose? Is death being treated as a biological state or as a status conferred by a decision; as an event or a process; and as necessarily irreversible — if so, irreversible in the body, or only relative to what we are willing to do about it? Which rival accounts of death would license these same practices just as well, so that the practices underdetermine the theory, and which would forbid something the practices permit? Finally, if a whole-brain criterion on the one hand, or a strictly circulatory one on the other, replaced whatever these practices presuppose, what would have to change?
