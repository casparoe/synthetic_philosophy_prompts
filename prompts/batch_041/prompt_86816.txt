Last term I joined a small reading group on practical rationality, and the session before last was taken over by an argument between two other members, Nadia and Callum, about the toxin puzzle; I have been chewing it over since and still cannot decide who had the better of it. The setup, as the group put it: a benefactor will pay you a million pounds immediately, if and only if you now intend to drink a toxin tomorrow; the toxin will make you seriously ill for a day but leave no lasting harm; and you know all of this, including that once the money has actually been paid, tomorrow's drinking will achieve nothing, since the payment turns only on the intention you form now and not on whether you go through with the drinking. What follows is the exchange as well as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Nadia said that you simply cannot intend to drink it: you can foresee with certainty that once the money is in your account you will have no reason whatever to drink, and an intention that you know in advance you will drop is not an intention at all. On her view the puzzle's real lesson is that intending is not something practical reason can produce on demand, so that here the rational thing to want — the million — is not reachable by reasoning your way to it. (2) Callum replied that this confuses intending with predicting one's own conduct: people intend to be up at six knowing perfectly well that they often are not, and they do not stop intending merely because they predict that they will fail. (3) Nadia said the cases are not parallel, because those involve uncertainty about how one will behave, whereas here you are certain, and to be certain that you will not do a thing is incompatible with genuinely meaning to do it. (4) Callum then changed tack and said that what is really on offer is a reward for a standing policy rather than for a one-off intention: adopt the rule 'when I have taken a fee to drink the toxin, I drink it', and under that rule the drinking is not pointless at all, since it is what keeps the rule in working order. (5) Nadia objected that the rule has already earned everything it was ever going to earn the moment the money is banked, so the only reason left to obey it is the rule itself, and you cannot conjure a reason out of a rule whose pay-off has already been collected. (6) Callum said that if that is the sticking point, he would simply arrange matters beforehand — sign something, announce it, rig a device that administers the toxin if he fails to drink it himself — and then he really would drink, so his intention would be perfectly genuine. (7) Nadia granted that he would then get the money, but said that he had managed it by rearranging the world rather than by intending for the sake of the reward, and that this is exactly what makes the belief version of the puzzle — in which the benefactor rewards your belief that you will drink rather than your intention — so much easier than the intention version, since there the rigging is precisely what makes the belief true. (8) Callum's closing move was that the stipulation which strips away all such devices is doing all the work: the puzzle forbids the agent to use tools that real agents have, and manufactures the certainty appealed to in (3) by fiat, so it shows only that a badly posed puzzle has no solution.

I would like you to adjudicate this properly. Take the eight moves in order and say, for each, whether it is sound, unsound, or somewhere in between, with a short justification for the rating — not merely who won overall. Then tell me what both of them are missing about what the puzzle does and does not show, and whether the contrast Nadia draws between the intention version and the belief version is a real difference or a distraction. Finally, say what your verdict implies for a variant that neither of them raised: everything as in the original offer, except that the million is to be paid to a stranger whom you will never meet and towards whom you have no feelings either way.

Please answer in British English, and finish with a single last line, with nothing after it, in exactly this form: Verdict: <Nadia | Callum | Neither> — <no more than twenty words naming the point on which the whole thing turns>.
