I am a software engineer at a mid-sized cloud company, and I have no formal training in philosophy. Our platform has a carbon-aware scheduler that delays batch jobs to hours when the local grid is forecast to be cleaner; we buy renewable energy certificates to match the electricity we do consume; and we purchase carbon offsets for the remainder. The customer-facing dashboard then describes our cloud operations as carbon neutral. The same company runs a get-out-the-vote campaign for employees and labels the carbon footprint of the meat options in the cafeteria, while still serving meat at every meal. I keep feeling that these practices are making incompatible assumptions about what it means for one company’s or one person’s action to contribute to a collective harm, but I cannot articulate the assumptions.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of individual causal contribution and moral responsibility that the carbon-aware scheduling plus certificate and offset purchasing presupposes. I am interested in the philosophical commitments rather than an evaluation of whether the practice works. Specifically: what must the practice assume about the causal structure of climate harm for a certificate or offset purchase to cancel the harm of the electricity actually consumed? Does it require a divisible, budget-like conception of harm that can be balanced, or can it be made sense of on a marginal-emissions view or a threshold view? One engineer on my team argues that shifting a job to a cleaner hour is a real reduction because it changes which generator is marginal, while buying a certificate does nothing unless it causes new renewable capacity to be built; another argues that both are just accounting conventions. Which rival accounts of responsibility in collective harms does the practice quietly rule out—for instance, an account on which the wrong is being complicit in a harmful practice, an account on which the wrong is following a rule that only works if enough others follow it, or the skeptical account on which no individual act makes a difference—and which of those could it be reconciled with? Are the company’s practices—scheduling, certificates and offsets, the voting campaign, and the meat labeling—presupposing consistent things, or do they pull in different directions? Finally, if the presupposed view were replaced by a threshold or complicity view, what would have to change in the practice itself?

Please state how confident you are in each part of your reconstruction and identify the point about which you are least confident.
