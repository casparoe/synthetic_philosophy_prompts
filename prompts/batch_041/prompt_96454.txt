I am a doctoral candidate in moral psychology, and I have been trying to make precise a distinction that my field often invokes but rarely defines: the distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias. The distinction matters because we treat biases as obstacles to be corrected and preferences as ideals to be tolerated, and I cannot tell which attitude is warranted in the borderline cases. My supervisor says the difference is that a bias rationalizes or stabilizes an unjust social hierarchy, while a preference merely expresses a contestable ideal of how to live. I am not sure that survives the cases. Here are my verdicts. A person who prefers monogamy and thinks polyamory is a lesser form of life: preference. A person who feels disgust at interracial marriage and thinks it wrong: bias. A person raised in a community that regards meat-eating as defiling, who now judges it immoral though they can give no independent argument: I want to say preference, but it has the same visceral, unargued texture as the disgust case. A wealthy heir who believes the poor are lazy and that welfare should be cut, where the belief conveniently protects his inheritance: bias. A poor person who holds the same anti-welfare belief, not because it benefits him but because it is what everyone around him says: I want to call it a bias too, but my supervisor's principle makes it a preference, and that feels wrong. A person who thinks it is morally better to have children than to be childfree and feels a pang of contempt at the childfree: preference, though an unlovely one. A person who thinks women should not serve in combat, not because he dislikes women but because he was raised in a military family and has a visceral sense of propriety: bias. A person who thinks it is wrong to eat octopus because she has a strong intuition that octopuses are persons, though she cannot defend it: I lean preference, but weakly.

My tentative principle is the one my supervisor offers: a moral judgment is a bias when it functions to rationalize or stabilize an unjust hierarchy, and a preference when it merely expresses a contestable ideal of how to live. But that principle makes the octopus case a preference, the interracial marriage case a bias, and the childfree-contempt case a preference, while the combat case is a bias only because of hierarchy. It also makes the poor person's anti-welfare judgment a preference and the rich heir's a bias, even though the two judgments have the same content. And it seems to leave no room for a judgment that is visceral, unargued, and demeaning without being hierarchical. I would like a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that does justice to these verdicts, says which of them I have to abandon, and tells me what to say about a case I have not listed: a person who has a visceral, unargued aversion to a novel technology for enhancing embryos, who thinks using it is morally degrading, but who is not defending any social hierarchy and would not want others punished for using it.
