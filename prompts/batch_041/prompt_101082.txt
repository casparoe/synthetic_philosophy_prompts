I'm currently taking a seminar on collective cognition and the philosophy of mind, and I need to choose what to write my final paper on — and possibly what to keep working on after the course ends. I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind who specializes in collective and distributed cognition, and to reason carefully and systematically rather than handing me a neutral survey. Please start by brainstorming the full space of open questions before you rank anything, and show me your reasoning as you go.

The question the seminar keeps circling is whether a collective — our paradigm case being an ant colony whose foraging, nest-building, and navigation look intelligent even though no individual ant understands what the colony is doing — can literally have a mind, or whether talk of the colony believing, thinking, or deciding is at best a useful shorthand. Our instructor kept pressing a deliberately awkward variant in order to break the usual intuitions: imagine a colony that behaves normally in every observable respect, but that, when you sample different subsets of its ants, yields mutually inconsistent "reports" about the same food source, so that the colony appears to hold two contradictory beliefs at once depending on where you look. Roughly half the class took this to show there is no single subject of belief at all; the other half took it to show merely that colony belief is distributed and has no privileged introspective vantage point, which is precisely what a mind without a central seat of awareness should look like. Nobody could agree on what would even count as settling the disagreement.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a summary of the field. Suppose I have roughly one semester of focused effort and a background in philosophy rather than in entomology or dynamical-systems modeling. Which open questions in this area should I prioritize, and in what order? For each one, tell me why it matters: whether it is upstream of the others (so that other questions depend on its answer), how tractable it is for someone with my background and timeframe, how neglected it is, and what specific argument or piece of evidence over the next year or so would move it up or down my list. Where your criteria pull in different directions — say, a question that is important but not tractable, or tractable but downstream — say so explicitly and tell me how you would trade them off. I especially want to know which questions I should deprioritize because their answers would not actually change much elsewhere, and I want the reasoning behind the ordering rather than just the list.

Two constraints. I am working offline, so do not give me citations, a reading list, or pointers to particular papers or authors — argue from the concepts themselves, and if you need to name a position, describe it rather than attributing it. And do not lean on any specific facts you would have to look up; if something is uncertain or contested, say so and reason about it rather than asserting it. Where the existing vocabulary of the debate is genuinely helpful, use it, but make clear which distinctions are doing real work and which are just labels.
