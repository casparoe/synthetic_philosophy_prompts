Ok so I've been arguing with a friend about this for weeks and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind my gut here or if I'm just making it up as I go.

Topic: when are you allowed to use something to learn about its own reliability. Here's where my gut lands.

Thermometer. I look at it, it says 70, I believe it's 70 in the room. Fine, that's knowledge. Then I keep going: "it says 70 and it IS 70, so it's reading correctly, so it isn't broken." That second part feels like nothing to me. Not knowledge, not even a decent reason to think the thermometer is ok. If someone asked why I trust it I'd never answer like that.

Scale and weight. I have a 1kg weight, I put it on my kitchen scale, the scale says 1kg, and I conclude the weight really is 1kg. That seems fine. But flip it around: I use the 1kg weight to check that the scale is accurate, and that also seems fine. Now I'm worried, because one of those two has to be the thermometer move in a hat.

Memory. I remember that my memory has mostly served me well, so my memory is reliable. Bad. Circular in a way the scale case somehow isn't.

Perception. I open my eyes, seem to see my hands, conclude I have hands. Completely fine. "I seem to see my hands, so I'm not hallucinating" — bad. "I seem to see my hands, so my visual system is working properly" — also bad, I think, though I can feel myself wanting to say it's ok.

The color one that breaks me. I'm worried my color vision is off. So I look at a bunch of swatches, name the colors, and check my answers against what's printed on the back of the cards. All correct. Then I conclude: ok, my color vision is fine. I want to say that's good reasoning — I checked! But it's the thermometer move again isn't it. Same with: I look at a red square and conclude "my eyes can tell red from green." If the swatch case is fine and the thermometer case isn't, what's actually doing the work? Is it just more trials? That can't be it, more trials of a bad inference is still bad. Is it that the card backs are a second source I'm checking against, and in the thermometer case the "it is 70" is just the thermometer over again? Maybe. But then why is the scale/weight case fine in both directions?

Another. My friend says the library closes at 6, I go and it's closed at 6, so I conclude they're reliable about the library and I keep trusting them about opening hours. Fine, obviously. But now: my friend tells me a bunch of stuff about history, it all pans out when I look it up, so I conclude they're reliable about history, so I trust the next historical claim without checking. That seems fine too. And it also looks like the thermometer if I squint — I used their reports to certify them.

And: I know I'm not a brain in a vat, because I can see my hands and vats don't have hands in them. Bad, pretty sure. But I know I'm not hallucinating right now, because the room looks totally normal and a hallucinated room would look... yeah no, that one's bad too. Fine, those two agree at least.

What I want: the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that gets all of these, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases myself. My own rough guess is "you can't use a source to certify itself," but that seems to kill the scale/weight case and maybe the friend case, so it's wrong or at least too crude. Tell me which clause is doing the work in each case, and which of my verdicts you'd have to give up to get a clean principle. Also: what does your principle say about two broken clocks that happen to read the same time, and about someone who checks their vision right after LASIK by reading the eye chart, then trusts the eye chart for other things? I have genuinely no idea on those two and they seem like a good test.

(I know alot of this is stuff people call bootstrapping, but I don't care about the labels, I care what the rule is. I keep going back and fourth on the swatch case in particular.)
