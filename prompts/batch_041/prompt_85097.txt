For the past four years I have been one of seven members of a national advisory panel on accountability for autonomous systems. We take referrals from transport, health, finance and defence regulators, and we issue findings that assign responsibility for harms involving autonomous or learned systems; the findings are formally non-binding, but in practice regulators and courts treat them as persuasive, so the wording matters. The trouble is that we have never written down a doctrine. Our record, as I would summarise it from memory, runs roughly as follows. (1) A warehouse injury: autonomous forklifts, an emergency-stop button placed where the designated supervisor could not see the aisle he was nominally supervising, a contractor hurt. We assigned responsibility to the facility operator, not the supervisor, and described his role in the report as supervision on paper only. (2) A surgical injury: a semi-autonomous suturing mode whose documentation understated its failure rate, with a resident in the room who could have taken over. We assigned responsibility to the device manufacturer, holding the resident's reliance reasonable on the information available to him. (3) A defence case: a loitering system with a human on the trigger and a veto window of several seconds. We assigned responsibility to the operator and declined to allocate any share to the developer, even though the developer had written the classification criteria the operator was authorising against. (4) A market episode: an execution algorithm operating inside its approved limits contributed to a disorderly few minutes; there was no window in which any person could have intervened and the firm had no kill switch. We named no individual at all and assigned responsibility to the firm as an institution, on the reasoning that the decision had been dissolved into the pipeline. (5) A content-moderation case: a classifier removed a large set of accounts, the human reviewers had no discretion above a threshold, and users suffered real harm. We assigned responsibility to the platform and rejected its argument that it was merely operating a model.

Now the case that is actually before us, which I will describe as it came to us. A ground-based autonomous weapon system was deployed in a densely populated area by a commander who chose the operational setting and the rules of engagement. One operator was monitoring three such systems simultaneously and had a veto window of about two seconds on each. The developer had disclosed, in the deployment documentation, a known limitation of the training data in exactly this kind of setting. The harm arose from an engagement strategy that the system had evidently developed in the field and that appeared in no test, no simulation and no design document; no one at the time knew it existed, and the operator could not in practice have recognised what he was looking at within his window. Two of my colleagues read our record as having been, all along, a last-clear-chance panel; another thinks we have really been asking who was in the best position to know; a fourth thinks we have been quietly declining to name individuals whenever the workflow made individual attribution artificial, and dressing that up as findings about institutions.

I would like a memo I can circulate before the session. Tell me what finding our record makes most likely here, and what finding would best cohere with that record if we want to be consistent with ourselves; set out the candidate rules our earlier findings are jointly consistent with and what each of them predicts about this case; say which precedent is the closest analog and why, including whether the manning level makes this operator's veto genuine in the way case (3) was genuine or nominal in the way case (1) was; and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the matter, state plainly what principle we would be adopting with each of the live options and what that principle would commit us to in future cases. Finally, name the further case — ideally one that could plausibly be referred to us — that would best tell the surviving rules apart, and say what each rule predicts about it.
