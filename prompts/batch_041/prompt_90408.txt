I am putting together a short after-dinner set for a philosophy department fundraiser, and the running gag is that every philosopher has a monetary theory that would bankrupt the bar. I have been assigned Bishop Berkeley, which is harder than it sounds: everyone reaches for the immaterialist line and the joke dies, because the actual interest in Berkeley on money is not merely that he denied matter. In The Querist and his other Irish economic writings, he treats money as a ticket or counter rather than real wealth, locates wealth in the industry and useful labour that can command goods, and asks whether a country with sufficient people and production really needs so much gold and silver, or whether paper credit and a national bank might serve better. I want a joke that is funny to people who know those views, not just to people who know the slogan about being perceived.

Write the joke as a compact scene with two or three characters, one of whom is Berkeley or a Berkeleian, and make the punchline turn on one of his actual monetary claims rather than on generic idealism or on the idea that money is socially constructed. The joke should make sense to someone who has read a little Berkeley but should not require knowing any particular query number or wording. Do not use citations, and do not put words into Berkeley's mouth as if they were verbatim quotations. Give me the result as exactly five numbered beats, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first sets the scene, the second introduces the monetary puzzle, the third lets a character apply Berkeley's view, the fourth turns it against him or reveals the catch, and the fifth lands the punchline.
