Ok so I've got two friends who've been arguing about this for like two years and I want them to stop and write the paper together instead. One of them says genuine moral dilemmas are possible: cases where you really are required to do A, really are required to do B, you can't do both, and nothing about the situation or about morality itself takes either requirement away. The other one says thats impossible — every apparent dilemma is either a case where one side actually wins and we just haven't spotted the winning consideration yet, or a case where neither side is required and the only requirement is the disjunctive one ("do one of them"), or proof that the persons moral code is internally inconsistent and needs repairing. Write the joint statement the two of them could both sign at the end of a real good-faith collaboration.

What I want in it, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both of them sign. It should cover: what "genuine" is even supposed to mean here; what a case would have to look like to count as one; what they agree about the agglomeration move (if you're required to do A and required to do B, are you thereby required to do A-and-B?); what they agree about "ought implies can"; and what they agree does and doesn't count as evidence for the possibility of dilemmas (feeling torn, guilt afterwards, the need to apologise, the fact that some cases stay unresolved no matter how long you think). Nothing vacuous, and nothing that quietly assumes one side's answer.

2. The disagreements, each written out twice: once in wording the "yes" side would accept for its own view, once in wording the "no" side would accept for its own. Where part of the dispute turns out to be about words — e.g. whether "ought" means all-things-considered or just pro-tanto, or whether a dilemma is a case with no permissible option versus a case with two permissible options that each involve real wrongdoing — say so explicitly, and say what survives the translation.

3. A table of cruxes: one row per sub-question that, if settled, would actually move one of them. My guesses, correct me if I'm off: (a) are requirements all-or-nothing, or do they come with strengths, such that two requirements can be "unranked" — neither stronger, nor weaker, nor exactly equal to the other? (b) is "ought implies can" a conceptual truth about the word "ought", or a substantive moral claim we could give up? (c) if you're required to do A and required to do B and you can only do one, is the requirement on the one you skip cancelled, overridden, or still standing? (d) is the feeling of being torn and the guilt afterwards evidence about morality, or just psychology?

4. For every row of that table: a case or test both of them agree would bear on it, plus each side's prediction, written down in advance. Please don't use the warhorses everyone uses in intro classes — I've made up a few for them to use instead, and they should use mine if mine work:

- The split shift: your mother will be alone and unsafe tonight if you don't stay with her, and your kid will be alone and unsafe tonight if you don't stay with them; you're the only option in both cases, nobody promised anything, no prior agreement ranks the two, and both will be completely fine afterwards if you show up. The "no" side should say whether "stay with one of them" is a complete answer to what you're required to do; the "yes" side should say exactly what each requirement is a requirement to do.

- The repeat: same two people, but it's a standing situation that'll come up every Friday for a year, and you've already gone to your kid twice in a row. Does the history change the requirements themselves, or only what you have reason to do?

- The by-your-own-hand: you can in fact do both things, but only by leaving one of them waiting in a way you'd have to lie about afterwards. Is that still a dilemma, or has it turned into a case about what you're allowed to do rather than what you can?

Then say whether they actually agree on what the result of each case would be, and where they don't, whose prediction it confirms.

5. A closing paragraph: if they still disagree, is the disagreement about morality or about how to use a word? And what would each of them need to see to give up their view?

Keep the whole thing tight — I want it short enough that both of them would actually read it, not a survey article. And no citations, obviously, they're not writing for a journal.
