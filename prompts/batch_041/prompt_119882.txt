I am a student in an epistemology course, and we have been discussing the Meno problem -- why knowledge is more valuable than true belief. I have noticed a puzzling pattern in my university's assessment practices. In my mathematics courses, a student who writes the correct answer with no work receives no credit, while a student who uses a correct method but makes a minor arithmetic error receives most of the credit. In my multiple-choice courses, however, a student who guesses correctly receives the same credit as a student who knows the answer. The academic integrity policy also punishes a student who obtains a correct answer by unauthorized collaboration, even though the answer is correct, while it does not punish a student who independently reaches an incorrect answer. What philosophical commitments about the value of knowledge over true belief does this pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose that knowledge is intrinsically more valuable than true belief, or only instrumentally valuable because it is more stable, reliable, or transferable? If truth is the only fundamental epistemic value, and justification matters only as a means to truth, can the pattern be justified, or does it require the further claim that justification has value beyond its truth-conduciveness? Which rival accounts of the Meno problem does it rule out, and is the pattern consistent across the three practices? Finally, if the university replaced the presupposed view with a straightforwardly truth-tracking model -- one that rewards correct answers regardless of how they were obtained -- what would have to change in these practices? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
