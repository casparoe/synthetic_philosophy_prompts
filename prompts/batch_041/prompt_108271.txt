I've got two people in my reading group who've been going at each other for months about 1914 and I'm tired of refereeing. One of them is basically a Fischerite: she thinks the German leadership wanted a war of expansion, used the July crisis to get it, and that the primary responsibility for the war is therefore Germany's. The other thinks "blame Germany" is close to a category mistake -- the war came out of a multilateral crisis in which everyone's statesmen misread everyone else, and picking out one nation is either arbitrary or is just the Versailles settlement talking. Write the joint statement they could both sign. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who's right, not a "why is this debate stuck" essay. I want the document an adversarial collaboration actually produces.

Here's what I want in it.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. What do both accept about the run-up -- Sarajevo, the blank cheque to Vienna, the ultimatum to Serbia, the Russian mobilization, the German move west through Belgium -- and about what a causal explanation of the outbreak would have to do, and about the standard of proof each is using? Include the boring ones. Boring agreed claims are the whole point of this exercise, and if a sentence can't be written so the other side wouldn't flinch at it, it belongs in the disagreements section, not here.

2. Before they disagree, make them fix what "responsibility" is even doing. I want the document to seperate at least four things: causal contribution; culpability, which needs intent or at least a plan; recklessness or negligence, i.e. running a risk you could foresee; and collective or national responsibility, which drags in the question of whether a nation can be an agent at all. And then split responsibility for the war breaking out from responsibility for the war it turned into -- Belgium, the annexation aims, the September Program, the way it was actually fought. Each side should say which of these senses its own claim lives in. My hunch is a chunk of the fight evaporates once they do, and I want the document to say so if that's what happens.

3. Then the disagreements. For each one, one sentence per side, written in wording that side would actually say out loud. No strawmen, no "some historians argue."

4. Cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling it would move at least one side. I think these are in there: whether the question is about one nation at all, or about a distribution of shares (if shares sum to one, everyone's share gets smaller; if responsibility is collective, each can be fully responsible -- make them say which they mean, because that one looks verbal to me); whether the relevant agent is the state, the government, the general staff, or particular men with names; and whether the accusers' own empires and their own decisions in 1914 bear on the truth of the accusation or only on the sincerity of the accusers.

5. For each crux, a case or piece of evidence both agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction about what it shows, written down in advance. I specifically want one crux run through a counterfactual-invariance test: hold the structure fixed -- same alliance system, same mobilization timetables, same Serbian crisis -- and vary the personnel and the domestic politics. Does 1914 still happen? Each side says what it predicts and what would falsify it. And one crux on the document trail: the blank cheque and the September Program. What would each side count as the decisive thing those documents do and don't show, and what would each side accept as evidence that its own reading is wrong?

6. Article 231. Both should be able to agree on what it actually is and does -- it sits in the reparations section, it's a hook for damages, the word guilt isn't in it, and it got read as a moral verdict anyway -- and then disagree about whether that makes the historical question and the political question separable or not.

7. Close with a short paragraph on which parts of the dispute they now think are verbal and which are real, and what both would name as the biggest open question.

Practical stuff: no citations, no reading lists, no bibliography, I'll put those in myself. 1500 to 2500 words. And it has to be signable by both of them -- so no hedging, no "many scholars hold," no alot of throat-clearing. If they can't both sign it, you've written it wrong.
