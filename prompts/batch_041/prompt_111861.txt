I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty years running a laboratory that grew thin films, then a decade on detector instrumentation — and I have spent the years since retirement reading moral philosophy as an amateur. The literature on responsibility gaps in autonomous systems interests me because it describes a predicament I recognize from my working life and never found paradoxical: as head of a laboratory I was answerable for what my group did at the bench even when I had no idea, that week, what any of them was doing, and the regulators who held me to that standard never asked whether I could have foreseen the particular mishap. So I set out to write the argument for a genuine gap in a form I could not wriggle out of, and I have landed somewhere I do not believe.

(1) An agent is morally responsible for a harm only if the knowledge condition is satisfied: at the time of acting she knew, or could have known, that her action would bring that harm about. (2) An operator who deploys a learning system and allows it to revise its own policy does not know, and cannot know, what it will do on the occasion that matters; what it does is the product of its training and its environment rather than of her deliberation. (3) So she is not responsible for the harm it causes. (4) The designer is in no better position, since the dispositions the system acquires in training are not dispositions anyone designed or foresaw. (5) The system itself is not a candidate for blame, being unable to act for reasons. (6) Hence when such a system causes harm, no one is responsible for it. That much is familiar. But now (7): each of (1) through (4) applies, with the roles reassigned, to a general who orders an operation whose particulars he cannot foresee, to a plant manager whose process chemistry carries a small probability of runaway, and to a physician who prescribes a drug that produces an idiosyncratic reaction in one patient in fifty thousand. Therefore (8) no one is ever responsible for any harm brought about through a process more complicated than the motion of his own limbs, and (9) responsibility is a fiction, so the honest response to the gap is to stop pretending otherwise.

I can accept (6). I cannot accept (9), and (8) reads to me as a reductio of something, though I cannot say of what. I would like to know which step to reject, why it looked so plausible when written in that order, and what rejecting it costs. I have heard the usual repairs — that responsibility admits of degrees, that it attaches to the design of the process rather than to the outcome, that it is distributed across an institution, or that we should stop talking about blame and talk instead about who is best placed to prevent the next one — and each of them strikes me as less a repair than a change of subject, though I may be missing the point. I would rather keep (6) than (9), and if both cannot survive, I would rather keep (1) than (2); tell me what that preference costs me. Two further matters, since I want reasoning rather than a reading list. First, is the opacity of a learned policy a difference in kind from ordinary causal complexity, or only a difference in degree — that is, does the gap argument survive if I say that the general's fog and the network's are the same fog? Second, if the regulator who held me answerable for my postdocs was relying on forward-looking considerations rather than on my having foreseen anything, was that practice defensible, or had it simply replaced responsibility with something else wearing its name?
