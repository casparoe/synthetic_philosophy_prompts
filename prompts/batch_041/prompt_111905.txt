I have no training in philosophy and would rather not pretend otherwise. I am a volunteer on my town's board of appeals, and I got interested in borderline cases the way most people do — by having to vote on them. Since then I have read around enough to know that two serious positions exist about the cases we see every month. On the first, a word like "tall," or "excessive," or "obstructs sightlines" does pick out a precise class of things: every building either falls in it or does not, and the boundary is simply unknowable to us, permanently, no matter how carefully we measure, because the obstacle is not our instruments. On the second, there is no boundary to be ignorant of. Whether a building is tall depends on where you draw the line; no line is the right one; and yet some things still come out true — true on every line you could draw — so "either it is over the limit or it is not" can be true even though neither of its two halves is, and a sentence can be true without either of its parts being true. I do not know what either view is called, and I would rather the piece not tell me.

Write me a satire that dramatizes this disagreement without ever naming it. The form: the public FAQ page and the front-counter script of a fictional municipal office whose only business is adjudicating a vague bylaw. I suggest a levy on structures that "exceed a reasonable height," but any vague threshold will do provided you keep it concrete and boring. The voice should be deadpan bureaucratic. No winking, no mugging, no absurdity for its own sake, and no real people, statutes, or court cases. The humor has to come out of the structure of the problem itself, and the piece should be recognizably fair to both positions, so that someone who has thought hard about vagueness laughs in recognition rather than winces.

Work in the following, each presented as ordinary procedure rather than as a puzzle. A threshold that nobody can locate and that the office insists exists. The office replying "yes or no" to the question of whether a building is over the limit, while declining to say which. The increments, run so many times that a structure forty metres taller than an unchallenged one is still, by the office's own reasoning, not tall. A standing rule that a building cannot be shorter than a building that is already tall and that it exceeds — cited constantly, and making the levy effectively unavoidable. And at least two grades of "clearly," so that the office keeps a form for deciding whether a case is clearly borderline and is now drafting a form for the form. The office's two standing replies should be given with equal confidence and never in the same conversation.

Under 700 words. Open with the one-sentence public notice posted on the office door, and close with a single line of the counter script that staff are instructed never to vary, whatever the customer says.
