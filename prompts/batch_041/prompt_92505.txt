ok so i'm in an undergrad philosophy of history seminar and the whole thing keeps circling back to whether the big historical breaks were inevitable or contingent, and my professor has this tic where every time someone says an outcome was structurally determined he recites the horseshoe nail rhyme at them — "for want of a nail the shoe was lost. For want of a shoe the horse was lost. For want of a horse the rider was lost. For want of a rider the battle was lost. For want of a battle the kingdom was lost. And all for the want of a horse-shoe nail." Last week he used it like this: if the nail had been in the shoe, the rider gets through, so the battle isn't lost, so the kingdom doesn't fall — therefore the fall wasn't inevitable, therefore (his word) structural explanations of history are "overrated". I'm pretty sure there's a bad step in there but I can't find it and office hours is Thursday.

What I want is the menu: the different precise claims that little rhyme could be making, ordered from the totally trivial to the genuinely radical, and for each one whether it's actually true and whether it would actually do the work he wants it to do (i.e. knock down the claim that the outcome was inevitable). Then tell me which reading he probably means when he says it, and which reading is the one where he's right.

Two things bug me and they might be the same thing. First, one of the readings has to be the chaos-y one, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the butterfly thing — but isn't that still deterministic? The end state is fully fixed by the start state, it's just that tiny differences at the start blow up. So if the nail is missing, the kingdom falls, full stop, no other option available — how is that supposed to support "it wasn't inevitable"? Is he sliding from unpredictable to undetermined, or am I the one conflating something? Second, someone in class said "well then every nail in history is a cause of everything after it" and he kind of waved it off, but I thought that was a real objection: if the outcome is that sensitive to every detail then there's nothing special about *this* nail, and the claim that small causes matter is true but useless. Unless the interesting claim is that only some small differences are load-bearing, and then we need some story about which ones, and I don't know if the rhyme gives us one.

Also: is there a reading where the rhyme is actually an arguement for inevitability instead of against it?
