I spent most of my working life designing experiments and arguing about whether a result was an artifact of the apparatus or a feature of the world, and only in retirement have I begun to read philosophy seriously. The question that has me by the throat is whether there is a real difference between a statement that is true because of what its words mean and a statement that is true because of how things happen to be — and, if there is, what a theory of that difference would have to accomplish to be worth taking seriously. Before I read any more of the literature, I would like you to specify the desiderata: the full list of things a theory of the analytic–synthetic distinction must get right, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with each marked as either non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp.

I care about this because I want an account that illuminates how the definitional parts of a scientific theory stand to its empirical parts — why some principles seem to function as constitutive rules while others function as claims that experiment could overturn, and how one can tell which is which when a term is redefined in the course of theory change. So the list should include, at a minimum: the agreed cases any theory must classify correctly; the disputed cases it must at least handle with a principled verdict (the arithmetic identity, the entailment from vixen to female, the zoological generality that cats are animals, an identity statement of chemistry, the incompatibility of a surface's being wholly red and wholly green at once, the bare tautologies of logic); the distinctions it must not collapse (analytic versus necessary, analytic versus a priori, analytic versus obvious, analytic versus definitional by convention); a non-circular statement of what makes a truth analytic, given how hard it has proved to unpack the phrase true by virtue of meaning; a verdict on whether the distinction is binary or graded; an account of how analytic truths are known and whether that account can be naturalized; the results it must not contradict — above all Quine's holism, on which our beliefs face experience as a corporate body and even the laws of logic are in principle revisable, together with the awkward fact that in the history of science revising a definition and revising a belief are often hard to tell apart; and finally whatever is needed to make the distinction do real work rather than merely relabel the cases.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories, and — the part I most want — whether any pair of requirements on the list is jointly unsatisfiable, since I suspect at least one pair is and that the tension is the whole story. If some entry is a prejudice of a school rather than a genuine demand, say which and why. Close with a brief assessment of how the logical-positivist programme and Quine's position in Two Dogmas of Empiricism fare against the finished list.
