Write a short story, roughly 1,800–2,500 words, that stages the paradox of phenomenal judgment from the inside. The setting: a near-future research institute where a technique called judgment tracing can reconstruct, from a subject's neural activity, the proximal causes of her verbal reports about her own experience. The institute's founding hypothesis is a thoroughgoing epiphenomenalism: conscious qualities are real but causally inert with respect to the physical processes that produce reports, decisions, and memories. A senior researcher, who accepts the hypothesis, is asked to certify that the institute's reports are reliable evidence about experience. A junior colleague argues that if the hypothesis is true, the reports are at best correlated with experience and at worst systematically disconnected from it, so the institute's entire evidential practice presupposes something closer to interactionism or to a deflationary view on which there is nothing over and above the judgments. The story should turn on a concrete audit, replication failure, or certification hearing, not on a seminar discussion, and it should let the characters articulate the strongest version of each position, including the point that epiphenomenalism seems to make first-person evidence about qualia either mysterious or superfluous. Do not resolve the paradox with a clever twist that makes it disappear; let the dramatic pressure come from the fact that the institute must act on one answer while the metaphysics remains unsettled.

Do not use citations, reading lists, or references to real papers; you may use standard terms like epiphenomenalism, interactionism, causal exclusion, and qualia, but do not attribute them to named authors. After the story, add a short author's note of 300–500 words that separates what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and does not present a contested view as the consensus. In that note, identify where the story takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading—for example, about whether epiphenomenalism can accommodate reliable introspective reports at all—and say which parts of the philosophical terrain the story deliberately leaves open.
