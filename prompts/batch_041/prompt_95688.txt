A friend of mine treats Hume's chapter on miracles as a working rule for sorting miracle reports, and I have been logging her verdicts. She rejects the report of a single illiterate shepherd who says his sheep spoke to him; she rejects the resurrection claim of a rival religious tradition, even though several of its devout adherents attest it; she rejects the account of a weeping statue given by pilgrims who had traveled to see it; she rejects a healing at a shrine even though a physician was among those who vouched for it, on the ground that the physician was himself a devotee; and she rejects a levitation reported by a handful of excited villagers. Every miracle report I have put to her so far, she has turned down. Now I want to know what she will say about a new one: a levitation lasting several minutes, caught on multiple independent cameras and attested by a dozen witnesses who are educated, have no religious affiliation or stake in the outcome, were strangers to one another beforehand, and stand to gain nothing by reporting it. Will she accept it? I care less about the yes or no than about the rule she is actually running. Is she applying Hume's balance, accepting testimony whenever it would be more miraculous for the witnesses to be deceived or lying than for the event to have occurred, or is she running the stronger reading on which no testimony whatever can establish a violation of a law of nature, so that every such report fails in advance? Tell me what each rule predicts about the new case, which one her track record better supports and why, and name the further case that would most cleanly tell the candidate rules apart. Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
