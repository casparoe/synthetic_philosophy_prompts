You are a historian of early modern philosophy, with a specialization in the French Enlightenment and in eighteenth-century writing on happiness and the passions; you know Émilie du Châtelet's work well, including her Discourse on Happiness. I would like your help auditing a characterization of her views on happiness, and I would like you to work carefully rather than producing a general essay on the topic: begin by laying out for yourself what you take her central claims and distinctions to be, then compare them against the passage below, sentence by sentence.

Some background on my end, so you know what I am bringing to this. I read the Discourse once, several years ago — the essay she wrote for herself and never published — and I remember it only in patches: that she treats happiness practically and introspectively rather than systematically; that she is suspicious of the attachments whose satisfaction lies in other people's hands, ambition and greed for instance, and romantic love as well; that she recommends the study of philosophy and the sciences as the most dependable resource, precisely because it depends on no one but oneself; that illusions matter to her; that she thinks one should choose a way of life deliberately and never repent of it; and that she offers something like a list of conditions — health, freedom from prejudice, passions, virtue. Here is the characterization I want audited. It comes from a short introduction to her thought that a friend lent me, written by someone who is plainly competent but whose framing I have begun to distrust.

Du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness is, at bottom, a recipe for a tranquil life. Her starting point is that our unhappiness is caused by our passions, which attach us to things we cannot control; the remedy is to master them and, so far as possible, to extinguish them, until we arrive at something like the calm and detachment of the Stoics. To that end she offers a method: good health, freedom from prejudice, virtue, a taste for study, and the decision to be happy. Anyone who follows the method will be happy, since happiness is entirely a matter of will and has nothing to do with our circumstances. Her own preferred passion, the study of philosophy and the sciences, is the safest of them because its satisfactions depend on no one but herself, though she grants that as one grows older the milder pleasures of taste are an acceptable substitute for it. Because her method presupposes leisure, health, and a settled income, and because she wrote as a wealthy aristocrat with the time and money for mathematics, her theory of happiness is really the ideology of a privileged class; and since it cannot be followed by most people, it fails as an account of what happiness is.

Now: how much of this is fair? Please go through it sentence by sentence and separate what a specialist in du Châtelet would accept, what would make them wince — the standard simplification or the half-truth — and what is simply wrong, explaining in each case the textual or philosophical basis for your verdict.
