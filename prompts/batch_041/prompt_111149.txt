I'm in a reading group on measurement, incentives and institutional epistemology, and we've spent the last few weeks on Goodhart's law and specification gaming. We keep hitting the same impasse: once you rule out an adversary — nobody cheating, nobody quietly optimising for the number, nobody even aware their work is being scored — it starts to look as though there is nothing left for the phenomenon to be about except the banal point that a metric can be an imperfect stand-in for the thing you care about. I don't think that's right, but I need a case that shows it rather than asserts it, and I'd like you to build the case to order. The scenario must be no longer than about 250 words, recognisably ordinary (a school, a clinic, a firm, a city agency), and it must not be a fable: the stock illustrations — the rat bounty, the nail factory, the cobra farm — are banned, as is anything with that flavour. The metric must not be a mere correlate of the goal. It has to be either a genuine constituent of the goal, in the strict sense that improving it while everything else is held fixed improves the goal, or a genuine downstream effect of the goal; say which of the two you have chosen and why the choice matters. Beyond that: nobody acts in bad faith and nobody's behaviour is best explained by the incentive; the measurement is exact, with no noise, no sampling error and no proxies-of-proxies; there is a single decision-maker making a single decision, with no comparison across many units and no repeated rounds of fixing the metric and re-optimising; and the degradation of the goal has to fall out of the structure of the case, not be stipulated by you at the end. If those constraints can't be jointly satisfied, say which one is doing the impossible work and what the nearest satisfiable case is, rather than fudging it.

Once the case is on the table, run it past the four standard explanations in plain words: that the metric only tracked the goal imperfectly, so optimising it selected for the error; that the metric tracked the goal fine where we started but the optimiser drove the system somewhere the relation had never been tested; that the metric is an effect of the goal, and moving an effect doesn't move its cause; and that someone with an objective of their own is feeding the metric. For each, say whether it applies, and where it doesn't, name the specific feature of your case that excludes it. If the case falls cleanly under none of them, I would much rather hear that, with the failure characterised in your own terms, than watch you force a fit. Then the part I care about most: identify the single feature doing the work, and tell me what becomes of the case when that feature is removed — in particular, which familiar, unremarkable case it collapses into. Then give me the strongest version of the objection from the sceptic in the group, whose line is that once bad faith, noise and selection are excluded there is nothing left but ordinary incomplete specification, so the example tells us nothing about Goodhart's law as such; and finally tell me whether the obvious remedy — measuring the goal itself rather than any stand-in, or measuring it more completely — would actually help here, and if it would not, precisely why not. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email: no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
