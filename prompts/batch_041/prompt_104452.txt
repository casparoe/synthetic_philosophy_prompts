I am a high school student who helps run our school's philosophy club, and I have been asked to lead a sixty-minute session for about fifteen students, ages fifteen to eighteen. I would like you to design a runnable classroom activity that makes them feel the force of what Ian Hacking called looping effects in psychiatric classification, rather than simply hearing the idea explained. By looping effects I mean the process by which a classification does not merely describe people but changes them—people interpret themselves through the label, behave differently, and are treated differently—and those changes then feed back into the classification itself, so that the category and the people in it are revised together over time. I want the loop to happen in the room, in what the students do, before anyone names it.

The setting is a philosophy club meeting, not a clinical setting. The students have no assigned reading and mixed familiarity with philosophy. I need the activity to be safe and non-stigmatizing: no real psychiatric diagnoses, no pressure to disclose anything personal, and no implication that the fictional categories are real conditions. The activity should use a clearly fictional diagnostic instrument. I have drafted a provisional one and would like you to build the activity around it or improve it. It is called the Provisional Index of Classroom Afflictions, and it contains three invented categories:

Discursive Restlessness: a persistent urge to change the subject before a silence becomes uncomfortable, often accompanied by a sense that one's contributions are either too much or too little.

Anticipatory Withdrawal: a tendency to predict that a group activity will be boring or exhausting and to prepare to leave early, sometimes by volunteering for a minor task that justifies stepping out.

Conscientious Overcorrection: a pattern of responding to feedback by over-preparing for the next task, then feeling that the over-preparation is itself a symptom.

The crucial design feature is that the Index's criteria are circular and socially dependent. A student meets criteria for a category if (a) they endorse at least two of the listed experiences, (b) at least one peer in their small group says the description fits them, and (c) they find the description more accurate than they expected. I want the activity to exploit this: the labels should become real in the group's behavior, not because the students are pretending, but because the descriptions are vague enough to fit ordinary experience and the group's recognition gives them weight.

Please design the activity with these elements:

- A minute-by-minute timeline for the sixty minutes, including a baseline round with no labels, a labeling round, a second task round with labels active, and a debrief.
- All rules, roles, and materials. I can make printed cards, handouts, and a timer. I do not need real money or anything expensive.
- A way to make the labels salient in behavior: for example, label cards could come with a small accommodation or role (a timer, a pass to step out, a checklist) that changes how the labeled student participates and how others perceive them.
- A simple way to collect data during the session—show of hands, private tally, or a short anonymous form—so the group can see whether the number of students meeting criteria changed from the baseline to the labeled round, and whether new symptoms appeared after labeling.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the looping-effects idea: where the classification changed behavior, where behavior changed the classification, where peers' expectations mattered, and where the category began to look self-confirming. The debrief should introduce the term looping effects only after the students have already experienced the pattern. It should also connect the activity to related ideas such as self-fulfilling prophecy and the way labels can shape perception and behavior, but without turning into a lecture.
- A candid list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—students hamming it up, students refusing to label each other, a category that fits no one, a category that fits everyone, ethical discomfort, someone taking it personally—and what I should do in each case.
- An honest note on which features of real psychiatric classification the activity cannot reproduce. I expect this to include the institutional authority of psychiatry, the long timescale of real looping, medication and insurance, stigma and power, genuine distress, and the difference between a game and a diagnosis. Please say explicitly what the facilitator must supply in the debrief because the activity itself cannot.
- No bibliography, reading list, or citations. I cannot search online and do not need sources; I need something I can run.

The activity should not begin with a lecture about Hacking or about looping effects. The students should encounter the phenomenon through the rules and incentives, and only then hear the name. I would like the design to be usable by a student facilitator, not a professional philosopher.
