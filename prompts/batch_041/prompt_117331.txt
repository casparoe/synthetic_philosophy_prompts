I need help with something that started as a joke at a family dinner and has since turned into a real dispute. I have come to suspect that it is not really about cooking.

There are three cooks in my family. My grandmother, who is eighty-eight, has made the same twelve dishes for sixty years. She cannot explain what she does — ask her for the recipe and she waves you off — and she cannot improvise: if the shop is out of an ingredient, the dish is simply off the menu. But she has never served a bad meal, and she can hear from the next room that a pan is about to burn. My mother learned from her, but not in the same way. She reads a dish once, cooks it through in her head before she starts, tastes, corrects, and can produce a passable version of something she has only eaten and never made; she can tell you why last night's sauce broke. My daughter, who is fifteen, is by any measure we have the most capable of the three: she can make pastry, breads, curries, dishes none of us have ever eaten, because she follows an app. Without the phone she cannot make toast. She also cannot judge a recipe until she has cooked it — she has never once looked at one and known it would fail, and she has never improved on one. (There is also my nephew, eleven months old, who screams at the blender, grips whatever is put in his hand, and puts everything in his mouth, none of which he has learned.)

My brother-in-law proposes what he calls the blackout test: cut the power and the network, cook from what is actually in the cupboard, and see who can feed the household. He says the result is obvious — my grandmother first, my daughter last, and the phone rather than the girl doing the cooking. My mother says this is nostalgia dressed up as rigor: someone who can cook anything is plainly a better cook than someone who can cook twelve things, whatever she consults. My daughter observes, not unreasonably, that she is the one who gets dinner on the table on a Tuesday.

I do not think either side is right. My suspicion — which I would like tested rather than flattered — is that these are not merely three points on one scale of skill but different kinds of competence, arranged in some order, and that the order matters, because what my daughter can do seems to rest on something the other two have and she does not. If that is right, I want to know what the levels are, whether the higher is really higher, and precisely what the blackout test does and does not show.

Then I want the practical answer, because this is a real household and not a seminar. Is my daughter acquiring a skill or a dependence? Is the brittleness of what she can do a genuine loss, or the ordinary price of every tool human beings have ever taken up? Would you take the phone away for a month, or is that simply punishing her for living in her own century?
