I keep running into a sentence I have started to distrust, and I would like to know what it can actually mean. The sentence is some version of "the truth has many sides," and it usually arrives with the story of the blind men and the elephant: each man feels a different part of the animal, each reports something different, and each is both right and wrong about the whole. Lately people have been telling me that the idea comes from Jain philosophy, where — as I understand it from a few popular pieces I have read — reality is said to be many-sided (anekāntavāda), and no statement is supposed to be made flatly, because every claim is meant to be qualified by a word meaning "in some respect," so that what is true from one standpoint may be false from another. I have also read that the tradition works this out as a fixed list of seven ways of predicating something of something, one of which is that the thing is, in some respect, beyond what words can capture. I have no philosophical training and cannot check any of this, so please treat my summary as the thing to be corrected rather than as something to trust.

What interests me is the work the sentence does in the rooms I am actually in. When a colleague complained about a supervisor, someone said "there are many sides to this," and that was the end of the question whether it happened. In an argument about whether my city's housing policy had made things worse, a friend said "it depends on your perspective" and then would not say anything more. In both cases the sentence functioned as a way of not answering. But I have also seen it used where it seemed exactly right: two people describing the same long relationship gave accounts that could not both be the plain truth, and yet both plainly were, and I do not think either was lying.

Here is the menu of readings I have managed to come up with on my own. I would like you to correct it, add to it, and then sort it:

1. The world is complicated, so any short description of it leaves something out.
2. Different descriptions pick out different features of the same thing; each is right about its feature, none is the whole story.
3. A statement is only true relative to a respect, and the respect belongs to the statement: "the pot exists" is true of the pot and false of the lump of clay. This is a claim about how predication works, not a licence to say anything.
4. Things genuinely have contradictory properties at once, and the contradiction is in the thing, not in our descriptions of it.
5. Truth is relative to a perspective, and there is no further fact about who is right.
6. Since every view is partial, nobody should be contradicted — which is what the sentence is doing when it ends an argument.

For each reading I want three things: whether it is trivial, substantive, or simply false; whether the people who deploy the sentence in an argument actually need that reading or only believe they do; and whether it really supports the move of shutting the question down. Then the two questions I keep getting stuck on. First, if every view is one-sided, what is the status of that claim itself, and how is the qualifier "in some respect" supposed to get the doctrine out of self-contradiction without becoming a magic word that lets one assert anything? Second, is there any reading on the menu under which it remains possible to say that something simply did not happen? Please give me a numbered list of readings with a verdict on each, rather than a general essay, because I want to carry it around and use it. No sources or reading list, please; I would not follow them up.
