I've been defending a view in my seminar that I've been calling tranquilism: the only thing that makes a life go better or worse is craving and suffering, and their absence is the whole of the good. Positive pleasure adds literally nothing -- a life with zero craving and zero suffering is as good as a life can get, and unbidden pleasures dont improve it.

Build me a case that isolates exactly this. It's for a paper where I'm arguing tranquilism can't be a theory of wellbeing at all, so I need the tranquilist's verdict to come out not just counterintuitive but absurd. Specs: realistic (two ordinary human lives, no brains in vats, no drugs, no experience machines); the setup in one paragraph; the two lives have identical craving and suffering but differ sharply in pleasure, and the pleasures that distinguish them have to be ones a tranquilist can't wave away as covertly craving-laden (so no anticipation, no striving, nothing that's a pleasure of getting what you wanted); and a normal person would obviously prefer one life to the other, so the tranquilist is stuck saying the difference is worth exactly nothing. Don't let the case turn on death or on comparing existence with non-existence -- thats a different fight and I dont want it here.

Then do the work: state the tranquilist verdict, the hedonist verdict, and the desire-satisfaction verdict, and say which single feature of the case is doing the work and whether the case collapses if you remove it. Finish with the strongest thing a tranquilist can say back -- how they'd try to show the case isn't a problem rather than just biting the bullet.
