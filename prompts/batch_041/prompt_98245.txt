I'm in a seminar on nineteenth-century political thought and I keep meeting a version of Mill I don't recognize — the harm-principle liberal whose late interest in socialism gets treated either as a sentimental dotage or as a full conversion, depending on who's talking. Neither fits what's actually on the page. So I want to do something more concrete: take a real episode and run it through the framework Mill actually develops in the late socialist material, the stuff he was writing from about 1869 until his death and that got collected afterwards as Chapters on Socialism, plus the earlier chapter in the Principles about the probable futurity of the labouring classes. The shape of that framework, as I read it: the existing property system is a historical arrangement rather than a natural one, its legitimacy depends on whether it delivers on its own promise of rewarding labour, the socialist alternative has to be judged on comparative productive performance and not only on justice, and the transition should come through voluntary association and experiment — cooperatives, profit-sharing, worker partnerships — rather than legislative fiat, because the moral capacities the new order needs have to be grown inside the old one, over what he clearly imagines as a long testing period.

The episode is the Paris Commune of 1871. I know the basic history and I don't need it retold: the siege, the Central Committee of the National Guard, the Versailles government under Thiers, the decrees on rents and pawnshops, the takeover of abandoned workshops, Bloody Week and the ten-to-thirty-thousand dead depending on whose estimate you take. And I can't use citations or reading lists, so please don't give me any. What I want is the mapping in both directions. Which features of the Commune's actual practice line up with what Mill's framework treats as the marks of a legitimate socialist transition, and which cut against it? It wasn't a party dictatorship or a state seizure of industry in the way the Blanquists and the Marxists wanted; it was closer to a federation of wards and workplace associations, it left most private property alone, and it had about two months and a war to work with. Does that make it, by Mill's lights, a promising experiment or a cautionary case of a transition improvised under siege instead of prepared by decades of cooperative education? Push on the hard bits: what does his insistence on voluntariness do with the fact that the workers' associations were partly a response to owners fleeing and normal commerce collapsing — is that consent or duress, and does the framework even have the resources to tell the difference? What does the comparative-productivity requirement say about a body that never got to produce anything? Where is Mill simply silent — about revolutionary violence, about what a socialist polity owes to the rest of a hostile country, about whether a commune can be a unit of transition at all? Then flip it: what does the Commune show about his late views? Does it confirm the gradualist, test-it-first picture, or is it the case he never anticipated, where the transition is forced on people by war and state collapse rather than chosen, and if it's the latter, does that tell us his framework was always a theory of peaceful transition in disguise? Be honest about where the framework runs out rather than stretching it to cover everything.
