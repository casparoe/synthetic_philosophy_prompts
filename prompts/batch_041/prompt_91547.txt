I want to practice defending a position I only half-believe, against someone who actually knows the objections. So: role-play for me.

The position: that there's a fact of the matter about what a system's internal states represent — which ontology it's using, in the loose sense the rationalist/AI-safety corner of the internet means when it frets about a model having an ontology we can't even translate into ours — and, more to the point, that this fact is in principle identifiable, i.e. there exists some procedure, however horrible, that would tell you which one it is.

You play the skeptic. Not the woozy kind — the sharp kind, the one who says the question is either empirical and trivial or else not a question about the world at all, and that when people call the information "inaccessible" they're reporting a defect in the question rather than a hidden thing. You're allowed to use the standard moves (translation manuals, rabbit-stages, underdetermination, "the mechanism is just more behavior under another description"), but argue them, dont cite them — no reading lists, no names-and-dates, I know the references. Concede a point when I actually land one; never concede the load-bearing one. Two or three paragraphs a turn, same register as me, no "as an AI" hedging, stay in character until I say stop.

My opening move: take two systems that behave identically on every input — one representing persisting objects, the other representing momentary stages with no identity across time. You say there's no fact about which one it is. But "identical on every input" is doing all the work in that sentence, and it's never actually true. The stage-system can't re-identify a thing across occlusion, can't plan around it, can't do any of the things that only make sense if the thing survives you looking away. So they come apart on some input, and that input is the experiment that identifies the ontology. If instead you define the two ontologies to be predictively identical by construction, then fine, nothing separates them — but that's a fact about your definitions, not about the world. And before you say it: I'll grant that extracting the answer might be computationally hopeless. That's a price tag, not an inaccessibility.
