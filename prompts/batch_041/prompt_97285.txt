I've spent the better part of a year reading my way through the literature on how states handle the aftermath of mass atrocities — truth-for-amnesty bargains and their relatives — and there is a shape to these arrangements that I keep running into and cannot find a name for. Roughly: the official record of what happened is assembled out of voluntary testimony, and the testimony was bought. People came forward because something was on the table — immunity, a lighter sentence, a chance to fix their own framing in the permanent account — so the set of people who appear in the record is close to the set of people for whom that trade was worth making. The man who knows he will never be prosecuted has nothing to purchase and no reason to talk. The family that wants nothing to do with the state's process stays home. The perpetrator who reads the offer as a trap keeps quiet and waits. Then the record hardens into the official history, and its silences begin doing work they were never designed to do: "it isn't in the report" shades into "it didn't happen," in courtrooms, in textbooks, in arguments between grandchildren. South Africa is the famous instance, but I doubt the pattern is South African.

Two features of it seem to me to escape the obvious vocabulary. The first is that it isn't a failure of care: the commissioners can be as scrupulous as you like, because the distortion sits in the mechanism that got people to talk at all rather than in the inquiry, and the obvious remedies — subpoena power, compelled testimony, a duty to appear — would destroy the very thing that produced the testimony in the first place. The second is that the record is not secretively partial: everyone involved knows it is partial, knows roughly who is missing and why, and it still functions as the baseline against which later claims are measured, with the knowing apparently doing no work at all. I've read enough rationalist-adjacent material to have a working grip on survivorship bias and selection effects generally, and I'm fairly sure that isn't it — the missing people here didn't die or fail a filter, they declined a trade. I also know the standard complaint that commissions manufacture a sanitized or negotiated narrative, and that strikes me as a different animal: it concerns what the commission chose to say, whereas what I'm after concerns who never walked in the door. I've likewise seen "archival silence" used in library-and-archive writing, but as far as I can tell that names gaps produced by who held the power to deposit records, which again isn't quite the thing.

Is there a term of art for this — in the transitional-justice literature, in legal epistemology, or anywhere else — and if so, what does it cover that my description doesn't, and how would you distinguish it from the three near-misses above? If there isn't one, coin it: give me a name, a definition, and the edges. In particular I'd like to know what the same phenomenon looks like outside transitional justice, and which of the nearby concepts you'd say comes closest, and why.
