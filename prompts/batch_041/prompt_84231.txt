I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a historical novel set in the ancient Mediterranean around the time of the Peloponnesian War, and I've been reading alot about how we know anything about the distant past. I've ended up with a working creed that I'm worried is just a pile of habits. I'd like a consistency audit, not a reading list.

Here's what I believe:

1. The past only reaches us through sources, and every source is a witness with an agenda. So the first question is never what happened but who is telling me this, to whom, and why.
2. Thucydides is basically right that you have to seperate the real cause of a war from the grievances the parties said they were fighting over. If you don't, you're just transcribing propaganda.
3. Herodotus is basically right too: the digressions, the gossip, the weird ethnographic stuff is where you learn how people actually lived and what they feared. A strict Thucydidean method throws out the best material.
4. I've started keeping what I call a silence ledger. For every source I write down not just what it says but what it has to not notice, or not say, in order for its account to work. I think the silences are often more revealing than the assertions. And I think every source is running on two clocks at once, the clock of the events it describes and the clock of the moment it's telling them, with the telling clock always trying to reset the event clock. So I'm reading silences and time-lag at the same time.
5. Hindsight bias is the cardinal sin of historical writing. The people I'm writing about didn't know how it would turn out, and if I let the ending leak into their choices I've written a costume drama, not a novel.
6. But you can't explain anything without knowing how it turned out. A narrative has to select, and selection means you know the ending. There's no story without a shape, and no shape without hindsight.
7. I think we should not psychologize ancient people with modern categories. Calling someone anxious or traumatized or a dissenter in the modern sense is usually a category error.
8. But I also think the only way to understand an ancient decision is to reconstruct the reasoning from the inside, to ask what it would have been like to think that way with those beliefs in that situation. So I'm constantly using my own mind as an instrument, which means using modern categories at least as scaffolding.
9. I think a historical novel has more freedom than a history, but that freedom makes it more responsible, not less. If I invent a speech, I'm making a claim about what was possible to think and say, and I can't hide behind it's fiction.
10. I think some accounts of the past are objectively better than others, closer to the evidence and more honest about the gaps. But I also think the past isn't just sitting there waiting to be recovered. It's constructed from the evidence we have, and the evidence changes.

So: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and which one has to go if I want the rest to hang together? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than telling me they're all in tension to some degree. For each real one, say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me. I'm not asking for citations, just the audit.
