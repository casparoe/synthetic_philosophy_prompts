Here are my verdicts about rotation in empty space, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they express. Newton's bucket: the concave surface is a real fact about the water, and it survives the water's catching up with the bucket, so it cannot be a matter of the water's motion relative to the bucket; I explain it by the water's rotation relative to the earth and the fixed stars. A universe containing only the bucket and its water, spun up until the two turn together: I think the surface stays flat. Two globes joined by a cord, rotating about their midpoint with nothing else in the universe: the cord strikes me as genuinely taut, and nothing beyond the two globes rotating relative to each other is needed to make it so. A single homogeneous sphere spinning about its axis with nothing else in the universe: I think it stays perfectly spherical. Add one distant dust particle and I still think it stays spherical; a single speck cannot flatten it. Replace the speck with a distant shell of stars and now I think the sphere does flatten, or at any rate that its inertia differs from the lone case.

My tentative principle: rotation is real only as rotation relative to other matter, but only sufficiently massive matter counts as a reference, so rotation relative to a speck does nothing while rotation relative to a shell of stars does everything. Please state as precisely as you can the best principle that fits these verdicts, tying each clause to the case that forces it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Also say what it predicts for a case I have not mentioned — a sphere at the center of a massive shell, with the shell rotating and the sphere at rest relative to the distant stars — and whether the principle can be stated without appealing to the universe's total mass distribution or to some privileged global frame. I take this to be the substantivalism versus relationism dispute; tell me which side, if either, the principle you give puts me on. Please answer in American English.
