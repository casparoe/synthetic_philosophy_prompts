I spent thirty-five years as a field geologist and retired a few years back; since then I've been reading philosophy fairly seriously — Burke, Oakeshott, and the people who think they're both wrong. Somewhere in there I ended up chairing the bylaws committee of the geological society I joined as a grad student, and now I have a problem. Our bylaws are a museum of accumulated junk: rules passed in 1911, 1948, 1973, nobody left alive knows why, and the younger members want the whole thing rewritten from scratch. I keep muttering about Chesterton's fence and they keep rolling their eyes, and honestly I'm not sure I beleive it myself anymore — so help me build something better than a slogan.

Give me a procedure the committee can actually run, one rule at a time. The order of questions to ask about a rule whose original reason has been forgotten; what actually counts as recovering the reason versus inventing a plausible one after the fact; what to do when the reason is genuinely unrecoverable; what to do when it's recoverable but the world that made it sensible is gone; and the point, if there is one, where we're entitled to pull the fence down. Assume five of us, none of us historians, two hours a month, and that our recommendations go up to a council that can and does ignore us.

Then run it on three of our rules: (1) the annual meeting must always be held in the town where the society was founded; (2) the curator of the type collection holds a standing veto over any specimen loan; (3) nobody who has worked for a mining company within the last five years can sit on council. I happen to think (3) is an insult and (2) is a fossil, so I'd like to see the procedure tell me I'm wrong at least once, and tell me why. Finish with what the procedure can't settle — the places where a human just has to choose, and I'd rather know that going in than find out mid-argument.
