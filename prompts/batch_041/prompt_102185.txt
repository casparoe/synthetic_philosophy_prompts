I have been arguing with my reading group about the precautionary principle and would like an outside diagnosis. One member has produced the following argument; every step looks plausible to me, and I find the conclusion unacceptable. It runs as follows.

(1) The principle, as our group has been stating it, holds that where an activity threatens serious or irreversible harm, the absence of full scientific certainty is not a reason for postponing measures to prevent that harm, and the burden of justification shifts to those who would proceed.
(2) Preventing an activity is itself an intervention with consequences, and those consequences are frequently uncertain.
(3) In any live environmental dispute, both proceeding and preventing carry some possibility of serious or irreversible harm, and in neither direction is the science fully settled.
(4) If the principle is a genuine rule for deciding, then it governs the choice between proceeding and preventing wherever that choice arises, including the choice of whether to prevent.
(5) Applied to the option of preventing: preventing may cause serious harm under unsettled science, so the burden shifts to whoever would prevent, and we should not prevent.
(6) Applied to the option of proceeding: proceeding may cause serious harm under unsettled science, so the burden shifts to whoever would proceed, and we should not proceed.
(7) Hence the principle forbids both available options at once. A rule that forbids every option is not a rule for deciding but a refusal to decide.
(8) Therefore the principle, understood as a rule for deciding, is incoherent; what remains is either an empty slogan or a mere presumption that never settles a case.

My colleague concludes that the principle should be discarded. I would rather keep something like it, since I think it has done genuine work in cases where a novel technology or practice is introduced before its risks are understood. My colleague locates the error in (2), insisting that declining to act is not an intervention in the relevant sense; I doubt that (2) is the problem, but I cannot say where the problem is. Tell me which step to reject, or, if the argument is valid as it stands, which premise is least costly to give up. For the step you target, explain why it looked compelling and what the alternative costs.

I would also like two specific questions addressed. First, does (3) smuggle in something illegitimate by treating the harms of acting and the harms of not acting as harms of the same kind, or is that symmetry precisely what the principle should be asked to confront? Second, is (4) entitled to treat the principle as a rule that decides, rather than as a device that shifts a burden of proof within some broader decision procedure — and if the latter, does that rescue the principle or merely postpone the reductio? Finally, our group has floated a narrower version that triggers only for harms that are novel, imposed on others rather than risked by consent, and irreversible. Say whether that version escapes the argument or merely relocates the trouble, and what it now leaves undecided.
