I'm trying to actually understand Hume's essay on tragedy and I keep bouncing off it. I dont have the text in front of me and I don't trust my memory for the wording at all, so let me just lay out the argument as I've reconstructed it and you tell me where I've mangled it.

Here's my version. The disagreeable passions a tragedy stirs up — pity, terror, grief — are genuinely painful; he's not saying they're secretly pleasant all along. But in tragedy they get converted into pleasure, and the thing that does the converting is the eloquence, the beauty of the telling. The pain remains real pain, but it becomes subordinate to the pleasure we take in the art, and the two run together into one agreeable sentiment rather than cancelling out. He seems to go further and say the more we're moved, the more pleasure we get, because the greatness of the passion feeds the pleasure instead of subtracting from it. And then he flips it: this doesn't happen when someone tells us bad news in gorgeous prose — eloquence doesn't sweeten real life — and he tries to say why tragedy is the special case.

What I want help with:

1. What is the mechanism actually supposed to be? Is the conversion a psychological process, or is it closer to a claim about attention and object — same emotion, but it gets fastened onto a different object (the work rather than the events), and that's all that changes?

2. The subordinate/dominant structure. Does he need it? Could you get the same result from a flat mixed-feeling account, or is the two-movement picture doing real work that a one-movement account can't do? I want to know which piece is load-bearing.

3. The real-life contrast. Is his explanation of why eloquence doesn't help with bad news actually any good, or is it ad hoc? What's the strongest form of the objection that he's just redescribing the phenomenon, and what could he say back?

Now here's a thought I'd like you to test against the argument rather than just nod along to. I suspect the real work isn't being done by eloquence at all but by permission — sad art hands you a strong negative emotion with no practical demand attached. Nothing you have to do, nobody you have to comfort, no decision pending, so the emotion can be had for its own sake and that's where the pleasure comes from. If that's right, eloquence is just one of several ways of granting the permission. Is that a rival to Hume or a re-reading of him? Where would he push back, and would he be right to?

And something I can't sort out on my own: if the pleasure really depends on the beauty of the telling, what's going on with a plain, ugly, clumsily written book or film that still leaves you wrecked and glad you saw it? Does that break the account, or does it just show that eloquence was standing in for something broader?

Also, is this supposed to be empirical psychology or conceptual analysis? If someone asked what observation would show the conversion story is false, is there an answer, or is the whole thing insulated?

Please dont give me a survey of the literature. I want to understand this one argument, and I want you to flag the places where my paraphrase is probably distorting what he actually says — especially if the distortion is what's making the puzzle look harder than it is.
