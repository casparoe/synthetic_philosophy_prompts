I spent thirty years at the bench in experimental physiology before turning to philosophy, and I keep finding that discussions of the readiness potential presuppose a standard of adequacy that no one bothers to state. Before I read any more, write me that specification: what any adequate account of the relation between conscious intention and the neural preparation for action has to deliver. Give me, with the consideration behind each, the phenomena it must accommodate -- a readiness potential that precedes the subject's reported awareness of the urge to move; the human capacity to cancel an incipient movement, and the question of what neural activity accompanies the cancellation; the demonstrated unreliability of introspective reports about when an intention formed; and the suspicion current among working neuroscientists that the averaged readiness potential is partly an artifact of filtering and averaging and may reflect a slow stochastic accumulation rather than a specific motor command. Give me the distinctions it must not collapse -- predicting a movement versus constituting a decision, the timing of an intention versus the timing of its neural precursor, freedom as absence of external constraint versus freedom as ultimate authorship, and a cause of behavior versus a reason for it. And give me the constraints it cannot contradict -- that single-trial prediction is far weaker than averaged prediction, and that the subject's report is itself a behavior with a neural cause. Tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are the prejudices of one camp, flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied, and name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate theories. No reading list and no scorecard of individual authors; I want the criteria themselves. End with one sentence beginning "Bottom line:" that states the requirement you would refuse to give up.
