In my university's endowment office there's a sentence that ends every argument: we can't sell the fossil shares, because somebody else buys them the same afternoon and then we've just lost the income and changed nothing. Same move for the supplier with the worse labor record, the donation with strings attached, the conference in the country we're supposed to be boycotting. Nobody defends it as noble, exactly — it's treated as just obviously decisive, like it closes the question. What bothers me is that the same university is absolutist the other way: fabricating data is a firing offense even when a rival lab has already published the fabricated result and your honest paper will be ignored, and the integrity office will tell you flatly that everyone else doing it is not a defense. A friend in my reading group says this is just a multipolar trap, a coordination failure, and the procurement logic is plain game theory with nothing moral in it; my gut says the two stances are assuming different things about what makes an act wrong in the first place. So what does the procurement reasoning actually presuppose — that a wrong only counts if I make a difference to the outcome, that my obligations are conditional on other people doing their part, that as a fiduciary I have a different duty than I do as a person, or something else? Does the somebody-else-would-do-it argument even need one of those, or is it running on a cheaper premise that happens to sound like them? And are the two stances really inconsistent, or is there a theory that covers both? If I swapped the procurement stance for the integrity-office one, what would change on the ground?
