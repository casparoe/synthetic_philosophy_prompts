My friend and I spent most of last Saturday evening arguing about the two-envelope paradox, and we went to bed without agreeing on whether anything remains to be explained. His line is that the puzzle is a museum piece: the switching argument needs a probability distribution over the possible amounts that cannot exist, and once you see that, there is nothing left over. Mine is that this is too quick. Granting the diagnosis, it identifies which premise fails in the standard setup; it does not explain what makes the setup seductive, and it says nothing about whether the same trouble can be reproduced for decision rules that never compute an expectation in the first place. That last thought is the one I want to develop, and it now has a deadline: a two-page proposal, due Friday, for a small departmental summer grant that would give me ten weeks of full-time work and, I hope, a paper.

Write that proposal. The idea, as half-formed as it is: I want to ask whether the two-envelope paradox can be reconstructed for decision rules that are not expected-value maximizers — maximin, minimax regret, satisficing against a fixed aspiration level, and a rule that weights losses more heavily than equal-sized gains — and what the answer, whichever way it goes, shows about where the paradox's engine actually sits. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, and say what would count as a yes and what as a no. Explain why the familiar resolutions — the impossible-prior diagnosis, the charge that the switching argument treats one and the same quantity as fixed in one step and random in the next, and the thought that my envelope and the other envelope are not symmetric descriptions — leave my question open rather than settling it; if one of them does settle it, say so plainly and tell me what is left to do. Describe how the work would actually go: what gets formalized, what a variant of the puzzle for a non-expectation rule looks like, how I would distinguish a genuine parallel from an artifact of a badly specified choice set, and why this is a better use of ten weeks than cataloguing the existing resolutions. Say what a result would look like in each direction, what would count as the project failing to produce one, and what I would write up in that case. Be honest about scope: what is achievable in ten weeks and what should be cut or saved for later.

Write it for a review panel of general philosophers rather than decision theorists. Do not give me citations or a bibliography — refer to positions and arguments by description and I will supply the references myself. Separate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. American English, please.
