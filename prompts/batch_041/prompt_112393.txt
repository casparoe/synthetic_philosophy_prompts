I'm a novelist, not a logician so bare with me. I'm writing a book about two people who share a lab and have stopped being able to talk to each other, and their fight is whether a self-improving AI can ever be justified in trusting its own sucessor. The setup I've handed them: an agent that reasons in a formal system strong enough for arithmetic, that wants to hand its goals to a rebuilt version of itself, and that wants to be entitled to the thought "if I've proven the successor will do X, then the successor will do X." One of them says that's doomed by the Löb/Gödel machinery — an agent that trusts its own soundness in that way can be made to prove anything, so it can't consistently trust a successor reasoning by the same rules, and you need either a genuinely weaker successor or an endless tower of them. The other says that's an artifact of modelling the agent as logically omniscient over a fixed system and treating self-trust as a theorem it has to prove rather than a standing policy; loosen any of those and the wall turns out to be a fence, or just a matter of degree.

I want the document the two of them should have written instead of shouting at each other: what they both can sign, the disagreement stated so each side would accept the wording of its own view, the cruxes, and for each crux something they'd both agree bears on it, with each one's prediction written down in advance. One thing I'd like the statement to actually engage with, because it's the bit my plot turns on: a colleague proposes the agent can trust a successor as long as each generation's self-trust is capped and shrinks by a fixed factor with every rewrite — is that a real escape from the obstacle or just relocating it somewhere the agent can't look? And does any of it change if the agent is allowed to be a little unsure whether it's consistent? Number the agreed claims, then give me a disagreements table.
