I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, and the thread I keep returning to is the old one about whether a statistical translator understands anything: it emits fluent sentences without grasping what the sentence says, and nothing inside the system can notice when it has gone wrong. Write me a satirical piece I can post there. Make it a company's internal, blameless postmortem after its translation system rendered a sentence that mattered — summary, impact, timeline, what went well, root cause, action items — around 600 words, deadpan, with no winking and no jokes that are merely jokes about corporate life. The comedy has to come from getting the substance right: the mistranslation was fluent and wrong in a way a competent human would not have been wrong; the reviewers signed off because it read so well; a round-trip check is treated as exculpatory; the confidence score turns out to be a fact about the system's own token probabilities, not about whether the translation is right; and the action item is more data for that sentence rather than anything that would let the system notice the error next time. Put the mistranslation itself in the timeline with enough context that a reader sees exactly what was lost, and have one participant ask the question the document is avoiding, with a metric for an answer. Nobody in it should say that the system does not understand; the reader should conclude that alone. The target is the practice of inferring understanding from quality scores, not any company or person, and nothing in the piece should be a claim that someone who has thought hard about translation would call unfair.
