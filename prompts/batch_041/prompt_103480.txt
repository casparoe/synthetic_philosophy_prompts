Im setting the final for a grad seminar on animal minds and I need one essay question for it. The usual stuff students write on this topic -- mirror tests, the Cambridge declaration, octopus anecdotes -- is exactly what I dont want them regurgitating, so the question has to get into the machinery of the inference instead.

Heres the background theyll have. For the last few weeks weve been on a criterion a colleague of mine keeps pushing: he says the marker of consciousness isnt any particular behavior or brain part but what he calls unlimited associative learning -- the open-ended capacity to keep laying down new associations with no fixed ceiling, as opposed to the old conditioning setups where an animal can only ever learn a bounded set of pairings. His argument is that this kind of open-ended learning needs some sort of global broadcast or workspace system, and that it shows up in the fossil record around the Cambrian, so the conscious club ends up being vertebrates, cephalopods and arthropods but not sponges, jellyfish or plants.

The trouble is the class has split into two lazy camps. Half of them now say "UAL, therefore conscious" as if it were a theorem, and the other half say its circular -- that all hes done is relabel flexible learning as consciousness. I want a question that forces them to actually make and then attack the inference, and to commit on a concrete case instead of hiding behind "the hard problem."

So: please write me (a) the exam question itself, (b) a model answer of about 700-800 words, and (c) a marking scheme. Constraints: they get 90 minutes and the question is worth 30 marks, and the question has to include a specific made-up case they have to handle -- a newly described benthic invertebrate that clearly shows UAL-type open-ended learning but whose nervous system is just a diffuse nerve net with no central brain or ganglion. The question should make them (i) lay out the UAL-to-consciousness inference as an actual premise-conclusion argument, (ii) say what UAL predicts about this animal and what the rival "you need a centralized substrate" view predicts, (iii) pick a side and say what evidence would change their mind, and (iv) say whether their answer makes consciousness a matter of kind or of degree.

For the marking scheme, give must-hit points with the marks each is worth (total 30), and for each one a one-liner on the mistake that costs students marks -- e.g. treating the substrate claim and the criterion as if they were the same claim, or using "correlation isnt causation" as a substitute for saying which premise fails. An A answer has to attack a specific premise or show the two claims come apart on the invertebrate case; a B answer just gestures at mystery or repeats the two camps. No reading lists or citations needed, the students already have the seminar.
