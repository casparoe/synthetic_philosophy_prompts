So I'm about 200 pages into a novel — it's about a woman, mid-thirties, who drifts into a wellness-adjacent online community after her brother dies, and the whole middle section is her becoming fluent in it, learning the in-jokes and the vocabulary until it's less a belief she holds than a room she lives in. I want to close one of those chapters with a poem, and ive gotten fixated on the pantoum, because the form literally does the thing the book is about. Here's the thing I keep circling: everybody calls this "being in a bubble," but I think there are two different states and the novel only works if I keep them apart. In the first one you're just not hearing the other side — nobody ever told you, and honestly you'd probably listen if someone sat you down. In the second one you're hearing the other side constantly, but only as something already reframed and dismissed; the community quotes the skeptics back and forth to each other, and the quotes have been re-staged until they mean the opposite of what they meant. A friend who reads too much philosophy told me the first is a bubble and the second is an echo chamber, and the reason "just expose them to other views" doesn't work is that exposure actually fixes the first and does nothing for the second, because in the second the outside view is already inside, just pre-digested and disarmed.

So what I want is a proper pantoum — five quatrains or so, lines 2 and 4 of each stanza coming back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the final stanza folding back into the first so the whole thing closes on itself like a lid. The trick I'm after: there's one line that's something an outsider actually said, a real doubt, and it comes back three or four times word for word, but each time the lines around it have shifted so it means something different — first a genuine question, then a joke the group makes about itself, then finally evidence for the group's own point, so by the end the outsider's words are working for them. I want a reader who's never heard any of this vocabulary to just feel the room getting smaller, and a reader who has to be able to point at the exact line where the bubble turns into the chamber and say why the form did it. No redemption ending, no villain, and for gods sake don't make her stupid — she's the smartest person in the book. Write the poem, and then give me two or three lines on where the form is actually doing the philosophy and where it's just decoration, so I can see if I pulled it off.
