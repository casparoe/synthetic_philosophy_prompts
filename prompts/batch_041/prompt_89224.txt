Ok so I'm writing a short thing for my decision theory reading group and I want you to be the referee. Take the role of a philosopher who works on decision theory and epistemology — someone who actually knows the value-of-information literature and not just the slogan version of it. Before you evaluate anything, spend a few sentences restating my argument back to me in your own words, and brainstorm a bit about where you think the weak joints are; then give the comments. Don't be nice about it, I'd rather find out now.

Here's the thesis. The result usually called Good's theorem says free information can never have negative expected value: if you're an expected-utility maximizer and someone offers you a costless reliable signal before you choose, the worst case is that you're no better off, because you can always ignore the signal and do what you'd have done anyway. I want to argue that this is true but nearly empty, and that it quietly stops being true the moment the agent has preferences about her own beliefs. Take someone with anticipatory preferences: she'd rather spend the week before the biopsy believing she's fine than know the answer early, even though the answer wouldn't change anything she does. Force the free result on her and her expected utility drops, because now she has to live with knowing. The theorem says that can't happen. I say it can, and the reason the theorem looks universal is that it individuates outcomes independently of the agent's information state. Once beliefs go into the outcome description, the fallback act — ignore the signal — isn't on the menu anymore, because you can't un-know. So the theorem is really a claim about a fixed preference order over a fixed act set, not a claim about agents at all; it's a scope restriction wearing the costume of a deep result.

I do want to head off one objection in the essay, because I keep going back and forth on it. If the signal comes in a sealed envelope and she's free to not open it, then the fallback survives and the theorem is untouched. So my counterexample only bites when the information is forced on you — a diagnosis, a confession you can't help overhearing. Maybe that's the real distinction I should be pressing, and the theorem has simply nothing to say about forced information, which is itself the point. Or maybe I'm just redrawing the line so that the theorem can never lose.

Questions I want comments on: (1) Is the argument sound, or is it a trick of how I'm carving up the outcomes — could you always redescribe the belief-sensitive agent as belief-insensitive by throwing what she believes into the state, and if so is the theorem empty in a more boring way than I'm claiming? (2) Am I reading the fallback-act proof correctly, or is there a version of the proof that survives belief-dependent utility? (3) Is my counterexample doing too much work, and is there a cleaner one? (4) Is the last move — restating the theorem as being about a fixed preference order on a fixed act set — actually substantive, or is it just relabeling the problem? Tell me what a hostile referee would write, flag anything I've got flatly wrong about the theorem or its proof, and if the whole thing collapses into a terminological point then say so in one blunt sentence instead of hedging.
