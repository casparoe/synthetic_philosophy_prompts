I'm teaching Quine's indeterminacy of translation in a phil of language seminar next month, and I want to screen a film alongside it instead of doing Arrival again (everyone's already written that paper). The film I want is Coppola's The Conversation, from 1974. Quick setup in case you need it: Harry Caul is a surveillance guy in San Francisco, he tapes a couple walking around Union Square, and then spends the whole movie filtering and remixing the recording to figure out what they said. The line he keeps coming back to is "He'd kill us if he got the chance." He takes it as a warning that the man who hired him wants the couple dead, and he gets obsessed with stopping it. But he's wrong -- the couple are the ones who do the killing, and the man who hired him is the one who ends up dead, written off as a car accident. The emphasis on us is the thing he missed.

What I want is an actual reading of the film against Quine, not a summary, and I want you to commit to a view. Here's what I can't settle. Quine's point in the gavagai case isn't just that the linguist lacks evidence for choosing between rabbit and rabbit stage and undetached rabbit part. It's stronger: there's no fact of the matter for the evidence to be missing, no ignorance to dispel, nothing that would make one manual correct and the other wrong. Harry, though, misheard a stress pattern that was really there. That's plain ambiguity, or at best underdetermination, and Quine of all people would say those are different animals -- he's happy to be a realist about physics while still holding that theory is underdetermined by all possible data; the indeterminacy he cares about is in meaning, not in the world. So does the film know the difference, or does it trade on the confusion? Because there's a real case for calling the movie Quinean anyway: the tape never gives up the right reading no matter how much Harry cleans it up, and the only thing that settles the question is the world -- a murder, a newspaper, a stopped-up drain full of blood. The meaning gets fixed from outside the evidence. Whether that's the most anti-Quinean thing in the film or the most interesting thing in it is exactly what I want you to decide. Same with the ending: he tears his apartment apart hunting for a bug he's certain is there and can't find, and the film leaves him with a certainty nothing backs. Same indeterminacy or a different one?

The medium should be part of this, I think. Murch's sound work keeps replaying the recording so we hear it the way Harry hears it, filtered and rebuilt, and we're stuck in his seat for most of the movie -- the audience is doing radical interpretation too, on the same thin evidence, and we only get rescued by the plot. Where is that a real move and where is it a cheat?

Then the part I care about most: what does the film add that Quine's example can't? His linguist is disinterested, or pretends to be. Harry insists he's just a recorder, not responsible for what clients do with what he hands them, and the movie is that stance collapsing in front of us. If meaning really is indeterminate, what's the status of the interpreter's guilt? And does the film show that nobody ever actually waits for the evidence to settle a meaning -- we act, and the acting is what makes it determinate? That would be a point argument alone doesn't make, and I'd like to see you defend it if you think it holds.

If you think the film is muddled about indeterminacy versus ambiguity, say so plainly; if you think it earns the Quinean reading anyway, tell me which scene earns it.
