Some background on where I'm coming from. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I think of the mind roughly the way I think of a distributed system: a collection of processes with partial access to each other's state. That picture is what generates my problem.

Here is the puzzle. For one agent to deceive another, two things seem necessary: the deceiver must know the truth, and the deceiver must intentionally produce a false belief in the deceived. Now suppose the deceiver and the deceived are the same agent. Then one and the same agent must both know the truth and fail to know it — must hold that P is false and also hold that P is true. That looks like a straightforward contradiction, not a psychological state anyone could be in. So I try the modular version: subsystem A knows that P is false and works to keep subsystem B from acquiring the evidence for that. But now the deceiver is A and the deceived is B, which is just two agents, one lying to the other; calling that self-deception seems like a category error, the same way it would be a category error to say that one database replica deceives another. So I end up with what looks like a dilemma: either self-deception is impossible — in which case everything we call by that name is really just ordinary error, or conscious pretense, or a plain inconsistency I would call a bug — or the term is a loose label for motivated error and adds nothing.

Three cases where I notice the problem in myself. I hold equity in a company I have decided is going to do well, and I have quietly stopped reading anything about the sector. I have a scan scheduled whose results I have not opened, and I have a vague sense that I will get to it. I am confident my partner is content with an arrangement we never discuss, and I do not raise it. In none of these can I point to a moment at which I knew the truth and then talked myself out of it, and in none of them can I point to a subsystem that is holding the truth while another one is kept in the dark. Yet they all feel like one thing rather than three.
