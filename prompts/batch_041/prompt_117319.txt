Hey — backend engineer here, no philosophy training at all, so apoligies if this turns out to be a thing with an obvious name that I just never ran into.

I keep noticing a pattern and I can't find a word for it. The shape of it: two parties that can't communicate and can't affect each other in any way — no channel between them, nothing either one does changes what the other does — still both do their half of a deal that only pays off if the other half gets done. Not because they agreed, not because anyone signalled. Because each one worked out what the other would do by assuming the other is the same kind of reasoner as itself, and then did its side. The two choices are good together, each is only good because of the other, but neither choice actually caused the other.

Two cases I've run into that made me notice it:

- Two engineering teams, totally seperate, no shared meetings, both shipped the same breaking change to a shared interface in the same week. Each one told me they did it because "the other team obviously would." I figured at the time it was just both of them having read the same design doc months earlier — i.e. a boring shared cause. But the reason they gave me wasn't the doc, it was the prediction.

- Two copies of the same model, started with no shared state and no way to message each other, both asked the same question, both give the answer that only works if the other copy also gives it. Each one is predicting the other by simulating itself.

Here's the part I can't name. In the first case I can point at a shared cause, in the second I really can't — the only thing linking the two parties is that they're alike. So: (a) is there an established name for this, and which one, because I suspect I'm about to mash two or three different things into a single word; (b) if it does have a name, where are the edges — does the pattern survive if the two parties share a source (same doc, same training run, same author), or is that a genuinely different and more boring phenomenon that I've heard called a focal point, or something like a correlated equilibrium, I honestly can't keep those two straight; and (c) if the version where the parties are linked only by being similar doesn't have a name, coin one and tell me what it rules out.

And please don't just hand me the label, that isn't what I'm after. I want to know what the link between the two parties has to be — is it enough that each can deduce the other's move, or does something have to actually make them both move — and which of those people mean when they use the term. Also: does the whole thing fall apart if one party is much smarter than the other, or if they aren't alike at all? Thanks.
