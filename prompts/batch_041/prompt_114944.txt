So I run a monthly "weird ideas" night at the local library. About 12–15 people turn up, ages all over, nobody with a philosophy or math degree, we've got one big table and about 90 minutes. I want to do the two-envelopes puzzle as a game the group actually plays, not me standing at the front explaining it while they nod. I've been chewing on this one by myself for like two months now and I'm pretty sure the way it's usually told is too tidy, and I want the activity to get people to the point where they feel the pull of the "you should always switch" argument in their gut, before anyone says the word "paradox."

Here's what I've got and where I need help. I pre-make matched pairs of envelopes — in each pair one holds twice what the other does. The small amounts come from a pool I keep secret, thinking pennies or candy so no real money: small values {2,4,8,16,32} and the big one doubles it, so pairs like (2,4) up to (32,64). I make a pile of these pairs, shuffle them, and that's my deck.

Round 1: each player takes one envelope from a fresh pair but does NOT open it, then has to choose keep or swap before seeing anything. I want them to notice swapping is basically a coin flip here, nothing to gain.

Round 2: now they open it and see the number, and only then get offered the swap. This is the round with the bite. I want to say the tempting argument out loud in plain words — whatever you're looking at, the other envelope is either double or half, and it feels like those are equally likely, so the other one's worth more on average, so obviously swap. Have everyone swap. Track what actually happens across many rounds. I want the gap between "the argument says swap every time" and "the results don't reward it" to land on them as a real itch.

Round 3, and this is the part I invented and I think is the interesting one: one player is the banker. The banker knows the amounts in every pair and gets to decide, after seeing which envelope the player already took, whether to offer the swap at all. Then I want a second version of round 3 where the banker is forced to offer the swap no matter what. I have a strong hunch the difference between those two versions is exactly where the whole thing falls apart, but I honestly can't say why, and that's the itch I want the game to give everyone.

Round 4: after they've swapped, offer the swap again. And again. Get them to notice that if the always-swap argument were good, they should happily keep swapping back and forth forever, and even pay a little each time for the privilege.

What I need from you: the full runnable thing — exact rules, materials list, how many rounds, how to keep score without real money, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, and roles for the banker rounds. Then a debrief that goes back over what happened and maps each moment onto what's actually going on with the puzzle — I want them to see the two different "probability spaces" people slide between without noticing, and why "equally likely to be double or half" quietly assumes something about the setup that maybe can't be true. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (someone will game the secret pool, someone will argue the math is just wrong, someone will refuse to play because it's pennies) and how I should handle each. And be honest with me about what the game can't do: my gut says that because my amounts come from a small known-ish pool, a sharp player can figure out the prior and kill the argument before it ever gets going, which would ruin the whole point — so tell me whether that's a real problem and how to keep the pull alive anyway, and which part of the real puzzle is just beyond what a tabletop game with a fixed deck can ever reproduce.

Last thing: tell me how confident you are in what you're telling me, and single out the one point you're least sure about — I'd rather know where you're shaky than get a smooth answer.
