I'm a novelist, and I've written myself into a decision I can't resolve on the page. A character is offered a wager settled by an endless procedure: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the amount won or lost is fixed by how long the run lasts, with the stakes rising as the run goes on and flipping sign with each toss. I sat down to decide whether my character should take it, and I found I could tally the possible outcomes in one order and get a plainly attractive wager, tally the same outcomes in a different order and get a plainly ruinous one, with nothing to tell me which order is the right one; if I define the wager's worth as a limit instead, the limit keeps changing depending on how I bracket the terms. This is not the old coin-toss puzzle as I understand it -- there the trouble is that the expected payoff is infinite, whereas here there is no single expected payoff at all, just a family of them that disagree. I suspect this is somewhere in decision theory's treatment of infinite or ill-defined expectations, but I want the specific thing if it has a name: what is it, precisely, how does it differ from the infinite-payoff puzzle and from the arguments that trade on a vanishingly small chance of an astronomically large payoff, and what is actually settled -- if anything -- about how a rational agent ought to choose when the value of an option refuses to be well-defined?
