I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a book where the same night gets told twice — same character, same carelessness, same intentions, only the outcome differs, fine in one version and fatal in the other — and I keep getting stuck on whether the two versions make her equally blameworthy. Before I commit to how the ending works I want to spend a month thinking hard about moral luck, and I dont want to spend it on the parts of the debate philosophers argue about but that wouldnt change a single sentence of the book. So: what are the live open questions here, and which two or three would you actually prioritise for someone in my position? Give me the ranking and defend it — which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for a non-philosopher in a month, which are overrated for my purposes, and what argument or evidence would move one up or down the list. I suspect the free-will stuff is a detour for me; tell me if I'm wrong. Two things I especially want a verdict on. First, does it matter that my reader sees both branches, or does that just relocate the luck out of the character and into the reader's judgment, so I've dissolved nothing? Second, is there a real difference between luck making it impossible to know whether someone deserved blame, and luck making the blameworthiness itself depend on how things turned out? The first feels usable to me as a novelist; the second feels like a metaphysical headache I can't cash out. And I'd rather have three questions that matter than a survey of twelve — no reading list, I'll find the texts myself, I just need to know where to point the month.
