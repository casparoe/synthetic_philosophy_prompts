I am putting together a short position piece for a graduate seminar on practical rationality, and before I circulate it I would like an audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. The subject is choice without preference: the donkey case, in which an animal equidistant between two identical bales of hay is said to starve. Here is where I have landed.

1. Where one available option is strictly better than every other, choosing anything else is irrational.

2. Otherwise rationality demands only that I not choose an option that some available alternative is better than. It never demands that I choose the best option as such, because in the cases I care about there need not be one.

3. Comparability outruns the trichotomy of better, worse and equal. Two options can be comparable while neither is better nor worse nor equal to the other; they are on a par. The small-improvement argument convinces me that such cases are parity rather than equality: if two options were genuinely equal, a slight improvement in one of them would make it better than the other, and in the cases I have in mind it plainly does not.

4. In ties and in parity cases alike, any option that is not worse than an available alternative is permissible, and the mechanism of selection is irrelevant to permissibility. A coin, a habit, or a taste for the left-hand bale will all do; a tie-breaker is permitted and never required.

5. The donkey is not irrational. Its two bales are on a par, so there is nothing it has most reason to do, and its starvation is a fact about the world rather than a defect in its reasoning.

6. An agent who keeps deliberating when further deliberation has no expected payoff is irrational. When deliberation is going nowhere, the agent has a reason to settle: a reason to choose, as distinct from a reason for either option.

7. When I deliberate well I find out what there is reason to do. Deciding is a response to reasons rather than a source of them.

8. In parity cases, though, it is the commitment itself that makes an option the thing to do. By settling on one, I give myself a reason to pursue it that I did not have before, and that is how the paralysis is broken.

Please tell me which of these pull against one another, which pairs only appear to, and what the cheapest repair in each case would be. I want the genuine tensions ranked by severity rather than collected in a heap, and for each one an account of what would have to give and what that costs me. Where a pair is in tension only apparently, name the distinction that dissolves it. I would rather surrender a commitment than discover later that I have been quietly helping myself to an assumption that does the work.
