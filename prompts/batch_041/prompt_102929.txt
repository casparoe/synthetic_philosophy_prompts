Hey, so I run our local rationalist reading group (we call it that, but honestly it's mostly people who met through an EA intro course and never left). Twelve-ish regulars, I've got a seminar room booked for a solid two hours, and next month it's my turn to run the session. I want to do the hinge of history hypothesis, but not the version everyone there has already absorbed — you know, the whole future is big, so our moment is pivotal, so longtermism thing that everyone can recite cold. It slides right off them. I want them to actually feel the force of it in the room, and then I want the floor to move a bit.

Here's what I think the standard telling skips, and what I want the activity built around. First: a hinge is leverage, not goodness. Everyone sells the hinge as the moment where we could make things go vastly well, but a hinge is sign-blind — it's just a point where how things go is enormously sensitive to what happens next, which means it amplifies your screw-ups exactly as hard as your wins. If you're anything short of confident about the sign of your own influence, a hinge makes boldness scarier, not obviously correct. Second: we can't all be the hinge. Every generation has people convinced theirs is the decisive one, and merely being alive at some moment gives you no evidence that it's special; the argument only goes through if you have some actual, independent reason to think this stretch is unusual. I want the game to make both of those land — and I want them to feel the pull of the naive argument first, genuinely, like oh god obviously we should be pouring everything into the far future, before the ground shifts.

So design the activity. Rules, materials, the payoff scheme (no real money, we're grad students), roles if you need them, a timeline that fits two hours with a break in it. It has to run as an actual game, and the point has to show up in the mechanics — in the incentives and the information structure — before anyone says the word hinge. Then the debrief, walking through what just happened and mapping it onto the real claims (the leverage, the sign asymmetry, the selection problem) and onto where the analogy breaks down.
