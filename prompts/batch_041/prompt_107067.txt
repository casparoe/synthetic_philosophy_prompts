I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been thinking about causation by omission for a while, and I keep running into two camps. Call them omission-inclusionists and omission-exclusionists. The inclusionists say omissions are genuine causes: the failure to water the plant caused its death, the failure to warn caused the injury. The exclusionists say omissions are not causes at all; they are absences, and causal talk about them is really about responsibility, norms, or counterfactual dependence. Please act as a professional philosopher of causation and draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. Start by brainstorming the cases both sides would agree need explaining: ordinary omissions, prevention, double prevention, and cases where a substitute would have produced the same outcome anyway. Then produce a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. In the agreed section, include what both sides accept about the metaphysics of absences, the role of counterfactuals, the difference between causing and merely allowing, and the relation between causal claims and moral or legal responsibility. In the table, state each remaining disagreement in neutral wording that each side would accept for its own position, identify the cruxes, and for each crux give a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please also flag any part of the dispute that is merely verbal. Use a case I call the two lifeguards: one lifeguard is asleep while the other is awake and chooses not to intervene; a swimmer drowns. I want to know whether the two camps can agree on what, if anything, each lifeguard's omission causes, and whether their disagreement survives once we separate causal status from causal selection. Think carefully and do not smooth over the residual disagreement.
