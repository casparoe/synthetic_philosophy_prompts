My friend and I have been meeting weekly to work through the foundations of quantum mechanics — we are both in a philosophy-of-physics reading group, he is the physicist and I am the philosopher — and our last three arguments have all ended the same way. He fills the whiteboard with a chain of reasoning, announces that it shows the celebrated Bell-type experiments have refuted something far more sweeping than anyone will admit, and I cannot find the loose brick. His argument runs as follows. (1) Bell's theorem, in its most general form, says that no theory can satisfy both of the following conditions and still reproduce the measured correlations: first, that the theory is locally causal — the outcome of a measurement on one particle is not influenced by events at spacelike separation from it, in particular neither by the setting nor by the outcome on the far side — and second, that the experimenters' settings are statistically independent of whatever state the pair was in beforehand. (2) The measured correlations exceed the bound of two that any such theory must respect, and climb toward twice the square root of two, which is the largest value quantum mechanics allows. (3) So at least one of the two conditions fails of the actual world. (4) Nobody seriously doubts that the experimenters' settings are free, so the casualty must be local causality. (5) But local causality simply is the prohibition on faster-than-light influence — that is the exact content of special relativity — so the experiments have refuted special relativity, and there really are influences that cross spacelike intervals. (6) Yet no one believes the experiments refuted special relativity, and it is a theorem that the correlations cannot be used to transmit a message, so something above (6) must be broken.

When I protest that quantum mechanics is provably non-signalling, so that no message can be sent, he calls this a verbal dodge: if nothing is transmitted, he says, then nothing is nonlocal, and the entire Bell industry is a confusion. When I protest that he has refuted only hidden-variable theories, he replies that the derivation in (1) never mentioned hidden variables, only those two conditions. His standing challenge to me is to name the thing that is supposed to be nonlocal and to explain how it does its work without travelling.

I would rather keep special relativity than almost anything else in physics, and I would rather not hand the field over to the superdeterminists either. Tell me which numbered step to reject, why it looked so innocent when it was written down, and what the alternatives cost. If I hold on to special relativity, what exactly am I conceding about the world, and if instead I insist on both conditions in (1), what does that commitment buy me and what does it cost?
