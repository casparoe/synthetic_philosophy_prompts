Write out the desiderata that any adequate theory of taste must satisfy — that is, the full specification of what an account of taste has to deliver before it can claim to tell us when a judgment of beauty or artistic merit is correct. Assume the standard of adequacy is set by a use: the theory has to be good enough to guide a prize jury that must defend its verdict to the losers, to the disappointed public, and to itself, while collapsing neither into a poll of preferences nor into a claim to detect beauty the way one detects a chemical property. Take Hume's essay on the standard of taste as the historical reference point, but treat it as one candidate among others rather than as the frame to be filled in.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say plainly whether it is non-negotiable or merely a prejudice of one camp. At minimum, I want requirements covering: the antinomy that judgments of taste are voiced as personal reports yet spoken with a claim to general assent; faultless disagreement and whether a standard can survive it; the possibility of a corrupt, immature, or merely untrained taste, and what makes that charge non-circular; the asymmetry between experiencing a work and taking someone's word for it; the superiority of the connoisseur, and whether it can be earned without smuggling in the very standard under construction; the case of two ideal critics who diverge despite equal training, attention, and freedom from prejudice; and the possibility that a whole community's taste is bad, together with what "bad" could mean there.

Then inject three cases of my own stipulation and say what each demands of the theory: (1) two perceivers with identical training, attention, and knowledge whose perceptual systems are wired differently, so that the same work is sweet to one and grating to the other and neither can be brought to the other's experience; (2) a genuinely new art form that no existing standard anticipated, and whether accommodating it is principled or ad hoc; (3) a jury that cannot resolve its disagreement, and what the standard tells it to do — defer to the most experienced, aggregate, or suspend.

Flag any pair of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the main contenders, and close with a brief pass over three families — Hume's ideal critic, a Kantian universal-subjectivity line, and a dispositional or expressivist account — judged only against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring is secondary, and no citations or reading lists are wanted.
