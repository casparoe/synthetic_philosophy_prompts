I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, though my background is in psych — I did a masters in behavioural genetics before switching over, and I still TA the methods sequence for the psych department, which is how I ended up in this dumb argument at a lab lunch. Someone mentioned that the heritability of some trait or other is 0.6, and I said fine, in that sample, under those conditions, and then spent twenty minutes failing to say clearly what else you're entitled to infer from it. So I want to think this through properly, and I'd rather do it by asking what the practice presupposes than by reheating the standard caveats.

Here's the practice, as concretely as I can put it. You run a twin or family design, you decompose the phenotypic variance into A, C and E (or some fancier version), you get a number, and you report it as the heritability of the trait — of the trait, note, not of the trait in this cohort at this moment. To get there the design has to assume that genetic and environmental influences are uncorrelated and additive, no rGE, no GxE, and that whatever is left once the genetic terms are accounted for is environment, even though that residual is also measurement error and plain noise. Then the number gets used in two directions that seem to pull apart. In animal and plant breeding a high heritability is a licence to select on, and a low one is a reason to change the rearing conditions instead; it reads like a budget split, and a high number really does mean that fiddling around within the current range of environments buys you very little. In human work the same number gets reported and then imediately hedged — heritability tells you nothing about malleability, nothing about any individual, nothing about what would happen under an intervention. And the awkward empirical bit is that these estimates move around: height comes out more heritable in rich countries than poor ones, which sounds backwards if the number is tracking how biological a trait is, and estimates for the same trait drift across cohorts and ages.

What I want from you is the implicit theory the practice commits you to, not a recitation of the caveats I already know. What picture of causation, and of populations, does reporting h² as a property of a trait presuppose — is it population-relativity all the way down, or is there a defensible reading on which the number is genuinely about the trait? Do the breeding use and the human use actually presuppose the same thing, or is one of them quietly leaning on an assumption (environments held fixed, the environmental range known) that the other denies — are they inconsistent, in other words, or just answering seperate questions with the same statistic? And what does treating the residual as environment presuppose about environments — that they are a bag of independent influences, that they are exogenous, that they are causes in the same sense the genes are? Sort it into what the practice strictly needs, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined, so that several rival theories of causation would license the exact same tables. If your verdict is that the practice is incoherent, be specific about which commitment has to go and what the field would have to stop doing on Monday morning. Also tell me where you think you're most likely to be wrong, and what would actually change your mind: if there's a reading of the breeding case, or of the cross-cohort variation, on which the two uses come out consistent after all, I want to hear it, because thats the bit I'd bet against.
