I teach a course on the problem of evil, and I want to rescue Leibniz from the caricature my students arrive with, so I plan to pair him with The Good Place: the sitcom whose characters are sorted after death into a neighborhood that its first season reveals to be a purpose-built torture chamber, whose middle seasons show its architect rebooting that neighborhood over and over in search of a version that finally works, and whose finale has the four leads redesign the whole afterlife and install a door through which the eternally happy may at last choose to end. Read the show's entire arc as a commentary on the best of all possible worlds.

Take the doctrine seriously on its own terms rather than in Voltaire's version. Leibniz does not claim that this world contains no evil, or that each part of it is as good as it could be. He claims that God, surveying infinitely many possible worlds, creates the best; that a world is a maximal set of compossible things, so an improvement is not always available, since a good thing can fail to fit together with the goods already in the set; that perfection is measured by richness of variety under order rather than by pleasure; that suffering and sin may belong to the best whole, evil being understood as privation rather than as something God positively makes; and that God's choice, though free, is morally necessitated, because a perfect being cannot fail to choose the best. What is chosen, moreover, is a whole history rather than a stage of one, and Leibniz pictures the universe as a well-governed city of minds — the nearest thing in him to the show's afterlife bureaucracy.

With that in hand: where does the show dramatize Leibniz rather than mock him — in an architect who searches and reboots, where Leibniz's God simply sees; in a points system that has admitted almost no one for centuries because actions are so entangled that no deed can be scored in isolation; in the suggestion that the four principals become decent only through long engineered misery, which is the old idea of a fortunate fall staged for laughs? Where does it break with him, above all in treating the afterlife as something that can be amended, and exited, which a Leibnizian God would have no reason to leave open? Where does the show refuse the reading, and what does it add that the treatises cannot — does the problem of evil look different when it is administered rather than merely permitted?

Then commit. My own suspicion is that the show is not the refutation it looks like: if the best world is a whole history, then a history containing the discovery that optimism is false, and a system that reforms itself into something kinder, may still be the best history available, so the show would be staging Leibniz's hardest claim rather than overturning it. Tell me whether that holds or whether it is a piece of framing no real Leibnizian can use, and say plainly where the reading distorts the doctrine or the show. I want a verdict with reasons, not a survey of positions, and I want you to mark which steps depend on a contested reading of Leibniz. No citations or further reading, please; I know the texts.
