I'm building a little exercise on Good's theorem — the 1967 result that a Bayesian who can get free information before she acts can never be worse off in expectation, because the max of an average is at most the average of the maxes, so looking first is worth at least as much as choosing now. I want to see whether a model can tell the real counterexamples from the cheap ones.

Here are five cases. Rate each one from 0 to 1 for how much it's a genuine problem for the theorem as stated, where 0 means the theorem is untouched and the person is just being irrational or is paying for the info, and 1 means the theorem has to be re-stated rather than just applied more carefully. One sentence each, saying which assumption of the theorem the case stresses. Then give me a single ranking from weakest to strongest, tell me which one you'd lead with, and hand me one more case I left out. If two of my cases turn out to be the same failure mode, say so and merge them rather than pretending there's two of them.

1. She's about to start a whodunit and could look up the ending first. She'd rather not: once she knows, the book stops being any fun.

2. An investor who reasons in terms of a whole range of probability models and plans against the worst one declines a free forecast, on the grounds that a sharper forecast can only make her worst case worse.

3. A psychologist has already put $1 in whichever of two boxes she predicted you would pick and $0 in the other one. I know which box it's in, and I offer to tell you for free. You'd rather I didn't, because if you know you'll take that box, and you're only 90% sure I'm right about it, whereas picking blind you're confident of getting the dollar.

4. A man turns down a free screening test that he could actually act on, because he knows himself: whatever the result comes back as, he'll panic and make worse decisions about everything else for a year.

5. A gambler is offered a free look at how the coin landed before he has to bet on it, and says no thanks.
