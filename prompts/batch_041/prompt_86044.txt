I'm a postdoc in cognitive science and philosophy, and next semester I'm convening a small interdisciplinary working group — six people, a mix of philosophy graduate students, two computational neuroscientists, and one person from the education school — that meets for two hours a week for twelve weeks. The theme we've settled on is Dennett's hierarchy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures: the idea that minds come in grades, from organisms whose behavior is largely fixed by natural selection, to those that learn by reinforcement and trial and error, to those that can simulate possible actions internally and let hypotheses die in their stead, to those whose cognition is transformed by thinking tools acquired from culture. I want the group to produce something more useful than a rehash of the standard summary, and I'd like your help setting the agenda.

Here are the candidate questions I've been turning over, some of which came from the group and some of which I've invented to provoke discussion. First, is the hierarchy a ladder of degrees or a set of separable capacities — can something be Popperian without being Skinnerian, or Gregorian without being Popperian? Second, what exactly does the Gregorian grade add: is it the possession of thinking tools, the capacity to acquire them from others, or the ability to re-engineer one's own cognitive niche? Third, does the framework need a fifth grade for systems whose cognitive tools are partly implemented outside the skull — sometimes floated as a Turingian or extended grade — or is that just a relabeling of the Gregorian grade? Fourth, does the Skinnerian/Popperian distinction survive contact with modern model-based reinforcement learning, where agents learn world models and plan, or does it collapse into a difference of degree? Fifth, if humans really are Gregorian, what follows for education — is the installation of thinking tools a testable claim or a metaphor? Sixth, is the whole hierarchy better treated as a pedagogical scaffold than a research program, and if so, what would a genuinely empirical version look like?

Please give me a defended agenda: which two or three of these, or others I haven't listed, the group should prioritize, in what order, and why. I care more about the reasoning than the list. For each, say what makes it upstream of other questions, how tractable it is for a group with our composition and time, how neglected it is, and what would move it up or down the ranking by the end of the semester. Also tell me which of the candidates are probably verbal disputes about how to use the labels rather than substantive disagreements about the world, and which are substantive. And if you think the framing is misguided — if the four grades are better treated as a pedagogical device than a research program — say so and explain what you'd replace it with.
