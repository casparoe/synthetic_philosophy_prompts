**The Half-Life of a Posit: A Rate-Based Reformulation of the Pessimistic Induction**

The familiar pessimistic induction runs: many theories once counted empirically successful — phlogiston chemistry, caloric thermodynamics, the luminiferous ether, Newtonian mechanics — have since been abandoned as false; therefore our current successful theories are probably false as well, or at least their central ontological claims are. The realist replies by narrowing what must be retained: only the parts of a theory that were doing the predictive work need to be believed, and those have a better survival record than the theories taken as wholes.

I want to suggest that both sides have been arguing about the wrong quantity. The interesting fact is not how many theories have died but how fast particular posits die, and whether that rate is stable. Reformulate as follows. Treat the history of science as a population of posits — theoretical entities, properties, structures — each introduced at a date and each either still in use or discarded. Define a posit's lifetime as the interval between its introduction and the date at which it stops being invoked in the relevant mature literature, as distinct from the later date at which a textbook drops it. If lifetimes are modelled as a survival process, the pessimistic induction becomes a claim about the shape of the hazard function: it asserts that the hazard of abandonment is roughly constant or increasing, so that a posit's having survived so far is no evidence that it will survive longer. The realist's narrowing reply is, in this vocabulary, a claim that the hazard is strongly heterogeneous — that posits introduced to discharge a specific predictive obligation face a much lower hazard than posits introduced as background interpretation — so that the population's average death rate is driven by a subpopulation the realist never committed to.

This reformulation is meant to do three things. First, it makes the disagreement empirical in a way that theory-counting is not: we are asked for lifetimes, not for verdicts about whole theories, and lifetimes can in principle be read off the record. Second, it exposes a defect in the usual exchange. Both sides have counted theories, and a theory is the wrong unit, because a theory's death is nearly always a partial death; the unit that survives or dies is the posit. Counting theories therefore understates realism's survival rate while overstating the drama of each episode.

Third, and this is the part I am least sure of, it suggests a realist argument that does not depend on any particular case study. If the hazard of abandonment is heterogeneous, and if the selection of posits into mature science has become more discriminating over time — that is, if the discipline has become better at admitting a posit only when it is constrained by several independent measurements — then the hazard facing any given contemporary posit may be falling even though the hazard facing posits drawn indiscriminately from the record has been high. On this picture the historical record is a biased sample of the hazard, because it is dominated by episodes from a period when posits were cheap to introduce. The meta-induction is then not so much refuted as shown to be drawing on the wrong stratum.

Objections I can already see. (i) Lifetime is not measurable: a posit can be quietly retained in one subfield and dropped in another, so a date of death is a convention, and a convention can be chosen to make the hazard fall. (ii) Heterogeneity is not enough, since the realist needs to identify the low-hazard posits in advance, and the only criterion on offer — discharging a predictive obligation — is applied retrospectively, after we already know what survived. (iii) The claim that the hazard is falling is a sociological prediction about the institution of science rather than a fact about the world, and if it fails the realist is worse off than before, having staked the position on a contingent trend.
