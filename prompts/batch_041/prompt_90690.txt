I run a monthly reading group for graduate students and advanced undergraduates in philosophy, and we open every session with a short joke written for the group — the kind that rewards the people who actually did the reading rather than just waving at the topic. The next session is on the paradox of deontology, and I'd like you to write that joke. The group already knows the material: that deontological constraints forbid particular acts (killing, lying, using a person merely as a means), that these constraints are widely taken to be non-aggregative — a constraint seems to forbid you from violating it even when violating it once would prevent several further violations of the very same constraint, which is what gives the paradox its bite — and that the standard defense treats constraints as agent-relative, so that they needn't be justified by the agent-neutral good their observance produces. I want a joke whose punchline turns on this actual structure rather than on the word "deontology" or on generic jokes about philosophers being impractical.

To keep it from being something everyone has heard a hundred times, please build it around a premise I'd like you to treat as serious: a firm that performs forbidden acts on behalf of clients who want the good outcomes of those acts without dirtying their own hands — a business, so to speak, of outsourcing constraint violations. The wrinkle I want you to exploit is that the firm is founded and staffed by convinced deontologists, who reason that because constraints attach to the agent who acts rather than to the outcome that results, their hands stay clean so long as someone else does the deed. You can deliver this as a short dialogue, a scenario that lands on a punchline, or a one-liner with a real setup; keep it under about two hundred words, and make sure that someone who genuinely understands the paradox would laugh at the right place and that someone who doesn't would at least be able to see why the laughing people are laughing.

Because the group will interrogate it afterward, close with a brief note in plain prose on where the joke takes liberties or leans on one side of a live disagreement — for example, whether constraints really are non-aggregative in the way the premise assumes, or whether the outsourcing idea is even coherent if using someone as an instrument is itself forbidden. Be explicit about which of these points are settled among people who work on deontology and which are genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as though it were the consensus. Please write in American English.
