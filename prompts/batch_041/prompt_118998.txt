I run a small interview podcast about biology and society — mostly I talk to geneticists and behavioral scientists and try to get them to say out loud what their statistics actually license. Next week's guest is a philosopher of biology who's just published a book arguing that basically everyone — researchers, journalists, pundits — has been running together two different quantities. The first is heritability in the ordinary sense: the share of the variance in a trait, within one population, at one time, across one range of environments, that's statistically associated with genetic differences among the people in it. The second is what he calls "portability": how much of that trait difference would survive if you changed the environment in specified ways — a new school system, cleaner air, a different diet, whatever. His claim is that almost every heated public dispute about genes and traits is really a dispute about portability, that heritability estimates are close to silent about portability, and that the two get swapped for each other in both directions (hereditarians sliding from high heritability to "you can't change it," critics sliding from environmental malleability to "so genes don't matter"). I think the book is definately right about a lot and slippery in places, and I want the interview to actually push on it rather than let him restate the thesis for forty minutes.

You're a philosopher of biology who knows this literature cold and has refereed in this area. Before you write anything, spend a moment brainstorming where the standard objections to a heritability/portability split come from and what the guest's most likely comebacks are, then draft me 9 to 11 interview questions. I want at least one question on each of these: (1) what a high heritability estimate actually tells you, if anything, about what interventions could or couldn't work — and whether "heritability says nothing about malleability" is itself an overstatement in the other direction; (2) how portability is supposed to be measured at all, given that it seems to require specifying a range of environmental changes in advance, which is usually the very thing in dispute; (3) gene-environment correlation — parents hand on environments along with genes, so how does the split survive the fact that environments are themselves partly heritable; (4) within-group versus between-group comparison, i.e. what the framework says when a trait is highly heritable inside each of two groups but differs between them, and whether his vocabulary defuses that comparison or quietly licenses it; and (5) whether these estimates are even stable enough to be worth reporting, since they seem to shift with things like socioeconomic conditions and across cohorts. For each question, add a follow-up aimed at the answer he's most likely to give (including the answers that would be a dodge), and finish with one closing question that's hard to answer with "that's a research program" or "that's a different question."

Keep the tone like a curious but informed host, not a hostile cross-examiner, and don't ask for reading lists or citations — I cant put those on air anyway, and I'd rather the questions stand on the ideas themselves. No softball origin-story questions either. If a question leans on a technical idea, add a half-sentence in parentheses the host can use to set it up for listeners who don't know the term.
