Act as a scholar of psychoanalysis and of the philosophy of religion who knows Freud's writings on religion closely and is careful to distinguish what he actually argued from what is commonly attributed to him.

I have been reading a popular book on secularization that offers this compressed account of Freud's view. "Religion answers three needs: it exorcises the terrors of nature, reconciles us to the cruelty of fate and the certainty of death, and compensates us for the renunciations civilization demands. Its psychological origin lies in infantile helplessness and the longing for a protective father, so that it is best understood as a childhood neurosis of humanity — an obsessional neurosis of a single patient writ large. Its deepest root, however, is the oceanic feeling, the sense of an unbounded connection with the universe, which Freud took to be the kernel of all religious experience. Since he held that the psychological origin of a belief settles the question of its truth, his genealogy of religion amounts to a refutation of it; and because he expected the growth of scientific knowledge to dispel the illusion, he predicted that religion would simply disappear as education advanced."

The author is a competent popularizer, so I am inclined to treat the passage as broadly reliable, but I want to know precisely where it is not. Work through it claim by claim. For each claim, say what Freud's own text supports, which parts are the sort of simplification that circulates in the secondary literature, and which claims he rejected or pointedly declined to make. Then tell me whether the closing inference — that Freud's genealogy of religion is itself a refutation of religion — survives once the inaccuracies are corrected, and if it does not, which correction does the work. Citations and page references are unnecessary; where a verdict depends on an interpretive judgment you cannot check, flag it as such. Think the matter through before you answer, and finish by saying what would change your mind: which piece of textual evidence, or which philosophical consideration, would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
