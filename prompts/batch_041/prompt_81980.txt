A couple of nights ago I was at dinner with two friends of mine, Mira and Toby, and we ended up spending alot of the meal on a decision problem I'd brought up from a course, and I still can't tell wether Mira or Toby was winning the arguement. I mostly just wrote things on the napkin and stayed out of it. The problem is this. Choice one: (R) a million dollars for certain, or (S) an 89% chance of a million, a 10% chance of five million, and a 1% chance of nothing. Choice two: (T) an 11% chance of a million and an 89% chance of nothing, or (U) a 10% chance of five million and a 90% chance of nothing. Nearly everyone picks R and then U, and Mira says that combination is simply incoherent, because R and S both give you a million in an 89% branch, T and U both give you nothing in an 89% branch, and the leftover 11% slivers are the same sliver in both pairs -- so whatever you think of that sliver, the 89% part is supposed to drop out of the comparison entirely. She called this the independence axiom and said a person who picks R and U isn't maximising anything, they're just hypnotised by the word certain, and that anyone who controls how the options are dressed up can take money off someone like that. Toby said that gets it backwards: the axiom isn't a law of thought, it's a modelling assumption, and someone who dislikes the 1% chance of walking away with nothing enough to pay for the guarantee has perfectly coherent preferences -- you can write them down as a ranking of lotteries, just not one that's linear in probability -- and you can't money-pump a person who's merely non-linear in probability; the pumps he knows about need a cycle or a dominance violation to get going.

Mira then tried to unfold the thing in time: imagine a machine that first decides, 89/11, whether you're in the world where you get the million regardless, or the world where you're then asked to choose between a million for sure and a ten-in-eleven shot at five million. Same two problems, just staged. She said if your answer to that second question is the same in both worlds, you can't have R over S *and* U over T, so you must be answering the same question differently depending on what's already banked -- which means your preference isn't between what you'll actually get, it's between descriptions of what you'll get. Toby said she's assuming the background can't matter, which is the very thing in dispute, and that a person is allowed to have preferences that don't decompose branch by branch; the whole shape of the distribution can matter, and calling that a mistake is just restating the axiom. So Mira changed the numbers on him: an 80% shot at four thousand against three thousand for sure, and then a 20% shot at four thousand against a 25% shot at three thousand, where people take the sure three thousand in the first pair and the 20% shot in the second. There's no certainty anywhere in that second pair, so whatever's going on can't just be about certainty. Toby said fine -- it's about probabilities getting weighted rather than used straight, some function of p instead of p -- and that this is a theory of risk attitude, not an error. Mira said that's a name for the pattern, not an explanation of it. We also ran the original pair on the other two people at the table with the amounts knocked down to a dollar and five dollars, and two of them still flipped, which Toby says shows it isn't about the million being life-changing and Mira says shows it isn't about the money at all.
