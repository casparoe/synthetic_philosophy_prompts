I'm a fourth-year PhD student in logic and philosophy of mathematics, and I've been handed the job of writing the take-home final for the graduate seminar I've been TAing on the philosophical significance of incompleteness. The students can all state Gödel's second incompleteness theorem and explain why it blocks a finitary consistency proof for arithmetic, and I'm tired of reading essays that do nothing else; a session we ran on the possibility that the axioms of arithmetic are themselves inconsistent produced far more heat than light, and I'd like the final to force candidates to actually think rather than recite. Please write me one essay question whose crux is that possibility — take the object theory to be PA, or PA plus whatever set-theoretic backing a candidate prefers, and let them choose — designed so that a candidate who only knows the standard theorems flounders while a candidate who reasons carefully about the situation does well.

To keep it off the well-worn track, anchor the question to a small packet of distinctions that we circulated in the seminar, stated inside the question itself so that no outside reading is required and so that the model answer never has to cite anyone. There are five: (i) the difference between an inconsistency that is *witnessed* — some finite derivation of a contradiction exists and could in principle be exhibited — and one that is merely *latent*, whose shortest witness might be so long that no human or machine could ever write it down; (ii) the difference between our confidence in the consistency of arithmetic being a theorem of some stronger theory and its being an inductive or empirical hypothesis supported only by the fact that centuries of deriving consequences have not yet produced a collision; (iii) the point that an inconsistent theory proves everything, so a discovered contradiction would not merely add one strange theorem but trivialize the whole system, and what that implies about how a rational user of the system should react to the mere possibility; (iv) the difference between the axioms being inconsistent and our reasoning being unsound — a defect in the underlying logic, or in the metatheory we use to argue about the object theory; and (v) the self-referential twist that if arithmetic is inconsistent then, because the claim that a contradiction is derivable is a simple existence claim about a finite object and such claims are provable in the system when true, the system itself proves that it is inconsistent — so the familiar escalation of moving to the theory plus its consistency axiom is futile, since in that scenario the theory already refutes its own consistency statement.

The question should require a candidate to say precisely what would have to be the case for the inconsistency to be a live epistemic possibility rather than a logical curiosity; to assess the inductive case for consistency honestly, asking how much the absence of a collision so far really supports; and to adjudicate whether the unruled-out possibility of inconsistency undermines mathematical knowledge at all, or whether the worry is either incoherent — the metatheory used to raise it is presumably at least as strong as the theory it is directed at — or on a par with unruled-out doubts in every other domain. Act as an examiner with genuine expertise in mathematical logic and philosophy of mathematics: start by listing the distinct technical sub-questions the prompt raises, work through the technical points carefully before committing to any of them, and don't paper over anything with a vague gesture at the limits Gödel supposedly revealed. Give me the essay question, a model answer of no more than 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points marked out of 20, each with its mark value, a one-line statement of what a top answer says on that point, and a one-line note on the mistake candidates typically make there. Keep the whole response under 1600 words, and include no references, citations, or suggested readings — the exam is closed-book and I don't want candidates to think a bibliography was expected.
