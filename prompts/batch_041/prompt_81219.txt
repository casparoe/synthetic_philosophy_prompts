I am a novelist by trade, and for the last three years I have also taught a graduate seminar in our MFA programme, informally titled Machines That Mind, in which fiction writers attempt to render the interior lives of entities whose interiors we cannot inspect. Setting the final examination is the part of the job I am worst at. My students arrive equipped with a stock of phrases about artificial suffering absorbed from journalism and from the safety literature, and they can produce two thousand fluent words without once coming near a question that has an answer.

This year's essay question is to be about reinforcement learning agents and the proposition that the reward signal is, or could be, a valence signal — the thing that stands to such an agent as pleasure and pain stand to us. I have roughed out the intellectual terrain, and I want you to turn it into an examination.

The terrain is this. There are at least three readings of the claim. On the design reading, reward is nothing but the training signal: a scalar that shapes a policy, with no valence anywhere in the picture. On the functional reading, a state counts as having valence if it plays the right role — it is aversive, it drives avoidance learning, it competes with other objectives and can be traded off against them — and nothing further is required. On the phenomenal reading, no arrangement of functions suffices; there must be something it is like to be the agent. The crux I want examined is a case I have been unable to resolve myself: an agent trained with negative reward for entering certain states, whose reward channel has since been severed and whose weights are frozen, and which nonetheless continues to avoid those states. Nothing in it is being optimised any longer. Which of the three readings calls this suffering, and does the verdict depend on a fact about the agent or on which concept we have chosen to apply?

Two further complications should be in play. First, what drives learning is the reward prediction error rather than the reward itself, so that a fully anticipated pain teaches nothing and a relief registers as a positive error; students should say whether a valence signal constituted by a difference, whose sign depends on what was expected, can still bear the name of feeling. Second, affective scientists distinguish liking a state from wanting it, and show that the two can come apart; the reward signal in a reinforcement learning agent resembles wanting far more than it resembles liking. Students should decide whether that distinction does any work or merely relabels the difficulty. The obvious objection that a scalar cannot represent the qualitative character of experience — burning as against aching as against grief — should also be addressed rather than waved at.

Please write the question itself, specifying ninety minutes, roughly nine hundred words, and twenty-five marks. Then write a model answer of about eight hundred words. Then a marking scheme built on must-hit points, each with its mark allocation and a one-line note on how candidates typically lose it. Close the model answer with a short section, no more than a hundred and fifty words, headed What would change my mind, naming the specific argument or consideration that would move the verdict and identifying where the reasoning is most exposed to failure. My students cannot look anything up during the examination, so do not let the model answer lean on a citation or a named authority as though it settled the matter; if a distinction is needed, state it and let it stand on its own.
