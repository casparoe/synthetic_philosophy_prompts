I have spent the last several months serving as an external reviewer for a research funding panel, and the experience has left me with a discomfort I have not been able to shake. More than once I found myself reading a proposal whose central claim I took to be false, and I noticed that I was scrutinising its methods with a severity I bring to nothing else, hunting for the flaw that would let me set the whole thing aside. What bothered me was not the disagreement but the sense of entitlement that came with it: because I already took the conclusion to be wrong, any evidence offered for it could be written off as artefact, and the more certain I felt, the less work I had to do.

When I mentioned this to a friend who teaches philosophy, she told me the structure has a name, the dogmatism paradox, and gave me a rough statement of it. I have since tried to write it up properly, partly to see whether the puzzle is as serious as it sounds and partly to work out what, if anything, it says about my behaviour on the panel. I would like your comments on the draft below. I am not after reading suggestions; I want to know whether the argument holds and whether it is worth pursuing.

The draft.

1. The paradox as I understand it. Suppose someone knows that P. Then P is true, so any evidence that appears to tell against P is misleading evidence. But there is no reason to revise a belief in the light of evidence that misrepresents how things are; a rational person responds to evidence about the world, and misleading evidence is not evidence about the world. So anyone who knows P is entitled to disregard whatever appears to count against P. Two features of this strike me as important. First, the entitlement is not something the knower has to earn case by case; it follows from the knowledge alone. Second, it scales, so the more one knows, the wider the range of evidence one may ignore, and the position of the expert turns out to be the position of least constraint.

2. The reply I keep meeting, and why I am not satisfied by it. The standard response is that the paradox confuses knowing something with knowing that one knows it. To use the premise that my evidence is misleading, I would have to know that I know P, and that is a further and much harder thing. So the paradox never gets off the ground as a piece of practical advice. I accept the point about the premise, but I do not think it disposes of the puzzle. It shows that the dogmatist cannot state why he is entitled to ignore the evidence; it does not show that ignoring it is a mistake. If he does know P, then his belief is true and will survive the misleading evidence, so ignoring it costs him nothing in accuracy. The reply seems to convert a paradox about closed-mindedness into a paradox about self-knowledge, which is a different and less worrying topic.

3. What I want to try. The paradox moves, I think, from a claim about a belief to a claim about a policy. That this particular belief is knowledge is one thing; that a policy of not attending to counter-evidence is justified is another. A policy has to be assessed by what it does across cases, not by what happens in the case in front of you, and a policy of disregarding apparent counter-evidence is a bad policy even when, on this occasion, the evidence is misleading, because one cannot tell in advance which occasions those are, and because the disposition to ignore is precisely what stops a person, or a community, from ever finding out. So the knower may be right about his belief and wrong about his policy.

4. The objection to this that I cannot answer. It looks as though the policy reply simply relocates the problem. If I know P, I know that in this case the evidence is misleading, and if I know that, then my policy of ignoring this evidence is not a general policy at all but a correct response to the facts. Say instead that I cannot know which case this is, and I am back at the know-that-you-know point. So either my distinction does no work, or it is the standard reply in different clothes.

5. A comparison I have found useful, and do not fully trust. The law has a doctrine of finality: once judgment is entered, the losing party may not reopen the matter by producing fresh evidence, however good. The justification is not that the judgment is correct but that a system of adjudication that never stopped would fail at the thing it is for. What interests me is that the law buys closure with an explicit convention, a rule adopted for reasons, revisable, with exceptions carved out. The dogmatism paradox appears to deliver the same closure without any convention at all, simply as a consequence of knowing. If that is right, then what we object to in a dogmatist may not be that he holds a false belief but that he has exempted himself from a procedure the rest of us are still inside.

6. Where this leaves me. My tentative conclusion is that the paradox is real but shallow as a paradox and interesting as a diagnosis: it marks the point where knowledge, which is a fact about a person's relation to the world, is misused as a deliberative entitlement. If that is right, the remedy is not better epistemology for individuals but institutions, review, replication, adversarial procedure, that keep the question open for people who are sure. I would like to know whether that is a real conclusion or a way of changing the subject.

The comments I would find most useful are these: whether the reconstruction in 1 is fair, or whether I have made the dogmatist too easy to refute; whether the belief-and-policy distinction in 3 survives the objection in 4; and whether the comparison in 5 earns its keep or imports something, a convention, a warrant, a shared procedure, that knowledge does not have. If the whole line of thought is uninteresting, I would rather hear that than be told it is promising.
