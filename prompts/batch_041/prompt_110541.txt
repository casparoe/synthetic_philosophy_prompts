I am in a reading group that has spent the last few months on the claim that culture evolves by natural selection and that cultural items — tunes, slogans, fashions, religious practices, turns of phrase — are replicators in their own right, the cultural counterparts of genes. The discussion keeps collapsing into the same dispute, and I would like your help in settling it. One member of the group, who has a background in population genetics, insists that the gene analogy is not a loose metaphor but a licence: because culture is a Darwinian process, he says, we may import the apparatus of population genetics wholesale. On his view there is no difference in kind between an allele spreading through a gene pool and a belief spreading through a population; both are replicators whose frequencies are governed by selection, drift, and the rest, and the fitness of a cultural item consists simply in its propensity to get itself copied. He then draws a substantive conclusion that the rest of us find suspicious — roughly, that we should stop asking whether a widely held idea is true or good, since its prevalence is to be explained by its merits as a replicator rather than by any merit it has as an idea, and that cultural success is therefore no evidence whatsoever of value. I want to know how far the analogy actually holds and, more pointedly, whether it can carry that conclusion.

Lay the correspondence out explicitly — a table would be welcome — saying what is supposed to answer to the gene, the allele, the phenotype, the mutation, the generation, the fitness landscape, and the population, and then tell me where the mapping fails. I am especially interested in the following candidate disanalogies, though I would like you to judge which of them genuinely matter for my friend's argument rather than merely listing them: that cultural items are not faithfully copied but reconstructed by each new host, so that transmission is lossy and systematically biased toward certain forms rather than replicative; that culture supplies no clean units of the kind genes supply, so that the very counting of copies may be indeterminate; that cultural inheritance is in a sense Lamarckian, in that whatever one generation acquires is directly available to the next, which has no genetic parallel; that cultural transmission runs largely sideways and in tangled lines rather than straight down, so that cultural lineages do not form a tree; and that a great deal of cultural change is guided by the intentions and judgements of the people doing the transmitting, rather than blind. Tell me which of these, if any, are fatal to the claim that culture is a Darwinian process, and which merely complicate it.

Finally, I would like you to separate two claims that my friend runs together: first, that a selectionist account of culture is possible, and second, that selection explains why the items that do spread are the ones that spread. If the analogy cannot support the second claim, say what it can support, and propose a comparison that would do the work better — whether something like the spread of a disease through a population, or the way a language drifts over centuries, or something else altogether. Keep the answer concrete by tying it to a case or two, for instance a catchy and false belief that spreads very widely, or a true and useful one that fails to catch on.
