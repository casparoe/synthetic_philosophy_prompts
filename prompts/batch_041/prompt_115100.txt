For thirty-five years I built and ran experiments — vacuum systems first, then detectors — and I retired believing that I knew exactly what it means to say that something happened. Since then I have spent my mornings reading philosophy, slowly and without much system, and I have run aground on a question I cannot get past: by what right does anyone assert what would have happened? Nothing in the record of the world reports it. In my old trade we made the assumption constantly — that it makes sense to say what an instrument would have registered had we pointed it elsewhere — and I confess I never once examined it. I am told the philosophical literature on conditionals is enormous and that I have read perhaps a tenth of it; what holds my attention is not the semantics as such but the moral weight that rides on the grammar. If a colleague says, while the pump is still running, "if the seal fails, we lose the batch," he has made a prediction. If he says, after the seal has failed, "if the seal had held, we would have saved the batch," he has made an accusation. The two sentences differ by a mood, and the difference is worth money, careers, and prison sentences.

I would like you to write a short story, of roughly 2,500 words, that takes this seriously as fiction rather than dressing an essay in narrative clothing. Set it in a near-future world where counterfactual liability has become a mature industry: an insurance and reinsurance market that pays out not on events but on what would have occurred in the nearest world in which some specified condition had obtained. Claims are adjudicated by a tribunal of actuaries and philosophers whose rulings on which counterfactual world counts as nearest have hardened into precedent worth billions.

Build the story around one hard case; my suggestion follows, and you may replace it if you find a better one. A coastal flood barrier failed and a district drowned. The engineering firm that maintained the barrier is sued under such a policy, and it defends itself by showing that an independent defect in a second gate, undiscovered at the time, would have flooded the district anyway. The claimants reply that the nearest world is not the one in which the second defect is quietly removed, but the one in which the firm's inspection report — filed nine days late, in breach of contract — was filed on time, a change that is small in extent, recent, and entirely within the firm's control. Both sides are measuring distance from the actual world; they are measuring different things. As it happens, the barrier itself was never overtopped, so the firm's written assurance to the district, that if the barrier were overtopped the pumps would hold, was never tested in the world that came to pass — and the claimants now read those same words in the subjunctive, as a promise about a world that never arose.

Three things would please me. First, let the tribunal's rulebook define the nearest world only as the one requiring the smallest departure from actuality, so that both parties can cite the same clause and neither can be accused of inventing a rule. Second, let the panel genuinely have to decide whether the change of mood matters to the payout, and let the disagreement between the two measures of closeness be articulated by characters who understand the objection to their own position. Third, end with the tribunal's written finding — a few sentences of reasoning a reader can weigh — rather than with a twist, a revelation, or a scene of private anguish; if a dissent comes with it, so much the better, for I have gathered that the dissent is usually where the philosophy lives. Please cite no scholarship and allude to none: the world of the story should be self-contained, and I would far rather you invent the institutions and their jargon than gesture at anything I would have to look up. A reader who knows nothing of this literature should be able to follow the case; a reader who knows it should find that the philosophy is doing the work.
