I run a ninety-minute session for our university's philosophy society on Roko's basilisk, and I want a plan for teaching it, not an explanation of it. The room knows the argument only as an internet meme and has dismissed it; none of them has any decision theory, and several will arrive assuming it is Pascal's wager with a robot. Give me the order in which the ideas must be introduced for the argument's shape to become visible rather than absurd -- what has to be in place before a threat from an agent you cannot causally affect, and which cannot be affected by you, can even be stated as a serious proposal -- and why each piece depends on the one before it. Name the single case that should carry the session; I take it that this must contain no artificial intelligence at all, and I want to know what it must have and lack so that the students feel the pull of the reasoning before they see where it leads. I am tempted by the desert hitchhiker case -- the only driver who stops will take you to town only if you promise to pay on arrival, and nothing can make you pay once you are there -- so say whether it can carry the weight or whether it quietly imports a prediction the basilisk cannot have. Then the misconceptions, in the order they will surface, each with the point in the sequence where I should head it off: that the basilisk is a prediction about a future machine; that it is Pascal's wager; that it requires backwards causation; that merely hearing about it is what exposes you; and that the argument is either a proof or a joke, when it is a conditional resting on a contestable decision theory. Finish with what I should openly refuse to settle in ninety minutes and one question to put to the room that would show whether they have understood what would have to be true for the threat to have any bite.
