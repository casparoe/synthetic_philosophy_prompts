I am preparing a short position note for a reading group on axiomatic bargaining, and I would like you to write the argument for me. The position I want defended is this: individual monotonicity — the requirement that enlarging the set of feasible agreements in a way that leaves the other party's maximum attainable payoff unchanged must not reduce what a bargainer receives — should not be accepted as a rationality constraint on a bargaining solution at all, and consequently the standard case for the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution over the Nash solution, which turns entirely on that requirement, collapses; the Nash solution should remain the default. My own reason for thinking this is a distinction I have been calling the primitive/derived restriction, and I would like it to carry the weight of the argument: an axiom may quantify only over the data that define a bargaining problem — the feasible set and the disagreement point, which I take as primitive in the usual way — and never over quantities computed from that data, such as the utopia point, the vector of each party's maximum attainable payoff subject to the other's receiving at least the disagreement payoff. The restriction is not ad hoc: Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance and independence of irrelevant alternatives all satisfy it, and it is precisely what the monotonicity axiom violates, since the other party's maximum attainable payoff is a derived quantity whose relation to the frontier changes as the feasible set is redrawn. Please make that case as tightly as you can, and do not let it read as a definitional trick — if the restriction is doing real work, it should be able to explain why the monotonicity axiom looked compelling in the first place.

Concretely, I want four things. First, the position stated in one crisp paragraph and then actually defended rather than announced. Second, a numerical two-person example with a piecewise-linear frontier in which enlarging the feasible set, while holding fixed both the disagreement point and the other party's maximum attainable payoff, strictly lowers the first party's Nash payoff; compute the Kalai–Smorodinsky payoff on the same two problems for comparison, and say in one sentence which feature of the geometry drives the difference. Third, the strongest defence of the monotonicity axiom you can mount — in particular the claim that a party's entitlement should track its claim, and that the axiom is nothing more than the requirement that entitlements be monotone in claims — followed by your reply to that defence. Fourth, an honest statement of the cost of the position, that is, what a defender of the Nash solution has to say about your example in order that it not count as a reductio, and how much that concession weakens the case you are making.

Do not cite any literature, offer a reading list, or attribute the criterion to anyone; I want the argument to stand on its own, and I care far more about the quality of the reasoning than about provenance.
