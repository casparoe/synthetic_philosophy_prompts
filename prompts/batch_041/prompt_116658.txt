For two years now I have sat in a reading group that keeps having the same argument about what the utility function in consumer theory and cost-benefit analysis is a function of, and whether 'preference' names a state of the person or a summary of what the person does. The argument runs its course in about forty minutes and everyone leaves where they came in, which is why I have stopped caring about who is right and started caring about what kind of disagreement this is.

The first side is the behaviourist one. Samuelson's original move was to reconstruct demand theory from choices rather than from any hedonic or introspective premise, so that utility becomes an index recovered from a choice structure; the axioms — weak and strong revealed preference, the generalised axiom, and the theorem that any finite data set satisfying the generalised axiom is exactly as if generated by maximisation of some ordering — are then constraints on choice data, and the representation theorem is a piece of mathematics, not a discovery about anyone's mind. Gul and Pesendorfer pushed this hardest: psychological states make economics unfalsifiable, and welfare economics is a separate normative exercise that the choice machinery neither underwrites nor needs to.

The second side is the one I find harder to dismiss but also harder to state cleanly. Little's old objection was that the new welfare economics either says nothing or smuggles in an ethical premise; Sen argued that internal consistency is neither necessary nor sufficient for rationality, so choosing consistently cannot be what rationality means; the preference-reversal literature shows the same person choosing one gamble and pricing the other higher; the Kaldor-Hicks compensation tests, once paired with Scitovsky's observation that they can rank A above B and B above A, look like a machinery for producing welfare verdicts that does not track anything; and the recent repairs — separating the utility that guides choice from the utility actually experienced, judging a person's situation by her opportunity set rather than by the satisfaction of a coherent ranking, or restricting welfare to 'welfare-relevant' preferences — all seem to reintroduce precisely the substantive psychology the first side refuses.

My hypothesis, which I would rather see tested than confirmed, is that this is three disagreements wearing one word. First, a semantic one: 'preference' is used for a disposition to choose in a specified situation, for a comparative evaluation the agent could avow, and for whatever ranking is authoritative for the agent's good, and these three come apart as soon as choices are unstable, uninformed, or coerced. Second, a methodological one about what economics should model, answerable by arguments about predictive content and falsifiability rather than about the mind. Third, a normative one about what welfare is and whether choice can measure it, which nothing in the first two settles. If that is right, most of the impasse is a category mistake, though I suspect a residue remains substantive: cases where there is no choice pattern to summarise at all — the dead, future generations, anyone never offered the pair — and the question whether as-if fidelity to choice is sufficient warrant for the willingness-to-pay valuations that cost-benefit analysis actually uses.

So: diagnose it. Is the three-way reading correct, or is there a single crux I am papering over? If it is verbal or three-way, say which moves in the debate are live arguments and which are two people answering different questions under one heading, and tell me what each side would have to concede for the exchange to become an argument. If it is substantive, tell me which sub-question carries the weight and whether it is empirical, formal, or normative. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or why none should be expected.
