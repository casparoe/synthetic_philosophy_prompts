I am a first-year PhD student in a group that does in vitro evolution. At our last meeting I asked whether the molecule we have evolved — a short RNA that copies itself in a test tube with a supplied polymerase and does essentially nothing else — should be described as alive in the paper we are drafting, and whether that is a substantive question rather than a stylistic one. My PI waved it off: "There's no fact of the matter; 'life' is just a word we use." A postdoc then said that life is metabolism, as if that settled it in the other direction. I have heard some version of the PI's line three times now and I cannot tell whether it is a deep point, a conversation-stopper, or two unrelated claims stuck together.

Lay out for me, as a menu of readings rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise things that sentence could be asserting — that "life" is vague, that it is a conventional label, that it names no natural kind, that the facts underdetermine any answer, that counting something as alive is a decision rather than a discovery — and for each say what it would amount to, whether it is a platitude that holds of almost any general term, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is false. Then tell me which of those readings my PI would have to mean for his remark to actually settle whether the word belongs in our paper, which readings would instead license writing "alive," and which would license writing "not alive," since the paper has to say something either way. I would also like to know which reading is being leaned on when someone says that life is metabolism and thinks that closes the matter.
