I run research for a small human rights NGO, and next month our board votes on whether we adopt a written standard for when we'll call something a genocide. I'm the one arguing for it, and for a deliberately wide version: not just the legal definition, but two extra triggers -- deliberate destruction by deprivation (starving a group, cutting off medicine and water) and cultural destruction (language, shrines, archives, cemeteries). My pitch is basically that waiting for a court to say the word means the word arrives years after it could have done anything, and that an org which only uses the term when a prosecutor would is outsourcing its moral vocabulary to institutions built not to use it.

The board is six people and I know roughly how each of them will come at me: a former ICC defence-side lawyer; the chair, who's family is from a region where this word is fought over and who is allergic to anything that looks like diluting it; a comms director who thinks the word is a fundraising grenade; our field director for the country we'd first apply it to, who has local staff and access to think about; a historian of mass violence who knows the Lemkin-era debates better than I do; and the treasurer, who mainly wants to know what this costs us.

Give me the Q&A prep sheet -- the objections I'll actually get in that room, not the ones I'd get at a law faculty seminar -- ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that member would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first minute and what to hold. And include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
