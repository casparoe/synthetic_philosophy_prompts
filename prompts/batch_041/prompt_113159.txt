I host a small, informal podcast on social philosophy, and I have no formal training in economics — I've been teaching myself the social-choice literature on my own. Next week's guest is a theorist who has spent years developing an unorthodox reading of Sen's liberal paradox. His claim, as I understand it, is that the paradox is not a genuine clash between liberty and the Pareto principle at all but a clash inside liberalism: it arises, he says, only because the liberal condition quietly lets a person's preferences about who ought to decide a matter count as though they were ordinary preferences about the matter itself. He proposes to separate what he calls jurisdictional preferences, about who gets to decide, from substantive ones, and to repair the framework in two stages — first settling the assignment of personal spheres by unanimity, then applying the Pareto principle only within those spheres, so that the two conditions never meet. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press him on the content of this position instead of letting him restate the theorem. I want at least one question on whether the formal result really depends on reading the liberal condition that way, one on what work the two-stage repair does that a simpler restriction of the Pareto principle would not, and one on whether his separation of jurisdictional from substantive preferences can be drawn without circularity; for each, give a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Where a point is settled in the social-choice literature rather than genuinely contested, I'd like the questions to bring out that difference, and please mark in your notes which of your claims are established results and which are interpretive, rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
