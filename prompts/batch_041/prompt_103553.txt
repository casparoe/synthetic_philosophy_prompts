I'm putting together a handout for the first meeting of my hedonism seminar and what I want is a decision tree, not an essay. Topic is the heterogeneity problem — the fact that the pleasure of a hot bath, the pleasure of finally getting a proof, the pleasure of a kid falling asleep on your shoulder and the pleasure of scratching a mosquito bite seem to have nothing whatsosever in common — and the tree should sort the *answers* to it, i.e. the competing accounts of what, if anything, unifies pleasures.

Two constraints on the build. First, every branch node has to be a question my students can answer about a pair of concrete cases *before* they know which theory sits at the end of the branch. So no nodes like "are you a quality theorist or an attitude theorist?" — the student shouldn't be able to see the leaf coming. Second, build the tree from the logical space first and the names second: work out which combinations of answers are actually available, put the views I list below where they land, and then tell me which slots come out empty and why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored).

Nodes I want in there, at least these: whether the unifying feature is a felt quality at all or an attitude toward an experience; whether the unity is intrinsic to the experience or relational to the subject's other states; whether "pleasure" is supposed to be a natural kind we discover or a stipulative/practical category we impose; and whether the unity is first-personal (only visible from the inside) or functional (visible from outside). Also give me one node on unfelt or borderline pleasures, because I think that's where a lot of these views quietly die.

Leaves that have to show up somewhere: a single hedonic quality; pleasure as a pro-attitude (liking); pleasure as desire, or as the satisfaction of desire; pleasure as representing its object as good; pleasure as a functional role (whatever plays the approach-and-continue role); the disjunctive/pluralist view on which there's no unity and hedonism has to be reformulated accordingly; the eliminativist or verbal-dispute view; and a two-factor view where you need both a quality and an attitude and neither alone is sufficient.

Cases to use, at least: bath vs. proof; bittersweet stuff (sad music, nostalgia); relief; wanting-without-liking and liking-without-wanting; a flow state where you're not attending to any pleasure at all; and one I made up — a pill that makes people keep taking it and report afterwards that it wasn't enjoyable, just compelling.

Two more things. I've got an axis I dont see in the usual treatments and I want it in: a view on which pleasures are unified not by any shared property but by a shared role in practical reason — they're all the things that give *this* kind of reason. Put it wherever it belongs and say whether it's really distinct from the functional-role view or just that view with different vocabulary. And flag any leaves your tree can't seperate from one another rather than papering over it.

Six to eight questions deep, no more. Audience is grad students who've read some ethics but nothing on this specific debate.
