I've been roped into giving the toast at the dinner the night before my cousin Dana's wedding — it's the big pre-wedding family dinner, sixty-odd people, and it's also the first time the whole sprawling crew has been in one room since my grandmother died a couple years back. Grandma was the one who held the whole thing together, and her thing was recipe cards: she must have written out a few hundred over the years and never wrote the same dish the same way twice, each card nicking a bit from the others, and there was no single ingredient that showed up in all of them. She used to say the family was like that. Now the awakward part: Dana's stepdad Ken has been around for twenty years and is honestly the reason half of us still talk to each other, but my uncle Pete has a habit of announcing, louder than he means to, that 'blood is blood,' and there's been some muttering about whether Ken gets a seat at the head table, plus Dana's fiance has a kid from a previous thing, which is its own tangle.

What I want the toast to do is make the case that Ken is family — not 'sort of' family, actually family — and I want to do it with Wittgenstein's family resemblance idea, the rope made of overlapping fibres where no single fibre runs the whole length. But I do not want the lazy version of it, where family resemblance just means 'everyone's family, there are no rules, whatever' — I know that isn't what he's after; the point is that there are still criteria and that some things clearly aren't games, it's just that the criteria overlap instead of all being present at once. So the toast has to actually do that work: answer Pete without calling him an idiot, land for the two cousins in the room who are philosophy grad students and will catch the reference, and not turn into a lecture for the other fifty-eight. About three minutes. And take a real position — I want it to come right out and say Ken's at the head table, and give the reason, don't hedge with 'both views have merit.' At the end, add a short note for me (not to be read aloud) on where you've taken liberties with Wittgenstein or leaned on a reading of him that's contested, so I know what I'm getting into.
