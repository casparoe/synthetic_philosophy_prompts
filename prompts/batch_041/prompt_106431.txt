I am trying to understand the family of arguments that a rational agent must maximize expected utility, and I keep running into a distinction I cannot find a name for.

The arguments seem to reach their conclusion in one of two quite different ways. The first is representational: if an agent's preferences satisfy certain structural conditions — completeness, transitivity, and a few others — then there exists a probability-and-utility representation relative to which the agent is maximizing expected utility; hence an agent who violates the conditions is not an expected-utility maximizer. The second works by exhibiting a loss: if the agent's preferences are defective, one can construct a sequence of trades, each of which the agent prefers at the moment of choosing, that leaves the agent certainly worse off than before.

What puzzles me is that these two arguments do not appear to pick out the same defect. The representational argument is about a preference ordering considered all at once. The loss argument is about a pattern of choices spread out over time, and it seems to go through only if the agent takes each step in isolation — without committing in advance to a policy, without seeing the sequence as a whole, and without revising strategy once the pattern becomes visible. Consider two cases. First, an agent with cyclic tastes over three goods, who nonetheless announces that they will make no trade except as part of a single package, so that the sequence never unfolds: intuitively they keep the very preferences that were supposed to convict them, yet the loss evaporates. Second, an agent whose preferences are impeccable at every moment but who is naive about how their own tastes will shift, so that they can be walked into a loss they would have avoided had they anticipated it. In the first case the preferences look incoherent but the agent seems safe; in the second the preferences look coherent but the agent is not.

So the thing the loss argument seems to indict is not the preference ordering itself but something more like a disposition to be walked through the sequence — a failure of forethought, or of commitment power, or of the ability to treat one's choices as a single plan.

Is there an established name for the defect that the loss arguments actually target, as distinct from the defect that the representational arguments target? Is there a name for the thesis that the two defects coincide, and for the opposing thesis that they come apart? And if the phenomenon I am describing is not already carved out under a single heading, what are the nearest concepts, and how exactly does each fall short? I am less interested in a label than in the carving: I want to know what the gap is between merely having incoherent preferences and being the sort of agent who can actually be made to lose by them, and where the boundary cases lie.
