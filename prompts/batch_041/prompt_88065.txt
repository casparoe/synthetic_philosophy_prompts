I'm a second-year PhD student and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a proper proposal — for my supervisor and for a small internal grant that would buy me roughly eight months of part-time work, realistically a paper of 7000-8000 words plus some formal scaffolding. The idea I keep circling, and which I've been argueing about with a friend in the pub for months now: all the familiar idealised-preference accounts — what you really want is what you'd want if you were fully informed, or vividly acquainted with the relevant facts, or if your preferences had been cleaned up until they were coherent — seem to presuppose an operator that takes a real person's messy, gappy, partly incoherent preferences and spits out a tidied-up set. Call it the idealisation operator. The literature, as far as I can tell, argues endlessly about which inputs it should take — full information, imaginative acquaintance, coherence, and then the more ambitious thing from the AI alignment world where you extrapolate the agent's moral development rather than merely informing it, coherent extrapolated volition — but hardly anyone asks what a well-behaved operator would have to look like before any of that input-talk even gets off the ground.

My hunch, and I want the proposal to test it rather than assume it, is that two conditions do most of the work and that the standard accounts each fail at least one. The first is idempotence: idealising an already-idealised agent shouldn't move its preferences any further, otherwise you've got no reason to think one pass rather than five is the authoritative one. The second is what I've been calling description-invariance: the output shouldn't depend on how the agent's preferences happen to be carved up or presented, since otherwise you can gerrymander the result by redescription. I suspect the failures are interesting rather than embarrassing — that they mark a genuine fork between idealisation as a discovery procedure (there's a fact about what you really want and idealising reveals it) and idealisation as a construction (the idealised set is stipulated, and its authority is moral rather than epistemic) — and I'd like the paper to work out which fork each account is really on.
