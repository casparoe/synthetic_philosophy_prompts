Last night a friend and I argued until nearly two in the morning about where the meaning of a message actually resides, and I have concluded that the question will not be settled by further argument. I would like you to write me a short story that puts it under pressure instead.

The picture we kept circling back to has three layers. First, the frame message: whatever tells a receiver that there is a message here at all, and what kind of thing to do with it — the difference between marks that are merely evidence that something happened and marks that are addressed to you and ask something of you. Second, the outer message: the scheme that says how to read what follows, the code, key, or convention that fixes which of the indefinitely many readings of these marks is the intended one. Third, the inner message: the content you are left with once the outer message has done its work — the warning, the request, the joke.

My friend's position is that only the third layer is meaning properly so called; the other two are apparatus, scaffolding, and they become invisible precisely when they are functioning, which is why we mistake them for nothing. My position is that the inner message is the least substantial of the three, since it is a product of the other two, and that the same words can be carried across a change of frame and come out meaning something entirely different — a threat in one setting, an endearment in another — without a single character changing. His strongest case was a shopping list: trivial frame, trivial outer message, meaning simply the list. My reply was that a list is a list only because something has already told us not to read it as a poem, a confession, or a set of instructions addressed to a stranger.

What I want from you is roughly two thousand words of fiction in which this disagreement is enacted rather than explained.

- No two characters debating the three layers over coffee. I want a situation in which the frame around some actual thing — a document, an object, a signal — is missing, forged, or disputed, and someone has to act on a decision about it.
- At least one scene must turn on a text whose meaning changes when its frame changes while its own content stays fixed, and a reader should be able to reconstruct the three layers from what happens without anyone naming them.
- Do not let the story settle the question in my friend's favour or in mine. A resolution that is announced as a resolution is a cheat; if anything is resolved, let it be practical — someone has to file the thing, act on it, pass it on — arrived at under uncertainty, with the cost of the decision visible.
- One further element: at least one character should be tempted by the thought that the outer message is itself the content, that the real meaning of a document lies in the fact of its having been written in a particular way for a particular reader, and that the business of decoding it is a distraction from what matters.

Possible starting points, though I would rather you invent something in the same spirit than take one off the shelf: an archive where the same set of drawings is catalogued by one curator as a chart and by another as a genealogy; a family that inherits a box of recordings and cannot agree on whether the instructions on them were ever meant to be followed; an analyst who receives a transmission that is legible under two codes at once and must choose the one under which it is not a declaration of war. Avoid hidden-message stories in the ordinary sense: the point is not that something was concealed, but that what it means depends on something outside it.

No moral at the end, and no narrator who explains the theory after the fact.
