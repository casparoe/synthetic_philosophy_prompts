I'm taking a seminar on rationality and strategic interaction, and in last week's session a classmate gave a short presentation on the centipede game that has been bothering me ever since. What he said, as near as I can reconstruct it from my notes, was this: 'In the centipede game two players alternate, each choosing either to take the larger share of a pot that grows every time someone passes, or to pass; there is a fixed and commonly known number of moves. Backward induction solves it: at the last move taking beats passing, so the player facing that move takes, which means the player at the previous move takes, and the reasoning rolls all the way back to the first move, where the first player takes immediately and both end up with almost nothing. The strange part is that this is the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, even though both would have been far better off had they both kept passing. So the game shows that rationality is self-defeating: each player is individually rational and together they are worse off than they had to be, which is really just the prisoner's dilemma with extra steps. The standard fix is that the whole argument rests on common knowledge of rationality; if each player thinks there is even a tiny chance the other one is irrational, backward induction never gets going and cooperation can hold, which is supposed to explain why experimental subjects pass for a while before somebody takes. Some people have even argued that common knowledge of rationality is inconsistent in this game, which I take to mean the backward induction argument is simply invalid. The practical upshot is that backward induction gives the wrong advice: if I ever found myself in a centipede game I should pass, because the equilibrium is a mathematical artifact of assuming that everyone is a perfect maximizer.'

I would like an audit of that, claim by claim. Please separate what a game theorist would simply accept, what she would wince at as an overstatement or a conflation, and what is flatly wrong -- and for each of the last two, say what a defender of the backward induction argument would say in reply, since I suspect my classmate has made the argument look easier to dismiss than it is. Two things I would especially like pinned down: whether the 'tiny chance of irrationality' point does the work he says it does, and whether the alleged inconsistency of common knowledge of rationality means what he takes it to mean. Finally, tell me whether his closing practical conclusion follows from anything that survives your audit -- I am inclined to think he is right that one should pass, since I gather that people do cooperate in these experiments, but the step from the proof to the advice felt too quick to me, and I want to know exactly where it goes wrong, if it does.
