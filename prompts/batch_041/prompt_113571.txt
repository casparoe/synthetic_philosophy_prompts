For the past few years I've written a weekly column on science and ideas, and I've been pressed into service to run the opening session of a five-day fellowship for about sixteen early-career science journalists and science-policy researchers: ninety minutes, one room, no laptops, and an explicit brief that they should spend the time arguing with one another rather than listening to me. What I want them to leave having felt in their bones, rather than having politely nodded at, is the unilateralist's curse — the way a collection of well-meaning, independently-acting people can end up taking an action that almost none of them would have endorsed collectively, for the simple reason that the action only needs one of them to go ahead. The structure I care about is this: there is some course of action any single person can undertake entirely on their own; whether it turns out well depends on how many people do it, so that it is good if it happens once or a few times and harmful if everyone piles in; and each person has only a private, partial, and partly overlapping sense of whether it is the right thing to do. The counterintuitive part, which I want them to discover rather than be told, is that the trouble gets worse as you add more thoughtful people and as their judgments diverge, because it takes only one optimist to act while it takes unanimity to hold back — and that the obvious remedy, a rule that nobody moves unless everyone agrees, simply flips the failure into paralysis. I'd like the activity to let them feel both of those failure modes in a single sitting if the time allows, and to feel how reasonable every individual decision looked from the inside at the moment it was made.

So design me a runnable game. I want concrete rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, and a timeline that fits ninety minutes with a group this size; arrange the incentives and the information each person holds so that the group runs into the problem themselves before anyone names it, and keep the phrase itself out of the room until the debrief. Build in at least one role for a participant who is right and knows it — someone who has seen through the proposed action and is firmly against it — so they can watch their own confidence fail to stop anything, since the point I'm chasing is that this is not a failure of any individual's judgment but of how independent judgments combine. If you can, let the group play a round under unilateral action and a round under a unanimity rule, so they feel the second failure mode with their own hands rather than hearing me assert it. Then write the debrief: map the specific moments — the round where a single confident participant carried everyone else along, the round where the consensus rule froze the room — onto the pieces of the idea, and connect it lightly, without turning into a lecture, to the kinds of stories on their beat where many separate actors can each act alone.

Tell me, too, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — the group that treats it as a puzzle with a clever answer, the participant who refuses to play, the one who guesses the concept in the first ten minutes. And be frank, in the end, about the feature of the real phenomenon the game cannot reproduce. My own guess is that a game has a fixed, discoverable right answer, whereas the whole sting of the real thing is that no one ever finds out whether the action was justified; tell me whether that's the gap, or a different one, and say what I'll have to supply out loud to make up the difference. Write all of this as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way you'd write a long letter to a colleague who has to run the session next week.
