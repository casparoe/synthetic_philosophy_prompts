I'm a third-year student finishing a seminar on transcendental arguments, and I've hit a case I can't settle on my own. A classmate has reconstructed a transcendental argument for moral realism, and I'd like your honest credence on whether it works, with the reasoning laid out.

The argument is this. (1) From the deliberative standpoint — the standpoint we occupy whenever we ask what to do — we cannot help holding ourselves and others responsible; the practice of demanding and giving justifications is not something we can set aside while remaining in that standpoint. (2) A practice of holding one another responsible is intelligible only if there are facts about what one is in fact responsible for; a practice that answered to nothing would be no more a practice of holding responsible than a game of chess with no rules. (3) Therefore there are facts about what we are responsible for. My classmate concedes that (3) is established only as a presupposition of the deliberative standpoint and not as a claim about the world considered apart from it, and she insists that this concession does not weaken the conclusion.

Please give me your credence that the argument succeeds in establishing its target, where I'll fix the target precisely as: there are mind-independent facts about what we are responsible for. Decompose the number. At a minimum I want separate probabilities for: (a) premise (1), that the practice really is inescapable from the deliberative standpoint rather than merely psychologically hard to shed; (b) premise (2), the bridge from our being unable to give the practice up to its answering to anything real — the step where, in the more familiar case of the categories of experience, the argument is usually accused of smuggling in a verificationist premise; (c) the further step from there being facts to those facts being mind-independent, rather than constitutively dependent on the practice that generates them; (d) whether moral responsibility is the kind of thing that can bear a transcendental argument at all, given how unlike the categories of experience it is; and (e) any term I've left out. Give each a number and a line of justification, then combine them and show the combination.

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what would move it most — an argument, a counterexample, or something else.
