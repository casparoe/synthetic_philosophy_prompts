Right, so I'm teaching a seminar on the School of Names this term and I want a bit of satire for the handout — something the students can chew on afterwards. Here's the idea, and don't go guessing at the philosophy, I'll sketch it for you: Gongsun Long's old puzzle, the white horse one. 'Horse' names the shape, 'white' names the colour, and naming a colour isn't naming a shape, so 'white horse' picks out something narrower than 'horse' — ask for a horse and any old bay will do, ask for a white horse and it won't. That's the engine; just run it.

Now what I want is a mock directive from a customs and excise office that has officially adopted this doctrine for classifying livestock for duty. Deadpan bureaucratic prose, the office believes every word, nobody inside it is being stupid — the comedy has to come from the logic actually holding. I definately want it to escalate the way real loophole-law does: what happens to grey horses, to a horse that's white in one eye, to the man who paints his bay. The reader should end up feeling the reductio without you ever announcing it.

Pick one reading of the paradox and commit to it — don't hedge by laying out the options and leaving me to choose, and no winking at the reader about how silly it all is.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered clauses of the directive, each a sentence or two, no intro and no sign-off. British spelling. Funny to someone who's read the dialogue, legible to someone who hasn't.
