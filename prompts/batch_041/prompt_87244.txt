Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about secession, and we kept returning to an analogy he would not let go of: a state is like a marriage, and secession is like divorce. He had worked it out in some detail. The spouses are the national majority and the aspiring nation; the marriage contract is the constitutional settlement; the prenuptial agreement is the terms of union; alimony is the system of fiscal transfers; the children are those citizens of the departing territory who would rather stay, together with those of the rump who have family and property across the new border; and the divorce court is the constitutional court or the negotiating table. From this he drew three conclusions: that a bare majority in a referendum ought to suffice; that the rest of the state has no veto over the departure; and that the matter is essentially private, so that outsiders — other states, the European Union, the United Nations — have no standing in it. He then produced a flourish I found harder to answer than I expected. Now that England and Wales have moved to no-fault divorce, we no longer think a spouse must prove the other's fault in order to leave, and we no longer think a court should force two people to remain married. On his reading, the Supreme Court of Canada's insistence, in the Quebec reference of 1998, that a clear majority on a clear question triggers a duty to negotiate rather than a right to exit is simply the law refusing to let an unhappy spouse go.

What I would like is an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on Scottish, Catalan or Quebec politics. Set out the correspondence as explicitly as you can, a table being fine, and then say where it breaks. Two things interest me especially. First, which of the disanalogies genuinely bear on the conclusions he wants and which are merely colourful — whether it matters, for instance, that a marriage is contracted by consenting adults, that children cannot be divided between the parties, or that even a no-fault divorce is unilateral in its grounds but not in its procedure. Second, whether the analogy quietly settles the very question at issue: who has the standing to decide, and whether a union is properly conceived as a contract between two parties rather than as a single political community. Tell me which of his three conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and which fall. Finally, if the analogy is worth keeping at all, say what it can still be used for, and whether some better comparison — a corporate demerger, the dissolution of a federation, the break-up of a household — does the work he wants without begging the question. Please answer in British English.
