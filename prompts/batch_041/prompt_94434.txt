I am a high-school student working on an independent study in artificial intelligence and philosophy of mind, and my advisor has asked me to write a "specification" section before I evaluate anyone's proposed solution to the frame problem. I would like your help drafting it.

The frame problem, as I understand it, is the difficulty an agent faces in keeping track of what does and does not change as it acts and learns — working out what is relevant to what, without having to state, one item at a time, all the things that stay the same. What I want from you is not a solution but the list of requirements that any adequate theory of this has to meet, with the consideration behind each requirement.

To keep the list concrete, here are the cases I have collected; I have invented most of them, and I would like the requirements to be motivated by them.

1. The warehouse robot. At noon it learns that aisle 4 is blocked. It should adjust its plan without re-checking every one of the several thousand facts it holds, and it must not conclude that the pallets' contents or the color of the walls have changed.

2. The lamp. The robot switches on a lamp; the room becomes brighter; a plant on the shelf now receives light. It seems to need the second and third facts, but not an endless list of further consequences.

3. The unlocked door. What matters is that a door is not locked and that a sensor has not gone off. Nothing has been done; the crucial facts are about what is absent.

4. The person in the aisle. The robot knows that a person is standing in aisle 4. When it learns that the aisle is blocked, the person's beliefs may change but her height does not; and the robot must also track what she believes about the robot's own beliefs.

5. The agent that thinks slowly. While the robot is deliberating, other things in the warehouse keep happening, and its own decision will change what it does later.

6. Learning from experience. The robot could learn which facts tend to change together, instead of having every rule about relevance written for it by a human.

For each requirement I would like: the case that forces it; whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp (for instance, whether a requirement that the theory be written as formal logic is a genuine requirement or a taste of one tradition); and whether any two requirements cannot be satisfied at the same time, since I suspect the most interesting result lies in that conflict. I expect at least one pair to clash — a demand that the agent never overlook a genuine change against a demand that it not have to check everything — but I would rather you find the real conflicts than agree with my guess. State each requirement in a sentence or two under a short label, so that the list is usable as a checklist.

Please also tell me which of my cases is not really a constraint, and which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate theories. I care about a theory good enough to guide the design of a system that actually works; where a requirement comes from the goal of explaining human minds rather than building machines, say so, because I suspect several disputes in this area are really disputes about that.

Finally, a short verdict: how do the main families of approach fare against the list — rules that state what changes, representations that build relevance in from the start, statistical or learned relevance, and the view that the whole difficulty is an artifact of trying to do this inside a formal system? No citations or references are needed; I only want the reasoning.
