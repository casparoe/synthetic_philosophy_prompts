I chair the biosafety committee at a mid-size research institute. Our containment policy was written back in the 90s and it keeps saying the rules kick in for "work involving living organisms," and honestly none of us can agree on what that means anymore. That was fine for twenty years and then this month happened.

We've got three things up for classification at Thursday's meeting and I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand, with an actual recommendation on each one and something on the policy itself. Here's what's coming:

(1) A synthetic-biology group has a "minimal cell." Genome written from scratch and assembled in the lab, something like 500 genes, it grows and divides in the broth they feed it -- but it dies within a day outside those conditions, and nothing like it exists anywhere in nature. The group says it's obviously alive, come on. Our facilities people say a thing that can't survive outside a flask isn't what the policy was ever worried about.

(2) Another lab works on a "giant virus" that infects amoebae. Genome bigger than a lot of bacteria, full of genes nobody can explain. But no ribosomes, no metabolism of its own -- it borrows all of that from whatever cell it infects, and it only replicates inside a host. Some of the committee wants to call it non-living so we can drop it to the lowest tier. Two members think that's exactly backwards: a thing antibiotics can't touch, with a huge unexplored genome, is the thing you'd most want contained, not least.

(3) A chemistry group has a "protocell" -- little fatty-acid vesicles with a self-sustaining reaction inside that copies a short strand of RNA and makes the vesicle grow and occasionally split. No DNA, no proteins, no genes in the usual sense. It only keeps going while they drip in a specific chemical feed and it stalls a lot. The group calls it a model system, not an organism. One of my members says if it grows and divides and passes on information, the label doesn't matter, the containment does.

Here's the wrinkle that's turning this into a headache. Our policy uses "living organism" as the trigger for two seperate things: the physical containment tier, and a biosecurity / dual-use review that fires for anything "living or potentially capable of self-replication." So in principle we could call the same thing alive for one purpose and not the other. I think at least two of these three might actually need that split, and I'd rather we decide it on purpose than back into it.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate. I need a clear recommended classification for each of the three -- which tier, and whether the dual-use review triggers -- the reasoning in terms a PI could actually push back on, and an honest statement of the strongest argument against each recommendation (I don't want a memo that pretends the other side is stupid). Then a short recommendation on whether we should amend the policy to define "life," or whether we should stop using that word as the trigger at all -- and if it's the latter, what we should key the tiers to instead, since we obviously can't key them to nothing. Keep it under two pages and make the bottom line something the committee can vote on Thursday.
