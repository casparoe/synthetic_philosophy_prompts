I work on the design team at a national statistics office, and we have been told to build a word-free visual system for presenting our headline claims to a public that reads several languages and includes many people with limited literacy. One of my colleagues keeps invoking Otto Neurath — the Vienna Circle philosopher who, through the 1920s and 30s, tried to construct a picture language for social and economic statistics, the forerunner of what later became known as Isotype — as proof that this is not merely possible but superior to prose, on the grounds that pictures can carry exact meaning that words only blur, and that a single visual grammar could be learned once and then read anywhere. I am skeptical, but I want to take the idea seriously rather than wave it away, so I would like you to turn Neurath's actual position on picture languages into a procedure my team can run. I care about what he really held, not the popular caricature: that the picture language was meant to be a genuine language with a strict grammar rather than decoration; that it was bound up with his physicalism, so that only observable, spatiotemporal states of affairs can be depicted and abstract or theoretical entities require auxiliary signs; and that its supposed self-evidence was always in tension with the fact that any such language has to be learned. Where the caricature and the real view diverge, follow the real view.

Please write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions we ask about a candidate claim before committing it to pictures: what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the point at which the procedure returns a firm instruction to depict it a particular way, the point at which it says this part must stay in words, and the points where the decision is a matter of editorial values rather than of Neurath's theory and therefore has to be handed to a person — assume our director has final sign-off. Then run it on three cases, including at least one where the procedure gives an answer we will not like. First, a simple quantity claim: that a certain fraction of households lost power for more than a day last winter. Second, a causal claim: that a newly introduced fee caused a drop in applications. Third, and this is the one that genuinely worries me, a claim carrying uncertainty and comparison at once: that the gap between two regions probably narrowed, even though the estimates overlap. Neurath's framework was built for counts of things you can point at, and I suspect it has nothing honest to say about probably or about overlapping, so be explicit about where his picture language reaches its limit and whether that limit is a defect of his program in particular or a general fact about pictures. Tell me at the end what the procedure cannot do, and where a reader will inevitably be misled no matter how carefully we design the thing. Please finish with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in one sentence, the single part of a claim that can never be safely pictured.
