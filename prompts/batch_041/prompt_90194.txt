Analyze Milgram's obedience experiments as a test case for Sartre's radical freedom and bad faith. Not the usual "Milgram shows the situation beats character" line — I want the mapping done properly, and done in both directions. Who's free in that lab, and free from what? Use Sartre's actual machinery: bad faith, facticity vs transcendence, the serious world, the look, and his account of the Third and the we-object (the subjects hearing each other through the wall, the experimenter as the one who objectifies them both) — does that machinery explain why the obedient subjects experienced what they did as a constraint rather than as a choice? Then the hard part: Milgram's own explanation says the subject enters an agentic state and stops seeing himself as the author of his acts. Is that a diagnosis of bad faith, or is it bad faith wearing a lab coat — and does Sartre's insistence that you can't hand your freedom over to anyone (same move he makes against the Freudian censor, which would have to know the repressed thing in order to repress it) survive contact with the roughly two-thirds who went to the end of the dial? Go the other way too: does the experiment embarrass Sartre, since situational variables predict behavior better than any fundamental project does, or does the split between the obedient and the refusing third actually fit him better than it fits the situationists? And be honest about where he's silent — the gap between the first-person report that one couldn't stop and the third-person claim that one could have. If the whole question is unfair because radical freedom is an ontological claim about the structure of consciousness rather than an empirical one, say so and tell me whether that makes it unfalsifiable or just a different kind of claim. Roughly 900 words, no need for citations or reading lists.
