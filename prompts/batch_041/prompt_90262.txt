In a seminar on French republicanism I keep running into the line 'the state must remain neutral between conceptions of the good.' A classmate invoked it last week to defend the law banning conspicuous religious symbols in French public schools: on his account, permitting the headscarf would amount to the state taking sides, so laïcité — correctly understood — requires the ban. An Anglo-American friend then used the identical phrase to argue the opposite: that a genuinely neutral state has no business dictating what students may wear, and that neutrality is what grounds religious accommodation rather than forbidding it. One of them must be loading the phrase with a different meaning, and I would not be surprised if one of the loadings turns out to be incoherent.

I do not want the slogan defended or attacked; I want it disassembled. Set out the distinct precise claims that 'the state is neutral between conceptions of the good' could be making. I have heard the vocabulary of neutrality of aim, neutrality of justification, and neutrality of effect, and the contrast between neutrality as non-establishment and neutrality as the privatization of religion — confining it to private life altogether — but I cannot tell which of these is the real fault line, or whether there are further readings I am missing. For each reading, give a verdict (platitude, substantive and defensible, or false), say whether it is a claim about the state's aims, its justifications, or its effects, and say whether it actually supports my classmate's ban, my friend's accommodation, both, or neither; if one reading is plainly the one my classmate is relying on, let that item say so. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one per reading, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
