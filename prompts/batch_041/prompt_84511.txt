I am a high school student in my school's philosophy club, and for the past few weeks I have been reading about the long quarrel over whether social phenomena are best explained by the actions, beliefs, and intentions of individuals or by social facts themselves — norms, institutions, conventions, class structures, and the like. What struck me is that people who agree on the slogans disagree completely once you press them: two students can both insist that society is more than the sum of its parts and still give incompatible answers about what that licenses you to say in an explanation, and the words individualist and holist seem to conceal almost all of the interesting disagreement. For our next meeting I would like to run a sorting quiz, and I would rather have the instrument than the lecture. Please write me a quiz of eight to ten questions together with a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto named positions, and, underneath it, the tree those positions live in.

Three requirements. First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of famous names: I want the branches to turn on whether the disputed claim is about what exists (are there social entities over and above individuals?), about what causes what (can a social fact cause an individual's action, or another social fact, without any individual-level mechanism mediating it?), or about what explains (may an explanation rest on a social fact as its fundamental term even if, in some sense, everything is composed of individuals?), and on whether individual-level facts are held to be always sufficient for explanation or merely necessary. Second, every question must be answerable by someone who has never encountered the labels: ask about a bank run, about a convention that persists although nobody prefers it (why the letters on a keyboard stayed where they are, say), about a language quietly losing a case ending, about a crowd crush, about inflation that is caused by the expectation of inflation, or about whether the very same people would have acted differently under different institutions. No question may be phrased as 'do you think society is real?' or as 'which label do you prefer?' I would also like the quiz to contain, somewhere, the position that social facts are nothing over and above facts about individuals and yet are not straightforwardly reducible to them, the position that social facts have causal powers of their own that bear down on individuals, the position that only individuals and their interactions exist and that social facts are a convenient shorthand, and the position that the whole dispute is a pseudo-problem because explanation has no single standard.

Third, each leaf should carry the name of the position (or unnamed, if nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment; and where the logical space permits a combination that nobody seems to occupy, list that cell and tell me whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored. If two positions come out indistinguishable on your questions, say so plainly instead of pretending the quiz separates them, and tell me what further question might pull them apart. Keep the tone light enough that I can hand the quiz out on a single sheet of paper, but do not soften the positions in order to make them easier to score.
