My younger sister has intractable epilepsy, and her neurologist has raised the possibility of a corpus callosotomy—severing the main bundle of nerve fibers connecting the two cerebral hemispheres—as a last resort. I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on the unity of consciousness to prepare myself, and I have also been helping in the lab of a professor, Dr. M, who is often asked to give an opinion on how many subjects of experience are present in patients with disconnection syndromes. Her track record is as follows. In a patient whose corpus callosum had been completely severed and who could verbally report an object presented to the right visual field but not one presented to the left visual field, while the right hemisphere could still retrieve the object with the left hand, she said there was one subject of experience, not two, because the patient behaved as a single person with dissociated access to information. In a patient with the same complete severing whose left hand sometimes interfered with actions the right hand had initiated and who expressed surprise at his own left hand, she said there were two streams of agency but still one subject. In a patient with congenital absence of the corpus callosum who showed no disunity on any task, she said there was one subject. In a patient with only a partial callosotomy and reliable cross-cuing between the hemispheres, she said there was one subject. In a patient whose right hemisphere could understand simple language and answer yes-or-no questions by pointing, and who gave opposite answers to a question about religious belief depending on which hemisphere was asked, she said there were two subjects.

Now a new patient is being discussed. She has had a complete surgical severing of the corpus callosum, but the anterior commissure—a smaller bundle of fibers that also connects the hemispheres—remains intact, and she shows some interhemispheric transfer on a demanding reaction-time task. On most everyday tasks she behaves as a single person, but when the two hemispheres are pitted against each other she reports a kind of mental fog, and her left hand sometimes acts on information she cannot verbally report. In addition, her right hemisphere can understand simple yes-or-no questions and answer by pointing, but on the questions tested so far it agrees with her left hemisphere rather than contradicting it. The lab wants to know what Dr. M would most likely say about this patient, which rule her earlier verdicts are most consistent with, which other rules fit those verdicts just as well, and which further case would best distinguish those rules. I am less interested in a verdict about the patient in the abstract than in the rule Dr. M seems to be running, the alternative rules that would fit her past judgments equally well, and what each would predict here.
