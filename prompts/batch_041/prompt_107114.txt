I host a small podcast on philosophy and fiction — mostly an excuse to talk to people whose work I'd otherwise only read — and the next episode is with a decision theorist who defends a version of updateless decision theory: a view on which rational choice evaluates whole policies rather than individual acts, and on which you should not treat your own impending decision as evidence about how the world is, since it is the policy that chooses the act and not the reverse. I've been trying to put the idea to work in the novel I'm writing, and I want the interview to do more than get him to restate the view for the tenth time. So I'd like eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give.

Some context on what I've been doing, since I'd like at least a few of the questions to press on it. The novel has a character — call her Mireille — who discovers that the deliberation she experiences as her own is in fact run by a routine that also runs, identically, in the heads of people she has never met, so that her choice on a given afternoon is not merely correlated with theirs but is the same computation. I've been calling the position I've backed into 'narrative updatelessness': the thought that an agent should not evaluate acts, or even policies, but whole life-stories, committing in advance to the sort of character she will be, so that any particular choice is assessed as one node in a narrative she is authoring rather than undergoing. I genuinely don't know whether that is an extension of updateless thinking or just the same view with a larger action space and a novelist's vocabulary, and I would rather not have him tell me it is 'a nice metaphor.'

I want the questions to go after the real joints: what work the ex ante standpoint is doing, and whether it can be specified without circularity, given that what it is rational to commit to in advance seems to depend on how the agent's own future algorithm will in fact resolve; whether the view has anything to say to an agent who is not sure she is an updateless agent, or who suspects she may be running the wrong decision theory altogether; how it accommodates the fact that in ordinary life we do learn which situation we are in, so that a policy chosen in ignorance can be rendered pointless the moment the ignorance lifts; and what the acausal-cooperation results actually assume about the other parties' algorithms — Mireille's strangers are stipulated to be copies, which has always felt to me like cheating. Please include one question that forces him to defend the view against the charge that it is not action-guiding at all, and one that asks what argument or evidence could turn him against it. End with a question about whether fiction is a better laboratory for these cases than the usual toy dilemmas; I would sincerely like to know, and I would like him to have to answer it rather than compliment me.
