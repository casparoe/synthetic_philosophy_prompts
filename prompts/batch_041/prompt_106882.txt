I'm putting together the take-home final for a small upper-level seminar on eighteenth-century philosophy of happiness, and I need help with the Du Châtelet question. We read her Discourse on Happiness — the one she never published in her lifetime — and the discussion keeps collapsing into one of two failure modes. Some students decide she's basically a Stoic with Epicurean seasoning and write that for two pages; others just summarise the piece: drop your prejudices, be virtuous, stay in good health, keep your passions, don't regret anything, and so on. I want a single essay question that neither of those can answer, that a good student can do in 90 minutes, and that doesn't depend on knowing her biography or any dates. I'll hand out the text, so no page refs or citations needed — I'm marking the argument.

What I keep chewing on is the illusion bit. She's hard on prejudices — they're false, they make you vicious, and a vicious person can't be happy — and then she turns around and tells us to hang on to our illusions, and from the example she gives (laughing at a comedy you know is fiction) I dont think she means something trivial. She also says we're the masters of our own happiness, which is a lot to swallow given that she admits happiness depends on circumstances, on age, and on the love she counts among its great sources, which plainly depends on another person. So I want the question to force students to say what, in her account, actually seperates a prejudice from a beneficial illusion — is there a principled line, or is it just 'false beliefs I happen to like'? — and then to press on whether the whole package hangs together, given that she also wants virtue and something she calls an inner satisfaction, a kind of health of the soul, and advises us never to regret and to keep sad thoughts (including the thought of death) at a distance. A top answer has to decide whether that's a coherent position, an unresolved tension she was aware of, or something that only looks consistent because she quietly shifts what 'happiness' means between sections.

So please write the question, a model answer of about 750 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 with five must-hit moves for a first, the marks for each, and one line per move on how a weaker answer typically botches it — i.e. what a solid B says about the illusion/prejudice distinction that a first doesn't. Also flag two or three things that should cost marks even when the prose is good: I keep getting essays that read illusion as plain self-deception and then declare her inconsistent without asking what work the word is doing, essays that treat the mastery claim as a thesis about willpower and ignore her concessions, and essays that settle everything by slotting her into Stoic or Epicurean boxes.
