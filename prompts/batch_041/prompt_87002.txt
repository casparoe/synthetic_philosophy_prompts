I have spent the past year building a cohort dataset for a policy institute, and I have come to think that the generational labels we all use — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — do not pick out real kinds. What is real is variation in what people of a given age confronted at a given time; the labels are a coarse discretization of that variation, and they are not innocent, because once they circulate they feed back into self-description, hiring policy, and marketing, and manufacture part of the difference they claim to detect. I want to defend a constructive alternative: keep birth cohort as a continuous variable, describe each cohort by an exposure profile — the ages at which it passed through specific shocks and slow-moving changes — and state openly which assumptions we are making when we try to separate age, period, and cohort effects. I have been asked to present this at a review meeting next month, and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense.

The room will be four people. Two quantitative demographers who work with age-period-cohort models, who already dislike the word generation but will distrust anyone who appears to want to abolish cohort effects altogether. A labor economist who uses birth-year fixed effects and believes the whole dispute is a measurement problem with a technical solution. Our communications lead, who sells generational segmentation to clients and has a commercial stake in the labels surviving. And a cultural historian who thinks generations are real shared formations with genuine common experience, and who will hear my thesis as a denial that history happens to people.

Give me the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede at the start, which objection I should stop trying to win, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Write it in continuous prose only — no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables — the way you would write a long email.
