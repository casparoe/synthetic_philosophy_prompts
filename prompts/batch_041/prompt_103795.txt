I am a novelist by trade — three books, the most recent of them a novel with a frame narrative I have never quite managed to close — and for the past four years I have taught a graduate seminar on frames: stories inside stories, the etiquette of the outermost layer, why readers do not notice a frame until it is taken away. Once a term I take the seminar off the fiction syllabus and into Hofstadter's chapter on the location of meaning in Gödel, Escher, Bach — the one with the record, the phonograph, and the DNA — because it is the best short account I know of the thought that a frame is not decoration: the frame is where a thing is told what it is. The midterm was a take-home, and it ended with a single long essay question on that chapter. I now have a stack of them on the kitchen table and no reliable sense of how to grade them consistently, so I would like you to grade one for me: a grade out of 30 against the allocation, and then the comments themselves, mapped claim by claim rather than delivered as an impression. I care more about whether the reasoning holds than about whether the answer sounds like the book. Please do not rewrite the essay for me, and please do not be generous with a sentence merely because it is well turned.

The question, with the allocation I circulated, was this:

Question 3 (30 points). Hofstadter asks where the meaning of a record is. (a) Set out his distinction between the inner message, the outer message, and the frame, as he uses these terms in the record case (8). (b) Explain why he takes the distinction to show that the meaning is not in the record (6). (c) Apply it to three cases, saying for each what the inner message is, what the outer message is, and where the outer message is stored: (i) a DNA molecule in a living cell; (ii) the score of a Bach fugue, as against a recording of a performance of it; (iii) an unlabelled wax cylinder in an archive, which turns out to bear a card reading, in a nineteenth-century hand, "transcribe on the machine with the 4:1 gear ratio" (9). (d) Objection: if an outer message is always required before an inscription can mean anything, and outer messages are supplied by whoever does the decoding, then Hofstadter's account makes meaning relative to the interpreter, which is just relativism. State the reply Hofstadter would give, and say whether it succeeds (7).

And this is the answer in front of me, transcribed as written:

Hofstadter's distinction is between three layers of a message. The inner message is what the message says: on a record, the music. The outer message is the set of instructions that must be applied before the inner message can be got at — the speed the record is to be played at, the equalization curve the lathe used when it was cut, the fact that it wants a stylus and not a laser. The frame is the outermost layer, the one that says what sort of object this is and that it is a message at all: not the first sentence of a novel but the conventions that make a bound block of printed pages a novel.

Why this shows the meaning is not in the record: the grooves are a wiggly line, and nothing about the shape of the line, considered by itself, prefers Bach to static. The meaning appears when the record meets a machine whose workings are isomorphic to the pattern in the groove, so that the wiggles are converted into the right vibrations. The record cannot be its own outer message — it does not say "play me at 33⅓" — so half of what is needed for the music to exist comes from the player and from us, and that is the sense in which the meaning is not located in the record.

DNA has the same structure. The inner message is the base sequence, which specifies the proteins; the outer message is what tells the cell how to read the sequence, i.e. which triplets go with which amino acids, and it is mostly written into the DNA itself — the start and stop codons, the reading frame — with the cell's translation machinery supplying only the mechanical part, the business of joining amino acids together. A Martian who found the molecule would have the inner message but not the key, and would be stuck.

The score and the recording work the same way. The inner message is the fugue in both cases; the outer message is notation in the one and playback convention in the other — what a clef means, what speed the turntable turns. The cylinder is the limiting case of an outer message that is almost explicit: the card is the outer message, and once the card is lost the cylinder is no longer a message for anyone, just wax.

On the objection: Hofstadter would say that the outer message is a matter of convention, and conventions belong to communities; the reason we play records at 33⅓ is that we agree to. So the meaning of a record is relative to the community that decodes it, and the objection is half right. His position is that there is no fact of the matter about what the grooves mean, only facts about what a given community hears in them, which is close to the later Wittgenstein's claim that meaning is use — which is why I think the objection is right in spirit even though Hofstadter would resist the label of relativist.

For the comments, I want to know which claims are right and why they are right, which are wrong or only half-right, and, where a step fails to follow, which premise it needed and did not have; if a term has been quietly exchanged for a neighbouring one, name both. If the answer is right for the wrong reason somewhere, say so. And please say explicitly where the partial credit should land, part by part.
