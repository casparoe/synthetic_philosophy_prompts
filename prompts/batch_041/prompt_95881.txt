I am a novelist by trade — three books now, all of them circling the same problem of people who hold firm moral convictions and cannot say why — and somewhere in the writing of the last one I stopped being able to keep my own metaethics at arm's length. What I have arrived at, as plainly as I can put it, is a version of expressivism: when someone judges that an action is wrong, they are not reporting a fact about a special property of the act but doing something practical — adopting a settled policy or stance, being for or against in a way that bears on what they will do and what they will demand of others. I do not take this to demote morality to taste. I take the Frege-Geach problem to be solved on expressivist terms, by explaining the apparent logical relations among moral judgments as relations of incompatibility among the attitudes expressed rather than as relations among propositions; and I want the package to yield truth, knowledge, and real disagreement without conceding anything to the realist. One further commitment matters for what follows: I hold that negation, the conditional, and the rest of the logical vocabulary are used univocally in moral sentences and in descriptive ones — there is no special moral negation — which is precisely what makes the embedding problem bite, and I regard that as a feature rather than an embarrassment.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. Not a survey of the literature on my side, and not a list of the standard objections recited for the hundredth time, but an honest accounting of what would count against the view: which arguments, cases, or formal results should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, with the fatal separated from the merely awkward. I am especially interested in three fronts. First, whether the machinery for negation and for embeddings under conditionals, questions, and indirect speech can be built without either covertly smuggling propositions back in or distinguishing wrong from not-wrong by a difference that has no counterpart in the attitudes themselves — and if it cannot, whether that is a technical gap or a proof that the programme is misdescribed. Second, whether my ambition to have truth, knowledge, and genuine disagreement makes the view indistinguishable from a deflationary realism; if so, I want to know whether that is a reductio, a benign absorption, or simply evidence that my view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and I would rather hear the unflattering answer. Third, whether the analogy I have been leaning on — that the inconsistency between two moral judgments is the same kind of inconsistency as that between two intentions that cannot be jointly executed — is an asset or a liability, given that intentions are revisable at will in a way that moral judgments seem not to be, and given that intentions can be weak-willed in ways moral judgments apparently cannot.

I would also like you to say, separately, what an expressivist of my stripe would concede on the spot rather than what his critics say about him, since a position's own friends are usually the best judges of where it is thin. If it helps you to rank, I am moved most by an argument showing a case the view cannot describe at all, or a consequence I am plainly committed to and cannot stomach; I can live with awkwardness. And if your conclusion is that the position risks nothing — that every objection has been absorbed by a qualification and nothing could count against it — say so directly, and tell me whether that is a defect peculiar to this kind of view or simply the price of any view ambitious enough to want truth and logic on its side.
