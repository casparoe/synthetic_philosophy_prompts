Right — novelist here, about two thirds through a book set in and around the Florentine chancery, and I've got a problem I need audited before I write the last act. I don't want an explainer on Machiavelli. I want you to tell me whether the man I've built actually hangs together, because I suspect I've quietly got two of him sharing a body.

Here's the character I've decided I'm writing. He believes:

1. Virtù is one thing — a settled quality a person either has or hasn't, the power to stamp your own form onto events. And it's the same quality whether we're talking about a prince, a condottiere, or the founders of a republic.

2. Fortune is genuinely open. Not a name for the bit we couldn't have predicted — real contingency, things genuinely could have gone the other way.

3. He's a realist in the descriptive sense: he tells you what men actually do, not what they ought to. Any 'should' in his mouth is conditional — if you want to hold the state, then...

4. The prudent man builds the dikes before the flood. Preparation is what seperates the competent from the merely lucky, so a man of virtù is never really at fortune's mercy.

5. He'd rather have a republic than a prince, because laws and orders outlast any one man, however brilliant.

6. And yet he thinks every regime decays in the end — the cycle — so over the long run fortune gets everyone anyway.

I'm keeping the bit where fortune is a woman who has to be beaten and coerced, by the way. It's too good a line for the final chapter to lose, so whatever you tell me has to leave that standing.

Some of these must be pulling against each other and I can't see which. Two things I've half-noticed. First, (2) and (4) look like they contradict — if fortune is genuinely open then the dikes can't guarantee anything — but I've a feeling that one's my mistake rather than his. Second, there's the argument he makes somewhere in the Discourses, that a man can't change his nature, so the very qualities that carry him while the times suit him are what ruin him when they don't. I keep feeling that one is doing something to (1) but I haven't worked out what, and I've been avoiding it because I don't want to lose (1). Also, the novel's whole politics live in (5), so if anything above has to give I need to know what it does to that.

So: audit the lot. Which pairs are actually in contradiction, which only look like it, and for the real ones rank them by how much damage they do. For each genuine clash, tell me the cheapest thing that has to give and what it costs me — not the tidiest fix, the cheapest one that keeps the book working. And if (1) has to go, say so plainly and tell me what's left of the man.
