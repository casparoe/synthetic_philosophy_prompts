Write a poem of thirty to forty lines, in free verse, whose form does the work of a distinction I care about: between a fact that two people each privately know and a fact that is common knowledge between them.

The scene is the coordinated-attack problem transposed into a domestic register. Two people must act at the same moment — I would rather they be colleagues, or partners, than armies — and they exchange messages over a channel that can lose a message. She tells him the thing; he confirms that he has it; she confirms that she has his confirmation; and so on. Each reply can vouch only for the message immediately before it, so at every stage one of them is exactly one level short of the other, and the chain of knowing-that-the-other-knows has no final link. I want the poem to climb that chain in the open: one further level of nesting per stanza, so the sentences lengthen and coil as the tower rises, and I want it to abandon the climb by the fifth or sixth level — before the sentences turn unreadable — rather than completing it.

Then the turn, which should arrive as a change of register rather than as an announcement. Two things are true and both belong in the poem, in the poem's own language and not in mine. First, no finite number of further confirmations closes the gap, however many are sent. Second, and stranger: making a lost message less and less likely does not shrink the gap toward nothing — as the channel becomes more and more reliable, the situation does not slide into the one in which the fact is simply public. So common knowledge is not the far end of a tower of deeper and deeper mutual knowing; it is something of another kind. I want that discontinuity legible in the shape of the poem rather than stated in it.

What does close the gap is a public event: both of them hearing the same bell, seeing the same blackboard, catching each other's eye as the same thing happens, each perceiving that the other perceives it. Let the poem end there, on a short, plain, un-nested statement of the shared act — no explanation, no summary of what has just happened, no closing couplet telling the reader what to conclude.

Keep the diction plain; the difficulty should live in the nesting and the break, not in ornament. A reader who has never met any of this should feel the tower rise and the ground arrive; a reader who works on it should find the discontinuity in the poem's architecture. Name no theories, no authors, and no technical terms.
