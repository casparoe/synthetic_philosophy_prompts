I've been trying to work out when it is actually true that two people did something together, as opposed to each of them separately doing something and the two things adding up to a result. I have a run of gut verdicts and I want to know what rule, if any, they add up to, and whether one of them has to be sacrificed.

Here they are. Two flatmates who never discuss it each wash up the dishes the other left out, and finish at the same moment: not something they did together. Two people carrying a heavy wardrobe down a staircase, one at each end, calling out "step" to each other and adjusting to each other's pace: plainly together. Two strangers who approach each other on a narrow pavement and do the mirrored shuffle, each stepping aside for the other: not together, even though every move each makes is a response to the other and each knows it. Two friends who go to a concert neither of them actually wants to attend, each going only because the other would feel let down by a cancellation, and each knowing this: I still say they went to the concert together. Two rival bakeries on the same street that each set their loaves at the same price because each expects the other to, and each knows the other reasons the same way: not doing anything together, however much each is acting on the other's expected move. And two people at a party who start clapping a rhythm, each hearing the other and coming in on the beat, with nobody having proposed it: by the third round I want to say they are doing it together, though there was never any agreement.

The pair I cannot get to sit still is the reluctant concert-goers and the rival bakeries. As far as I can see, each of the four people is doing what they are doing because of what they expect the others to do, and everyone knows it, yet one case is a joint outing and the other is two shops minding their own business. So what is doing the work — some promise or obligation the concert-goers have and the bakers do not? If so, how do the clappers count, since they never promised anything? My own stab at a rule was this: two people are doing something together just when each is doing their part because they expect the other to do theirs, and each knows the other expects the same. That seems to make the bakers and the pavement-shufflers joint actors, which I do not accept, and it makes the clappers joint only by accident. Can you give me the principle, or the two or three rival principles, that best fit these verdicts, tell me precisely which clause of each is doing the work, and say what it rules about cases I have not mentioned — in particular a long-distance couple who each quietly save money toward the same trip without ever mentioning it, and who later discover what the other has been doing; before the discovery I cannot decide whether they were saving for the trip together, and afterwards I am sure they were, which worries me, since nothing they did changed. If no principle keeps all my verdicts, tell me which one you would give up. I would also like you to say how confident you are in what you claim, and to flag the single point you are least sure about.
