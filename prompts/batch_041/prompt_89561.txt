I am a novelist at work on a book of ideas — a philosophical thriller, if that is not too grand a phrase — whose central figure is a theorist who believes she has found the one correct way to learn from experience. Give her any stream of observations, she claims, and her rule will converge on the truth about whatever process produced them; what the rest of us call good judgment about simplicity she can derive rather than assume. (I gather the modern versions of this idea travel under the names Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity, though I confess I can never keep them straight, and I would rather you tell me how the pieces fit than trust my summary of them.) Her antagonists — a statistician, a computer scientist, and a philosopher of science, who between them get most of the good lines — insist that the whole edifice is either empty or impossible. I want their arguments, and hers, to be technically credible, and for that I need to know what an account of this kind would actually have to accomplish before anyone should concede that it has settled the problem of induction.

So please act as an expert on algorithmic information theory, formal epistemology, and the foundations of inductive inference, and write out the desiderata: what any adequate formal account of learning from experience, and of the place of simplicity within it, must deliver. Think carefully first, and begin by brainstorming the space of possible requirements if that helps, because I care far more about the completeness of the list and the motivation behind each item than about a tidy final form. For each requirement I want the case or consideration that drives it — the phenomenon it is meant to capture, and the counterexample or absurdity that follows if you drop it — and I want the requirements that pull against one another made explicit. I have a suspicion, which may well be ignorant, that the demand for a predictor provably as good as any computable alternative sits badly with the demand that the predictor be something we could actually compute or approximate, and that the demand that the verdict not depend on how we happen to describe or encode the competing hypotheses sits badly with the demand that it be well-defined in the first place. If some subset of your requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so plainly — that is the part I most want to see — and tell me which single requirement, enforced strictly, eliminates the greatest number of otherwise attractive positions. You may close with a short assessment of how the leading contenders fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves; I will do the scoring in the manuscript. Please spare me a bibliography or a reading list, since I have no way to check it, and write in prose I can actually read aloud, not in a flurry of notation.
