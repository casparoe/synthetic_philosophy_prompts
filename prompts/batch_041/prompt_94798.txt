I've spent the term defending a particular reconstruction of Aristotle's account of natural slavery in Politics I, and before I commit it to a long seminar paper I want to stress-test it rather than merely present it. On my reading, the doctrine is not just a rationalization of Athenian practice but a substantive, empirically answerable thesis with three separable components: a deficiency claim — that there exists a class of human beings who are lacking in the deliberative faculty to the degree required to direct their own lives well; a dependence claim — that such people live better, and more fully, under the direction of someone who possesses that faculty in full; and a permissibility claim — that it is therefore just, and not merely expedient, for the former to be owned and directed by the latter. I take (ii) and (iii) to get off the ground only if (i) is true, and I take (i) to be exactly the sort of claim that evidence about human psychology could in principle settle. I also try to take seriously two things Aristotle himself seems to grant: that nature's intention in these matters frequently fails to be realized in practice, and the comparison he draws between the natural slave and the child, whose deliberative faculty is present but undeveloped rather than absent.

What I want from you is a map of this reconstruction's exposure. Which arguments, observations, or conceptual results would count against it — and which of those would count against my reading rather than against Aristotle? I am especially interested in defeaters that a sympathetic defender would themselves have to concede, not just the ones critics enjoy rehearsing. For instance: does the doctrine survive if the deficiency claim turns out to concern a threshold or a spectrum rather than a natural kind? What follows if the deliberative deficiency is socially produced rather than natural — is that fatal, or merely awkward? Does the analogy with children cut for the doctrine or against it, given that children are not held to be fit for ownership? And does anything in Aristotle's own commitments — say, about the polis as a community of free and equal citizens, or about the conditions under which slavery by convention is unjust — render the doctrine not merely false but internally incoherent, such that it collapses into a rival account of what slavery is?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing those the defender can absorb by qualification from those that would force the abandonment of a component, and tell me honestly whether my reconstruction has become so elastic that it risks nothing at all — and if so, whether that is a defect in the reading or simply what any charitable reconstruction of a dead philosopher will look like. It would help me most if you could also say what evidence would actually have moved Aristotle himself: what would have had to be true of the people around him, or of human beings generally, for him to conclude that no such class exists.
