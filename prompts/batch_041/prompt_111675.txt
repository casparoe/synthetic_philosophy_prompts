I am a novelist, and I am at work on a story that I can only get right if I get a piece of philosophy exactly right.

The story is about a refusal. A woman is offered information — reliable, free, and directly relevant to a decision she must make — and she declines it. I want her to be right to decline, and I want the rightness to be structural rather than psychological: not squeamishness, not superstition, not a secret wish for one answer over another, and not doubt about the information itself. I want the refusal to be rational, and I want its rationality to come from a failure of one of the premises of a theorem that is usually stated as though it had no exceptions.

The theorem is the one commonly attributed to I. J. Good: for a Bayesian agent who maximizes expected utility, the expected value of costless, reliable information is never negative. The proof is almost embarrassing in its brevity. Whatever the information turns out to say, the agent can choose the act she would have chosen without it; she can act as though she had never opened the envelope. From this it seems to follow that a rational person can never be worse off for knowing more, and that the only reasons to refuse knowledge are the reasons we are ashamed of.

I want a story that takes that theorem seriously enough to find the seam in it. Choose one of the following three failures of premise, and build the story on it.

First, the agent cannot ignore what she has learned. The proof requires that the act she would have chosen in ignorance remain available to her after she knows. If she knows herself well enough to know that the knowledge will act on her — that she will not be able to bring herself to do the thing she had decided to do — then the counterfactual act is not in her repertoire, and the theorem's guarantee does not reach her. Her refusal is not a refusal of information; it is a refusal to become someone who cannot choose as she has chosen.

Second, the information is not a passive partition of a fixed world. The theorem treats the observation as a reading of a state that is already there, unaffected by being read. But if the state includes other people's beliefs and intentions, then being known to be informed changes the state; and if the information concerns her own future conduct, then her learning it may be one of the causes of that conduct. In such a case the act she would have chosen without the information may not exist in the world where she has it. She is not choosing between knowing and not knowing; she is choosing between two different worlds, and the theorem has not priced the difference.

Third, she cannot bind her future self. The theorem computes the value of information using the preferences the agent has now, and it assumes that the agent who receives the information is the same agent who will act on it. If the information will change what she cares about, and if she cannot make her future self honor the commitments of her present self, then the benefit the theorem promises is a benefit to a person who will not be there to collect it. Her refusal is a refusal to hand her life over to a stranger with her name.

I want the philosophy carried by the situation, not by exposition. There may be one scene of argument — a friend, an actuary, a doctor, a rival who puts the theorem's case in plain language and is answered — but the answer must be concrete, and the rest of the story must show it rather than say it. By the end, a reader who has never heard of the theorem should be able to state in a sentence which premise failed and why. No character should speak in the vocabulary of decision theory.

Constraints:

- Third person limited, close to the woman who refuses. No omniscient commentary, no authorial gloss on the philosophy.
- Realist mode. The information should be of the ordinary reliable kind — a test result, an expert forecast, a records search, a professional's judgment — and the story must not invite us to doubt it. The refusal must not be epistemic.
- The information must bear on a decision she will actually make, and the story must not turn into a story about fate or determinism. She has real options; the question is what knowing does to her grip on them.
- Do not resolve the story by revealing that she was wrong to refuse, or that the information was a trap, or that she secretly wanted a particular answer. The refusal must remain defensible at the end, even if its cost is visible.
- Let the cost be visible. I want the reader to feel the pull of the theorem — to see what she gives up — and to understand why she gives it up anyway.
- 2,000 to 3,500 words. Give it a title. No epigraphs, no quotations, no citations.

I am less interested in a clever twist than in a scene in which a person, knowing exactly what she is doing, closes a door on knowledge and is not diminished by it.
