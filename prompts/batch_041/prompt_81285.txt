Write a satirical piece in the form of an official guidance document issued by a fictional public regulator — call it the Bureau for the Prioritisation of Health States (it is a British agency, hence the spelling), a statutory body that maintains a single national ranking of medical conditions for the purposes of rationing care. The document, of roughly 800–900 words, should be in the deadpan register of a real regulator: numbered clauses, a definitions section, a table of states, a brief "Frequently Asked Questions", and at least one footnote signed by the Bureau's philosophical adviser, an invented figure whose intervention makes matters worse rather than better. The document is responding to a difficulty of the Bureau's own making. The Bureau ranks conditions by pairwise comparison, asking of any two states of health which one the public would rather endure. Applied honestly, this method generates a chain: a constant mild toothache, then a state marginally worse, then one marginally worse again, and so on through several hundred imperceptible steps, until it arrives at a state of unrelieved pain that nobody would hesitate to call the worst of the lot. The Bureau's current guidance therefore implies that the mild toothache is better than the agony, and a later revision of the pairwise data has produced a cycle in which each of three states is ranked above the next. The piece should never break frame, never signal that it is joking, and never resolve the difficulty with a platitude; if the document does resolve it, the resolution must follow from one of the positions below rather than from a gesture at "balance".

The comedy has to come from getting the philosophy exactly right, so that someone who works on value theory laughs in recognition rather than wincing. Give voice, in the document's own bureaucratic idiom, to at least four of the live responses: an internal faction holding that "preferable to" is simply not transitive and that the Bureau should say so, publish, and move on; a faction that bites the bullet and insists the ranking is correct, the public's reluctance being mere squeamishness; a faction claiming that for very close comparisons there is no fact of the matter about which state is better, so the guidance is not false but incomplete; a faction proposing a third relation standing between "better" and "worse" for states that are neither; and a faction content to keep the pairwise results provided only that no cycles appear. Work in the practical stakes: a "patient choice pathway" in which someone who willingly trades state A for B, B for C, and C for A ends up both worse off and poorer, with the Bureau's assessment of this outcome as an artefact of the model or a documented feature of the framework; the question of what the Bureau owes such a patient; and the difference between abandoning transitivity and abandoning completeness, which the document should at some point conflate and at another point carefully distinguish. A short quoted remark from the invented philosophical adviser — a sentence defending the Bureau's procedure, not a quotation from any real text — would be welcome.

The target of the satire is the practice, not any person: the habit of building policy on pairwise comparisons and then treating the resulting relation as though it must be a linear order, together with the institutional instinct for papering over conceptual trouble in a footnote. Aim the piece at a reader who knows the territory, and keep it readable for one who does not; the jokes should be dry enough that a specialist finds them fair. Please write the deliverable in British English, and do not include citations, reading lists, or references to real published work.
