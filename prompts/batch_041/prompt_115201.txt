I cover logic and the philosophy of mathematics for a general-readership science desk, and for the past year I've been reporting on a split inside the small community of logicians who work on paraconsistent logic — logics in which a contradiction does not entail everything. Two camps inside that community have been arguing past each other for decades, and I want to commission the document they should have written together: a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration that both camps could put their names to.

Here is the split as I understand it, and it is the split the statement has to address. The first camp are dialetheists. They hold that the correct logic is paraconsistent and that some contradictions are true — that the Liar and the paradoxes of naive comprehension are not puzzles to be dissolved but sound arguments whose conclusions should be accepted, so that the law of non-contradiction, read as a universal prohibition, is itself false. The second camp accept the logic but not the claim about truth. They agree that explosion is a bad principle, that a contradiction must not entail everything, and that paraconsistent consequence is the right regimentation of reasoning with inconsistent information — in databases, in inconsistent legal codes, in scientific theories that have not yet been repaired — but they hold that no contradiction is true, and so treat the paradoxes as showing that something in the derivation must be given up rather than that truth itself is inconsistent. Neither camp thinks the other is merely confused; each thinks the other's position is unstable in a specific way, and each has a diagnosis of where the other goes wrong.

Act as a philosopher of logic who has spent years drafting joint statements and mediating disputes between formal philosophers, and who knows how to write a sentence that neither side can wriggle out of. Before you write anything, work through the dispute in your head: which claims are genuinely common ground, which are common ground only in wording that is secretly slanted, and which apparent disagreements are really disagreements about what a word means. Then produce the document, in the voice of a text jointly authored by the two camps ("we"), with exactly these sections, in this order:

Drafting notes (150–200 words): the candidate agreed claims you considered and discarded, and why; and the two or three formulations you found hardest to make non-slanted.

Framing (200–250 words): the dispute stated in neutral terms both camps would recognise as fair, including what each side takes to be at stake if it is wrong.

Agreed claims (numbered list, 12–18 items, grouped under subheadings): everything both sides can sign. Cover at least: the consequence relation and what explosion is; the status of the paradoxes as valid or invalid arguments; what a solution to a paradox owes us; the role of classical reasoning in consistent domains; how a theory of truth should relate to a theory of meaning; and the methodology both sides accept for evaluating a logic. Each item must be a claim a committed member of each camp would sign without crossing their fingers, and must not be trivially true or merely definitional.

Disagreements (table, 5–8 rows): one row per genuine disagreement. Columns: the issue; the dialetheist's own formulation, written so that a dialetheist would accept the wording as theirs; the other camp's own formulation, likewise; and the type of disagreement (formal, conceptual, about truth, about method, or merely verbal).

Cruxes (3–5 entries, 100–150 words each): the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each, say why it is load-bearing, name a concrete case, argument, or formal result that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about how that case comes out. Include, among them, the question of whether a glut theorist can express the thought that a sentence is simply not true as opposed to false, and the question of what has to be given up to keep a usable conditional once the paradoxes are admitted.

What is merely verbal (150–200 words): if part of the dispute is about words rather than about the world, say which part, name the expression whose meaning is at issue, and give the two readings. If nothing is merely verbal, argue that, and explain why the obvious candidate does not qualify.

Conditions for signature (100–150 words per camp): for each side, what it would take to make that side regard the whole exercise as a failure, and what each side would count as a refutation of its own position.

Residual disagreement (100–150 words): what remains once every crux is settled in one direction or the other, and whether it is a disagreement anyone outside the field should care about.

Constraints. Do not include citations, footnotes, reading lists, or the names of authors, papers, or named theorems; if you want to invoke a known argument, reconstruct it in plain prose. Assume you have no access to any literature. Introduce technical notation only if you need it, and never in a way that requires the reader to know a particular formalism in advance. Do not resolve the dispute or declare a winner; the deliverable is a text both camps can sign, not an adjudication. Do not hedge by calling something a matter of semantics unless you have named the expression in dispute and given both readings of it. If you find that some claim cannot be made both non-vacuous and acceptable to both sides, say so explicitly in the section where it belongs, and say what that failure reveals about the dispute.
