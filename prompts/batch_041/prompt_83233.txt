I am a sixth-form student preparing for a school debate on whether unemployment benefits should be made conditional on actively seeking work, and there is one line that keeps ending every argument: 'work is the best route out of poverty'. A classmate used it last week to shut down my objection to sanctioning people who miss appointments — if work is the best route out, he said, then pushing people towards it is plainly right, and anyone who objects is just excusing idleness. I am fairly sure the slogan cannot carry that weight, but I cannot say precisely where the trouble lies.

I would be grateful if you could set out the distinct claims the sentence could be making, ordered from those that are nearly true by definition to those that are genuinely contentious, and give a verdict on each: which are trivial, which are defensible, which are simply false. I am particularly unsure about the following. When it says 'best', best compared with what, and measured along which dimension — likelihood of escaping poverty, speed, durability, cost to the public purse, or something about self-respect? Is it a claim about individuals or about the country as a whole, and would it survive the shift from one to the other? Does calling work a 'route' quietly assume a fixed destination, and a starting point that many people are not at? And is it really a description of how poverty ends, or a normative claim about how the poor ought to be treated dressed up as a fact? I would also like to know how it relates to the nineteenth-century poor-law principle of less eligibility, which I met in history and which seems to say something similar in much harsher terms.

Most important of all, please say which of the readings would actually license my classmate's conclusion about sanctions, and what further premises he would need to add before the slogan could do that work. Please answer in British English.
