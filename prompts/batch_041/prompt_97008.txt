Ok so I've ended up on the little working group that's drafting our museum's restitution policy, and I keep noticing that everyone argues case by case — the Marbles, the Benin bronzes, some Nazi-looted drawing — without ever saying what an account of this is supposed to deliver. Before we fight over individual objects I want the spec. Give it to me as a numbered list, exactly five requirements, one or two sentences each, no intro and nothing after the fifth one. Each item should name the case or fact that forces the requirement, and say whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's preference; if two of the five can't both be met by anything, say so inside the item where the clash shows up.

Things the list has to survive, because our cases are messier than the headlines. The Marbles are one dismembered monument while the bronzes are thousands of objects spread across hundreds of collections, so a theory that treats return as a single act will get one of them wrong. The 1970 UNESCO convention only bites on transfers after it entered into force, so it does nothing for 1801 or 1897, and the British Museum Act 1963 basically forbids its trustees to let go of anything — a court applied that even to Nazi-looted drawings — so the theory has to be honest about the gap between law and morality without just ratifying whatever the law currently says. It has to say who the claimant is when the polity that lost the object wasn't a state yet or doesn't exist in that form anymore (Greece in 1801, the Kingdom of Benin annexed in 1897, Nigeria as a state a twentieth-century construction), and it has to take a position on the case I keep worrying about, where a bronze is sent back and the palace and the federal commission in the source country both say it's theirs. It should handle objects that left through trade rather than force, or that someone inside the community sold, and the small regional museum where one Benin plaque is the only non-European thing in the building and the local schools teach with it. And the encyclopedic-museum defense (2002, the big universal ones) has to be answered on its merits — access, comparison, conservation — and you should say what would count as evidence against whichever requirement ends up doing the most work.

Yeah I know, that's a lot to squeeze into five. Seperate items, keep them tight.
