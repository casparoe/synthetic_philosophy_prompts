I run a small forecasting club, and for the past year I have kept a notebook of paired comparisons — two members, the same question, and a note on which of them I think is doing better epistemically — in the hope that the pattern would eventually tell me what I actually believe about epistemic value. It hasn't, and I would like you to find the principle, or to convince me that there isn't one.

The entries I trust most are these. Nadia was 0.9 in a proposition that turned out true, while Omar was 0.6 in the same true proposition: Nadia is doing better. Priya was 0.5 in a true proposition and Quinn was 0.5 in a false one, with no difference in how either arrived at her number: Priya's state is the better one. Zoe holds 0.8 in p and 0.8 in not-p; on p itself she is closer to the truth than the members who split 0.5 and 0.5, yet I think she is doing worse epistemically than they are. Those three feel settled. The next three I cannot fit together at all. Uma began at 0.1 in a proposition that is true and, after genuine inquiry, ended at 0.8; Tom began at 0.9 in the same proposition and never moved. Tom's final number is nearer the truth, but I am confident Uma is the better epistemic agent. Vera forecasts only questions she is already sure of and has a better record than Will, who takes on hard questions and misses more often; I would not trade Will for Vera. Xavier copies the club's best forecaster's numbers each week without following any of her reasoning, and his numbers are exactly as accurate as hers; I do not think he is doing as well epistemically as she is.

My best attempt at a principle, which I suspect is too crude, is simply this: an agent's epistemic standing is the sum, over the propositions she has credences about, of how close her credence is to the truth value, so that accuracy is the only epistemic good and the whole credence function is the unit of assessment. That gets Nadia, Priya, and Zoe, but it ranks Uma below Tom, it ranks Xavier exactly level with the woman he copies, and it ranks Vera above Will — three verdicts I will not give up without a fight. So: is there a repaired version of the accuracy idea — accuracy plus a second good, or accuracy relativized to something other than the truth value, or accuracy applied to something other than the final credence function — that delivers all six verdicts, or must I surrender one? If the repair requires a second epistemic good, say precisely what it is, how it is measured, and how it trades off against accuracy when the two conflict. And tell me what your principle predicts for an entry I have not written down: an agent who is both accurate and much improved, but who got there only because a coin flip decided whether she would look at her evidence at all, and the coin came up heads.
