I teach an upper-level philosophy of science course (mostly third and fourth years, plus a couple of grad students who sit in) and I'm writing the final. I am so tired of the thought-experiment question that everyone answers with the same two moves -- "a thought experiment is just an argument in disguise" vs. "no, it's a genuine experiment you run on an ideal world" -- and then stops. This year I want a question that can't be answered that way, so I wrote my own case to hang it on, one they definately haven't seen. Here it is, roughly: imagine a universe identical to ours except that exactly one particle is a "wildcard" -- its next position is genuinely undetermined, no law fixes it -- and everything else is fully deterministic. Call it the Wildcard Universe. The catch is that we don't know whether our universe is the Wildcard Universe or not. A student in my office hours claimed the Wildcard case "proves" that determinism is irrelevant: even if our world is like that, one undetermined particle can't change anything that matters, so all the hand-wringing about free will and predictability is empty. I think that's exactly the kind of thought experiment that looks like it yields knowledge and doesn't, but I want the exam to make them work out for themselves whether it does and why.

So please write me one essay question, pitched at that level, that uses the Wildcard case to get at the general question of when (if ever) a thought experiment about a merely possible world can give us knowledge about the actual world. I want the question to force them to (i) actually reconstruct the Wildcard argument rather than gesture at it, (ii) say precisely what it can and can't establish, and (iii) connect it to the general issue -- including what someone who thinks thought experiments are experiments in their own right would have to say about this case, and whether that defense survives. Give me a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a top answer has to make, the marks for each, and the specific mistake a typical B answer makes at each of those points. Time limit is 90 minutes, closed book. Please don't have them cite anyone -- they can't look anything up and I don't want name-dropping standing in for the argument, so no reading lists or references anywhere. End with a final line that starts "Crux:" and says in one sentence what a B answer gets wrong that an A answer gets right.
