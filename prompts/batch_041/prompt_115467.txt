I'm assembling a short piece of seminar humor and would like a joke about the socialist calculation debate — the exchange between Mises and Hayek on one side and Lange on the other. It should be funny to people who actually know the arguments, which means the punchline has to turn on the real content of the dispute rather than on generic jokes about shortages, bureaucracy, or Soviet inefficiency. The angle I care about is the gap between the two anti-socialist arguments, which are often run together but are not the same. One is a claim about rational calculation: without market prices for the means of production, a planner has no common denominator in which to compare alternative uses of resources, so the allocation problem has no solution to compute. The other is a claim about knowledge: the information needed is dispersed, local, and largely tacit, and does not exist anywhere in a centralized form ready to be fed into anyone's equations. The market-socialist reply — a central board that sets prices and then corrects them by trial and error as shortages and surpluses appear — is naturally read as an answer to the first argument, and the joke should bring out why it leaves the second one essentially untouched, or, if you prefer, why a board that discovers prices by watching what people do has quietly become the thing it was meant to replace. A short dialogue or scene is fine — a planner, an economist, and a board member, say — but keep the humor in the logic rather than in the setting. Assume the audience knows the positions but not the literature, so no citations, titles, or name-dropping are needed, and try not to make either side look merely foolish: the joke should land because the arguments are as strong as they are.
