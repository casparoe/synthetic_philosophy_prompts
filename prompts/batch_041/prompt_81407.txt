I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and the one habit I have kept from them is this: when my stated beliefs and my actual verdicts come apart, I write the verdicts down and work out what principle they imply, rather than adjusting the verdicts to fit the belief. That is what I am asking for here, on a subject where I have never once managed it.

By conviction I am a consequentialist. I do not believe in karma as a causal ledger, in rebirth, or in any form of cosmic bookkeeping. But when I write down what I actually think about particular acts, the verdicts come out looking like something out of Buddhist practice, and I cannot tell whether that is because the practice got the structure of morality right or because I absorbed the intuitions without the reasons. I do not want an answer about what any tradition says, and I do not want texts cited at me — I cannot check them and it would not help. I want a principle that fits the verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought of, along with an honest account of what it costs me. I would rather be told that one of my verdicts has to go than be handed a principle with a new clause for every case.

Here are the verdicts.

1. A pharmacist lies to a customs officer about what is in a parcel so that a medicine gets through to a patient who will die without it. Clearly fine. I would do it without hesitation.
2. The same pharmacist steals the medicine from a warehouse, because the owner will not sell at a price the patient can pay. Also fine, though less comfortable.
3. A doctor, at the family's request, gives a dying woman a dose that ends her life. Wrong, and I am not moved by the fact that she would have died in a few days, in pain.
4. A doctor lies to a confused patient about what a procedure is, so that she will accept a treatment that will save her. Fine.
5. One person gives away most of her savings anonymously; another gives the same sum at a gala where she is thanked for it. The first is better, and not because the second's money does less good — the money does the same good.
6. Someone is privately pleased that a rival's project has failed, does nothing about it, and tells no one. Bad, and I do not think that is only a matter of what such pleasure tends to lead to.
7. The same true words about a friend's manuscript, said to him alone, are right; said in front of two other people, are wrong. Nothing about the words or their effect on him changes.
8. A man drinks alone at home on a night when nothing comes of it. Not wrong at all, though the precepts I half-absorbed say otherwise.
9. I eat meat and think it is wrong, and I do not think it would stop being wrong if the animals were raised painlessly and the farming were carbon-neutral — which suggests the wrongness is not located in the suffering.

The pair I cannot reconcile is 1 and 3, or rather the classes they stand for. I will break the rule against deceiving to prevent one death without a second thought, and I will not break the rule against killing to prevent several, even when I am sure the arithmetic favors it. Cases 2 and 3 are in tension in the same way: taking what is not mine is available to me as a solution; killing is not.

The only thing I have come up with that fits most of this is what I have been calling the consent clause. What matters to me is not how much harm comes about, but whether the agent had to consent to that harm as the thing she was doing, rather than merely foresee it as something her doing would bring about. Under that clause, killing one to save five is out, because the death is the instrument; lying to save a life is in, because the deception is not the point of what I am doing, only the shape my speech has to take. But when I say it out loud it sounds less like a principle than like a restatement of the verdicts. It also seems to misfire: I think an engineer who ships a system she believes has a small chance of being used to kill many people is not off the hook just because that harm is a foreseen side effect rather than her aim, and I think someone privately glad at a rival's failure has done something bad without doing anything at all.

What I want from you:

- the principle, or the two or three candidates, stated precisely, with each clause attached to the case that forces it;
- whether what I have is a form of consequentialism — with side constraints, or a threshold, or a restriction on what may be intended — or a genuinely non-consequentialist view that happens to agree with Buddhist practice on many cases; if the latter, say exactly where the arithmetic is overridden;
- the verdicts of the best candidate on cases I have not listed, including at least one I will not like;
- which of my nine verdicts I should give up, and what I would be buying by giving it up.

If the honest answer is that no single principle fits and the best I can do is a list of clauses, say so, and say what the list would have to contain.
