I'm head of history at a moderately traditional secondary school, and this year I've been handed the commemeration day address. Normally it's twenty minutes of the usual "our founding values" stuff and nobody listens, but this year it's actually loaded, because the governors have agreed to consult on renaming Wainwright Hall. Wainwright is the Victorian benefactor who paid for most of the west wing and the chapel, and his money came partly out of a family firm that was up to its neck in things I won't rehearse here. There's been a petition from the sixth form and a counter-petition from a group of alumni. The room on the day will be roughly a third alumni and donors (mostly over sixty, genuinely fond of the place, quite allergic to being lectured), a third current pupils (the ones who signed, plus alot who didn't and are bored of it), and staff. The staff room has been having the usual Jefferson-and-Churchill arguments for weeks and everyone reaches for the same defence. I want to give an address about how we should be judging him at all, not about what the governors should do with the name — that's not my call and I don't want to pre-empt the consultation.

The bit I keep getting tangled in is the man-of-his-time defence. The reading group I run ended up splitting it in two, and I've found the split genuinely useful: there's the availability question — could he have known better, was the objection actually on the table in the intellectual world he moved in? — and the uptake question — were there in fact contemporaries making exactly that objection, and did he simply ignore them? I think the defence is decent on the first and collapses on the second, and what bugs me about most of these addresses, on both sides, is that they do the second while sounding like they're doing the first. I'd like the speech to be honest about that, and honest that the two questions come apart, without it turning into a philosophy seminar with a hymn at the end.

So: could you write the address? Seven or eight minutes when read aloud, and it has to work for the donors without flattering them and for the pupils without recruiting them. It shouldn't resolve the renaming — it should leave the room with a clearer sense of what they'd actually be deciding about. Please don't put any quotations in it; I'm the head of history and I'm not going to stand up and misquote somebody, so anything that would want quote marks should just be said in my own words. No bullet points, no "firstly, secondly", and it should read as British English (we're in the UK and "gotten" would be noticed). Also — I know this is fussy — please don't let it end on a rousing note about learning from the past, it's the one move everyone in that hall will have heard before.
