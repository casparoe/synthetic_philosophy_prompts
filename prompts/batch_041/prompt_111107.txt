I have drafted a short paper on interpersonal utility comparison and would be grateful for your comments, particularly on whether the central distinction is sound and whether the argument commits any error. Please lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.

The draft:

**Comparison Without a Common Currency**

Work on interpersonal utility comparison usually begins by asking what kind of measurement utility admits — ordinal or cardinal, and if cardinal, whether the unit and the zero point can be compared across persons. I argue that this measurement framing, whatever its use for social choice, obscures the deeper question. What matters is not whether two people's utilities share a unit but whether there are reasons that count in favor of one allocation over another when the people affected are different people. Call this the reasons-first view.

On the reasons-first view, comparison is not a matter of reading off a common numerical scale. It is a matter of determining whether the considerations favoring one person's gain and the considerations favoring another's can be weighed against each other at all, and if so by what standard. Two people's situations can be compared even when their preferences are not commensurable in any single currency, provided the reasons on each side fall under a common standard of assessment. I call this the parity constraint: comparison is possible whenever there is a standard under which the reasons on both sides are assessable, even if that standard assigns no numerical magnitudes.

This dissolves a familiar worry. If utility is only ordinally measurable, we are told, interpersonal comparison is impossible. But that inference assumes comparison requires a common cardinal unit. The parity constraint requires only a common standard of assessment, which may be ordinal or even non-numerical. The measurement problem is therefore a red herring; the real work is done by the reasons. And because the parity constraint needs no common unit, it also needs no cardinal comparability: ordinal comparability of levels is sufficient, since the constraint itself supplies the standard of assessment.

The view also accommodates the intuitive cases. We can say that a small gain for a badly off person may outweigh a large gain for a well off person — not because we have measured their utilities on a common scale, but because the reasons of priority and sufficiency are assessable under a common standard.

I close by suggesting that the standard taxonomy of comparability assumptions, which distinguishes level from unit comparability and ordinal from cardinal measurement, is less fundamental than it appears.
