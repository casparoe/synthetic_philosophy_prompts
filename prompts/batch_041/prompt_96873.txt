In a long-run economic history seminar I sit in on, the metaphor everyone reaches for is a horse race. Europe and China, on this picture, ran neck and neck for centuries, with China leading for most of the distance, and then one runner — Britain — pulled away. A colleague presses the image hard: there was a single track, a single finish line, and a genuine lead, so "why Britain and not China?" is a question about a race, and its only deep answer is that races have winners — someone had to break first. On his view the contrastive question dissolves into the general one of why the field was capable of running that race at all. I want the analogy audited, not the historiography rehearsed.

First, give the correspondence table. What plays the role of the track, the lanes, the start, the lead, the finish line, the runners, the rules, and the official — and for each, say whether the historical case actually supplies an analogue or whether the metaphor has to invent one. Second, list the disanalogies and sort them: which ones matter for the conclusion my colleague draws, and which are merely picturesque. Third, say what the race picture imports that the case does not obviously contain — in particular whether it presupposes that the outcome space was fixed in advance, that the competitors were the same entities throughout the contest, and that industrializing first was a determinate prize rather than a description we apply backwards to whoever industrialized. Fourth, keep two distinctions separate and use them explicitly: the distinction between a level question (why was the whole field positioned to industrialize?) and a contrast question (why one competitor rather than another?), where a factor common to both sides of a contrast cannot explain the contrast however causally important it is in general; and the distinction between a token question (why this case?) and a type question (why cases of this kind?), as "why did I get the disease and not my brother?" differs from "why do men of my age get it?" Then say which parts of my colleague's conclusion survive the audit and which were being carried by the race picture all along. Finally, another member of the group proposes a different image: not a race but nucleation — a supercooled liquid that must crystallize somewhere, where the interesting question is why the liquid was supercooled at all and the residual "why here rather than there" question is small and perhaps unrewarding. Assess that alternative on the same terms, and if you have a better image, give it.
