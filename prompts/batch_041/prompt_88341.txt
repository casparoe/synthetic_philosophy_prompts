I'm in a small reading group that's been chewing through population ethics and I keep getting into it with a freind of mine who holds what he calls a lexical threshold negative utilitarian view: suffering isn't just weightier than pleasure, it's lexically weightier, so above some threshold of suffering no amount of pleasure buys it back, while below the threshold the two trade off at some rate. I want to put a designed case in front of him instead of another argument, and I'd like you to build it. Give me two outcomes, A and B, that are identical in every aggregate respect -- same number of people, same lifespans, same total quantity of suffering, same total quantity of pleasure -- differing only in how the suffering is spread, such that in A somebody's suffering sits above the threshold while in B it's spread thin enough that no individual crosses it. Then do the demonstration: what the threshold view says under the per-person reading (a life whose suffering is over the threshold can't be redeemed by that person's own pleasure) and under the population reading (only the total amount of suffering matters, so two outcomes with the same totals come out equal). I want the two readings to actually come apart in their verdicts, not just in emphasis, and I want you to be honest if one of them turns out to be blind to the case by construction -- that's part of what I'm trying to show him. Also give me the verdict of a plain commensurating totalist, pain and pleasure trading at a fixed rate and summed over persons, and tell me whether any exchange rate at all lets it reproduce the per-person threshold verdict; if none does, say exactly why. Keep it realistic -- ordinary lives, chronic illness or grinding work or grief, no sci-fi devices, and nothing that stipulates its own verdicts.

Two more things off the same case. First, add a third outcome C that's just like A except the one person's suffering lands barely under the threshold, and use it to show how little the world has to change for the view's verdict to swing -- then tell me whether that swing survives if you swap the lexical step for a very large finite penalty instead of an infinite one, and whether the ordering you end up with can be represented by any real-valued value function at all. Second, the part I actually care about: what does the case show about whether the view can keep both its lexicality and any sensitivity to distribution, which one it has to give up, and what a defender would say to try to keep both (that the threshold is meant to apply within lives and never across them, or that value isn't even the right currency here)? Finish by naming the one feature of the case doing all the work, what happens when you take that feature out, and whether the whole thing collapses back into an ordinary trade-off problem once you do.
