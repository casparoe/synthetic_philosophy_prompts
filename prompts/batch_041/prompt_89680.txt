I'm on the board of a small foundation that has to make one big bet a year: put a large chunk of our reserves into a single high-variance program, or spread it thin across safer ones. Our analyst won't give us point probabilities anymore -- we get ranges, and sometimes the ranges are so wide they seem to swallow the decision. She says that's the honest answer, not a failure, and she wants us to pick whichever option has the best worst-case expected value, computed only after widening every interval to cover our disagreement about which model is right. A second board member says that when the two options' intervals overlap we shouldn't call it a tie but should treat the choice as unsettled by expected value and decide on other grounds. I want the most important implications of actually running the foundation this way, not the philosophy-seminar version: what does taking imprecise probabilities seriously force us to change about how we decide, how we merge several members' different ranges into one, and what we have to stop pretending to know? And of the usual selling points -- handling ambiguity better than a single number, deferring to experts without collapsing them into a point estimate, being more honest about model uncertainty -- which ones actually bite for a decision like ours and which are close to empty? If our intervals are wide, does that give us any real reason to act differently than if we'd just taken the midpoint or flipped a coin?
