Ok so I'm a 4th-year PhD in phil of language and I'm stuck on a section of my dissertation chapter on derogatory terms, and I think the only way through is to actually watch the two views fight it out on the page rather than reading another twenty pages of people talking past each other.

Set me up a dialogue between two people, both of whom know what they're doing: one who thinks the derogation carried by a slur is part of its literal, truth-conditional content, and one who thinks the derogation lives on a seperate pragmatic/expressive layer (or maybe isn't part of the term's meaning at all). Neither should be a dunce — I want to finish it feeling like either could be right.

Heres the cases I need them to actually chew on, not just gesture at:

- Negation and embedding. "There are no [term]s in this building" and "if any [term]s show up I'm leaving" still land as derogatory, which is awkward if you think the badness is just descriptive content, since negation and conditionals usually cancel or suspend descriptive content.
- Ignorant speakers. Someone who grew up somewhere the word is a neutral label, has no prejudice, and is sincerely just classifying people can still make hearers wince. Does the offensiveness travel with the word or with the community? This one seems to cut against both views in different ways.
- Reclamation. In-group members use it of each other affectionately, and outsiders who try the same move can't seem to pull it off. Did the word change meaning, or did the speakers change what they're doing with it?
- The case I actually care about: an in-between word, not a full slur but not neutral either — basically a demographic label with a history, one where people deny the sting ("I just meant it descriptively"). Both of them should have something real to say here.

Couple of constraints: use a made-up word for the term rather than any real slur, so it reads as a thought experiment and we don't get derailed. And I don't care about who said what in the literature — no citations, no name-dropping, just the arguments.

The thing I want most is genuine back-and-forth: the semanticist has to answer the ignorance and reclamation cases, the pragmatist has to explain why the derogation survives negation and embedding if it's not part of what the sentence says, and each should press the other's weak spot rather than restating their own position louder. If they end up locating exactly where they disagree — like whether they're even using "meaning" the same way — even better.
