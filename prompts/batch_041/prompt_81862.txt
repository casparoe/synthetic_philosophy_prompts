I've more or less convinced myself that interpersonal comparisons of welfare are real — that there's a fact of the matter about how much worse my back pain is than yours, the way there's a fact about which of two rooms is hotter, we just don't have the thermometer yet and maybe never will. What would count against this? I don't mean the "utility is only a representation of choice, so the comparison is meaningless" move, I've heard it and it doesn't land for me. I mean: if you grant that welfare has a magnitude and that magnitudes belonging to different people can go on one scale, which thought experiments, formal results, or empirical findings should actually make me nervous, which would a defender of my view just wave off, and how would you rank them from fatal to merely awkward? I'd also like to know whether my view risks anything at all or has been hedged into unfalsifiability, because if it can't lose I want to know that's a definate cost. One specific thing I keep tripping on: it feels like nailing down a unit ("this much pain = 1") is the easy part and nailing down a zero is the hard part — am I even pointing at the right difficulty, or is the whole one-scale picture incoherent once you notice that the utility decision theory talks about and the utility the welfare people talk about seem to be doing totally different jobs?
