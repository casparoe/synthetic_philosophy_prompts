You're a philosopher of physics who's spent way too long thinking about supertasks — the kind of person with actual opinions about whether Thomson's lamp is on at the end. I need you to build me a new one, to order. Think it through before you write: brainstorm three or four candidate designs, quietly throw out the ones that cheat, and only then give me the one that survives.

Here's the spec.

Its got to be a genuine supertask: infinitely many stages, each taking half as long as the last, so that if you ran them all you'd be finished at some finite time T. There is no last stage — that's the whole point of the thing.

No lamp. Nothing binary, no on/off toggle. Also no jar of balls, no collection of infinitely many labelled objects. I want a single continuous quantity — a position, a volume, a temperature, a charge — so we can't wriggle out by relabelling.

The sequence of states at the stage boundaries must not converge. So the cheap answer, that the state at T is just the limit of the sequence, is off the table by construction.

But here's the interesting bit, and it's what I actually care about: design the case so that a physical principle still pins down something determinate about the state at T anyway. Conservation of energy, continuity of some potential, a symmetry, a topological invariant that can't change — something like that. I want "there's just no fact of the matter" to look too quick.

Every finite stage has to be completly unobjectionable physically: no infinite forces, no infinite energies, no mass conjured from nothing. The trouble lives only in the limit. And no exotic physics, no time travel, no Choice-flavoured set theory.

Then the write-up. State the supertask precisely — the stage rule, the timing, what the quantity is doing and why each stage is fine. Then run the three standard verdicts against it: the line that the description simply doesn't determine a final state so the question is ill-posed; the line that supertasks are physically impossible so the case never arises; and the line that the final state is fixed by the physics rather than by the mathematical sequence. Say which one your case supports and why the other two can't get a grip on it. Then tell me which single feature of your design is doing the work, what happens if I remove it (does it just collapse back into the lamp?), and how someone who likes the verdict you're arguing against would try to defuse the case. I'd rather have the strongest version of their reply than a strawman.

Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or so; the commentary can run as long as it needs to.
