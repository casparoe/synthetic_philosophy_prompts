ok so I'm a fourth-year philosophy of science PhD and next Thursday I have to present a chapter to an actual machine learning reading group. These are not philosophers. It's mostly deep learning people who work on generalization and scaling, plus two stats folks who do learning theory (one of them lives in minimax lower bounds and thinks everything reduces to a bound), and exactly one other philosopher, who is there mostly to watch. My chapter argues that philosophers have misread the no-free-lunch theorems. The standard move is: the theorem shows any learner that does well on some tasks must do badly on others, so induction needs an a priori bias, and that just relocates Hume's problem rather than solving it. I think that conclusion only falls out of how the theorem quantifies, averaging over all objective functions under a uniform measure, and that the uniform measure is doing all the work. My line is that in any physically realizable learning situation the task distribution is inherited from the causal structure of the environment, so what's up for grabs is the naturalness of the measure, not the inductive principle, and the theorem is really a result about what makes a measure natural rather than about justification. Working title is 'the measure is the message,' don't judge me.
