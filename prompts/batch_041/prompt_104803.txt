I teach a seminar in philosophical logic and epistemology for advanced undergraduates and beginning graduate students: fourteen students, one 90-minute session, whiteboard and projector available. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the problem of logical omniscience from the inside — specifically the way a standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge makes knowing a fact about an information state rather than about reasoning, so that it becomes automatic that an agent knows everything her information entails, knows every logical truth, knows a disjunction without knowing either disjunct, and knows absolutely everything if her information is inconsistent.

Please build the activity around the following mechanic, improving it wherever it does not actually work. Each participant is an agent holding a sealed information card: a subset of the eight possible worlds over three binary switches (a card might be "A is on," or "A and B agree," or a deliberately contradictory pair of constraints leaving no worlds). The facilitator runs an auction over a fixed list of propositions about the switches, and a proposition scores for an agent exactly when it is true in every world on that agent's card. Run it in three rounds. Round one, the reasoning round: players may score only propositions they can back with an inference they can state aloud from their card. Round two, the closure round: the house rescores every proposition true in all of a player's worlds, whether or not the player inferred it. Round three, the awareness round: only propositions a player explicitly wrote down before the bell count. The shocks I want them to hit are, in order, that their scores jump for free and become identical for any two players with the same card regardless of how much reasoning they did; that the player holding the contradictory card scores every proposition on the list, including the contradiction itself; and that the awareness fix rescues the ordinary verdict but quietly gives up the closure property they just watched generate the free knowledge.

Deliver a runnable design: the exact scoring scheme (points, no real money), materials and how the cards are generated, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the precise propositions to put on the auction list and why each is there, and instructions that keep the facilitator from lecturing up front — the idea has to show up in what happens, not in what is said. Then a debrief that maps each round's shock onto a specific feature of the possible-worlds semantics: closure under entailment, knowledge of tautologies, the known disjunction with an unknown disjunct, closure under conjunction, substitution of logical equivalents, and inconsistency collapsing into omniscience. The debrief should also show what it costs to resist each result — for instance, that an awareness-based fix restores the ordinary verdict only by reintroducing a distinction between what an agent has and has not entertained, which is exactly the kind of machinery the semantics was supposed to avoid, and that it still leaves the agent knowing everything she has entertained the closure of. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be frank about which features of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply by talking: the genuine infinitude of the consequences, the fact that real agents violate closure for reasons other than unawareness, and the contested status of omniscience as a deliberate idealization of the model rather than a straightforward error. Do not name the problem to the students until the debrief.

Finally, include a short note on which of your design choices encode contestable readings of the formal semantics rather than neutral facts about it, and say what would change your mind: what you would have to observe in a trial run, or what argument you would have to be shown, to conclude that the activity misrepresents the problem or teaches the wrong lesson.
