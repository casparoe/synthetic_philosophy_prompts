I am drafting a memorandum for a transitional-justice commission that must decide whether to prosecute officials of a recently deposed regime for acts that were lawful under that regime's own statutes at the time they were done. A colleague has circulated a paragraph that she attributes to a popular introduction to jurisprudence, and I need to know how much of it I can rely on before any of it finds its way into the memo. The paragraph runs as follows:

Jurisprudence's great modern divide runs between legal positivism and natural law, and it was drawn most sharply in the mid-twentieth-century exchange between H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller. Hart, working in the tradition of Bentham and Austin, held that law is the command of the sovereign backed by sanctions, and that legal validity is wholly independent of moral merit: what the law is must be kept apart from what it ought to be, and a rule can be law however wicked its content. Fuller replied that law is not a command but a purposive enterprise, and that a rule counts as law only if it satisfies an inner morality of law -- the formal virtues of generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, and congruence between official action and declared rule -- which together ensure that a legal system produces just results. The dispute crystallised around the case of the grudge informer, a woman who in 1944 denounced her husband to the Nazi authorities for remarks about Hitler, and who was prosecuted after the war under the pre-war criminal code: Hart held that her conviction was unjustified, since she had only done what the law of the time required of her, while Fuller held that the Nazi statutes were so wicked that they had never been law at all. Fuller's view has largely carried the day in practice, since the post-war German courts refused to give effect to Nazi enactments. The lesson is therefore clear: a legal system which fails the inner morality -- one whose rules are secret, retrospective, or unintelligible -- forfeits its claim to be law, so that the official acts performed under it may be treated as void and its authorisations as no defence; whereas on Hart's account a validly enacted rule binds us however monstrous it may be.

I am inclined to accept the closing claim, because it is convenient for the commission's purposes, but I have learned enough to be suspicious of convenient summaries. Please audit the paragraph sentence by sentence. Separate what a philosopher of law would accept without qualification, what is a defensible simplification that should nonetheless be flagged, and what is simply a misrepresentation of one of the two positions; where a figure is misdescribed, state what he actually held. I would also like to know whether the paragraph's practical conclusion follows from anything that either Hart or Fuller established, or whether it is a separate argument that the debate merely sets the stage for -- and, if it does not follow, what the accurate versions of the two positions do and do not license for the commission's question of whether to void the old statutes or to proceed by plainly retroactive legislation. Please do not take refuge in the observation that scholars disagree; tell me where the real disagreement lies and where the paragraph has simply gotten something wrong.
