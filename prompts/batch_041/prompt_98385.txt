I'm putting together a handout for a graduate metaphysics seminar on persistence, and I want a decision tree that takes a reader from a bare commitment — that ordinary objects like tables and people persist through time — to a named position on how they do so. The usual first question, whether there are temporal parts, gives the game away and sorts people by vocabulary rather than by belief, so don't use it as the entry node. Make the first branch a question about what the reader takes the persistence question itself to be: a first-order question about what exists, a question about how our language and thought represent a world that may not itself contain the relevant distinctions, or a question about which of several descriptions, all in some sense correct, best carves the world at its joints. Build the rest of the tree from questions a reader can answer without knowing where they lead — concrete cases (an object that changes colour, an object dismantled and reassembled, a person who survives gradual replacement of every part, a statue and its clay) and small claims about what is true of them.

Give the leaves names, including at least: endurantism; perdurantism or four-dimensionalism; the version of perdurantism on which ordinary objects are the temporally extended wholes rather than their instantaneous stages, and the version on which the stages themselves are what ordinary names pick out; presentism and eternalism insofar as the tree can separate them; and at least one view on which objects lack temporal parts but change is handled by treating temporal qualifications as modifying the predicate or the copula rather than as introducing an extra entity. Gloss whatever you name in a sentence, since the reader may not know the labels. Where a branch admits a combination of answers that no one seems to hold, mark the leaf as unoccupied and say in one line whether you take the view to be incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, and say why the questions you used fail to pull them apart.
