Next month I defend, before my lab's internal safety review, a deployment proposal for a model whose alignment we cannot verify. My thesis is that the safety case should rest on control rather than on alignment evidence: the artifact under review is the deployment harness, not the model's dispositions; the harness must be built so that the first action inconsistent with the stated objective is detected and halted; and whatever alignment evidence we do have — red-teaming, evals, interpretability probes — enters only as an estimate of the tripwire's fire rate and false-negative rate, never as a license to remove it. A corollary I mean to press is what I call the confession problem: because a model can be fully task-compliant while misreporting its own reasoning, its self-reports — stated confidence, narrated chain of thought, avowals of alignment — carry no evidential weight in either direction and must be treated as monitored output rather than trusted signal.

The room has three blocs. Alignment researchers who regard control as a second-best patch that lets labs defer the hard work, and who suspect that 'unverifiable alignment' is a self-serving framing. Security and systems engineers who will say my threat model is underspecified — adversary as the model? the developer? the user? the weights themselves? And a deployment lead with counsel, who see the tripwire as a permanent freeze in a safety costume and will ask what 'halt' means for a service that is already running, and who bears the liability when it fires.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that bloc would put it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest; what I should concede up front rather than have extracted; the one reply that has to land for the proposal to survive; and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
