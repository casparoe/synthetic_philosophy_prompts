I'm putting together a story for a small reading group that has spent the term on the Vienna Circle and its aftermath; we read our way through Carnap and Schlick and have now wandered into Otto Neurath's corner of the movement, especially his conviction that a genuinely universal language might turn out to be a pictorial one. As I understand him, Neurath spent the twenties and thirties running a social and economic museum in Red Vienna and then, in exile, an institute in England, where together with a designer he built up a system of picture statistics — the one that later acquired the name ISOTYPE — for the explicit purpose of letting people read social and economic facts off a wall without any statistical training at all. What interests me is less the design history than a handful of his commitments: that this was a language in its own right rather than a word-for-word rendering of prose; that turning a table of numbers into a picture was a matter of transformation rather than translation; that a quantity should be shown by repeating one standard symbol a fixed number of times instead of scaling a single symbol up and down; and — the part I keep circling — that he distrusted verbal language as a nest of pseudo-problems and metaphysical traps, while remaining clear-eyed that pictures carry their own severe limits. I'd like a short story that takes those commitments seriously enough that someone who has read Neurath would recognize them doing real work, without the story ever collapsing into a lecture about him.

Here's a possible setting, though you should feel free to bend it: an institute of visual statistics in some plausible near-future or lightly fictional city, staffed by a few draftsmen and one editor whose job is to decide what gets drawn. The story should turn on a single chart. The editor has to render some public fact whose entire moral weight lies in something a picture language cannot easily say — an absence, a negation, a thing that failed to happen, a number of people who are not here because a decision went the other way. The drama is that the institute's grammar has no comfortable way to draw a negative or a counterfactual: to make those people visible, they would have to be drawn as though they were present, and drawing them that way may be a lie or may be the only honest thing the wall can carry. Let one character defend the pictorial language on recognizably Neurathian grounds — it forces us to speak concretely, it belongs to everyone, it is not the private property of the lettered — and let another press the objection that a language which cannot say not is not a neutral instrument but a standing decision about what will count as real, and that the institute's standard human figure, repeated a thousand times, quietly settles what a person is. Give me a scene or two of actual drafting — hands, ink, ruled lines, an argument over whether one symbol should stand for a hundred or for a thousand — and end the story without adjudicating, in the place where both positions are still standing. Somewhere in there it should come through that choosing what to count is itself a political act rather than a technical preliminary to one. Aim for roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words. The story should be readable by someone who has never heard of Neurath or of picture statistics, and accurate enough that someone who has will not wince.
