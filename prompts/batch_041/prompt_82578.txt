Having spent a career in physiology and only recently turned to philosophy, I have come to hold that the badness of pain consists entirely in its affective-motivational component—the felt demand that one stop or escape—and not at all in its sensory-discriminative quality. My central case is pain asymbolia, where patients report a pain sensation but evince little or no affective-motivational response; I also want you to consider an imagined case of a pain that is fully felt as sharp, localized, and intense but carries no felt demand to stop. If such cases are possible, what counts as bad about pain is the demand, not the sensation. What would count against this view, and what does it predict that rival accounts of pain's badness do not? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, including the possibility that asymbolia is misdescribed, cases where aversiveness and sensory intensity dissociate in the other direction, and whether the view collapses into a general desire theory of badness or has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Say what the view's defenders would themselves concede. I would be most persuaded by clinical or experimental dissociations and least by intuitions about stoic endurance. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
