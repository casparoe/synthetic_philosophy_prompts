I'm a fifth-year PhD student, and next month I'm giving a colloquium paper that I expect to be the most contentious thing I've presented. The paper is about what is usually called transformative experience: the class of choices -- having a child, emigrating, converting, starting a medication that may reshape what one values, enlisting -- that will alter not just one's circumstances but one's preferences, values, and capacity to evaluate the options, so that the person choosing now cannot know what it would be like to be the person who has chosen. The standard framing treats the transformative option as the problem and the status quo as the safe baseline, and asks whether one can rationally opt for the former at all. I argue that this framing is asymmetric in a way that cannot be sustained: declining the child, the move, the conversion is itself transformative, so there is no epistemically privileged default from which to evaluate the adventurous branch. Once the symmetry is granted, the question is no longer how one can rationally choose the unknown, but how either branch can be evaluated at all, when each leads to a self one cannot now occupy. My answer is that these choices should not be assessed by their outcomes. They are better understood as constitutive commitments -- closer in structure to a promise, whose authority over later action is internal to the commitment rather than derived from prior reasons for making it -- and the norm governing them is therefore not the maximization of expected value but something closer to integrity, the requirement that the choice be one the agent can own as an act of self-authorship. The conclusion is not that such choices are rationally undecidable, but that they are governed by a different rational structure than the expected-value model recognizes.

The audience is what worries me. My department is mostly analytic epistemologists and decision theorists, with two ethicists and one philosopher of mind; a colleague from the medical school who works on informed consent and on adaptive preferences will probably attend, and the department's resident Kantian will be there. The decision theorists will have little patience for a paper that declines to formalize, and will want to know why the problem cannot be handled by a richer state space or by preferences over life-plans; the epistemologists will press on whether my symmetry claim is empirical or conceptual, and on whether "cannot now occupy" names an indeterminacy in the values or merely an ignorance of them; the Kantian will want to know what distinguishes a constitutive commitment from a maxim one has simply decided to adopt, and whether my appeal to integrity is anything more than bootstrapping; the clinician will ask what any of this implies for how consent should be sought from someone facing, say, a transformative intervention. Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually get, attributed to the audience members most likely to raise them and phrased the way each would phrase it, ordered by how much damage each does to my position rather than by the order in which they will arrive; for each, a reply I can deliver in a minute or two and a candid note on where that reply is weakest; tell me what I should concede early to buy credibility and what I must not concede at all; and identify the one objection I am hoping nobody raises.
