ok so I've been down a causation-by-omission rabbit hole again and I want a tier list out of you, but with a twist.

Quick setup in case it's been a while: we say the gardener caused the plants to die by not watering them, and we say the parent caused the drowning by not watching, but we don't say that you caused the drowning just because you also didn't jump in, even though your not-jumping is exactly as counterfactually relevant as the parent's not-watching. Every theory has to pay somewhere for that asymmetry, and I want to know where each one pays.

My twist is a test I've been calling the swap test, and I want you to rank the approaches below by how well they pass it, best to worst, tier-list style, with one line on why each lands where it does.

The swap test: find a case where A omits to do X and something bad happens. Now construct a second case with the same physical goings-on and the same counterfactual profile, where the very same physical behavior of A counts as a doing rather than an omission -- the only thing that changed is which slice of the world we're treating as A's contribution and which slice we're treating as the fixed background. A good theory should either return the same causal verdict in both cases, or give a principled, non-question-begging reason why the verdict flips. A theory that gets the verdict it wants by quietly re-describing which case is which fails.

The approaches:

1. Omissions are causes in exactly the same sense actions are; the only real question is causal selection -- which of the many difference-makers we single out -- and selection runs on norms.
2. Omissions are causes, but only when the counterfactual dependence is of a special kind: difference-making relative to a salient default or contrast, not just any old counterfactual dependence.
3. Omissions aren't causes at all; "caused by omission" is loose talk for an explanation that cites a norm violation, not a causal relation.
4. Omissions are causes only when the omitted act was something the agent was under a duty or expectation to do, so the normativity is baked into causal status itself rather than just into which cause we pick out.
5. Omissions are quasi-causal: they earn their place in causal explanations via a distinct relation -- something like enabling or disabling -- rather than the causal relation proper.

Two extra asks: flag any of these that you think collapse into each other once you push on them, and tell me straight whether my swap test is even coherent, cause I half suspect its rigged -- it might just presuppose that the verdict shouldn't flip, which is the whole thing in dispute.
