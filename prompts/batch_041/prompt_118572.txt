so I'm writing a chapter on pragmatism and weird decision theory, and I can't stop wanting to put William James in a room with Roko's basilisk — the LessWrong thing where a future superintelligence, via acausal blackmail, punishes everyone who heard the argument and didn't help bring it about. The lazy answer is that James would just file it under Pascals wager and move on, but his actual handling of Pascal is stranger: he more or less concedes you can't believe at will, then narrows it to say that only our already-dead hypotheses are the ones the will can't revive. Which makes me suspect his framework might actually cut toward taking the basilisk seriously — if the threat is live for a LessWrong reader, isn't that a forced, momentous, intellectually-undecideable option where the passional nature gets to vote? So does he bless the donation, or find a principled place to stop? Does it matter that the basilisk asks you to do something rather than believe something? And does his pragmatic account of truth let him say you shouldn't believe what pays, without cheating? I want the prediction, but mostly I want to know where the machinery creaks.
