I teach ancient philosophy, which is my excuse for the fact that the following question is probably about forty years out of date. I've somehow ended up chairing three different commitees this year, and I've noticed a pattern in how we vote that I can't put a name to, and it's started to bug me. Here's the shape of it. Take a committee that has to answer a series of yes/no questions whose answers are supposed to hang together — the cleanest case I've actually sat in on was a hospital ethics consult: (1) is the patient competent, (2) has she been adequately informed, (3) should we honour her refusal of treatment, where (3) is supposed to follow from (1) and (2). We take the questions one at a time and go with the majority on each. What comes out is a set of answers that nobody in the room would endorse: roughly 60% say competent, roughly 60% say informed, but only about 40% say we should honour the refusal — because the 60% who say competent and the 60% who say informed are not the same 60%. Every individual member is perfectly coherent; the group's answers, taken together, are not. I've seen the same thing on a hiring committee, except that there the third question ("do we want to hire them") doesn't strictly follow from the first two — it's just that everyone agrees you shouldn't say yes to the third while saying no to the first two, so it's a looser sort of hanging-together, and I can't tell whether that difference matters. I assumed this was Condorcet's paradox and felt briefly clever, then realised it isn't: nothing cycles, we're not ranking anything, nobody's manipulating the agenda. It also doesn't feel like Arrow, though I'd bet it's in the same neighbourhood.

So: does this have a name? And I'd like more than a name — I want the carving, the boundaries, the near misses. In particular: (a) does the phenomenon require the connections between the questions to be strict logical entailment, or does it also arise when the connection is merely something every member accepts, as in the hiring case — and if that looser version is a genuinely different thing, does it have a different name? (b) Is there a name for the two escapes I can see — deciding the whole package in one vote, versus voting on the parts and reading the answer off — and is there any principled reason to prefer one, given that each seems to buy coherence at a price (in the one case nobody's own view survives, in the other the group contradicts itself)? (c) Does any of this turn on the numbers — does it evaporate if we require two-thirds instead of a bare majority, or if the senior people's votes count double? And (d) when a group ends up in this state, is there a name for the further question of whether the group can be said to have a view at all, or is that just the same issue wearing a different hat? I'm not fishing for a reading list — I just want to know what to call the thing and where its edges are.
