I'm 68 and retired now — thirty-odd years in radio astronomy, mostly pulsar timing, a little SETI-adjacent work on the side — and since I packed it in I've been working through philosophy of science and, lately, decision theory, which I find harder than the math ever was. A friend's kid, a grad student, has been trying to sell me on something he calls evidential cooperation in large worlds. The gist, as I understand it: when you're deciding what to do, you shouldn't only ask what your action will cause, you should also ask what it makes it more likely that you are — because any other agent anywhere in the universe who's in a situation like yours and reasons the way you do will likely land on the same choice. So if there are enough such agents out there, my choosing the costly-but-cooperative thing could be "evidence" that all of them are doing it too, and the summed benefit could swamp anything I could ever cause directly. He thinks this hands us real reasons to make sacrifices for civilizations we can never contact and can't affect. I think it smells like a shell game, but I've been wrong before and I'd rather find out where the disagreement actually lives than trade intuitions.

So here's what I want, and please give me a number with the machinery showing rather than a polite shrug. What's your credence that this kind of reasoning can ever be correct in a way that should genuinely change what we do — that is, that there's at least one real choice where the evidential link to other agents, and not the causal effects, is an action-guiding reason? Break it into the sub-questions you think it turns on, with a number and a one-line justification for each — for instance whether evidential or functional decision theory is the right account of rational choice at all; whether the universe actually contains enough comparable agents facing comparable choices for the correlation term to be non-negligible; whether their choices would really be correlated with mine in the relevant way; whether the whole thing is self-defeating or collapses once you consider the population reasoning in the same way; and whatever else belongs. Then combine them, show me the arithmetic, and tell me which term my final number is most sensitive to and which single sub-question would move you the most if it came out the other way. For what it's worth I put myself around 0.15, mostly on the self-defeat worry, though I suspect that's just a reflex from a career of assuming that if I can't point an antenna at it, it doesn't count. American English, please, and don't spare my feelings — I've had referees before.
