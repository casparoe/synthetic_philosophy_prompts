I convene a reading group that is working through the first book of Aristotle's Politics this term, and next month we are pairing the account of natural slavery with The Tempest. Everyone will arrive with the colonial reading ready-made — Prospero the usurper, Caliban the dispossessed native — and I would rather the discussion tested Aristotle's theory against the play than gestured at it. So give me a reading of The Tempest through the account of natural slavery as it actually stands in the Politics, not a précis of it. The components I want in play are these: that the natural slave apprehends reason without possessing it, and so can follow orders but cannot deliberate for himself; that he is animate property, a living tool, a separable part of the master's body; that his subjection is meant to be to his own advantage, since it is better for the inferior to be ruled by the superior's reason; that he must nonetheless have a share of virtue, enough not to fail at his work through cowardice or intemperance, though not the virtue of a free man; and that Aristotle concedes that enslavement by capture and conquest often fails to track the natural distinction, which is why the justice of the institution is disputed even within his own text.

My suspicion, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that the account is unstable in a way the play exploits. On Aristotle's own showing elsewhere, virtue requires deliberative choice; but the natural slave has no deliberative faculty, and must nevertheless be virtuous enough to serve well — and a master who requires his slave's virtue has already conceded something about the slave's soul. Prospero needs Caliban's knowledge, his labour, and his obedience, and says as much; Caliban curses, conspires, remembers, hears music, chooses, and at the end promises amendment. Is that a refutation of the theory, or does the play show instead that the natural slave is manufactured — by conquest, by a pedagogy that teaches only enough to make the creature useful, and by a master who then calls the result nature?

The reading should tell me which scenes to build it around, and what to make of the scenes that cut against my line: the attempted rape Prospero charges him with, and Caliban's unrepentant answer to it; the drunken conspiracy with the butler and the jester, in which Caliban seeks not freedom but a different master, and is made ridiculous at precisely the moment he claims the island; Prospero's claim to have taught and cared for him, and Caliban's reply that the only profit of that teaching is that he knows how to curse; and the ending, where master and slave each appear to give something up. Set Ariel beside Caliban, since one servitude came with a term and a promise and the other is a condition of birth, and say what the play adds to the argument that Aristotle's text cannot supply — or say plainly where the play resists the reading and the honest critic must give it up. I have both texts at hand, so there is no need to quote; the interpretation is what I am after.
