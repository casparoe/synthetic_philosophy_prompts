I'm a research fellow and I've got a workshop paper to present in a couple of weeks to my own faculty plus whoever wanders over from the philosophy department, and I'd like to walk in ready for the Q&A instead of finding out in real time that my whole framing is naive. The paper makes two moves. First, I want to split what we lump together as "interpretation" into two stages and keep them strictly apart: a fixing stage, where the meaning of a statutory phrase is just whatever the linguistic conventions in force at enactment settled, full stop, with no appeal to purpose smuggled in; and a remedial stage, where an interpreter who thinks the fixed meaning produces an intolerable result is allowed to depart from it, but only under a constraint I call the symmetry condition -- she has to be able to state her rule of departure in general form and then say honestly that she would apply the same rule to a statute she loathes. Second, I claim the usual textualism-versus-purposivism shouting match is largely a category error: the real disagreement is about who bears the burden of a bad outcome (the regulated party, the intended beneficiary, or the court's own conscience), and once you see it that way alot of the familiar moves look like burden-shifting in disguise rather than rival theories of what meaning is. My audience is: two committed textualists, one who's into corpus linguistics and thinks a big enough database can settle plain meaning, one more old-school and convinced legislative history is just cheating; a legal historian who works on late-19th-century regulatory statutes and gets visibly annoyed at present-day intuitions about what counts as plain; two legal realists who will say my two stages are the same discretion with extra paperwork; a philosopher of language who'll press on whether a "fixing stage" is even coherent given how much of meaning is pragmatic; and the law-and-econ person, who'll be thinking about error costs and about who actually bears the cost of my symmetry condition.
