I am a novelist at work on a scene in which a man is asked a direct question by someone who intends to do grave harm, and has to decide whether to answer falsely. The philosopher I consulted for background keeps assuring me that Kant says one must never lie, not even to the murderer at the door, and takes this to settle what my character is obliged to do. I have learned to distrust such one-liners. Lay out for me, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making: what exactly the prohibition ranges over, and whether it condemns only the false declaration or every deceptive expedient, so that silence, evasion, or a literally true but misleading answer might escape it; whether the wrongness is intrinsic to the act, or consists in a violation of the questioner's humanity, or in the damage done to language and mutual trust, or in the liar's becoming answerable for consequences he cannot foresee; and whether the duty is owed to the person deceived, to oneself, or to humanity at large. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and end by telling me which of these readings, if any, would actually support my adviser's verdict — that my character must answer truthfully whatever follows.
