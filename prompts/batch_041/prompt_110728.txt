So I need to write a short research proposal -- 4-5 pages, for an internal fellowship that buys me one semester with no teaching and no empirical work, just me and a whiteboard -- and I've got a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into something that actually looks like a proposal.

Here's the area. There's a whole family of views that tell you what you ought to do by pointing at an idealized counterpart: what you would choose if you were fully informed and fully coherent, what an ideal advisor would want for you, what you'd converge on under ideal deliberation. I'm not asking about any one of them in particular, I'm asking about the whole stratgey. What bugs me is that the idealization has to be specified, and there's no principled place to stop. Idealize the information and you get one verdict; idealize information plus coherence plus motivation plus computational power plus your position in time and you get a different one. Everyone seems to pick the set that delivers the answers they already wanted, and the picking never gets defended.

My half-formed fix: treat the choice as a parameter instead of a problem. Call each admissible set of idealizations an "idealization profile." Then say an action is indirectly normative if it's at least as good as every alternative under every profile in the admissible family -- robustness instead of uniqueness. The question I want the proposal to be about: does robustness actually buy anything, or does it collapse at both ends, either admitting so many profiles that almost nothing survives or restricting them until a substantive first-order view has been smuggled in? And if it collapses, is the lesson that the ideal-point picture of normativity is just the wrong picture, or only that we've been demanding something from it -- guidance for agents who are stuck being non-ideal -- that it was never built to supply?
