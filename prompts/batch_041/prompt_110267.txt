I am a controls engineer by training. I build numerical simulations of systems that switch between distinct modes of operation, and I spend my working life with sequences and their limits, convergence, continuity of a state trajectory as a function of time, event-driven integration, and the particular headache of systems that fire infinitely many discrete transitions within a finite span of time — what my field calls Zeno behavior, usually handled by regularizing the switching law, by replacing the infinitely fast chattering with its averaged effect in the way we do with sliding modes, or by pointing out that real hardware has delays, dead zones, and sensor thresholds that break the infinite pattern before it ever gets started. Philosophy of mathematics I have read essentially none of, and I have been trying to follow the literature on supertasks: the lamp puzzle usually called Thomson's lamp, in which a lamp is switched at the half-minute mark, then the quarter-minute mark, then the eighth, and so on, so that infinitely many switchings occur before the minute is up, after which everyone asks whether the lamp is on or off at the end.

What I would like from you is a systematic translation of the main positions in that debate into my vocabulary. Take the argument that no infinite sequence of actions can be completed because there is no last action available to complete it, the reply that the description of the lamp merely underdetermines the final state rather than generating a contradiction, and any further positions you think are carrying real weight; restate each of them as faithfully as you can in terms of sequences and their limits, the difference between the limit of a sequence of states and the state of the system at the limit time, continuity as a function of time, the behavior of event detection as the switching times accumulate, and what a numerical integrator or a floating-point representation can actually do near the accumulation point. It matters, I think, that the absolute switching times collapse to the same value in double precision after roughly fifty steps, so that a simulated lamp would simply stop switching rather than oscillate forever, and I want to know whether you regard that as a numerical accident or as a stand-in for something deeper. For each position, say what survives the translation intact, what has to be distorted in order to fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Is the leftover a defect of how engineers and numerical analysts talk about limits — something we cannot say and perhaps should be able to — or is it evidence that the philosophers' question was less well posed than it appeared? I want a verdict on my own instinct, which is that asking whether the lamp is on at the end is like asking for the value of a function at a point where the specification determines no value, and that the engineering habit of declaring the regularized or averaged behavior to be the real behavior is a stipulation dressed up as a discovery. Tell me whether that instinct is doing any real work or whether it quietly assumes exactly what is in dispute, for instance by treating the whole matter as a question about mathematical specification when the real question is what a physical process can do — which is where the relativistic constructions some people bring up, spacetimes in which a worldline of infinite proper time lies entirely in the past of a single event so that an observer can collect the result of an endless computation and return with it, are evidently aimed. No references are needed; I will not be looking them up.
