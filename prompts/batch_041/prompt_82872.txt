I am a junior in high school, and for a civics project last semester I looked into what actually happened after my state downgraded simple possession of small amounts of drugs from a misdemeanor to a civil violation. The reform was advertised as a way to stop funneling people into the criminal system, and on paper it did exactly that: possession is no longer an arrestable offense, so arrests for it fell by something like eighty percent in the first year, which my father pointed out was guaranteed by the law itself. Two other changes are the ones I cannot fit together. First, the total number of people who now end up with an official file opened on them — a ticket, a mandatory appointment with a health counselor, a follow-up they have to prove they attended, and eventually a larger fine if they ignore the whole thing — rose sharply, and now runs several times the old arrest count. Second, the people in that file are a different population: more teenagers, more people who were stopped for something unrelated and had a ticket added on, and more people who under the old system would most likely have had the charge dropped at their first court date. My civics teacher says this proves the reform failed. The organizer who spoke to our class says it proves the reform is working, since the file is a health file and reaching more people was the entire point. My sister, who is a sociology major, says it is neither, that it is a well-documented trap that reforms of this kind tend to fall into, and that there is a name for it, but she could not remember it, which has been bothering me for weeks.

The same thing appears to have happened at my school, which is why I doubt the pattern has anything specifically to do with drugs. Two years ago we replaced out-of-school suspension with a required sit-down conference between the student and whoever was harmed, followed by a written agreement and a check-in meeting. A student who once would have spent three days at home now goes through a longer and more thoroughly recorded process, and in a given year far more students pass through it than were ever suspended, including students a teacher would previously have handled with a warning in the hallway. Nobody decided to widen anything; each individual decision was reasonable on its own.

So my question is not whether the reform worked. Is there an established name for this specific pattern — an institution that was meant to pull people out of the heavy machinery of punishment and instead pulls a larger number of people into a lighter version of it? I do not want only the label; I want to know what the phenomenon actually is, how it is distinguished from three things that are easy to confuse it with (the problem simply relocating to another neighborhood or another category, a reform that straightforwardly fails, and a count that rises only because someone redefined what is being counted), and where its boundaries lie — for instance, whether it still counts when the newly caught people are genuinely better off, or when the lighter process is entirely voluntary. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and tell me what it does and does not cover. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no concluding summary.
