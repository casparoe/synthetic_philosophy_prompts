I'm the TA for a seminar that splits its time between philosophy of mind and normative ethics, and I'm putting together the take-home final. Last week I had a marathon argument with my roommate — he's a condensed-matter physicist, the kind who thinks philosophers just make stuff up — and it ended up being almost exactly the question I want on the exam, so I'm going to build it around that. His move was this: suppose panpsychism is true, i.e. experience is fundamental and goes all the way down, so every basic physical entity has some faint sliver of inner life. Then, he said, physics stops being a neutral description of matter and becomes a census of experiencing things, and suddenly a bunch of stuff we do without a second thought — smashing particles in colliders, building computers out of who-knows-what, even just being a big composite creature that presumably consumes simpler ones — looks morally loaded. Maybe even mass suffering at the bottom of reality. I pushed back that having some kind of inner life and being the sort of thing that can be wronged are two totally different things, and that a quark having a blip of experience doesnt automatically mean there's anyone home to be miserable — but I couldnt actually give him a clean, non-hand-wavy account of the gap, and I'd like the students to do better than I did. What I really want them to see is that the interesting question isn't whether panpsychism is true (they can recite the usual pro and con arguments for that in their sleep) but what panpsychism would have to add before any ethical conclusion followed from it, and whether that addition is even coherent.

So here's what I need. Write me ONE essay question for a 90-minute final, pitched at advanced undergrads / early grad students, that forces them to bridge philosophy of mind and ethics instead of keeping them in separate boxes. To stop them from just regurgitating the combination problem, the question should include a specific twist I want them to grapple with: a version of panpsychism on which the fundamental experiential entities do have experience, but nothing is ever pleasant or unpleasant to them — no valence, no felt good or bad, just a bare quality of experience with nothing attached to it. The question should make them decide whether suffering could exist in a world like that, what would have to be true of a micro-experience for it to count as suffering at all, and whether the micro-level experiences could add up into a unified subject who is the one doing the suffering, or whether the requirement that there be someone home blocks that. They should have to land somewhere and defend it, not just survey the options.

Then give me a model answer of roughly 650–750 words that actually takes a position and shows the moves I'm looking for — in particular the distinction between experience and morally relevant sentience, the role of a unified subject, and a clear-eyed statement of what the panpsychist would need to show for the ethical conclusion to go through. And a marking scheme: say, 25 marks total, broken into the must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a short note on the specific wrong moves that should cost marks — the ones where a student either collapses experience into sentience without argument, or waves at the combination problem as if citing it settles the ethics, or just declares panpsychism obviously false and dodges the question. I want a scheme where the difference between an A and a B answer is the difference between someone who says exactly what has to be bridged and someone who only names the bridge.
