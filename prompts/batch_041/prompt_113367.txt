I have been reading the arguments about levels of selection—gene versus organism versus group—for years now, and I have come to suspect that most of the disagreement is downstream of people not agreeing on what a theory in this area is even supposed to deliver. Before I read another round of dueling examples, I want the specification on the table. So: write out the desiderata that any adequate account of selection acting at a level must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp, and flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied—that last part is the thing I most want, because I suspect the whole dispute is one of those situations where the list itself is the result. And if it turns out to be a verbal dispute dressed up as an empirical one, say so plainly.

The requirements should at minimum cover: what it takes for a trait to be selected for the good of a unit at some level rather than merely correlated with that unit's success; the difference between what gets copied and what gets selected; whether a level has to be heritable to count as a level at all; how selection is to be told apart from drift at each level; and whether the theory is meant to be a criterion I can apply to a concrete case or a redescription of a result I already have.

The cases it has to adjudicate are where I would test it:

- Opposed selection within a single lineage: a mutation that proliferates inside a tissue while reducing the survival of the organism that carries the tissue. Both levels are doing real causal work simultaneously, and the theory should be able to say that rather than pick a winner.
- Community-level bottlenecks: two microbial species, each producing something the other needs, propagated by transferring the whole consortium every generation, with cheaters that outgrow cooperators inside each consortium. The consortium is heritable and its productivity varies. I want to know whether that makes it a unit of selection, and whether the answer turns on anything about whether it counts as an organism.
- Small demes: high variance in deme productivity, no heritable differences between demes. Can the theory avoid calling drift group selection, and equally avoid calling every group-level covariance a group adaptation?
- Ledger equivalence: the same total evolutionary change can be decomposed as selection among individuals or among groups, with identical numbers but different labels for which level is doing the work. The theory has to tell me whether that label disagreement is a fact about the world or a fact about the formalism, and if the latter, whether it dissolves the debate or merely relocates it.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and which of the usual positions—gene's-eye, multilevel, pluralist—survives the list best. Keep the scoring brief; the criteria are the deliverable.
