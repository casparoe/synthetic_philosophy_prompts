I'm a high-school senior, and in three weeks I have to present a twenty-minute paper to my school's philosophy club and then survive the discussion. The audience is really three audiences at once: our mathematics teacher, who has studied the incompleteness theorems properly and will not let a loose use of them pass; a handful of students who read Gödel, Escher, Bach over the summer and now invoke the strange loop to explain everything; and three members of the robotics club who think the whole Gödel-and-minds business is a category error and will say so without softening it.

My thesis is that the analogy between Gödelian self-reference and self-aware minds is normally propped up by the anti-mechanist argument — the one associated with Lucas and Penrose, that the theorems show a human mathematician outruns any formal system — and that this is the worst possible foundation for the analogy. The Gödel sentence for a system S is unprovable in S, but what a person actually does is see it, accept it, and add it to S as a new axiom; so the theorem points, if it points anywhere, not to privileged access to unprovable truths but to the capacity to revise the rules one is already reasoning by. I am calling this the escape-hatch reading, and I want to argue that it is both more modest and more interesting than the standard one: self-reference gives you a system that can describe itself, and what is distinctive about a mind is that it can also rewrite the system it is describing.

Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. I don't want a reading list or citations, since I have no way to check them, but I do want you to keep apart what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and to tell me plainly if my escape-hatch reading is just a well-known objection under a new name — and if it is, what the live disagreement actually is.
