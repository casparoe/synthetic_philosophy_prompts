I have no training in philosophy and no one to ask, so I have been working this out on my own for the better part of a year. Here is the shape of it. Imagine a modest number of people whose lives go very well, and then imagine a vastly larger number whose lives are only barely worth having — not wretched, not sick, not cruel, just thin: each day holding slightly more of what makes a day worth keeping than of what makes it not. The second world can be reached from the first by steps that each seem to cost nothing. Add people who are glad enough to be alive and take nothing away from anyone. Then let the good lives thin out a little as they multiply. Keep going, and you arrive at a world of enormous numbers in which every single life sits just above the line — and by the plain way of counting up what is good, that world is the best of all of them. I find that conclusion repellent, and I cannot find the step where it goes wrong. (I have seen it called the repugnant conclusion, which strikes me as a name that has the argument on your behalf before you have had it.) My friend says the repellence is only a failure of imagination: nobody can truly picture a life that is barely worth having, so we quietly substitute poverty, or illness, or boredom of a painful kind, and then we are moved by the wrong thing entirely. I have never been able to settle whether he is right, and I would like to try settling it as a poem rather than as an argument.

So: write me a poem of seven or eight stanzas, each one line longer than the stanza before it, beginning as a single voice and ending as a crowd, somewhere in the range of thirty lines. Each stanza should be another batch of people arriving, and each new line a voice saying, in its own words, that it is all right here, that there is enough. Nothing in the poem should be sad. No poverty, no illness, no cruelty, nothing to be pitied; if the poem disturbs, it must disturb because of what it keeps doing rather than because of anything that happens inside it. I would rather you never wrote the phrase worth living at all — I want to see the thing that phrase stands for and not the phrase, since the phrase is what turns the whole business into bookkeeping. Let it be addressed to whoever is doing the adding, which is to say to me, because I think that is the part that sticks: the sense of being the one who says yes every time.

By the poem's own rule the last stanza is the best one, being the largest; I want it to be the one a reader most wants to skip. And somewhere I want a single line that the arithmetic has no place for — a line from outside, from someone who would not be added, or from the world the poem is leaving behind — and I do not want to be told whether that line is a real objection or merely homesickness. Do not resolve it for me. It should be a poem and not an argument in verse: someone who has never heard of any of this should find it good, and someone who has thought about it for years should find that it gets the thing right.
