Norman Malcolm, in his 1959 book Dreaming, argued that dreaming is not a species of experience at all. On his account the concept of dreaming is anchored to the sleeper's waking report: a dream cannot properly be said to occur independently of the impression formed on waking, and the sentence "I am asleep," uttered by someone who is currently asleep, is senseless. Daniel Dennett, in his 1976 paper "Are Dreams Experiences?", reached a superficially similar verdict by a very different route. He treated the question as empirical rather than conceptual, and proposed — in what is often called his cassette-tape hypothesis — that dreams are not experiences undergone during sleep, but rather narratives or pseudo-memories inserted into the mind at the moment of waking.

Please compare these two accounts. In doing so:

1. State as precisely as you can what each thinker means by "experience," and what each takes to be the primary datum that any theory of dreaming must explain.

2. Assess whether their shared conclusion that dreams are not experiences is substantive or merely verbal — that is, whether they are denying the same claim, or two different claims that happen to be expressed by the same sentence.

3. Say what each account is committed to in the following case. A woman, unambiguously asleep and not due to wake for hours, speaks aloud a fluent and detailed description of what she is dreaming. When she eventually wakes, she gives a report that matches the sleep-speech in detail. Assume both the sleep-speech and the waking report are sincere and uncoached.

4. Finally, say which account you find more defensible, and identify the most serious objection facing each.
