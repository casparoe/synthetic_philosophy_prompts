For several years now, without any formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to work out for myself what distinguishes genuine consent from mere acquiescence, and I have reached a point where I would like a second mind on the problem. The familiar answer treats an agreement as valid consent when three conditions are met together: the person is competent to decide, adequately informed about what they are agreeing to, and free from coercion. I understand the point of each condition, but I have come to suspect that the list, taken as a whole, is doing less of the work than it appears to, and I have been testing an alternative of my own construction. I would like you to compare the two and tell me which one I have better reason to trust.

The alternative runs as follows. An agreement counts as valid consent only if the person, confronted with the entire causal history of how their agreement was obtained — every incentive placed before them, every fact withheld, every feature of their circumstances that made refusal costly — would still, judging from their own values, endorse having given it. In short: consent is valid only if it could be given again under full disclosure of its own origins.

I would like you to press this against three cases. First, a case where a party spends years quietly cultivating a desire in someone — through no lie, but through shaping their tastes and surroundings — and then sells them the thing they have come to want; the standard conditions are satisfied, yet the alternative may reject the agreement, because the person, shown how the desire was planted, might disown it. Second, a case where someone in genuine peril is offered rescue in exchange for everything they own; here the standard view may accept the agreement while the alternative also accepts it, since the person might say they would pay anything again — which makes me wonder whether my test is too permissive precisely where exploitation is most obvious. Third, a case where a threat that would ordinarily void consent leads someone into an arrangement they would, in hindsight and with full knowledge, endorse.

So: do these two ideas genuinely draw the line in different places, or is my alternative merely the standard account restated in more elaborate words? Where they disagree, which verdict should I trust, and are there cases where my test returns an answer only because the concept of consent has quietly stopped applying?
