I have been reading a much-praised trade book on comparative moral cultures — the kind of book that receives a long review in a weekly, is discussed on podcasts for a season, and then quietly supplies the framing of op-eds for a decade. Its thesis concerns German and American folk morality, and before I repeat any of it in my own writing I would like a careful audit of the argument. I claim no expertise here, and my sympathies happen to lie with the book's verdict, which is part of why I want the audit done by someone who is not.

As I understand it, the book's case runs roughly as follows. German moral culture descends from Kant's insistence that morality is a matter of duty rather than sentiment, from Hegel's conception of ethical life as the custom of a community, from Luther's teaching that the Christian owes obedience to the secular power, and from the Prussian official's ethos of service; the German good person is accordingly the one who does what is required — who waits at the empty crossing, sorts the recycling, files the permit, keeps to the rule. American moral culture descends instead from the dissenting conscience, the Puritan who answers to God rather than to the magistrate and the transcendentalist who answers to himself; the American good person asks whether the rule is right rather than whether it is the rule. On this account the German question is 'Is it permitted?' and the American question is 'Is it just?' The book's verdict is that the American side has the better of it, because a people trained to obey the rule rather than the conscience will in the end obey any rule, and that is how a civilized nation talked itself into the worst crimes of the twentieth century.

I suspect the book is doing something wrong, but I also suspect it is not simply inventing its material, and I would rather know which of its moves are sound than be told that the whole thing is unfashionable. Please give me an audit along the following lines.

First, claim by claim: what a philosopher who works on Kant and Hegel and a historian of the two countries would accept as accurate, what is a defensible popularization, and what is a misrepresentation — including whether the figures the book invokes actually support the cultures it attributes to them, and whether it has quietly substituted a folk practice for a philosophical position, or the reverse.

Second, what a defender of the German side would object to, and what a defender of the American side would object to; the book may be unfair to both, and I want to know where each would say it has been turned into a caricature.

Third, whether the contrast is genuine or a false dichotomy. Is conscience the opposite of rule-following, or is some notion of law — even self-legislation — internal to the very idea of conscience? And does the German tradition contain its own critique of mere conscience, as I half remember it does?

Fourth, whether the book is running together three different things: a descriptive claim about how two populations behave, a conceptual claim about the relation between rules and conscience, and a normative verdict about which is better. What premise would be needed to get from the description to the verdict, and is any such premise available?

Fifth, what evidence could bear on the descriptive claim at all — what would be measured, in whom, against what baseline — and whether the book's evidence, which looks like a handful of visible behaviors and a few historical episodes, can carry the weight it puts on it. What should I make of within-country variation and of counterexamples running the other way, such as traditions of conscientious refusal in Germany and traditions of order, covenant, and legalism in America?

Finally, please state the strongest version of the book's thesis that survives all of this, and say whether its verdict still follows from that version or whether the verdict needs an argument the book never supplies. I would like to finish knowing which sentences I can rely on, which I should hedge, and which I should drop.
