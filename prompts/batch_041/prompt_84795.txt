I'm closing out my aesthetics unit on authenticity with a screening of Welles's F for Fake, and I want a reading of the film that actually argues with the philosophy instead of just admiring the sleight of hand. Here's what bugs me. Nearly all the literature on forgery -- Goodman's two perceptually identical pictures, Dutton on artistic crimes, Radford on why we're bothered when a fake turns out to be fake -- is about copies. But Elmyr never copied anything. He made brand-new Modiglianis and Matisses that had never existed and sold them as lost originals, and (the bit I keep coming back to) he says he never signed a single one. Welles sets that against the anonmyous masons of Chartres, whose names nobody knows and whose work we still venerate, and I think that analogy quietly backfires, because the cathedral builders never claimed to be anyone else. So one thing I want out of the reading: does the corpus matter? Is the real damage of an Elmyr that he adds fake items to someone's oeuvre rather than that he fools the eye?

Second thing. The film is a documentary that lies to us on purpose, and Welles is careful about it -- he promises the next hour is factual, and the Picasso and Kodar story lands only after that hour is up, so technically he never broke his word. Does the film therefor count as a forgery too, in the sense that matters, and does that make its case about authenticity stronger (it practices what it preaches) or does it just buy itself immunity? And where does the film resist the reading -- is there a stretch where it plainly just wants us to enjoy the con and the philosophy gets in the way?

I've seen it a dozen times, so spare me the plot summary and don't quote dialogue at me. What I want is the argument: what the film adds to the forgery debate that Goodman and Dutton don't have, whether it relocates the wrong of forgery from the art to the market, and whether it finally comes out saying forgery is harmless because the picture is still beautiful.
