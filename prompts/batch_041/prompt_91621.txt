Here is the puzzle. Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) is standardly presented as four "corners": P; not-P; both P and not-P; neither P nor not-P. The standing worry is that the fourth corner either collapses into the second, or is flatly contradictory and unusable, or forces us to import a non-classical logic whose semantics we then owe an account of. Much of the debate turns on treating the four corners as four *truth-values* or four *propositional contents*.

I want you to try a different framing, stated in my own terms so that you have to work with it rather than reach for a stock citation. Distinguish two things a speaker can do with a sentence:

- *locutionary negation*: putting a negated content into play ("it is not the case that P");
- *illocutionary retraction*: declining to assert the content at all — not asserting its negation, but refusing the assertoric move.

Now consider the hypothesis that each corner of the tetralemma is best read not as a content but as a *speech-act option*, and that the fourth corner is the illocutionary retraction of both the assertion and the denial, rather than the assertion of a contradictory content.
