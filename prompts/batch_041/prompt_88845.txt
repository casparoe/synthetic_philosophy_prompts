Six months ago I became chair of the grants committee of a small family foundation — my aunt's bequest, about four million pounds in assets, something in the region of a hundred and fifty thousand a year in grants of five to twenty thousand pounds each — and I have discovered that I have no idea how to decide what to do with it. I spent thirty years as an actuary, so I am at ease with expected values, which turns out to be precisely the problem. At our last meeting we gave ninety minutes to a proposal from a four-person outfit that put its own chances of averting a catastrophic pandemic at one in two hundred thousand; multiplied through by a hundred million lives, that number had quietly swallowed every other item on the agenda, including a literacy programme we have funded since before I joined the committee. Two of my trustees found this exhilarating, one found it preposterous, and I found that I could not give a principled reason for siding with any of them. Since then I have read enough about the philosophical problem of fanaticism in expected-value reasoning to see the shape of it, but everything I have found either defends the arithmetic or reaches for a technical fix — a cap on the value of a life, a discount on small probabilities, a shift to imprecise probabilities — and none of it tells me what four trustees in a two-hour meeting are meant to do on a Tuesday afternoon. I should add that I took early retirement at fifty-eight and this is the first thing I have done since that feels as though it matters, so I am keen not to make a fool of myself or of my aunt.

What I want from you is a procedure we can actually run: a numbered sequence of questions, in the order we should ask them, with a note at each step about what would count as a sufficient answer, who among us is competent to give it, and what we should do when the answer is not forthcoming. I have a half-formed taxonomy that I would like you to test, repair or replace. It seems to me that a proposal can inflate its expected value in three quite different ways — through the number of beneficiaries, through the magnitude of the benefit to each of them, and through attaching a non-zero probability to a payoff that is unbounded or infinite — and that the third is not simply the first two pushed harder, because it makes the arithmetic degenerate rather than merely large; if that is right, the procedure should treat the unbounded case as a stopping point rather than as one more term in a sum. The device I keep groping towards for the other two is what I would call a switching probability: instead of arguing about whether one in two hundred thousand is too optimistic, ask the proposer to name the probability at which their project would fall below the best conventional alternative on our list, and then ask how far that threshold lies below the bottom of the range of probabilities they themselves would defend. If the threshold sits inside that range, the decision is hostage to an estimate nobody in the room can make; if it sits well below the range, the project survives on the proposer's own uncertainties. Tell me whether that test is sound, what it misses, and how to run it when the two options are not commensurable in the same units.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show me the working: the pandemic outfit above; the literacy programme; and a third in which an applicant argues that because the long-run future may contain an enormous or unbounded number of lives, any action with any non-zero chance of influencing it has an expected value that cannot sensibly be compared with anything else — I want to see exactly where the procedure stops and says no, and where it hands the decision back to us because the question is one of values rather than of fact. Our constraints are these: four trustees, consensus rather than majority, a quarterly meeting of two hours, no one with a mathematical background but me, a duty to publish a short rationale to the wider family (some of whom think the whole enterprise is a scam), and a solicitor who has warned us that the charity's purposes must be defensible as being for the public benefit — I am not confident that people who do not yet exist can count, and I would rather the procedure flagged where that bites than settled it for me. Be explicit about the places where the procedure is doing nothing except forcing a value judgement into the open, and about what it cannot do at all; I would sooner have an honest list of failure modes than a promise of rigour. I suspect it will come out against the local programmes I am fond of, and I would rather know that now than at the next meeting. Three pages at most, in British English, and no reading list or citations — if you need to mention a position, do it in passing and I will look it up myself.
