I'm a philosophy PhD student with an amateur's enthusiasm for financial economics, and next month I have to present a position statement to a joint reading group that mixes epistemologists with a couple of economists — people who will enjoy finding the seams in what I say. Before I stand up in front of them, I want a consistency audit of what I actually believe about markets and knowledge. Please act as an expert in the economics of information and the epistemology of markets — someone fluent in decision theory, general equilibrium, and social epistemology alike — and think carefully before answering. Begin by brainstorming what each of my commitments most plausibly drags in its wake, then give me the audit itself.

Here is the set. (1) I'm a Hayekian about prices: market prices aggregate dispersed and largely tacit information in a way no central planner or single expert could, and that aggregation is their central epistemic virtue. (2) I accept the Aumann-style agreement result: two rational agents who share a prior and whose posterior beliefs are common knowledge cannot agree to disagree, so persistent disagreement is a symptom of differing priors or private information rather than of admirable open-mindedness. (3) I accept the no-trade result: if traders share a prior and it is common knowledge that all are rational and that the current allocation is already Pareto-efficient, no one will trade at a common price — speculative trade therefore requires heterogeneous priors, asymmetric information, or a failure of common knowledge. (4) I think a great deal of actual speculative activity — prediction markets, index funds, high-frequency arbitrage — is rational and epistemically valuable, not merely noise or a Keynesian beauty contest. (5) I think the efficient-market idea is approximately right: you cannot systematically beat the market, and prices sit close to the best available estimate of value. (6) I think information acquisition is costly and happens only when it is rewarded, so a perfectly efficient market would destroy the very incentive that makes it efficient.

Now the audit. Tell me which of these are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the set. Identify the pair that looks most damning but dissolves on inspection, and explain precisely why the appearance is misleading. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most trouble for the least damage, and which one commitment I have not noticed is quietly carrying most of the load. I would rather you discriminate carefully than flag every pair as conflicted — several of these are meant to be compatible, and the point of the exercise is to find out which, so please do not hedge by declaring the whole package incoherent.
