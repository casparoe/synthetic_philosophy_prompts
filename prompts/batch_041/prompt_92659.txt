I am a doctoral student in political theory, and I have been trying to state my own view about the justice of holdings without smuggling in a pattern I do not believe in. What follows is not a request for a survey of entitlement theory; I want the principle that actually earns these verdicts, or the admission that none does.

A chess prodigy earns a fortune from spectators who each buy a ticket they want more than the money; I take the resulting inequality to be just, and compulsory transfer from her to be unjust, whatever pattern it leaves. A lottery winner holds his winnings justly, though he deserved nothing and the losers bought in knowing the odds. A woman inherits a business her grandmother built through honest trade; her holdings are just even though she did nothing for them. By a long chain of purchases, each mutually advantageous and uncoerced, a man comes to own every well and spring in a desert region and then sets a price the poorest cannot pay: I judge his holding unjust and think the price may be capped, even though no seller was made worse off at the moment of sale. Peasants sell land for a pittance on a contract valid at law; they read the sum but not the value, and there is no fraud and no coercion. I want to call that a voluntary transfer in the thin sense the first case seems to require, and I still want it undone. A current owner's title runs back through a theft two centuries old; every transfer since was in good faith and he has improved the land. I judge that something is still owed to the dispossessed family's descendants, though I cannot say what the counterfactual baseline would have been and the present owner did no wrong. A man gambles and invests his way to destitution; I think the state may compel a transfer to keep him from starving — which, on its face, contradicts my verdict in the first case. And a man contracts to serve another for life in exchange for a lump sum paid to his family; I think he may nonetheless leave, so some voluntary transfers do not bind.

My tentative principle is this: a holding is just if it was acquired without wronging anyone, every later transfer was uncoerced and adequately informed, and no one is left beneath a floor of basic need. I suspect the third clause is patterned and is doing illegitimate work in the starvation case, and that the second is doing ad hoc work in the land and service cases. Tell me whether a single principle can keep all these verdicts, what clause each one forces, what the resulting account predicts for a case I have not listed, and which of my verdicts I should give up.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or the verdict that there isn't one — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
