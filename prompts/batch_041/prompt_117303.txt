I am preparing a research proposal for a one-semester graduate seminar in epistemology; the deliverable is a 6,000-word paper with a work plan attached, and I will be assessed as much on whether the plan is viable as on the eventual argument. My half-formed idea concerns the dogmatism paradox, and I want the proposal built around it rather than around a survey of what others have said. The target, as I understand it, is this: if you know that P, then by your own lights any evidence you later encounter against P is misleading evidence; a person is entitled to disregard evidence he knows to be misleading; so knowledge entitles you to disregard counterevidence you meet, which is a licence for dogmatism and therefore absurd, so something in that chain must give way. The responses I am aware of fault the step from knowing that P to knowing that the counterevidence is misleading, or the step from the evidence being misleading to my being entitled to disregard it, or the assumption that the entitlement is anything more than a licence to believe rather than a licence to act. My suspicion is that all of these stay with a synchronic question — what a knower may do when counterevidence arrives — and that the version of the paradox with real bite is diachronic: whether knowing that P entitles you to stop looking. If knowledge is the state in which inquiry is supposed to terminate, the paradox reads as a reductio of that picture; if it is not that state, the interesting question becomes what, if anything, closes a question, and whether the answer can be given in epistemic terms at all rather than by appeal to the practical costs of searching.

Turn this into a proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, and make clear which of the several things the paradox might be taken to show you are addressing. Explain why the existing positions leave it open, and why the diachronic framing is not merely a restatement of something familiar — in particular, address the obvious objection that the stopping problem is just the familiar thought that practical stakes bear on knowledge, and say what would have to be true for the framing to be doing genuine work. Set out the method: what you would actually do, section by section or month by month, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives. Say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and give a scope that one semester can bear. Do not include citations or a reading list; I will add those myself, and the plan should not lean on any particular text.
