I'm a postdoc and next Thursday I'm presenting at our department's game theory reading group (I'm only in it because my advisor is on the mailing list) and what I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense. The room, roughly: two people who work on equilibrium refinements and know Selten's stuff backwards, one evolutionary guy who basically only believes claims he's watched survive in a simulation, one philosopher of decision theory who spends his life on Newcomb-adjacent puzzles, and a mechanism designer from the econ department who comes for the coffee and then asks the question that ruins your day. So the center of gravity is equilibrium concepts, refinements, and stability under perturbation, with one outlier in each direction.

Here's what I'm defending, so the objections have something to bite: that superrationality is best understood not as a rival to expected-utility maximization but as an equilibrium selection principle for symmetric games. The claim is that when a game is symmetric and has a symmetric payoff-dominant profile, the fact that each player knows the other will reach the same conclusion as them — not that they're literally the same algorithm, just that they're both running this kind of reasoning — is enough to select the payoff-dominant profile, and that all the similar-minds talk in the original presentation of the idea is doing no real work. My two showcase cases are the stag hunt (I say superrationality gets Stag right) and a version of the prisoners dilemma where the players are only approximately identical, i.e. there's a small chance the other guy's reasoning process differs from mine, which is where I expect to get killed. I'd also like to claim this is continuous with the standard symmetry argument for symmetric games, but I'm honestly not sure that helps me or hurts me.

So: give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they do, each phrased the way these particular people would phrase it (the refinements people will put it one way, the evolutionary guy another, the decision theorist another — that difference matters to me, I want to recieve them in the vocabulary I'll actually hear). For each one: a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should just concede up front rather than fight. Tell me which objection I should preempt in the first two minutes of the talk itself, and where the weakest point in my own position is that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. Please don't give me the objections I'd get from the online rationalist / functional-decision-theory crowd — I know those, and half this room has never heard of them and wouldn't be moved by them anyway; I want the ones this specific audience will actually raise. And include the one question I'm praying nobody asks, because I'm fairly sure there is one and I'd rather see it in writing on Sunday than seperate from my dignity in the seminar room on Thursday.
