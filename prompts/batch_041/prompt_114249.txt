I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about whether there is any moral difference between lying and misleading, and I came out holding a position I'm not sure I can defend: that once you hold fixed what the hearer ends up believing, what the speaker intended, and the harm that follows, the bare fact that a falsehood was asserted rather than left for the hearer to infer makes no independent moral difference. On my view the sense that lying is worse is an artifact of detectability and attributability -- lies are easier to catch and to pin on someone -- reinforced by the unusual crispness of our norms of assertion. My friend says I have made the two cases artificially identical precisely where they differ, and that the difference is real: assertion puts the speaker's word behind the content in a way that an implicature or a strategic omission does not, so lying is a breach of a trust the hearer was entitled to extend, whereas misleading merely exploits reasoning the hearer was doing anyway. I feel the pull of that, and I also see how to turn it around -- that exploiting someone's own inference treats them more like a tool than lying does, so if anything the misleading is worse.

Play it straight and tell me what would count against my position. I want the arguments, cases, and distinctions that should move me if they hold up, sorted by how much weight they really carry, and I want to know which of them a defender of my view would simply concede and absorb. In particular: is the appeal to the norm of assertion doing independent work, or is it redescribing the intuition it is meant to explain; does the position collapse into a merely verbal thesis about how we label act-types; could the difference be located not in the hearer at all but in what each act does to the speaker's own relation to the truth; and are there cases where the two come apart and we confidently judge the misleading worse, or the lie more forgivable? Tell me honestly whether my view risks anything, or whether I have quietly defined the comparison so that nothing could count against it. Answer in American English.
