I'm a backend engineer, no formal philosphy background, and I was at a security meetup last week where a guy told a story that finally put words to something I've been noticing for years but dont have a name for. It shows up whenever a bunch of people can each independently take some action that affects everyone, and none of them can coordinate first. Say there's a nasty bug in a popular open-source library. Ten different security researchers find it around the same time. Each one thinks: if I don't publish a write-up or a proof-of-concept, one of the others will, and they might do it worse -- sloppier, or with a working exploit, or before the maintainer has a patch. So each one publishes, and the result is that the bug goes public way earlier than any of them wanted, and everyone is worse off. The weird part is that nobody is being selfish or reckless. Each person's reasoning is actually pretty reasonable given what they know about the others. It's the fact that there are many of them, all reasoning the same way, that makes the bad outcome nearly certain. I've seen versions of this in startup teams shipping features, in city councils approving permits, and in AI labs deciding whether to release a model.

I know there's probably a name for this and I'm guessing it's something like a "many hands" problem or a "tragedy of the commons" thing, but those don't feel right. In a tragedy of the commons, each person is taking a resource for themselves; here everyone is trying to do the right thing, and the problem is that their separate attempts to do the right thing add up to a disaster. And it's not the bystander effect, because in that one nobody acts; here everybody acts. Is there an established term for this exact configuration -- where the number of independent agents itself makes the bad outcome likely, even though each agent is acting in good faith and with decent information? And if there is a term, what are its edges? Like, does it require that the agents can't communicate, or that they're uncertain about what the others will do, or that the action is irreversible? I'm less interested in the label than in the carving: what makes this different from a coordination failure, from a collective action problem, and from the case where one person's action is just multiplied by many?
