What I'd said: I've been reading about how hard it is to know the real effects of what you do, and I said it reminded me of my own field, where you can never run the controlled experiment on the system-level effect, only on the little bit you can isolate.

Ruth: Take something as clean as donating to a deworming charity — the trials show it helps kids. But the consequentialist question isn't whether those kids get better, it's the total difference the donation makes: different childhoods, different families, knock-on political effects, and so on forever. Nobody can trace that. So 'do the act with the best consequences' is either empty advice or a lottery.

Danny: That's just uncertainty, and we live with that all the time — you don't know the other driver won't run the light either. You assign credences and maximize expected value. And if you refuse to act because the tail is unknowable, that's still an act, with its own unknowable tail. The cluelessness cuts both ways, so it can't favor doing nothing.

Ruth: It's not the same kind of uncertainty. Ordinary gambles have a fixed menu of outcomes; here the chain never stops, so the 'sum over all consequences' isn't a number you're unsure about, it's a number that doesn't exist. And it isn't just how much — for plenty of acts you can't even say whether the long-run effect is net good or net bad. Expected value over an unsigned quantity is a coin flip with math painted on it.

Danny: Two replies. One, even if I can't sign the far future, I can sign the near term, and the near term is where my evidence is strongest — so I act on the part of the chain I can estimate, and I don't pretend to have duties to the year 3000. That's a fact about the world being unfair to consequentialists, not an argument for a different ethic.

Ruth: Your first reply is the thing under dispute. If the right act is the one with the best total consequences, then truncating the chain at the near term isn't licensed by the theory, it's a different theory wearing the same name — rules, or virtues, or a threshold, doing the work that consequences were supposed to do. Your second reply I half accept: maybe it shows the world is unfair. But then the honest conclusion is that consequentialism can't be a decision procedure at all, and it isn't doing any work in your hands.

Danny: Then here's a distinction. Call it simple cluelessness: I don't know whether my act helps or hurts. Complicated cluelessness: I'm confident it helps, I just can't trace how much or through what. Only the first is a problem for expected-value reasoning. You keep building your case out of the first, but I think we're mostly in the second, and the second is fine.

Ruth: And I think the cases that actually matter — the big ones, the ones people agonize over — are exactly the first, and you don't get to just assert that we're usually in the easy case. That's the whole question.
