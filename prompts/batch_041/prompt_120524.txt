I am currently taking a course on Bayesian confirmation theory, and we have been working through the problem of old evidence: a hypothesis H appears strongly confirmed by evidence E even though E was already known, and therefore already certain, before H was formulated. A classmate has proposed a principle she calls evidential amortization, according to which such evidence should still count, but only in proportion to how improbable E would have been had we been asked to predict it in advance using only the background knowledge available before H was introduced — antecedently certain evidence would carry no confirming weight, antecedently surprising evidence nearly full weight. Please compare evidential amortization with the alternative treatment that evaluates the support of E for H using the credences the agent actually held before she learned E, rather than reconstructing a counterfactual prior from her present background knowledge. Are the two ever genuinely different in their verdicts, or does the second collapse into the first once we ask what the agent's pre-E credences ought to have been? And which of them better accommodates the intuition that old evidence can confirm a hypothesis even when the agent's earlier credences were, by her own later lights, unreasonable?
