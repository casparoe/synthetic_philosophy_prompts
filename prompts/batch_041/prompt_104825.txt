My roommate Marcus and my buddy Devin got into a two-hour fight at our place last night about whether it's irrational to have a cycle of preferences, and I was stuck on the couch listening to the whole thing. Marcus says cycles are irrational: say you'd rather have an apple than a banana, a banana than a cherry, and a cherry than an apple. Then he can drain you — hand you an apple, offer to trade it for a banana plus a penny (you prefer the banana, so you take the deal), then trade the banana for the cherry plus a penny, then the cherry back for the apple plus a penny, and your right back where you started, minus three cents, and you'll do it again. Anyone you can milk like that has a broken preference ordering, he says. Devin says thats only because the person is a sucker — the second you notice the pattern you just stop trading, so the pump shows that being naive costs you money, not that the cycle itself is irrational. Marcus shot back that this just admits the rational move is to refuse a trade you prefer at the time, which gives up the idea that you should always take the option you like best right now. Devin said fine, maybe rationality is about your plan over time and not about every single moment. Marcus said then Devin's own defense proves his point for him: the pump only ever got going by assuming you have to grab the better option at every step, so if you're allowed to stick to a plan instead, the pump never starts, and if you're not allowed to, he's just assumed the conclusion. Devin said Marcus is the one smuggling in an assumption — that rationality means maximizing your satisfaction at each moment. I honestly couldn't follow who was landing hits. Can you adjudicate this move by move — where Marcus is right that a cycle leaves you open to being drained, where Devin is right that seeing it coming defuses the pump, where each of them is quietly assuming something they never defended, and what both of them are missing?
