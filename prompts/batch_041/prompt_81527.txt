You are a philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of machine learning, asked to produce a classification instrument for an interdisciplinary reading group that meets fortnightly on the question of how we should talk about the inner lives of large language models. The group includes cognitive scientists, two interpretability researchers and a policy officer, and its recurring argument is not whether such systems are conscious but the narrower question of when, if ever, it is true — or warranted, or merely convenient — to say that a system believes something. What I need is an instrument that sorts participants by what they say about cases rather than by the labels they prefer, so that a realist and an instrumentalist can discover exactly where they part company.

Please write a decision tree for this purpose. Begin by thinking the matter through: brainstorm the distinctions that are genuinely doing work here, sketch the logical space they generate, and discard any question that both sides of the dispute would answer in the same way. Only then arrange the surviving questions into a tree, with each node phrased as a question a participant could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf carrying the descriptive name of a position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the questions first and locate the positions in the space afterwards; do not simply reproduce the familiar realist / instrumentalist / eliminativist triad with new labels.

The following cases must all be accommodated by the tree, since they are the ones the group keeps returning to:

- A model trained on a corpus containing a false claim repeats that claim in an unrelated context: has it believed something false, or merely carried a trace of its corpus?
- A model's answer depends on information that appears nowhere in its stated reasoning: did it know it, or was the information merely causally present?
- A model's professed goal or preference shifts with the framing of the question: does it have goals at all, or only locally stable dispositions to answer?
- Two identical deployments of the same weights, given different system prompts, disagree with one another: do they believe different things, or is belief simply not the kind of thing to be individuated by context?
- A model states a rule it then violates: does it believe the rule while acting against it, and does that force a distinction between belief and mere verbal report?
- A model trained before some event asserts, in a sincere-sounding way, a state of affairs that has since changed: does the training cutoff date its beliefs, or date only its information?

Your tree must find a place for at least the following positions, if the logical space permits: a view on which belief-talk about such systems is a warranted predictive convenience with no fact of the matter behind it; a fictionalist view that endorses the practice while denying it is literally true; a quietist view that rejects the practice altogether; a functional-role realist view; a view that makes phenomenal consciousness a necessary condition; a view on which content is fixed by the interpreter's theory rather than by the system; and a mechanistic view on which interpretability research is the arbiter. If the space you construct cannot accommodate one of these, say which question you were unable to formulate and why.

Include the diagnostic work that makes such an instrument useful rather than decorative. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone defends, mark it as unoccupied and diagnose why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two positions give identical verdicts at every node, flag them explicitly as a pair the tree cannot separate rather than forcing a distinction. Keep the whole instrument to a single page: no more than twelve nodes and no more than twelve leaves. Close with a short paragraph on what the instrument reveals about the dispute, including which single question does most of the sorting.

Do not include citations, references, reading lists or author names of any kind; the instrument must be usable by someone with no library access, so name positions descriptively by what they claim. Write in British English throughout.
