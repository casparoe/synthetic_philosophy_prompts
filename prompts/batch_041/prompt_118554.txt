I am a high school junior preparing a proposal for an independent research project in philosophy of mind, to be supervised by my school's humanities department over one semester — roughly three months of part-time work — and I need you to write the proposal itself. The topic is the question at the centre of the debate over whether large language models genuinely understand language or only reproduce statistical patterns from their training data.

Here is my half-formed idea, which I would like you to test and then either sharpen into a workable project or replace with the nearest idea that can carry one. Nearly everyone in this debate argues about the successes: whether the model's correct answers are produced by the right kind of mechanism, or whether its competence generalises the way genuine understanding would. I want to come at it through failure instead. My hunch is that understanding is most visible in the shape of a system's mistakes. Someone who has a mistaken theory of a subject — a student who thinks heavier objects fall faster — is not wrong at random; their errors are stable, they survive being reworded, and they shift in predictable ways when the underlying facts change. A system that has only soaked up the statistics of text should instead be wrong in ways that track the surface of the prompt: its phrasing, the order in which options are given, how often something appeared in the training data. So the criterion I want to test is roughly this: does the model fail like something that has a picture of the world, or like something that has only a picture of sentences? I do not know whether that contrast can be made precise, whether it is a genuine distinction, or whether any behavioural test could separate the two cases.

Write me the proposal. It needs to do all of the following, and I would rather it be dense than padded:

- State the research question so that it has a determinate answer, rather than restating the general puzzle.
- Explain why the standard positions leave my question open, without walking me through the debate at large — I already know it.
- Give a method I could actually execute: one laptop, no budget, no lab, no access to the inside of any model, only publicly available chat models and small open models I can run locally. I need concrete probes, a way of classifying a given error as intelligible or surface-tracking that does not come down to my own impression, and a plan for what to compare against.
- Say what a result would look like, and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the distinction I am proposing is empty.
- Be honest about whether this fits in one semester of part-time work by a high school student, and cut it down if it does not.

Two further things. Commit: pick one way of sharpening the idea and argue for it; do not hand me three variants and let me choose. And do not include any citations, references, or reading lists — I will add those myself, and I would rather you not guess at titles or authors.

Maximum 750 words.
