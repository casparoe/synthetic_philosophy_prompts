Hey, so I've been reading LessWrong and the old decision theory forums for like a decade now, and I run a small local meetup — usually 14 to 16 people, we've got a 75-minute slot this month. I want to do a session on updateless decision theory. Problem is I've tried this before and it always goes the same way: I tell the counterfactual mugging story, everyone nods, someone says "oh so you precommit," and then we all go home and act like CDT people the rest of the week. Hearing the story is definately not the same as feeling the pull of it.

So design me a game, not a lecture. The idea should live in the rules and payoffs, so people run into the weirdness themselves before anyone says "updateless" or "counterfactual mugging" out loud. They shouldn't hear either name until the debrief.

Here's a mechanic I was kicking around, feel free to fix it or throw it out: before anything is revealed, everyone privately writes a policy card — what they'd do at Table A and what they'd do at Table B. Then we randomly assign people to a table, and here's the trick: the payoff you get at your table is partly determined by what your *card* says about the *other* table, the one you're not sitting at and can never act in. So the only thing you actually control is the card, and half the value is in a branch where your decision node doesn't even exist. I want them to feel how the local node always wants to defect and how the ex-ante policy obviously shouldn't, and to notice the disagreement between those two feelings. Maybe add a point where people can pay a small cost to rewrite their card after seeing their table — that usually flushes out who actually gets it.

Constraints: no real money, use tokens or candy or points, whatever's cheap. I need the full rules, materials, setup, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the actual distinction (policy vs node, why evidential and causal reasoning both give the "wrong" local answer here, why the ex-ante evaluation treats all branches as yours). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing some people will just treat the card as a binding contract and totally miss the point, and others will try to game the random assignment — and what to do about that.

And be straight with me about what the activity *can't* reproduce, the part I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. I have a feeling it can't really give you the acausal / logical-correlation cases where the other branch is connected to you by something thinner than a card you wrote, and I'd rather you tell me that honestly than pretend the game covers everything.
