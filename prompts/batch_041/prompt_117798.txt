Okay, so I've been trying to actually understand Dutch book arguments rather than just nodding along, and I've gotten stuck. The arguement goes: if your credences don't obey the probability axioms, then there's a collection of bets — each of which you'd accept given your own credences — that leaves you a sure loser, so your credences are irrational. But it seems to me the whole thing quietly assumes that a credence just is a bundle of betting dispositions: that having credence 0.7 means you'll buy or sell at 7:3, that you'll do it at any stake, and that you don't care which side of the bet you're on. And that's just not true of me. I'm risk-averse — I'll turn down a bet that's fair by my own lights if the stake is big enough. And I've got the Ellsberg thing, where I'd rather bet on the urn with the known mix than the one with the mystery mix even though by my own numbers they should come out the same. If I can simply refuse the bets, where's the sure loss? So has the argument actually shown anything about my credences, or only that a certain idealized bettor would get fleeced?

I also keep running into the claim that the argument is merely pragmatic — that it only shows you'd lose money, not that your beliefs are bad in any distinctively epistemic way — and that you need a so-called depragmatized version to get real irrationality out of it, something about replacing the money with the agent's own degrees of belief, or her own preferences, so that the loss is one she'd concede is bad by her own lights. But I cant tell whether that's a genuine fix or just the same trick in a lab coat. Does it actually get you from the claim that you'd lose a bet to the claim that you're inaccurate, or that you're incoherent in a way that's bad as a believer?

So what I want is: spell out what a credence would have to be for the Dutch book argument to even get off the ground, say whether dropping those assumptions leaves anything of it standing, and explain what the depragmatized version is really supposed to buy you. And if the plain argument doesn't establish probabilism on its own, what does it establish?
