I am applying for a three-year doctoral studentship in metaphysics and need a research proposal of about 2,500 words. What I have is a half-formed idea, and I would like it turned into a proposal with a question sharp enough to have a determinate answer, a method, and an honest scope.

The starting point is the old puzzle about two spheres alike in every intrinsic respect, alone in space, and whether there could be two such things rather than one. I have never found that case as troubling as it is meant to be. What does trouble me is a mathematical case with the same shape: the field of complex numbers is unchanged by the operation that interchanges the two square roots of minus one. Every equation, every relation, every structural fact about the field survives the interchange. So there appear to be two distinct objects and no fact of the matter as to which is which. Something similar is said of two electrons in a singlet state, whose individual properties are held to be indiscernible even though there are manifestly two of them.

I suspect these cases are not one problem wearing three sets of clothes, and that the differences between them matter more than the resemblances. I also suspect that the standard reply — that any two things can be told apart by some relation they bear to each other, such as being two metres apart — either answers a weaker question than the one asked or quietly presupposes the distinctness it is meant to establish, since a symmetric relation singles out neither of its relata.

I want the proposal to do the following. State the question precisely, and in particular settle what is being quantified over: objects in a world, positions in a structure, or something else. Say which reading of the principle of identity of indiscernibles makes the three cases comparable and which makes them come apart, and why the existing positions — those that posit a primitive thisness, those that forbid it, and those that treat the principle as a triviality about language rather than a truth about the world — leave the question open. Explain what would have to be true for the mathematical case to be decisive for the metaphysical one, and what would show that the comparison is a pun on the word 'object'. Set out the method: what formal apparatus I would need, how the case analysis would be organised, and the order of work term by term. Say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the three cases cannot be brought under a single question at all, in which case the honest deliverable is a taxonomy rather than a thesis. Argue for the approach you choose over the obvious alternatives, say what turns on the answer for anyone outside the debate, and give a candid assessment of what could make the project too large for three years.

No bibliography or citations; I will supply those myself. Write in British English.
