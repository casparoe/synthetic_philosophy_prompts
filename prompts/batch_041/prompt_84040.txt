I'm not a philosopher, just someone who watches a lot of movies and reads too much on Wikipedia at night. I've been stuck on A Clockwork Orange for a while — both the Burgess novel and Kubrick's film — and I keep feeling like the usual conversation about it, the one where everyone says they took away his free will, is missing something. Then I read about Strawson's idea of reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, hurt feelings, forgiveness, love — and the thought that being a moral agent is mostly about being the kind of thing that can take part in those attitudes, and that we sometimes step back into an objective stance toward someone (treating them as a case to be managed, a child, a patient, a specimen) instead of a participant. And I thought: wait, that's the whole movie.

So here's what I want. Act as someone who works on moral responsibility and knows the Strawson literature well, and who also knows these two works. Start by brainstorming the scenes that matter (the chaplain's objection, the Ludovico demonstration, the beating by the police who turn out to include an old droog, the tramp, Alexander's revenge, the suicide attempt, the hospital ending, and in the novel the twenty-first chapter where Alex meets Pete and seems to have outgrown it all — the chapter the American edition cut and Kubrick left out). Then give me a reading of the novel and the film through Strawson's reactive attitudes.

I want you to be honest in both directions: where Strawson illuminates the work, and where the work pushes back on him. A few specific things I can't sort out:
