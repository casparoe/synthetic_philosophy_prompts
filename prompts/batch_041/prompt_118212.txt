My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether there are true contradictions. My friend is a dialetheist: she thinks some sentences are both true and false, that the liar paradox is a genuine case of this rather than a mere breakdown of language, and that classical logic should be replaced by a paraconsistent logic that does not let a contradiction explode into triviality. I am a classical logician: I think “true and false” is at best a confusing way of saying “true in one sense and false in another,” and that giving up principles like disjunctive syllogism or modus ponens in order to accommodate the paradoxes costs more than it saves. By the end we could at least see that we were talking past each other. I want us to write the document we should have produced: an adversarial-collaboration joint statement that both of us could sign.

Please draft it. It should have two parts.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than truisms: what both sides accept about the behavior of the liar and Curry paradoxes, about what a paraconsistent logic is supposed to do, about the difference between rejecting a sentence and accepting its negation, about the recapture problem (how classical reasoning is to be recovered in consistent or uncontaminated domains), and about what would count as a decisive objection to either side. Where agreement depends on a formulation, give the formulation both sides would accept.

Second, a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the dialetheist’s position, the classical logician’s position, the case or argument both sides agree bears on the crux, and each side’s prediction about that case. The cruxes should include at least: (i) whether a paraconsistent negation can express exclusion — whether “not P” can mean that P is not the case rather than merely that P is false-or-both — without the theory collapsing into triviality; (ii) whether rejecting disjunctive syllogism is a tolerable cost or a reductio of the paraconsistent program; (iii) whether the “just true” versus “true and false” distinction is a real semantic distinction or an ad hoc label; and (iv) whether the classical logician’s own account of the liar paradox is stable once we ask what makes the relevant semantic predicates determinate. For at least one crux, use a concrete non-paradoxical case, such as two reliable instruments issuing contradictory readings about the same quantity, and ask what each side predicts about which inferences should be allowed.

For each crux, do not just assert that one side is right. State the strongest version of each side’s position in wording its own adherent would accept. Where you suspect a disagreement is merely verbal — for example, about whether “true” in “true contradiction” means the same thing as “true” in ordinary truth-talk — say so explicitly and explain what would remain if the verbal issue were set aside. Also identify at least one claim that sounds like a disagreement but is actually shared, and at least one claim that sounds like agreement but hides a crux.

The document should be constructive, not a verdict. Neither side should be able to say it was strawmanned. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to particular authors; I want the argument itself. Format the agreed claims as a numbered section and the disagreements as a table.
