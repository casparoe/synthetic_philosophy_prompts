"Suppose both the July Crisis decision-making and the longer-run structural pressures were, taken separately, each sufficient for the outbreak of war in 1914. And here's what the student wrote:

"Mill's target is our habit of singling out one antecedent and calling it *the* cause. Strictly speaking the cause is the whole set of conditions, positive and negative, that together suffice for the effect, and which one of them we single out for praise or blame is a matter of practical interest rather than metaphysics. So on Mill's view there's no fact of the matter about whether the July Crisis or the structural pressures was *the* cause of 1914 — both are conditions of the larger whole that produced the war. Mill also observes that the same effect can have a plurality of causes, which is often cited as one reason the Method of Difference is hard to apply in social and historical inquiry. Overdetermination — two sets of conditions each sufficient for the same effect in the same instance — is that plurality of causes by another name.

Mackie's INUS analysis says a cause is an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition. The N is doing the real work here: to count as a cause, a factor has to be necessary for the effect. On the stipulated setup neither the July Crisis nor the structural pressures is necessary, since the war would have happened without either one. So Mackie's analysis implies that in this case neither factor is a cause. That seems like the right result for redundant causation, and it means Mackie agrees with Mill that hunting for the cause is a mistake, though for the opposite reason: for Mackie there is no cause at all, whereas for Mill every condition is one.

The counterfactual account says that c caused e just in case, had c not occured, e would not have occured. Neither factor passes that test in 1914 — the war would have happened anyway — so the counterfactual theorist has to either deny that anything caused the war or add machinery. Lewis does add machinery, quasi-dependence, for cases where the counterfactual dependence is masked, and that's the same fix that's needed here.

(ii) I think overdetermination shows that the causal question is simply the wrong question for historians. If we can't say that either the July Crisis or the structural pressures caused the war, we should stop asking what caused it and ask instead which factors raised its probability, or tell the story in a way that shows how the war came to seem inevitable. Probability-raising has the advantage that it doesn't require necessity, so it survives the case that breaks the counterfactual test."
