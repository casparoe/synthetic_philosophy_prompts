A friend who edits for a living has been arguing that the way to fix politics is to fix prose, and he rests the argument on a comparison Orwell draws in his essay on politics and the English language: a man takes to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fails all the more completely because he drinks, which Orwell says is rather the same thing that is happening to English. Since an effect can become a cause, my friend concludes, the loop between sloppy thinking and sloppy writing is the thing to attack, and because it is the cheapest place to intervene, plain-writing rules and style guides are political instruments rather than cosmetics. Having spent a lot of time in rationalist blogs, I am suspicious of the loop itself: the usual view in those circles is that thought runs on something prior to English and words are a transmission layer, which would make the causal half of the analogy run backwards, though I also notice how much we enjoy stories about self-reinforcing feedback loops and cannot tell whether that makes me too credulous or too dismissive here. So audit the comparison. Give me the correspondence table — who or what plays the drinker, the drink, the initial failure, the recovery — then tell me which features of the drinking case the analogy quietly imports into the language case, which of the disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion my friend wants, and whether that conclusion survives once they are on the table. And if some other self-reinforcing pattern fits language change better, say what it is and what it would change.
