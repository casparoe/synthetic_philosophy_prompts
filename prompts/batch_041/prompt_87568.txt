I am a novelist at work on a book whose climax is a procedural maneuver, and I want its intellectual machinery to be sound. The episode I am fictionalizing is real: the United States Senate's use of the so-called nuclear option, when a majority unable to muster the sixty votes that its own Rule XXII requires to close debate instead sustained a ruling from the chair that the rule did not mean what it appeared to mean — first for most nominations in 2013, then for Supreme Court nominations in 2017 — and so altered the rule by a procedure the rule did not authorize. I have been thinking about Nomic, the game in which amending the rules is itself a legal move, some rules being entrenched against ordinary amendment while others lie open to it and at least one licenses its own revision. Read the Senate episode through that lens. Who are the players, and what precisely is the move being made? Does the paradox of self-amendment leave it undecided whether the maneuver was a rule-change at all or merely an interpretation with the same effect, and does the distinction between entrenched and ordinary rules explain why the change stuck — or does it reveal that entrenchment is only a convention a determined majority can reinterpret away? Finally, tell me where the analogy fails, and what difference it would make that my fictional chamber, unlike the Senate, has an explicit self-amendment clause, given that in the real case the body empowered to judge the amendment's legitimacy was the very body that stood to gain from it.
