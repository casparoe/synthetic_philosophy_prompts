I have been teaching myself decision theory in my spare time — no coursework behind it, just a pile of lecture notes and a couple of textbooks I have been working through slowly — and I keep circling one idea that I cannot decide whether to admire or resent. The idea is this: if two people are both rational, if each knows the other's beliefs and knows the other is rational, and if the two of them began from the same prior probabilities, then they cannot knowingly keep on disagreeing. As soon as they compare notes and it becomes common knowledge that they differ, the difference should collapse, because a persistent gap would have to come from some piece of information one of them is holding and the other is not. A companion result says that agents who share a prior cannot find a bet that both of them consider favorable, so a rational person should never take a wager from someone whose information structure is the same as their own. I understand these are theorems, and I understand they are proved for idealized agents who never tire, never flinch, and never hold two beliefs at once. What I want to poke at is a different question: what would it look like if an institution stopped treating the shared prior as a harmless assumption in a model and started treating it as a fact about its own employees?

So please write me the internal dispute-resolution handbook of an organization that has done exactly that — a company, a ministry, a university department, whatever you like, with whatever name suits it — presented as a deadpan corporate policy document of roughly seven hundred to nine hundred words, laid out in numbered clauses and written in the flat, soothing, we-are-all-in-this-together register of a human-resources compliance manual. The humor should come entirely from the office getting the underlying theory right and then following it without mercy. I would like the document to commit, clause by clause, to the shared-prior doctrine and let each commitment have its absurd consequence: a procedure for what happens when two colleagues file a disagreement and the office insists that the disagreement must therefore be a hidden cache of private information, which it will extract by interview until the two opinions coincide; a rule that once both parties have publicly announced their differing beliefs the disagreement is no longer recognized as a valid state and must be resolved by the end of the quarter; a ban on internal betting and office pools on the grounds that no two employees with the same information could both find the odds acceptable, together with the awkward exception the company makes for its own vendors; and a waiver process for employees who insist their prior actually does differ from the company standard, which requires them to justify the deviation and be re-educated until they no longer need it. I would also like the handbook to quietly betray where the theorem's preconditions fail to hold — that real people are not perfectly rational, that a disagreement is often not common knowledge even when it is public, that nobody can actually inspect anyone else's prior — without the document ever admitting that these are failures, since it should treat the whole thing as settled procedure. It should be funny to someone who already knows the agreement theorem and the no-trade result and readable to someone who has never heard of either, and it should never wink at the reader or step outside its corporate voice to explain the joke.
