I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of physics and I've somehow been roped into TAing the master's-level foundations of quantum mechanics seminar this term, which is honestly how I got dragged into the Everett stuff in the first place — I'd always filed "many-worlds" under "silly pop-sci thing I don't need to think about" until I had to teach the probability objection and realized I couldn't actually say, in my own words, why you can't just count the branches. Now I can't stop thinking about it. Anyway, the prof is away at a conference for the last meeting and has (generously, annoyingly) asked me to write the take-home exam question on exactly this, the Born-rule problem, and I want to get it right because it's also going to be the seed of my own chapter.

I want ONE essay question, about 90 minutes of work, and I want it to be unanswerable by anyone who's just memorized "Deutsch gave a decision-theoretic derivation and Wallace defended it." The thing I keep noticing in office hours is that half the class can tell me branch-counting fails and the other half can recite the decision-theoretic move, but almost nobody can put the two together — i.e. say precisely what goes wrong with counting branches (why the count is basis-relative, why you can't just relabel outcomes so that every branch gets equal amplitude, what work the amplitudes are secretly doing all along), and then say exactly what a derivation of the probability rule would have to accomplish for it to be more than assuming the conclusion. I'd also like the question to force them to take a side on whether the decision-theoretic approach gives you genuine probability or only a rule for betting, and to say what would have to be true for the objection that a theory with no probability rule has no empirical content to actually bite. Please don't make the question depend on any specific paper title or year — I don't want them rewarded for citation-dropping and honestly half the class wouldn't know them anyway. And write it so a smart student who knows the physics but hasn't read the literature can still get a B, while only someone who can genuinely reconstruct the argument gets an A.

So: the question itself (give it some real bite — maybe a short setup they have to respond to, not just "discuss"), a model answer of about 700-800 words written the way a strong student would actually write it, and a marking scheme that names the specific moves a top answer has to make — I'm thinking five or six must-hit points, each with a mark value, total 25 — plus, for each, a one-liner on the mistake that costs a student that point (e.g. conflating the failure of branch-counting with a failure of the whole interpretation, or treating the decision-theoretic derivation as if it were an ordinary derivation from the formalism). End the whole thing with a single last line beginning "Summary:" that tells me, in one sentence, what separates an A answer from a B answer, so I can paste it into the rubric header.
