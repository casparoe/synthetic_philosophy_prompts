I'm running a reading group next week on the trolley problem and the doctrine of double effect, and I've gotten stuck on the orginal text — Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 64, art. 7, "Whether it is lawful to kill a man in self-defense?" The bit I keep rereading is this:

"Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental... Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful... And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful... Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's. But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good... it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority..."

What I can't work out is what "beside the intention" is actually doing here. He grants that the act can result in the aggressor's death, and that you can see that coming — so if the killing is foreseen, in what sense is it not intended? Is this a claim about the agent's psychology, about the structure of the act itself, or just a way of keeping the act's "species" clean? And then he adds that a private person may not *intend* the killing, which makes it sound like the very same physical act is lawful or not depending on something going on in your head. Does that distinction actually survive once you set it next to the trolley cases, where the whole point is supposed to be that you may foresee a death without intending it — or does Aquinas need something stronger than "I didn't mean it"? And that line about being bound to take more care of your own life than another's seems to sit badly with the impartiality the trolley literature takes for granted; is that a real tension in the doctrine or am I misreading it? I dont want the whole history of double effect, just help reading this one paragraph. Keep it under 500 words.
