I have been following, for years now, the argument between the historians who write at the scale of all humanity — the sweeping syntheses that run from foraging bands through the first states to global modernity — and the critics within the discipline who insist that at that scale the evidence cannot bear the claims, and that the narratives carry a politics while presenting themselves as mere summary. What strikes me is that nothing moves: the critics press the trade-off between generality and evidential warrant, the synthesisers reply that the critics have chosen a smaller canvas and renamed it rigour, and both go home. My hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about the past at all but about what a historical explanation is for — one side after the causes that recur across every case, the other after the texture of particular cases — and perhaps also about who has standing to speak for the whole. I would like you to diagnose it. Is the impasse empirical, in that the large-scale claims are simply false; methodological, a disagreement about what counts as evidence; or conceptual, two different questions wearing a single name? Tell me which side's moves genuinely engage the other and which only pass by, and then say what would count as progress — a shared standard of warrant, a distinction that lets the two kinds of question coexist, or nothing at all, because the two sides want incompatible things from history. Please write it as continuous prose, the way you would explain it in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
