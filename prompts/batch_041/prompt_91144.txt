I am a novelist at work on a near-future political novel set in a midsized city that has adopted futarchy: the city council sets broad goals, and prediction markets are used to choose among policies meant to achieve them. My protagonist is the first director of the Office of Decision Markets, a three-person agency that must triage every proposal reaching the council and decide whether it goes to a market, to ordinary deliberation, or back to the department for more work. The office sees dozens of proposals a year, usually on a two-week clock, and its recommendations can be overridden by the mayor only at a political cost. I want the novel to feel intellectually honest, so I need you to act as a political philosopher with a working knowledge of mechanism design and municipal governance, and to help me build the decision procedure the protagonist actually uses.

Please think carefully before you write. Start by brainstorming the failure modes of futarchy-style governance at the scale of a city: manipulation, low liquidity, metric myopia, legitimacy problems, value disputes disguised as empirical ones, and anything else you judge important. Then turn the underlying principle -- that representatives should settle values while markets settle empirical questions about how to achieve them -- into a procedure a smart, overworked staffer could run on a proposal. The procedure should be a numbered sequence of questions or tests, in the order they should be asked, with clear decision rules, stop conditions, and explicit notes on which steps are judgment calls rather than mechanical checks. It should be usable by someone who understands the theory but is not a mechanism-design expert.

I want the procedure built around three variables I have been using in my notes, and I need you to define them operationally and integrate them into the steps: the manipulation surface of a decision, meaning the gap between the metric the market would settle on and the outcome the public actually cares about; the metric half-life, meaning how long the outcome measure is likely to remain a good proxy before it is gamed or becomes obsolete; and the legitimacy budget, meaning how much acceptance a market verdict can expect from the side that loses. The procedure should say how to estimate each, what to do when the estimate is uncertain, and where the office must simply hand the decision to elected officials because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals, showing the steps in action and the recommendation that falls out: first, whether to site a supervised injection facility in one of two neighborhoods; second, whether to choose between two flood-defense designs, one cheaper but less resilient and one more expensive but with a longer horizon; and third, whether to rename a public square after a historical figure whose legacy is contested. At least one of the three should come out against using a prediction market in a way my protagonist will find frustrating, and you should say why the procedure nevertheless requires that result.

Finally, add a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of decisions it will misfire on, the ways a bad actor could still capture the process, and the questions it must leave to ordinary politics. Do not give me a reading list, bibliography, or citations; I need the procedure itself, in plain prose, with enough detail that I can put it in a character's mouth or a fictional operations manual. Write in a formal, polished register.
