So I've fallen down a rabbit hole on incommensurable values and hard choices — the whole "is there a fact of the matter about which of two options is better when neither dominates" thing — and I want to turn it into one of those "which position do you actually hold" quizzes for my blog, in the vein of the old philosophical personality tests but way more in the weeds.

Here's the deal. Write me the quiz: nine questions, each one a concrete case or mini-scenario, each with two or three answer options. The point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they already like, so no question should be a disguised version of "do you think values are commensurable?" Every question has to earn its place by actually pulling the positions apart.

I want the scoring key to map answer patterns onto these positions (rename them if you've got better names, but hit all of them):

- strong commensurability: everything's on one scale, hard choices are just hard computation
- weak commensurability: there's a single covering value in each case even if the options score on multiple dimensions
- incomparability: some options genuinely can't be ranked
- parity: the options aren't equal and one isn't better, but they're comparable — and I especially want the small-improvement argument doing work here, i.e. a case where a tiny improvement to A still doesn't make A better than B
- lexical priority pluralism: multiple values, but some always trump others
- particularism / no covering value: what to do is case-by-case, there's no general ranking to appeal to
- the deflationary option: all "hard choices" are just uncertainty about your own values, nothing deep going on
- the existentialist option: hard choices are hard because you have to make a reason, not find one

Some cases I want in there for sure: the two-careers case where one is better on every dimension you can name but the other still doesn't feel worse; the case where you'd rationally regret whichever way you go (or would you?); a mundane no-good-options case that isn't Sophie's Choice, something like a hospital bed allocation or choosing which of two sick relatives to visit. And at least one question that seperates parity from incomparability, since that's the pair I can never keep straight.

Two more things. Flag any pair of positions your quiz can't actually separate with nine questions and say why — don't paper over it. And if some combination of answers lands in a spot where nobody holds the view, say so and tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Begin your answer with the quiz itself — questions, options, then scoring key — no throat-clearing.
