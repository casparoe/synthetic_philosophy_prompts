Hey, I'm deep in dissertation revisions and I want a consistency audit before my committee gets ahold of me, because I have a feeling two of my commitments are quietly fighting and I can't see where. Here's the pile. (1) I buy the epistemic-dominance critique of my field (I work on comparative philosophy): concepts and questions that come out of the dominant European tradition get read as being about everyone — "the self," "knowledge," "freedom," no qualifier — while a concept from any other tradition gets read as being about that tradition, local, "their theory of the self." I've been calling the first kind of reading ascription universality, i.e. reading a claim's scope as unrestricted, about all people at all times, and I think the whole problem is that we hand out ascription universality asymmetrically. (2) So the fix, for me, is a policy: default to ascription universality for everybody. When I hit a claim from any tradition, I read its scope as unrestricted unless I have a positive reason not to, and I refuse to let "it's a local concept" be the lazy default it usually is.

Then the rest of the pile, which is where I worry it doesn't hang together. (3) I'm an orthodox semantic externalist — meaning is fixed by the community's practices, not by what the speaker meant or what I charitably decide she meant, and scope comes along with meaning. (4) I'm a realist: one world, claims true or false independent of any framework, no "true for them." (5) I'm all in on conceptual engineering; half of philosophy is just revising our concepts until they earn their keep, and concepts aren't sacred. (6) I get genuinly annoyed when people deflate cross-traditional disagreements as "they're just using words differently" — I think those disagreements are almost always substantive and calling them verbal loses the plot. And (7), the verdict I'd go to the mat for: when someone says the Confucian material about roles and relationships is "just a local ethics for a particular social order," that's the parochializing move I'm against, full stop, and I want to insist it's a universal ethical theory like any other.

Can you audit this for me? Which of these actually pull against each other and how badly, which pairs only look like they conflict but don't, and for each real conflict what's the cheapest thing that has to give and what I'd lose by giving it up? Please rank by severity instead of telling me they're all in tension — I'm pretty sure at least a couple of these are fine together and I'd rather you tell me which ones those are.
