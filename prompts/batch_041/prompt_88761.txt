I've been chewing on something about how the US does health care and I want to turn it into a philosophy paper. The pattern that bugs me: if you walk into an ER having a heart attack, they have to stabilize you no matter what — no insurance, no wallet, no immigration status, doesn't matter. But if you can't afford insulin you just... don't get it, thats legal, and people die of it regularly. Same body, same underlying need, same health system, opposite treatment. And I dont think it's just "politics is messy." I want the strongest reconstruction of the theory that makes the pattern look coherent.

So what does the practice actually presuppose? Some options I've been kicking around, but push past them: is health care treated as a right only when the need is acute and sudden — a right indexed to time and to how imminent death is? Is it a rescue-based ethic, where the duty is a duty to rescue the person dying in front of you, and never a duty of fairness that everyone gets what they need? Does it presuppose that medical care is a commodity in the ordinary case and a commons only in emergencies? Which rival theories does it quietly rule out — does it rule out luck egalitarianism, or is it secretly compatible with it?

Here's the part I actually care about, and I think it's the under-worked part: the emergency mandate always attaches to an identifiable person standing in front of a clinician, whereas the insulin rationing is purely statistical — nobody knows in advance who dies. So maybe the practice presupposes that a moral claim on the system is generated by being seen, by being the object of an encounter, rather than by having the need. Run with that. Is there a coherent theory there or does it collapse into "we help whoever we happen to bump into"? And be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits nicely with — I suspect a couple different theories could license the same pattern and I want to know which ones.

Then flip it: tell me what would actually have to change in the practice if we swapped the presupposed view for a flat "health care is a right, full stop" view. Would the emergency mandate survive, get absorbed into something bigger, or become redundant?

Skip the literature review and any citations, I'll handle that. But take a position — I dont want "here are three readings, you decide." Tell me which theory you think the practice really presupposes, why that one over the others, and where your confidence is lowest.
