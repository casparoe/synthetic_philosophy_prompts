I run calibration at a small forecasting shop. Every week our analysts post probabilities and then revise them, and there's alot of disagreement about when a revision is learning and when it's just wobbling. My boss wants a rule. I know there's this thing called Van Fraassen's reflection principle — the rough idea that if you're sure your future self will assign probability x to A, then you should already assign x, or at least be constrained by that — but I need something an analyst can actually run, not a lecture. Act as a philosopher of probability who has also had to make this work inside an org. First brainstorm, for yourself, the main ways reflection can fail or be misapplied: future self has new evidence, future self is tired or biased, memory loss, the forecast is self-referential, the future self is more expert but under pressure, the future self has different standards, and the seperate issue of when you're deferring to your own future credence versus deferring to a future expert. Then give me a decision procedure: a numbered checklist in the order an analyst should go through it, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops and says defer to the earlier credence, where it says revise, and where it hands off to a human because the issue is trust or values rather than probability. Keep it usable by someone who doesn't know the formalism. Then run it on three cases: (1) an analyst wants to move a 70% down to 40% after a new poll; (2) an analyst wants to move it because they slept badly and feel gloomy, no new info; (3) an analyst says they are certain that after the election they will say they were at 90% all along, and wants to lock that in now. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like — say which and why. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do, where I'm just dressing up a judgment call as a rule, and which steps would need my boss to sign off. Don't give me readings or citations. Think carefully and don't just recite the standard formulation.
