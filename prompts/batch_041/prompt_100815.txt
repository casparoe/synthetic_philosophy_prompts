Write a deadpan self-help column of roughly 600 words, in the voice of a lifestyle guru, offering practical techniques for self-deception: how to come to believe a comforting falsehood that one already knows, on some level, to be false. The humour must come from getting the philosophy right rather than from exaggeration, so a specialist finds the piece fair rather than embarrassing. Since belief is not directly under the will, no advice may simply instruct the reader to decide to believe something; the techniques must be the genuinely indirect ones — managing attention, curating one's evidence and company, rehearsing the story until it feels worn-in, letting time and repetition do the work. The column must also walk straight into the real puzzle it cannot dodge: a self-deceiver who deliberately sets out to acquire a false belief seems to need to forget the setting-out, so that the strategy either collapses into ordinary wishful thinking or requires the self to be divided against itself. Let the guru resolve this in one direction — partitioning the self, or denying that any intention was ever really present — without naming a theory, and let the reader feel what that choice costs. Keep the prose legible to someone who has never read a word of the literature, sustain the voice throughout without winking at the reader, and let the irony be visible from the content alone.
