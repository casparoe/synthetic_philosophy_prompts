I retired from the physics department four years ago — forty years of statistical mechanics, and alot of time spent thinking about determinism, which I now realise was a kind of philosophy apprenticeship I didn't know I was serving — and since then I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on rational choice. The instructor has gotten into the habit of asking me to draft exam questions because I keep bringing physics into the discussion, and this term she wants one essay question from me for the take-home final. So here's the thing I need, and it's the one puzzle from the course that still gets under my skin.

I want a question on Newcomb's problem — the predictor, the two boxes, the thousand dollars, the million — but not one a student can answer by reciting one-boxing versus two-boxing and attaching a couple of the usual labels. I can get that much out of them already. What I want the question to force is the thing that took me about a year to see. My physicist's instinct was always that if the predictor is reliable then the money is already sitting in the box before I choose, and no choice of mine can reach back and change what's in there, so obviously take both boxes. I still think that argument is valid as far as it goes — the trouble is it only goes as far as assuming that my choice and the contents of the box are causally independent of each other, which is the exact point in dispute, and I want a question that makes them earn that observation themselves rather than just hear me say it. Second part: I want them to connect the puzzle to the version with no predictor at all, the one where some genetic factor makes you likely to smoke and also causes the cancer, so that smoking is evidence of the bad gene without causing anything. Because I convinced myself that if the predictor works by simulating me, then the predictor case has the same causal shape as the gene case, and if that's right then all the talk about backwards causation is a red herring and the whole fight is really just between the two ways of computing expected utility. If I've got that wrong, I want the question built so that a sharp student can show me I've got it wrong. Third part, the old complaint that the two-boxer is going to walk away poor while the one-boxers drive off in their nice cars — I want to see whether they can say what a two-boxer ought to say in response, and whether the same answer still works in the gene case.

Please keep it from turning into a name-dropping exercise: no citations, no reading list, and I'd rather nobody writes the word dominance unless they've actually stopped to check whether it applies here. It's a 90-minute take-home, one question, 20 marks total. Give me the question, then a model answer of about 700-800 words, then a marking scheme that names the specific moves a top answer has to make, the marks each is worth, and what it costs them to miss each one — and throw in a line for the mistake I should expect from a mediocre answer, which I'm told is reliably the confusion between the contents being already fixed and the choice making no difference to the contents. Flag that one seperately with its own deduction so I can spot it coming.
