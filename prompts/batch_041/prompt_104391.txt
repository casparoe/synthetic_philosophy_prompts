I have recently come back to the practice of my faith after many years away, and I have been reading about Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism — the view that creatures possess no causal power of their own, that God alone produces each event, and that what we mistake for the necessity of fire to burn cotton is merely the constancy with which God brings the burning about. I am a hospital doctor, and this has unsettled the way I speak and think at work, where I say and write things like 'the sepsis caused the organ failure' or 'the delayed dose caused the death', and where I sit on reviews that must decide whether a colleague's error caused a patient harm. I would like you to set out the most important implications of occasionalism — understood against its rivals, the concurrentist view that God and creatures both genuinely cause, and the mere-conservationist view that God sustains creatures but leaves them their own causal powers — for how I should understand causal claims in medicine and how I should hold myself and my colleagues responsible when a patient comes to harm. Please give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, in British English.
