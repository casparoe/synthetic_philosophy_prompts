I'm a science and ideas reporter — I write a column about philosophy of science and I do a couple of podcasts — and my alma mater has asked me to give this year's commencement address. Small liberal arts college, about 200 grads, so the crowd is mostly parents and grandparents and a few bored siblings, plus maybe ten faculty who actually read philosophy for a living. I've got twelve minutes.

Here's what I want the speech to do. I keep running into this thing in my reporting and I can't let it go: some choices are hard not because you don't know which option is better, but because there's no fact of the matter about which is better — the two things aren't measured on the same ruler, so there's nothing to line up and compare. And I think we get this wrong in the way we talk to young people. The standard advice is weigh your options, figure out what you value most, which quietly assumes everything can be put on one scale and ranked. I want the speech to push against that without turning into a lecture.

I want it built on a distinction I've been trying out in my own notebook, between a cost and a loss. A cost is the kind of thing you can be compensated for — you give up X, you get Y, and the ledger balances. A loss is different: you give up X, you get something genuinely good, and X still isn't made up for. I want the grads to leave feeling that a life with real commitments is going to have losses in it, and that this isn't a sign they chose wrong. I don't want the speech to wave that away by saying the loss is just psychological, or that you're being irrational for feeling it.

I want the whole thing to hang on one small concrete story — you can invent it, keep it plausible and a little funny, maybe someone choosing between two cities or two summers — and the story should make the point before the point ever gets named. Don't name it as a concept until near the end, if at all.

Some constraints. No name-dropping philosophers and no quotes from books — this is a commencement, and half the room would smell a TED talk. The faculty in the room will know the literature on this (incommensurability, value pluralism, the whole thing), so the speech can't be sloppy or say something a specialist would wince at, but it also can't presume the parents know any of it. It should feel like something a journalist would actually say out loud to a crowd, not an essay read aloud. And skip the usual commencement furniture — follow your passion, dream big, that kind of thing. Twelve minutes is roughly fifteen hundred words, so don't run long.

One more thing. After the speech, add a short note to me — not part of the speech — telling me where it takes liberties with the actual idea or leans on a reading of it that's contested. And this is the part I care about most: tell me what would change your mind about the framing. Like, which argument or example, if it turned out to go the other way, would make you say the cost/loss distinction doesn't do the work I think it does, or that the advice comes out wrong for these graduates. I'd rather know where its weakest than get something polished I can't defend on a panel.
