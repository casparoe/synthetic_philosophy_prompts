I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about slurs, and we kept talking past each other because we never settled on what a theory of slurs is even supposed to deliver. Before we take it up again, I want the specification on paper: the desiderata that any adequate account of slurs — semantic, pragmatic, or a hybrid of the two — has to meet.

Start with the phenomena such an account must get right, each with the case that motivates it. A slur and the neutral term for the same group pick out the same people, yet swapping one for the other changes what is said and done, so the account must explain the difference between two co-extensive expressions. A slur can wound even when the speaker intends no derogation — reported, quoted, read aloud from a dictionary, or uttered by someone ignorant of its history — while the same word, used by members of the target group among themselves, gives no offense; the account therefore has to locate the offensiveness somewhere that is neither simply the speaker's intent nor simply the word's dictionary sense. Some group pejoratives have no neutral counterpart at all, and some are aimed at groups defined by ideology, religion, or chosen affiliation rather than by anything ascriptive, so the theory must say whether these belong to the same class and whether having a neutral counterpart is really part of what makes a term a slur. Slurs differ in severity, and particular terms drift in status over a generation — from ordinary to taboo, or from taboo to reclaimed — so the account must accommodate a gradient and its movement in time. Slurs resist uses that their descriptive content would otherwise license: it is hard to utter one in a flatly classificatory or neutrally reporting way without the utterance sounding like endorsement, so the theory must explain the missing innocent uses. Slurs embed — under negation, in conditionals, in questions, in compounds, in reported speech — and their offensiveness survives some embeddings and not others, which constrains any compositional treatment. And a slur derogates a person through a group, whereas a plain insult like 'idiot' aims at the person directly, so the account should say whether one mechanism covers both and what marks the difference.

Then the distinctions it cannot collapse: use versus mention; a slur versus a mere insult versus a taboo or profane word; the act of derogating versus the effect of giving offense versus the attitude of contempt; what the word means versus what the speaker means; and the descriptive content a slur shares with its neutral counterpart versus whatever else it carries.

For each requirement, give the consideration behind it, then tell me which of them are genuine desiderata and which are merely the commitments of one camp dressed up as requirements. I especially want you to flag any pair that cannot be satisfied together — I suspect that the no-intent case and the reclamation case pull in opposite directions, and that the demand for one uniform mechanism fights the demand that the account capture how heterogeneous slurs are — and to identify the single requirement that does the most to rule out candidate theories. Skip the scorecard of the familiar positions; the list is what I am after, and I will do the scoring myself once we agree on the criteria.
