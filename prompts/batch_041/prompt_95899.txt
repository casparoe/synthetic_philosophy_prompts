I am a novelist, two-thirds of the way through a draft, and the book turns on the failure of a certain kind of certainty. I have been reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty not for its mood but for its epistemology, and I have reached the limit of what I can do with it alone at my desk. What I want from you is not an essay on the book but a procedure: an ordered set of questions I can run on each candidate certainty in my manuscript — there are about a dozen — to decide which can be made to give way on the page, which cannot, and how the giving way should be staged. I have the book; do not send me a reading list, citations, or quotations, and do not tell me where the commentators disagree. I need something I can run in ten minutes per scene.

Work from the assumption the book is built on: some of a person's certainties are not beliefs she holds on evidence but the frame that makes her doubting anything possible at all. For my purposes a certainty can give way in one of three modes, and I want the procedure to assign each case to a mode or to say that it has none. Withdrawn: she comes to doubt it and can say so. Broken: the practice around it collapses, and she is left not with a doubt but with no ground to stand on. Exposed: nothing changes for her, but the reader sees the hinge for what it is.

Here is the raw material. My protagonist is a woman of sixty-three on an estuary. She holds beyond question: that her brother drowned there when she was nine; that the tide will turn twice a day and the channel will hold; that the woman who raised her is her mother; that the words on a page mean what they seem to mean when she reads them; and that the people across the water are not the sort whose suffering makes a claim on her.

Build the procedure so that it does not sort hinges from ordinary beliefs by content, or by how strange they seem — anything can be a hinge, and the cases that matter to me are the boring ones. Say what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to me because the question is one of design rather than epistemology.

Then run it on three of the five certainties above: one it handles cleanly, one where it misfires, and the one I suspect it will tell me I cannot write the way I want to write it — the last, where a certainty is false, undoubtable from the inside, and repellent to the reader. Close by naming what the procedure cannot do.

Keep the whole reply to at most 1,000 words, in American English.
