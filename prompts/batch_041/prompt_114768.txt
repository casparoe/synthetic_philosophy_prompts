I'm in a third-year course on the Anthropocene and last week we finally got to the whole fight over whether it's a real geological unit. Every time the 2024 vote comes up, my prof says some version of "the Anthropocene isn't an epoch, it's an event" and then just moves on like that settles the matter. I don't think it settles anything, partly becuase I'm honestly not sure what he's even claiming.

Here's my rough grasp of the geology, correct me if I've got it wrong: an epoch is a formal chunk of the geologic time scale, and to add a new one you don't just declare it — you have to pick a golden spike, one specific point in one specific layer of rock or sediment somewhere on Earth (they proposed a lake in Canada, pinned to around 1950, on the plutonium from H-bomb tests), and the whole trick is that this marker has to show up basically everywhere at the same time so that everyone can point at the same line. Then committees have to vote. An "event," meanwhile, seems looser — a mass extinction, the Great Oxidation Event — a thing that happened, smeared out over time and space, no single base point, with different places crossing into it at different moments.

So what could he actually mean by "it's an event, not an epoch"? I want a menu of the distinct precise claims hiding in that one line, from the almost trivial ones up to the ones that would be a genuine thesis about what the Anthropocene is. For each one, tell me whether it's a claim about geology, about language, about politics, or about what kind of thing the Anthropocene is; whether it's true or at least defensible; and — this is the part I care about — which of them would actually license the move he makes with it, i.e. waiving away both the 2024 rejection and the whole "when did it start" argument as beside the point. If none of them really do, say so. Lay it out as a list I can bring to office hours; I've got a paper due and I'd rather not just repeat his line without knowing what I'm signing up for.
