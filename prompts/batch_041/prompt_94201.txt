I teach a 300-level undergraduate seminar on action theory and medieval philosophy; there are twelve students and one 90-minute session. I want a runnable classroom activity that makes them feel the force of the puzzle that circulates under the name Buridan's ass, but not the cartoon of an animal starving between two identical hay bales. What I want them to feel is the underlying problem of choice without preference: the way a demand for a sufficient reason can produce paralysis, and the way an arbitrary act can dissolve the paralysis without ever supplying the reason. By the end they should have experienced, in their own deliberation, the difference between a tie-breaker that merely settles what happens and a tie-breaker that is supposed to justify the choice. They should also have felt how quickly a group can mistake a decision procedure for a justification. The activity should not announce the puzzle at the start; the rules and incentives should arrange for them to run into it themselves, and they should not hear the name Buridan's ass until the debrief.

The conceptual target is a little more specific than the standard lesson. I want them to see the gap between symmetry and incommensurability: two options can be equally good on a common scale, or they can be valued in ways that do not reduce to a common scale, and the puzzle changes depending on which is in play. I also want them to distinguish indifference from equipoise, that is, no preference at all versus equal and opposing reasons, and then to ask whether either state can itself be a reason for action. A term I have been using in my notes is the arbitration gap: the space between a decision procedure that produces a determinate outcome and a justification that makes that outcome rationally required. I would like the activity to give them something to do that makes the arbitration gap feel like a practical problem rather than a verbal one. I am imagining something with a forced choice under a timer, two options that are either identical or incommensurable, and a written justification requirement, but I am open to your design.

Please design the activity for a room with movable chairs and a projector, but no special technology; assume only small tokens or slips of paper, a timer, and index cards. Include roles or rules, a payoff scheme that does not need real money, a minute-by-minute run of show, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinctions above. No lecture up front. Be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
