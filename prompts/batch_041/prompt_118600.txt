Some years ago I decided I wanted to actually understand what pragmatism was, instead of just nodding along when people used the word, and I've been reading Peirce and James on truth on and off ever since — Peirce's early essay on making our ideas clear, and then the later piece where he gives his own view a new name precisely to keep it from being confused with what James was up to; and on the other side James's lectures on pragmatism and the follow-up book on the meaning of truth. The more I read, the less the standard summary satisfies me. Everyone says James thought true means useful and Peirce thought true means whatever inquiry would converge on, and that the argument is over there — but it can't be the whole thing, because James keeps insisting he is not saying a belief is true because it is comforting, and Peirce keeps insisting that his account is a method and not a metaphysical thesis about what truth consists in. It reads to me like two people who share a starting point (the function of a belief shows up in what it commits you to expect and to do) and then split over something much narrower, and I genuinely cannot tell whether the split is about truth itself, about how far the method is meant to reach, or just about what the word "true" is being asked to do.

So here is what I want, and I would much rather have this than a summary of who said what. Treat Peirce and James as two parties to an adversarial collaboration — as if a referee had sat them down and told them to produce the document they can both sign. Give me a numbered section of agreed claims, each phrased so that neither of them would wince: about what a belief's practical consequences are supposed to reveal, about what work the word "true" is meant to do, about what it would take for reality to correct a belief, and about the status of an inquiry that never finishes. Then a table of the remaining disagreements, with each position stated in the wording its own side would accept rather than a paraphrase tilted toward the other, and with the cruxes kept separate from the disagreements themselves — the sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one of them to change something. My own suspicions, which you should feel free to reject outright: that one crux is whether we are being offered rival definitions of a word or rival accounts of one and the same phenomenon; that another is whether the consequences are read off the content of the belief (what it would be like for the thing to be true) or off the effects of holding it (what it does for the person who holds it); and that a third is scope — whether the method was ever meant to leave the laboratory, or whether the fight about truth is really a fight about whether it reaches moral and religious belief.

For each crux I want the case, thought experiment, or piece of reasoning that both of them would agree bears on it, followed by what each side predicts about that case, written down in advance rather than after the fact. If part of the quarrel turns out to be about words — the two of them answering different questions with the same vocabulary — say so plainly and say which part, and don't soften it. And where they genuinely cannot be reconciled and something has to give, commit: tell me which formulation you would keep and why, instead of handing me two options and a shrug. I have no academic training in any of this and no way to look anything up while I read your answer, so skip the references and page numbers and make the reasoning stand on its own.
