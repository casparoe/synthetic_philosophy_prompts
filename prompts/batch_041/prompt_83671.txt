The question:

'Hesperus is Hesperus' and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' are both true, and they have the same grammatical form, yet only the second one was a discovery. (a) Explain the puzzle this raises. (b) Say how Frege's sense/reference distinction is supposed to solve it. (c) State whether on Frege's view the two names must share their reference, their sense, or neither. (d) Is the difference in informativeness explained by the two names having different referents? (e) What, if anything, does Frege's account imply about whether the identity is knowable a priori?

The student's answer:

'Hesperus is Hesperus' is an instance of the law of identity (a = a). Anyone who understands the sentence can see that it's true, and it tells you nothing you didn't already know. 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is different: both names refer to Venus, but that the morning star and the evening star are one and the same body was something people had to find out. So the two sentences must express different thoughts even though they're about the same object, and that's the puzzle for the view that a name just means its bearer (Mill).

Frege's answer is the sense/reference distinction. The reference of both names is Venus, but their senses differ: 'Hesperus' presents Venus as the star seen in the evening, 'Phosphorus' presents it as the star seen in the morning. The sense is the idea or image a speaker associates with the name, which is why two people can attach different senses to the same name, and why the same name can have a different sense for the same person at different times. A declarative sentence expresses a thought, and the thought is built out of the senses of its parts, so the two sentences express different thoughts. That's what the difference in cognitive value comes down to. Frege's main reason for introducing senses in the first place was to handle identity statements.

So on Frege's view the two names must have the same reference but different senses: if they had different references the sentence would be false, and if they had the same sense it would be as trivial as 'Hesperus is Hesperus.' And since the sense of a name determines its reference, once you've grasped both senses you can work out that Hesperus is Phosphorus without having to look at the sky. So although we in fact learn it empirically, on Frege's account it comes out knowable a priori, and arguably analytic.
