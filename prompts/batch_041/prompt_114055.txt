I am the rules manager for a mid-sized tabletop card game with an organized tournament circuit. Our comprehensive rules run to roughly a hundred pages, and card text may refer to other cards, to the rules themselves, and to the card it is printed on. That last freedom is a recurring embarrassment: playtesters find self-referential cards that make the game incoherent, the card ships anyway, two judges rule differently at two events, and we spend a season issuing errata and apologies. I get a handful of these per set and one or two more with every monthly rules update, and I want to stop adjudicating them one at a time.

Write me a decision procedure I can run on a single piece of card or rule text, and on an entire card set at once, that sorts self-reference into three outcomes: benign (ship it), vicious (no coherent outcome; ban or rework), and underdetermined (coherent, but not uniquely so; needs a tiebreaker or a priority rule). Give me the tests in the order I should apply them, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure may stop with a no, and where it must hand the decision back to a person.

Ground it in what Russell's paradox licenses and what it over-licenses: the collection that belongs to itself just in case it does not; the fact that a definition quantifying over a totality that contains the thing being defined is not automatically vicious (as with the longest card text in play); the difference between a self-referential system with no consistent outcome, with exactly one, and with several; the notion of grounding, that is, whether the evaluation has a base case to stand on; and the classical repair of stratifying a language so that nothing can speak about its own level, which works but is usually blunter than the case requires. Skip the history; I need the tests. No set-theoretic notation — my team has no formal logic, and whatever we ship has to be applied, mid-round, by a judge at a table.

Constraints: I have about two hours before the rules committee meets. Anything I declare unshippable has to be defended to the committee chair, a lawyer, who will ask why we cannot simply reword it; so the procedure should prefer rewording wherever rewording exists, and say what the rewording should look like. Errata are expensive and bans are worse.

Then run it, out loud, on the following, and tell me where it strains:

1. Nullstone: "Ignore the text of any card that refers to this card." A fan favorite; I would hate to lose it.
2. Grand Ledger: "The controller of Grand Ledger draws a card if Grand Ledger's printed text is longer than the printed text of every other card in play."
3. Rules 9.1 and 9.2, unchanged since our first edition. Rule 9.1: "This rule does not apply if rule 9.2 applies." Rule 9.2: "This rule applies if rule 9.1 does not apply."
4. Rules 12.4 and 12.5. Rule 12.4: "This rule applies if and only if rule 12.5 applies." Rule 12.5: "This rule applies if and only if rule 12.4 does not apply."

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and what I should say to the committee about the limits of the whole exercise.
