I host a small podcast on method in the human sciences, and next week's guest is an archaeologist defending a specific and contested programme. Her claim is that recurring depositional patterns — pottery and animal bone placed deliberately in foundation trenches, beneath thresholds, and in the fill of abandoned houses — license inferences about what the people who made the deposits believed, not merely about what they did. The distinctive part of her position is that this can be done without leaning on ethnographic analogy to living societies; the warrant, on her view, is only a general principle of charity, namely that we may assume people act for reasons they could in principle give. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press the programme at its joints rather than letting her restate it, with at least one question each on: how she separates a deliberate deposit from ordinary refuse, abandonment residue, or material that simply accumulated over a long stretch of time; whether attributing a belief (say, that thresholds are dangerous) adds anything testable to a description of the deposit, and what would count as evidence against the attribution; whether the inference should be to beliefs at all rather than to habits, conventions, or dispositions that no one could have put into words; and whether a principle of charity is sufficient on its own, given that the reasons we can imagine avowing need not be the reasons actually in play. Give each question a follow-up aimed at the answer she is most likely to give. Keep the whole reply under 700 words, and close by saying how confident you are in the portrait of her position you have built from this description, naming the single question you are least sure actually lands.
