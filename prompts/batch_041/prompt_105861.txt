I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size SaaS company and I've somehow become the unofficial referee in an argument between two people on my team, and it's well above my pay grade. Some background: last year we tied part of everyone's bonus to "escaped defects per engineer" (bugs that made it to prod), and it worked great for two quarters, the number fell, and then people quietly stopped filing bugs and started slicing changes small enough that nothing could be traced back to anyone, and our actual incident count went up. Classic. Our EM, Dana, says this is just Goodhart's law and the lesson is "don't tie a bonus to one number," full stop. Our staff engineer Marcus says that's a bumper sticker, not a law. His line is: if you were measuring the thing you actually wanted, then targeting it couldn't degrade it, so all the "law" says is that you optimized for something you didn't actually want, which is a tautology, not a prediction, and it can't tell you in advance which metrics will rot or how fast. Dana says no, it has real content: an imperfect metric used only to measure stays fine (noisy, maybe, but unbiased), and the same metric used to steer breaks, and the breakage comes from the optimization pressure rather than from the metric's imperfection. So the law is really a claim about what happens when you attach control to a number, and it's falsifiable, you could run the same team with and without the bonus and watch the correlation between the metric and the thing you cared about collapse only in the bonus arm. Marcus came back with: fine, but "optimizers exploit gaps between proxy and target" is a fact about optimizers, not about measures, and it only bites when the proxy and the target can come apart, and for something like engagement or code quality the target is basically defined by the proxy, so there's no gap, so what's left of the law? Dana's answer to that was the part that actually lost me: she said the metric doesn't mis-measure a fixed thing, it changes what the job is, so once calls-made is the number the sales team stops doing sales and starts doing calls, and the metric has become part of the world it was supposed to describe, which is why it isn't a tautology. Marcus said that's just "people respond to incentives" wearing a lab coat, which is econ 101, and Dana said no, it's more general than that, it's the same phenomenon as reward hacking in RL, where there are no humans and no bonuses involved, so it can't be about incentives specifically, it's about optimization against a fixed proxy in general.
