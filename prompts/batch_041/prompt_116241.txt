I was listening to a philosophy podcast episode about skill and expertese the other day, and the host gave a gloss of Ryle's knowing-how versus knowing-that distinction that I'd like a second opinion on. Here's more or less what he said, as best I can remember it.

Ryle's target was the intellectualist legend — the idea that whenever you do something clever you first have to think a thought about how to do it, and then act on that thought. Descartes is the villain, and the underlying picture is of the mind as a ghost in the machine, sitting inside the body and issuing instructions. Ryle says that leads to a regress, because applying a rule would itself require knowing a rule for applying rules, and so you'd never get started. So knowing how is not a species of knowing that; it's a set of dispositions to behave, and the test of whether someone knows how to do something is just whether they can do it. That's why he says the expert's knowledge is in her fingers rather than her head, and why he thinks knowing how is really just a habit or a knack that gets drilled into you by training. On this view you could in principle be the greatest proof-writer alive and yet be unable to state a single thing you know, and the difference between an expert and a beginner has nothing to do with anything either of them knows. He wrapped up by saying Ryle's argument was so decisive that intellectualism about know-how basically died with him, which is why nobody has seriously tried to revive it.

I mostly buy this, and I'm fairly anti-intellectualist about skill myself — I do think there's a real difference between being able to ride a bike and being able to say how you ride one. But a mate of mine who did a bit of philosophy reckons the podcast has mangled Ryle, and that the know-how-is-really-a-kind-of-knowing-that view is alive and well, defended by serious people in the last twenty years or so. He mentioned some case where a person knows how to do a thing but can't actually do it — an instructor who knows how to ski but can't ski any more after an injury — and says that's supposed to sink the whole ability view. Who's right? Please go through the summary more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful reader of Ryle would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong, and whether his closing claim about the death of intellectualism follows from anything true in the summary. I don't need citations or a reading list, just the audit. American English please.
