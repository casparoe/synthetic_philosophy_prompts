I am a novelist at work on a novel set on a small, self-governing island, and I need your help with a scene that turns on a piece of reasoning I cannot quite get right on my own. I want you to act as a philosopher of decision theory and game theory who is also a competent dramatist: someone who can keep two intelligent people arguing without letting either of them become a fool. Think the problem through carefully before you write a word of dialogue, and begin by brainstorming, in your own words and in private, the strongest version of each side's case.

Here is the situation in the novel. The island holds a single annual surplus, a harvest of 40,000 units. At the start of the season every one of the four hundred households is handed a sealed card. A household may leave its card blank, or write its own name on it, in private. When the cards are opened, the surplus is divided equally among all the households that wrote their names, and each household that wrote a name must also pay a fixed levy of 150 units into the island's repair fund. Households that left their cards blank pay nothing and receive nothing. So if a household writes and k households in total have written, that household ends up with 40,000/k minus 150; if it leaves the card blank it ends up with nothing. Nobody can communicate with anybody else about the decision, and nobody learns who wrote until the cards are opened. The numbers are public knowledge.

There is one further wrinkle, and it is the wrinkle the scene is really about. Forty of the four hundred households belong to an old clan that is exempt from the levy and whose members are known, as a matter of settled habit, always to write their names no matter what. Everyone knows this. The remaining three hundred and sixty households are ordinary islanders who may reason however they like, and who each face the same card, the same numbers, and the same knowledge.

Two islanders are standing at the harbour wall on the morning the cards are due. Ilse holds that the islanders should reason from their sameness: she and the others are alike and have the same information, so whatever conclusion she reaches about what to do, each of them reaches too, and the only sensible question is which action, if adopted by all of them together, leaves them best off. Corvin holds that this is a piece of mysticism: the other three hundred and fifty-nine islanders are separate people whose choices will not budge by a hair because of what she decides, and the only sane question is which action gives her the best expected outcome given her best estimate of what the others will do. Each of them should be able to make the other's case harder, not merely to restate their own. Let Ilse's view be pressed to the point where the sameness assumption bites in an uncomfortable way, and let Corvin's be pressed to the point where the private coin he is forced to rely on starts to look like a dodge rather than a solution.

Write the dialogue between Ilse and Corvin as a scene for the novel. I want it to run at a decent length, with genuine back-and-forth: each character should answer the actual point the other just made, concede where a concession is honest, and change the shape of the argument as it goes rather than repeating a fixed speech in turn. Keep both of them equally sharp; if one of them has the better of some particular exchange, let it show, but do not rig the scene so that one is simply the winner. The dialogue should be usable in a literary novel, so let the setting and the weather do some work and let the two of them be recognisable people with tempers and habits, but the intellectual substance must be exactly right and load-bearing; a reader who knows this literature should feel that both characters understand the problem, and a reader who does not should still be able to follow it.

Write in American English.

After the dialogue, add a short note in your own voice, addressed to me rather than in the characters' voices. In it, tell me plainly where the scene leans on a contested reading of the ideas rather than on settled ground, and name the single argument or consideration that would most likely change your own verdict on which of the two has the better of it. If you think the verdict is genuinely open, say so and say what would close it; if you think one side is simply right, say what would have to be true for you to abandon that judgment. I would rather have an honest statement of the weak joint in the reasoning than a confident summing-up.
