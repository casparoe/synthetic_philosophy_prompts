I'm in a philosophy of probability seminar, and two of us have spent a month talking past each other about deference to objective chance. The first is a defender of the chance-credence link in its standard form: a rational agent's credence in a proposition A, conditional on the proposition that the objective chance of A is some value x and on any evidence that is admissible with respect to A, must equal x — where information counts as admissible roughly when it bears on A only by bearing on A's chance, so that the clause is meant to exclude information about A that does not reach the agent through the chance. The second is a revisionist who accepts that some chance-credence norm is binding but denies that it can be stated in terms of objective chance as such: she holds that the quantity to which credence should defer is the assignment delivered by the best systematization of the world's history, and that this assignment is itself characterized partly in terms of the very credences it is supposed to govern, so that the two can come apart.

Please draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I want, first, a numbered list of claims both accept — about what the chance-credence link is for, about what it does and does not establish regarding the metaphysics of chance, and about the fixed points they share (for instance, whether both agree that an agent with no inadmissible evidence and a chance of one for A must be certain of A, and whether both agree that the link cannot be read as a reduction of chance to credence). Second, a table of their remaining disagreements, each stated in wording that its own side would accept, including whether the admissibility clause is a substantive constraint or a placeholder that does its work by fiat. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — with, for each, the case or argument that both sides agree would bear on it and each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance.

To make this concrete, and to keep it from collapsing into a recitation of the literature, treat the following four cases as the shared test material and say what each side must say about each. (a) A device of impeccable reliability tells you today what the objective chance of a future event will be at a later time, and the value it reports cannot be the present chance without contradicting itself. (b) A world whose probabilistic law assigns a nonzero chance to a future event that would falsify the very systematization that generated that chance assignment. (c) A world with no chances at all — deterministic but chaotic — in which an agent has nothing but frequencies and symmetries to go on; does the deference norm have any content there, and does either side's position about the status of the norm depend on how (c) comes out? (d) A case in which the agent possesses information that is genuinely about the future event but does not reach her through its chance. Finally, if any part of this dispute turns out to be about what the word chance is being made to mean rather than about how the world or our credences are, say which part, and say what would remain in dispute once that is set aside. I care about the positions rather than their pedigree, so do not bother with attributions, dates, or reading lists.
