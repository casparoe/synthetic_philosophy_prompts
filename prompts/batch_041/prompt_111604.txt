Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about the problem of universals, and I came away realizing that I do not understand the passage we kept invoking nearly as well as I pretended. The passage is from Porphyry's Introduction (Isagoge), in an old English translation:

"I shall omit to speak about genera and species, as to whether they subsist (in the nature of things) or in mere conceptions only; whether also if subsistent, they are bodies or incorporeal, and whether they are separate from, or in, sensibles, and subsist about these, for such a treatise is most profound, and requires another more extensive investigation."

My friend insists that this is basically a tidy anticipation of the later medieval menu of options for universals—before things, in things, or after things. He says Porphyry's three questions are really one question, namely whether genera and species have any reality outside the mind, and that his refusal to answer is just a rhetorical dodge, since the rest of the Introduction quietly assumes some form of realism. I think that reading is too neat, but I could not articulate why. Could you help me understand what Porphyry is actually distinguishing here, especially the contrast between "subsist (in the nature of things)" and "in mere conceptions only"? What would it mean for a genus or species to exist "in mere conceptions"? Is that a psychological, linguistic, or logical status? And what does the phrase "subsist about these" add to the question of whether they are "separate from, or in, sensibles"? Finally, why might Porphyry regard these questions as requiring "another more extensive investigation" rather than as matters he can settle in an introductory logical treatise? I am not looking for a bibliography; I want to understand the conceptual moves in the passage.
