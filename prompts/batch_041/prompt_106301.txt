I am a PhD student in philosophy of art, and I need a decision tree rather than an essay. My graduate reading group is about to take up the paradox of fiction, and I want a branching instrument that carries a reader from the bare datum of being moved by fiction to a named position, where every node is a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Please do not write me prose about the paradox, and do not supply citations or a reading list; the deliverable is the instrument and, where useful, a short annotation under each leaf.

Fix the root at one plain observation: a reader can be genuinely distressed by the death of a character she knows never existed. Build the first level of the tree out of the logical space of the paradox rather than out of a list of -isms. The top-level split should be over which of three commitments the reader abandons: that we are actually moved; that emotion toward an object requires believing the object exists; or that we do not believe in fictional characters. Within each branch, add further nodes that separate the ways that commitment can be given up. A reader who keeps genuine emotion and keeps the existence requirement must explain what she does believe; a reader who drops the existence requirement must say what kind of object a thought or proposition is and why it can be the object of a real emotion; a reader who drops the belief in non-existence must say whether she is inconsistent, half-believing, or merely pretending to believe.

Make the tree sort people by what they say about concrete cases, not by the label they prefer. At least four nodes must be cases, each phrased as a question with two or three answer options: interactive fiction, where the reader chooses the protagonist's actions and then feels responsible for the outcome; grief over a persona one knows to be an AI chatbot and not a person; being moved by a character one invented oneself and never wrote down; and the contrast between sorrow at a fictional death and sorrow at the death of a real stranger one never met. You may add one or two more cases if they separate positions the above do not.

The leaves should include, at minimum, the make-believe or quasi-emotion account, the thought-theory account, the illusion or inconsistency account, the simulation or offline-emotion account, and at least one member of what I will call the object-shift family, in which the real emotion is directed not at the character but at the fiction itself, at the author's act, or at a real-world theme the fiction discloses. Each leaf should carry the name of the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two further requirements. First, identify at least two combinations in the logical space that no one seems to occupy, and give a one-line diagnosis of each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, rather than forcing a distinction the cases do not support. Aim for roughly five levels and twelve to sixteen leaves, addressed to first-year graduate students who know the puzzle but not the literature.
