I run a 3rd-year undergrad seminar (18 students, 80 minutes, they've had a little Wittgenstein but nothing on the rule-following stuff yet) and I want a session where they walk into the rule-following paradox with their own hands instead of watching me draw it on the board. Here's the shape I'm after, though you should make the actual calls.

The engine I want is a hidden-rule guessing game. Small groups get a card with a finite list of input → output pairs (you pick the pairs and the rules; keep them tiny and arithmetic-ish) and have to predict the output for a fresh input, with tokens at stake so there's something to lose. Then a sceptic -- I want this to be a real student role with a script and its own way of scoring, not me playing devils advocate -- produces a rival rule that agrees with every single pair on the card but disagrees on the fresh input. Use the quus trick as the seed: agree with ordinary addition everywhere you've ever added, but give 5 as soon as one of the numbers is 57 or more. So 68 + 57 is the disputed case (125 vs 5), and the point is that nothing on the card rules out the 5.

Three things I'd like layered on, if you can make them work:

1) A simplicity budget. The rival rule has to be written in a fixed little language and can't use more symbols than the original, so nobody wins by inventing a monstrous ad hoc rule. I want them to feel that even a fair, equal-simplicity rival survives the evidence.
2) One student is secretly dealt the bent rule at the start and plays the whole first half as an ordinary participant. At the debrief I want to turn to that student and ask what they meant by the rule all along, and have no good answer available.
3) An "archivist" whose job is to comb through everything the group has said, written and done and produce the fact that settles the disputed case. When they can't, the facilitator reveals the twin's private notebook -- which contains nothing but a symbol for the bent rule, and that symbol is now in exactly the same boat. That's the moment I'm aiming for, if it lands.

Deliverable: roles, materials, a minute-by-minute 80-minute timeline, the scoring, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the actual philosophical moves -- the sceptical challenge, why the straight answers (dispositions, the community, the private note) keep sliding, the difference between a straight solution and a sceptical one. Plus a blunt section on what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it. One thing I already know will happen: someone will say this is just Hume, or just grue, it's about predicting the future -- tell me how to head that off without lecturing at them.

And be honest about what the game can't reproduce. I suspect it only shows that finite evidence underdetermines the rule, which is much weaker than the claim that there's no fact of the matter at all, including about your own mental life -- say plainly what the facilitator has to supply.

Make the calls yourself and commit to them. If one of my three layers is a bad idea or won't survive contact with a real room, dont hedge and dont hand me three alternatives -- drop it, put something in its place that you'll defend, and tell me why.
