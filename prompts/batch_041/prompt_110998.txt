I've just started a job in the comms office of a mid-sized public agency — water and public health stuff — and I keep tripping over something I can't get straight. Everything we publish for the public has to hit a reading-level target. The style guide says short sentences, short words, active voice, no nominalisations, and there's a bit of software that scores every draft and flags it if it's too dense. My manager treats this as a moral matter rather than a design preference; there are training sessions. But the same agency's statements about anything actually contested are pure fog: "the situation is being reviewed", "learnings have been identified", "any impact on residents is expected to be minimal". Nobody in the building seems to notice the seam between the two. And the comms team also keeps a messaging guide that tells us to use one phrase rather than another — "heat-trapping pollution" instead of "emissions", that kind of swap — on the obvious assumption that the words people hear change what they believe and eventually what they do.

My book club just did an Orwell essay and one line stuck with me, that "the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts." So maybe all of this is just him, and maybe it's all one thing, but I don't think it is. What I want from you is the implicit theory. What does this bundle of practices — the readability target, the active-voice rule, the fog, the deliberate word swaps — have to assume about the relationship between language and thought, and between language and honesty, for it to make sense at all? Is it one view or two that don't sit together? The plain-language push seems to assume that fixing how you write fixes how you (and your reader) think; the fog seems to assume the opposite, that the writing is just downstream of something the agency doesn't want to say out loud. Both can't be the whole story, and I'd like to know which bits of the practice strictly require which assumption and which bits would survive on a much weaker view — that words are a symptom of thinking rather than a cause of it. Does the reading-level check need the extra assumption that clarity is a property of the text by itself, countable in syllables? Does the whole thing need the idea that plain concrete speech is politically neutral — that concreteness is honest and abstraction is a mask — and what happens to the argument if plain talk turns out to be the demagogue's favourite register too? And if you took the deflationary view seriously, the one where bad writing is only ever a symptom, what would actually have to change in the office I work in — would there be any point to my job, and would the euphemisms be any less damaging? I'd rather you tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that three or four different theories would each license everything I've described, than pretend there's one clean answer.
