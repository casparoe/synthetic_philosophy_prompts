You are a philosopher working at the intersection of normative ethics and social choice theory. I'm assembling a prospectus for a one-semester, full-time dissertation chapter, and I want you to help me think the idea through before I write it up.

My half-formed thought: the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism is almost always stated as an objection to aggregation — the complaint that summing welfare across people treats the many as though they were one person with a single large life. I suspect that locating the objection in aggregation is a mistake, and that its real force lies in a thesis about the unit of moral accounting. Here is the distinction I want to build on. Suppose we grant that we can compare two people's welfare in level (who is worse off) and also in unit (how much a given gain matters to each). Standard treatments take these to be independent technical assumptions about the measurability of a scale. I want to treat them instead as encoding two pictures of what a moral claim is: on the level picture, each person's situation generates a claim whose strength is a fact about that person alone; on the unit picture, claims are commensurable by construction and can be traded against one another without remainder. If that is right, then separateness is not a constraint on whether we aggregate but a constraint on what we are aggregating — and it would follow that the objection bites equally against non-utilitarian views that reject the utilitarian total but still aggregate commensurable claims, while some forms of utilitarianism might survive it.
