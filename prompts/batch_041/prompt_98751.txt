Here's the setup. Someone starts out with a very strict anti-adding-up line: you can't justify a rule by pointing to alot of tiny benefits to lots of people, because each person has to be able to accept the rule for reasons that speak to them, and it's good for the group isn't a reason that speaks to any particular person. Fine. Then the numbers get big. A million people lose a week of sleep versus one person being kept awake for a year? Suddenly the same person says, well, obviously you go with the million, and they don't think they've changed their view. What changed is that the individual complaints are now treated as too small to count as complaints at all -- not outweighed, but disqualified from the ledger. I saw the same shape in a planning fight: a preservationist said you can't demolish one person's home for a mall that slightly benefits many, then when the many was a whole city, said the homeowner's loss was too small to register as a loss.

I know there's a general debate about aggregation in ethics, and I know about thresholds in deontology. What I'm after is the specific thing where the switch from person-by-person justification to group-level summing is triggered by the sheer number of people, and the individual complaint gets reclassified as trivial rather than defeated. Is there an established name for that exact move? If not, can you coin one and define it so I can use it in the piece? I care less about the label than about the carving: what exactly is happening, how is it different from (a) plain aggregation, (b) a lexical threshold where a right is absolute until catastrophe, and (c) the idea that small harms don't count because they're not morally relevant? And where are the edges -- does it matter whether the many are identifiable, whether the harm is divisible, whether the one is responsible for their own predicament?
