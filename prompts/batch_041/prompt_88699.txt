My roommate and I were up late after a philosophy of mind seminar — I'm a bystander here, I was making tea while the argument happened and I genuinly don't know which of them is right. She'd been reading something about epiphenomenalism, the view that conscious experiences are caused by brain processes but don't themselves cause anything, so they're like a shadow or a hum that comes along for the ride. He thinks that view eats itself, and here's roughly how it went.

(1) He said: if my pain doesn't cause anything, then when I say 'ow, this hurts,' the words are caused entirely by whatever's happening in my brain and not at all by the hurt. But then my report is just a lucky coincidence — a zombie version of me, with all the same brain stuff and no inner life at all, would say exactly the same words. So my saying 'it hurts' gives me zero evidence that there's any hurting going on. Epiphenomenalism knocks out the only evidence anyone has for its own central claim. (2) She said that's confused, because she never said the feeling causes the report. She said the brain state causes both the feeling and the report — they're two effects of one cause, like a thermometer's reading and the expansion of the metal both being effects of the heat, neither of them causing the other. Reliability doesn't need the feeling to reach into my mouth; it only needs a stable law-like correlation between the feeling and the report, and that's exactly what the epiphenomenalist posits. A zombie saying the same thing isn't a problem — he's the thermometer in a world with no heat, and the fact that he'd read the same number there tells you nothing about whether the reading tracks the heat here. (3) He said the thermometer analogy actually proves his point, because the reading means 'it's hot' only because there's a causal chain running from the heat to the mercury to the number. A thermometer with no causal connection to the temperature would be displaying a number, not a temperature reading. So if the feeling is nowhere in the causal chain that ends in my saying 'it hurts,' then nothing hooks that word onto the feeling rather than onto the C-fibers. In my mouth 'hurt' would mean the brain state, and when she says 'there are feelings' she'd be talking about neurons without knowing it — her sentence wouldn't even mean what she thinks it means. (4) She said that just assumes a purely causal theory of what makes words mean things, and that's one contested option among several. Maybe what fixes the reference is that the feeling is simply present to me when I introspect — a kind of direct acquaintance, no causal relay needed. Or maybe 'the feeling' is fixed by a description, like 'whatever it is that's going on in me right now that's intrinsic and not physical,' and descriptions don't have to be caused by their objects. (5) He said acquaintance is doing the smuggling: if my acquaintance with the feeling is what lets me know about it and talk about it, then the feeling is making a difference to what I know and say — which is exactly the causal role epiphenomenalism denies it. And the descriptive route has the same problem in reverse: if the description never touches the feeling, a zombie with the same description and no feeling satisfies it equally well, so the description doesn't pick out feelings after all.

They went around a few more times but that's the gist. Can you adjudicate this move by move — rate each of (1) through (5) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say briefly why — and then tell me who's more right overall? And I'd especially like to know what both of them are missing: I have a nagging feeling the whole thing keeps sliding between a claim about what's true and a claim about what we could know or mean, and neither of them ever separated those out, but I might be wrong about that too.
