I advise a foundation that is about to commit roughly eight researchers for three years to conceptual work on the safety of advanced AI systems — philosophy, decision theory, formal epistemology, and the theory side of machine learning, with no empirical ML and no engineering. Our program officer wants to orient the portfolio around a single question: when is a worst-case guarantee the right target, and when is average-case reasoning the honest thing to do? To make the discussion concrete she has sketched a way of writing out any proposed guarantee as a quadruple — a class of systems it covers, a class of environments, a failure predicate, and a resource bound; call these the guarantee's envelope and its predicate. Her diagnostic is that tightening any one coordinate is normally paid for by loosening another, that the environment class and the adversary class overlap rather than sitting side by side, and that a guarantee goes empty when the system class is narrowed until it excludes the systems we would actually build, or when the failure predicate is weakened to something a system can satisfy while remaining unsafe.

Tell me what the open conceptual questions in this area really are, and which ones deserve the researcher-years, in order. I care much less about the list than about the defense. For each question: why it is upstream of the others rather than downstream of them; what a competent philosopher or theorist could actually produce on it in three years; how much of the answer would survive contact with the fact that deployment decisions are made under uncertainty by people who are not theorists; and what result or argument over the next two years would move it up or down the ranking. Include the following candidates if you think they belong, and say explicitly if you think they do not: whether there is any common currency in which a worst-case guarantee over a narrow envelope can be compared with an average-case guarantee over a broad distribution, given that one takes a maximum over a set and the other an expectation under a measure; whether the failure predicate should be behavioral, about what the system does, or internal, about what the system is; how to handle adversaries that are themselves products of optimization, so that the adversary class cannot be fixed in advance; and whether guarantees are best treated as ends in themselves or as inputs to a decision rule usable by someone who has to decide whether to deploy.

Finally, evaluate the program officer's four-coordinate diagnostic. Is it the right cut through the area, or does it render invisible a question more important than any it makes visible? Name that question if you think there is one. And identify at least one line of work that looks both tractable and important but that you would advise us to leave alone, with your reason.
