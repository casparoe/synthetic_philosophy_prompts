I want to put a position of mine under pressure, and I would rather be told where it is exposed than where it is merely unfashionable.

The position is this. A liberal state may levy and collect a church tax on behalf of religious bodies — the German arrangement, in which the state adds a percentage surcharge to the income tax and remits it to the recognized religious community of which the taxpayer is a registered member, is my paradigm — without thereby violating the neutrality that liberalism requires of it. The reasoning is that the state occupies the role of a collection agent and nothing more. It does not choose the recipient, does not direct the funds, and does not condition any civic right or benefit on religious membership; membership is voluntary and, crucially, revocable. On this view the arrangement is a convenience the state offers to voluntary associations generally, and the fact that the association happens to be a church is incidental. I would extend the same defense to an established church whose privileges are purely ceremonial, and I would resist the claim that establishment as such is disqualifying — but the church tax is the case I actually care about.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this, ranked by how much it should move me, with the decisive separated from the merely awkward.

I have been bothered by a distinction I drew for myself and can no longer tell whether it is doing real work. Call it the difference between collection neutrality and recognition neutrality: the state might be neutral in the act of collecting while non-neutral in the act of deciding which bodies are eligible to have their dues collected. I am inclined to say that only the first matters, and that eligibility criteria, if formal and publicly stated, are like the criteria for any other legal status. Tell me whether that is a genuine separation or whether the second collapses into the first — and if it collapses, whether anything recognizable survives.

I also want you to assess one hypothetical, because I suspect it is the kind of thing that would actually decide the matter for me. Suppose it were established that a large majority of those who pay the tax do so only because paying is the default: they are registered, the money is withheld, and they never affirmatively chose the arrangement. Suppose further that if they were required to re-affirm membership actively, at intervals, most would decline. Does that bear on the voluntariness my defense rests on, or is it irrelevant, since at any moment they could leave? I would like to know whether my answer to this is principled or a rationalization.

Three further things I would like worked through, with a verdict on which of them I should concede as counting against me and which my critics would be wrong to press:

First, what does the position predict that its rivals do not — for instance about what would happen to affiliation rates, or to the finances of the religious bodies, if state collection were abolished while the underlying voluntary contributions were left untouched?

Second, is the same machinery, offered on identical terms to a body whose teachings or internal governance the majority finds repellent, a consequence I must accept or a reductio of the view?

Third, does the state's role as collector draw it into adjudicating membership, discipline, or employment within the body, and is that an avoidable administrative accident or something intrinsic to the scheme?

Finally, tell me honestly whether the position has been insulated. I can feel myself retreating, when pressed, from the claim that the state may collect to the claim that it may collect only so long as it does nothing else, and I cannot tell whether that is a legitimate refinement of the thesis or a device for making it immune. If it is immune, say so, and say whether that is a defect in the position or simply what a correct position looks like.
