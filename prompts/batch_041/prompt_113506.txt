I'm a retired experimental physicist — 32 years in a lab, and for the last three I've been reading philosophy, mostly badly. A grad student friend of mine has left me stuck on something and I can't find the exit.

Set-up: bits arrive one at a time and you guess each next bit from the ones you've already seen. Any way of doing that is a rule. He showed me this: add up, over all 2^N possible strings of length N, the number of correct guesses your rule makes, and you always get exactly N·2^(N-1) — the same total for always-guess-0, repeat-the-last-bit, majority vote, a neural net, anything. So every rule averages N/2 right out of N, which is exactly what a fair coin gets. I checked it by hand for N=3 and it came out right, so I'll believe him on (1). Then he lays this out:

(1) For any rule, the average number of correct guesses across all strings of length N is N/2 — no better than chance.
(2) So no rule is more accurate than any other, and none beats chance, over the space of possible data.
(3) A method is epistemically justified only if it does better than chance over the space of possible data.
(4) So no method of predicting from experience is justified, induction included, and our empirical successes are pure luck.
(5) But that's absurd. My old lab's methods really did beat coin-flipping at predicting which samples would crack, and so does a farmer's almanac.

His reply is that (5) is just survivorship, that (3) is precisely what the no-free-lunch results mean, and that if I want out I have to assume the world is simple — which is itself an empirical claim I'd need induction to justify, so its circular either way.

I want to keep (1), I can check it, and I want to keep (5), I've lived it. So which step has to go, and what does dropping it cost me? And if the answer is "restrict the space to problems that can actually arise," tell me straight whether that's a fix or just the inductive assumption in a lab coat.
