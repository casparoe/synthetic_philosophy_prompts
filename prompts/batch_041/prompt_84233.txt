I am a novelist, and I am drafting the afterword to a book I have just finished — a novel that follows a family through several of the categories below — and I want to set down, for that afterword, the view of those categories that I take the book to commit me to. Before I send it to my editor, who reads philosophy for pleasure and will not be gentle, I would like an audit.

Here, then, is what I believe.

(1) Race, gender, and money are social kinds. Whatever boundaries they have are drawn and held in place by our collective practices, and there is no natural joint in the world that they trace.

(2) For all that, there are objective facts about them. There are facts about the size of the racial wealth gap, about who is counted as a woman under a given legal code, about the rate at which a currency sheds value — facts that hold whether or not anyone likes them or believes them.

(3) Fiction is a site of knowledge about such kinds, not merely a site of illustration, and I would put the point strongly: when a category becomes legible — when a novel, a census, or a diagnosis names it — the people inside it tend to shift in response, so that the thing a novel represents is a moving target the novel has itself moved.

(4) A person's own account of their gender is at least presumptively authoritative. Others are not entitled to overrule a self-identification by consulting the category's rules, because there are no such rules sitting outside the practice.

(5) Whether a given arrangement of these categories is just is not itself a social construction. That some ways of drawing the lines are better than others is a fact about the world, not a matter of what we have happened to agree.

Which of these are actually in tension with which, which only appear to be, and for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what that would cost me? Please order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and tell me if any of the five turns out to be idle — doing no work the others do not already do. I would rather be told that one of them is decoration than that all five quietly support one another.

Finally, a request about how to answer. Much of this is contested ground, and I do not want a briefing that smooths it over. Where people who have thought hard about these matters genuinely disagree, say so, and say what the disagreement turns on; where something is settled, say that as well. Do not let a live dispute pass for a consensus, and do not let my own framing of a question pass for the only way of framing it.
