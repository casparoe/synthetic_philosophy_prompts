I am partway through a cognitive science seminar on analogy, and we have just finished Hofstadter and Sander's *Surfaces and Essences*. I find the central thesis exhilarating and I suspect it is either profound or empty, and I cannot yet tell which. So before I write anything of my own about it, I want the specification rather than the sales pitch. Set out the desiderata that any theory of analogy-making has to satisfy if it is to be adequate to the strong claim that analogy is not one faculty among others but the engine of cognition as such — that categorisation, perception, memory retrieval, and the everyday application of a word are all special cases of one underlying process. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and say what would count as failing it, and how much of the strong thesis would survive the failure. The phenomena I would expect the list to cover: the toy letter-string puzzles in which a transformation of one sequence has to be carried over to a second sequence under a construal the solver has to invent (as when one is told that one string becomes another and asked what a differently structured string becomes); the cross-domain analogies of the sort that let a doctor's tumour problem be seen as a general's fortress problem; the metaphors that populate ordinary speech and that speakers produce without noticing; the way children generate analogies well before they can state the rule behind them; the graded, halo-like character of analogical judgment, where near-miss answers feel wrong without feeling arbitrary; the dependence of every analogy on what happens to have been retrieved from memory; and the well-replicated shift from surface-feature matches to structural matches as expertise grows. I am asking this as someone who wants to know whether the thesis is worth building on empirically, not as someone after a metaphysical verdict.

The part I most want is your judgment about which of these are genuine desiderata and which are merely the preferences of one camp, and above all where the list becomes jointly unsatisfiable. I am already suspicious of one pair. If the theory is required to count the humdrum application of a concept to a new instance — calling this a chair, understanding this sentence — as an act of analogy, then the thesis is sweeping but risks being trivially true, since nothing in cognition would be left for it to exclude; if instead it is required to supply a nontrivial criterion separating analogy from mere categorisation or similarity, then the thesis is far bolder but gives up most of its explanatory reach. Say whether that is a real dilemma, and if it is, what the least costly way out looks like and what it costs. I would also like to know whether the theory can avoid a circularity, given that computing an analogy seems to require some measure of similarity, while the whole point is that similarity judgments are supposed to be analogy all the way down; whether the requirement that the theory be mechanistically implementable — that it be a claim about a process, not a compliment paid to a phenomenon — pulls against the requirement that it accommodate the open-endedness of analogical construal; and whether there is any hope of keeping the claim falsifiable once it is pushed to the point where every act of recognition counts as an analogy.

Tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the slogan version of the thesis, and then finish briefly with how the computational models that came out of Hofstadter's group fare against the list — not a full scorecard, just the one or two requirements they look weakest on.
