Two of my friends spent most of a long walk arguing about whether there can be reasons where there is no one to have them, and I have not been able to settle it since. Neither of them has any training in philosophy, and neither do I; I said very little at the time, which is exactly why I would rather have your judgment than my own. Please work through their exchange and tell me who is right at each step, not merely who came out ahead.

The argument started over something small: a path worn diagonally across the grass in the park near us, cutting the corner between two gates. Mira said the path knew the best route. Daniel said a path cannot know anything, and that the phrase was the whole problem.

Mira: The path is where it is because of a reason, namely that the diagonal is the shortest way between the gates and the ground along it is firm. Nobody planned that, and no single walker chose it, but the reason is still the reason the path has the shape it does. It never had a home in anybody's head, which is the interesting part. A bee's dance is the same: the angle at which it is performed carries the direction of the nectar, and that is why the dance has the shape it has, even though the bee is not entertaining anything.

Daniel: You have described a cause and called it a reason. A reason is something that can be followed or ignored, met or missed. Feet wear the grass thin where it is soft; nothing is being followed. Saying the diagonal is the reason is a compliment paid to a drainage pattern.

Mira: Something is being followed. A walker who steps onto the path is guided by it; the path tells them where to go and they go, with no instruction issued anywhere. And it can be wrong as well as right, which is the test you set: a path that led into a bog would be a bad path, and the traffic would abandon it. That is all a reason needs.

Daniel: You are sliding between two things. The path is a record of past traffic and a cue that steers present traffic, and neither is a reason. A record can be left without being meant, and a cue can be obeyed without being understood. My real objection is worse for you, though. What you call the reason depends entirely on how you describe the thing. Describe the path as a strip of compacted soil and the reason disappears; describe it as a way to the far gate and a reason appears. If the reason comes and goes with your description, it belongs to you and not to the path.

Mira: By that test nothing has reasons, since anything a person does can be redescribed as chemistry. And the description is not idle. Saying the path is there to get people to the far gate lets you predict where the next path will be worn, and where people will step once the grass grows back. A description that keeps on predicting is tracking something.

Daniel: Predicting is not the same as being right, and what is being tracked may be the traffic rather than any reason. I can predict where a river will run from the shape of the valley without claiming the river has an argument for its route. You keep pointing at a shadow and calling it a person.

I tried to help by raising a case of my own, and it only made matters worse, so I would like you to take it up too. A friend of mine looks after a piece of software. There is a check in it that everyone who has ever read the code describes as being there in order to block a particular kind of bad input. But that input can no longer reach that part of the program; the door was closed years ago. Delete the check anyway and something unrelated breaks, because later code grew up around it. So is the original purpose still the reason the check is there, or has the reason changed into something else, or is there no reason at all and only a history? Both of them stalled on this one.

Please go through the exchange move by move and rate each of the six moves, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, what both of them are missing, and how the software case should be answered and which of them, if either, it supports.
