I'm writing a term paper for a course on Kant's ethics, and I want to try something a bit unusual. We have spent weeks on the thesis that an action possesses moral worth only when it is done from duty rather than from inclination, and on how tightly Kant binds this to the agent's own reason and autonomy. For the paper I want to imagine Kant confronted with a proposal that did not exist in his day: a hypothetical conscience implant, a device that, once installed, monitors the agent's inclinations and, at the moment of choice, dampens self-interested impulses while heightening her awareness of what duty requires, so that she reliably performs right actions and reports acting willingly, even though she has not herself cultivated the disposition. Its advocates claim it would multiply morally worthy deeds; its critics reply that the resulting deeds, however right, are no longer the agent's own and therefore lack moral worth. Drawing on Kant's actual commitments regarding autonomy, the good will, the motive of duty, and the relation between reason and inclination, argue for the verdict he would most likely reach on whether actions produced by such a device could have moral worth, and on whether a person would be permitted to have one installed. Make clear which of his doctrines you are extending and where the extension becomes genuinely uncertain.
