ok so I'm in second year and I'm taking this seminar on philosophy and science fiction, and one of our assessment options is a creative piece — I want to do the dark forest thing, the Liu Cixin one, where any two civilizations that become visible to each other are basically doomed: you can never verify anyone's intentions, and the uncertainty compounds the longer you think about it, and on top of that anyone might have a sudden tech explosion that turns them from harmless to lethal overnight, so staying silent or striking first are the only sane moves.

Here's what I want. Write the monthly newsletter of a neighborhood association made up of civilizations that have all nominally agreed to the arrangement where everyone stays quiet and nobody kills anybody. Deadpan, fully in-universe, no winking at the reader, 600–700 words. All the humor has to come from taking the argument's premises completely literally at a domestic scale — so the association should be structurally impossible, and every ordinary thing a newsletter does (announce a meeting, welcome new members, offer advice, run a letters page) should quietly fall apart under its own logic without the editor ever noticing. The editor should treat each collapse as a triumph of good administration.

Sections I'd like: a note from the chair; a welcome to new members; safety tips; notices/classifieds; an advice column; and a letter to the editor that makes the real objection — that universal hiding is only one equilibrium among several, and if anyone could send a signal that couldn't be faked, or commit to a verification protocol, a quieter and less paranoid arrangement would be stable. The editor's reply should dispose of that objection in a way that quietly demonstrates the argument can't be falsified by anything, without ever saying so.

Stuff to avoid: no citations, no quotes, and don't try to use the novels' actual characters, planets or invented tech — make up your own names, I don't want you guessing at canon details. Same for the real-world concepts, describe them in your own words rather than name-dropping.

Tone test: someone in my seminar who knows the arguement well should find the game theory in it correct and the jokes fair rather than lazy, and my flatmate who's never heard of any of this should still follow what's happening and laugh. I'd rather it be genuinely unnerving in places than joke-dense.
