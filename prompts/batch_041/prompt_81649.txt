You're a philosopher of cognitive science who works on bounded rationality and the heuristics-and-biases literature. I'd like you to think carefully before answering, and to start by brainstorming the correspondences before you commit to anything -- I care more about the reasoning than about a tidy summary.

Context. I'm in a lab that studies judgment under uncertainty, and my PI has a line she repeats every time someone reports a new bias: heuristics are like optical illusions. The eye is cheap and usually right, but point it at the Muller-Lyer lines and it will lie to you, and knowing the trick doesn't stop the lie. Same story with judgment, she says: availability or anchoring gets you a good enough answer most of the time, but in the wrong kind of display it throws a systematic, predictable error that you can't introspect your way out of. She uses this to get three conclusions -- that biases are just the price of a cheap and mostly reliable system, that debiasing is more or less hopeless, and that the whole research programme is really just the psychology of perceptual illusion applied to belief.

I half agree and half suspect the analogy is carrying all the weight. Can you audit it? I want the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the eye, of the illusion, of the display, of the thing that knowing better can't fix -- then where the mapping breaks, and crucially which of the breaks actually matter for her three conclusions. Separate the disanalogies that are harmless from the ones that do damage.

Two things I especially want you to press on. First, whether the illusion picture quietly assumes there is a right answer that the heuristic is approximating, and what that assumption does to the satisficing side of bounded rationality -- the idea that agents set aspiration levels and stop when they've got something good enough, rather than approximating an optimum. Second, whether the same image would work for someone in the Gigerenzer camp, who thinks most of the "errors" are artifacts of unrepresentative lab tasks. There's also an occaisional worry in the back of my head about whether illusions are errors by the perceiver's own lights in a way that judgment biases aren't -- see if that goes anywhere.

If the analogy is beyond repair, say so and give me a replacement, plus what the replacement buys you that the illusion doesn't. No reading list, no citations, I just want the arguement. And be concrete -- use actual heuristics and actual effects rather than talking about heuristics in the abstract.
