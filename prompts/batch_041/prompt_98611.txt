I am assembling a comparative study of how military defeats get converted into founding or identity-defining narratives, and before I commit to any one account of the process I want the specification on the table: what must a theory of defeat-to-foundation myth-making actually deliver to count as adequate? Write me the desiderata — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must be consistent with — each requirement paired with the consideration that motivates it, and a judgment about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp.

The theory must accommodate at least these episodes, and I want the consideration behind each requirement to be drawn from one of them: the Confederate Lost Cause, in which a defeated slaveholding republic was, within a generation or two, recoded as a defense of states' rights and a noble lost war; Masada, a first-century mass death that lay largely dormant and then became, many centuries later, a byword for resistance; Kosovo, a defeat in 1389 that was still being mobilized for political purposes at its six-hundredth anniversary; and Dunkirk, an evacuation and retreat that was narrated, almost immediately, as a deliverance. Treat these as a test set, not as illustrations to be gestured at.

Among the requirements I expect the list to have to address, at minimum: how the theory selects — why these defeats and not the many others that were equally catastrophic; the mechanism by which a defeat is recoded as a moral victory (purification, witness, martyrdom, promise of return); the temporal lag, since founding myths commonly crystallize or revive long after the event; the question of agency, since some of these narratives are plainly manufactured from above, through monuments, school curricula, and commemorative ritual, while others seem to grow from below; and the plurality of function, given that one and the same myth can console, mobilize, legitimate, and reconcile, sometimes all at once.

I also want the list to force certain distinctions rather than smudge them: between a commemorative practice that merely happens to be inaccurate and a founding myth proper; between defeat and martyrdom; and between a myth kept alive by the losing side and a myth later taken up by the winners or by third parties. And it should carry the constraints the theory cannot violate — it must not require the myth to be false, since some founding narratives are substantially true; it must not require the defeat to be total; and it must respect chronology, since a narrative cannot predate the event it narrates.

Then two things I most want to see. First, flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I would bet that a requirement to explain selection by the intrinsic drama or scale of the event will collide with a requirement to explain selection by later political need, because the same kind of event is mythologized in one polity and left unmarked in another, and I want to know which requirement you would sacrifice. Second, as a check on the whole list, tell me what a defeat that never became a founding narrative lacks, name one such defeat, and say why. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; if you sketch how the familiar contenders — functionalist, invented-tradition, and elite-manufacture accounts — fare against the list, do it briefly.
