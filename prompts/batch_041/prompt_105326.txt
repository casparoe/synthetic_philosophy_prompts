A large academic medical center has settled on an explicit policy about which quantitative measures it will and will not act upon. It publishes, and allocates staff and budget by, a risk-adjusted mortality score for each surgical division, ranking the divisions against one another. Its leadership readily concedes that surgeons have learned to game the measure — declining the sickest patients, coding comorbidities in ways that raise the expected mortality against which they are then judged — and defends the practice anyway on the ground that one cannot manage what one does not measure. Yet the same leadership refuses to use patient-satisfaction scores for any decision that carries consequences, on the theory that staff would game them by pandering, and it bars publication counts from appointment and promotion decisions on the ground that they can be inflated at will.

What conception of the relation between a measure and the value it is meant to track does this pattern presuppose? A proxy is accepted in one case though admittedly gamed, and rejected in others though gamed no more flagrantly; reconstruct the implicit theory that would render the policy consistent. Does the distinction rest on a principled difference — say, that mortality is partly constitutive of the goal of surgery while satisfaction is merely instrumental to it, or that gaming the mortality score redistributes scarce resources whereas gaming satisfaction scores does not — or is it ad hoc? Distinguish what the policy strictly presupposes about the nature of clinical quality, about measurement, and about the rational agency of those measured from what it merely happens to fit well with. Say which rival views of value it quietly rules out, and identify where the policy is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the very same arrangement. Finally, state what would have to change in the policy if the underlying good were treated as plural and incommensurable rather than as a single measurable quantity.
