I write fiction, and my characters spend an implausible amount of their time not wanting to know things, which has driven me to defend in conversation a proposition I am not certain I can defend on paper. The proposition is this: no agent who maximises expected utility and updates by conditionalisation can be made worse off, in expectation, by receiving free information. She may be saddened by what she learns, but the learning itself cannot hurt her, because whatever she is told she remains free to act exactly as she would have acted in ignorance. I am told this is a settled result, usually filed under Good's name, and that everything hangs on that last clause. I should like it tested rather than restated.

Assume the thesis is mine and that I believe it. Tell me what would count against it — formal results, counterexamples, the intuitions of people who refuse to look, and the possibility that the thesis, once properly hedged, was only ever about a special case. I want to know which assumption is carrying the weight: that the set of available acts is the same before and after learning; that the utility function is unchanged; that the possibility space was fixed in advance, so that what I learn was already among the things I had assigned a probability to; that I will in fact conditionalise; that the information is genuinely free; that I am an expected-utility maximiser at all. For each, say what has to be given up, whether giving it up is plausible, and what survives the loss.

I also want to know whether the result is, as I suspect, close to a consequence of the probability calculus — a maximum taken after averaging cannot fall below an average taken before it — and if so, whether that emptiness is a defect in a claim of this kind or simply what such a claim is. And whether the clause about being free to ignore what one has learnt is doing all the work, given that in my experience nobody can ignore what they have learnt. I am told that certain ambiguity-averse or otherwise non-expected-utility preferences can reverse the result; tell me whether that is a genuine counterexample to the thesis or a change of subject, and whether the same reversal can be had without abandoning expected utility.

Finally, tell me what the thesis predicts that a rival view does not. If nothing, say so plainly, and tell me whether a position that risks nothing is thereby worthless or merely a theorem. The case that would most move me is one in which the agent is a full expected-utility maximiser, updates impeccably, pays nothing for the information and is still worse off in expectation; if no such case can exist, I should like to be shown why, not merely told it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, mark which ones a defender of the thesis would concede and which he would dismiss as irrelevant, and if the honest verdict is that the thesis is true, well insulated and almost empty, I would rather have that than encouragement.
