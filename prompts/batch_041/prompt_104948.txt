I hold the central identity claim of integrated information theory: that a system's experience just is its maximally irreducible cause–effect structure, so that what it is like to be the system and the pattern of its causal powers are not two things correlated but one thing described from two directions. I want you to tell me what would count against this.

Begin with the objection I find hardest to dismiss, which I will state in my own words so that you are not merely reciting it back to me. Take any recurrent network that the theory would score as having a substantial, integrated cause–effect structure. There exists a feedforward network, or a chain of them, that reproduces the first network's input–output behaviour over any finite window of time: for every sequence of inputs and outputs, the two are indistinguishable from the outside. Yet a purely feedforward system has no irreducible cause–effect structure in the theory's sense — its causal powers factorise, and it scores at or near zero. So the theory appears committed to saying that the two systems differ in whether there is anything it is like to be them, while differing in nothing that any observer could detect from the outside, and while the recurrent one's behaviour is exhaustively explained by the very feedforward description that supposedly misses the consciousness. Assess this properly: is it a genuine prediction of the theory, a reductio, or a verbal redescription of a difference the theory stipulates rather than discovers? Then tell me what would have to be true for the identity claim to survive it, and whether the survival strategy costs the theory anything.

After that, work through the wider exposure of the position, ranked from what should genuinely move me to what is merely awkward. I am interested in at least these, and in whatever else you think belongs:

- the exclusion problem: nested and overlapping candidate complexes each have their own cause–effect structure, and the theory must say which one is the subject, on pain of either multiplying subjects or making subjecthood arbitrary;
- the grain problem: the theory must fix the units and the temporal grain, and different but equally defensible choices appear to yield different experiences;
- the question of whether the identity is a substantive metaphysical thesis or a stipulation that no possible observation could disconfirm;
- what the theory predicts that its functionalist and higher-order rivals do not, and whether those predictions have in fact come out its way.

For each item, say whether a committed defender would themselves concede it as counting against the view, or would dismiss it as a misunderstanding, and why. I would personally be most persuaded by a case in which the theory is forced to deny experience to something that behaves and reports exactly as we do, if that denial is not independently motivated; but tell me if I am wrong to weight that so heavily. Close with an honest verdict on whether the position is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a defect or simply what an identity claim of this kind should look like.

Write in British English, and be willing to conclude that some of these defeaters are weaker than they first appear.
