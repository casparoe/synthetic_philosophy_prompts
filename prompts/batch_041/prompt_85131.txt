I run a ninety-minute reading group for philosophy graduate students who are at home in propositional and first-order logic and in possible-worlds semantics for modal logic, but who have never studied epistemic logic. The next session is on the problem of logical omniscience, and I want a plan for how to teach it, not an exposition of it. Lay out the order in which the components must be introduced and defend each dependency: what has to be in place before the problem even looks like a problem, where the closure of knowledge under logical consequence actually comes from, and how to make the group see that it falls out of the semantics rather than being an optional assumption that someone chose to add. Say when to introduce the distinction between reading knows that p as a semantic clause and reading it as a claim about a psychological state, since I suspect most of the confusion lives there.

Build the session on a single carrying example, and defend the choice. Specify what the example must contain — an item the agent plausibly knows, a logical consequence of it the agent plausibly does not know, and a gap that is not merely empirical ignorance — and what it must avoid, so that it does not quietly collapse into a point about limited computational resources or about not having done the arithmetic. I would rather you propose and justify the example than assume the usual one, and if you reject an obvious candidate, tell me why.

I have a specific framing I want the session to turn on, and I want your judgement about whether it carries the weight: the difference between closure failure that comes from an agent's not having performed an inference she is capable of performing, and closure failure that comes from her not possessing some of the concepts that appear in the consequence. Tell me which of these the session should treat as the core phenomenon, which of the standard repairs — awareness restrictions, impossible worlds, procedural or algorithmic notions of knowledge, relevance-sensitive consequence — addresses which, and at what point in the sequence each repair should appear.

Then map the misconceptions I should expect and mark exactly where each must be headed off: that the problem is just an idealisation to be patched with a cost function; that dropping closure is the obvious fix; that it concerns omniscience over contingent facts; that impossible worlds are an ad hoc device; that an awareness parameter solves the problem rather than relocating it; and that it is the same as the problem of assigning credences to logical claims. Finish by giving me the one question to put to the group in the last ten minutes that would show whether they have understood the problem rather than memorised its slogan, and by telling me what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud.
