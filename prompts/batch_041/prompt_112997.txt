You are a philosopher of religion and a metaphysician with a specialist's command of two things: the late-sixteenth-century Jesuit–Dominican controversy over grace and the modern analytic debate over divine foreknowledge, counterfactuals of freedom, and metaphysical grounding. I need you to produce a single document of the kind that adversarial collaborations are supposed to produce but rarely do: the joint statement that two parties to a long-running dispute could both put their names to. Think the problem through carefully before writing, and do not show me your scratchwork; let it shape the finished document instead.

The two parties are these.

(A) A Molinist. She holds that God has middle knowledge, and she understands it as Molina did: in addition to his natural knowledge of necessary truths and his free knowledge of what he has decreed, God knows, logically prior to any decree of his will, what each possible free creature would freely do in each possible set of circumstances. The objects of this knowledge are counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. They are contingent, they are prevolitional (not settled by any divine decision), and they are true independently of God's causal contribution to the acts they describe. The Molinist uses them to explain how God can be providentially in control of a world of genuinely free creatures without determining them.

(B) A critic of middle knowledge who is nevertheless a libertarian about freedom and who accepts that God knows the future acts of creatures who actually exist. He presses the objection usually called the grounding objection: if a counterfactual of creaturely freedom is true, then something makes it true; but its only candidate truthmaker is a free act that, by hypothesis, never occurs, and a non-existent act cannot make anything true. So such counterfactuals are not true, and there is no middle knowledge for God to have. His positive alternative is that God knows what creatures might do and what follows from his own creative intentions and the natures he gives, but not what a free creature would have done in circumstances that never obtain; there, he says, there is nothing to know.

Both parties should be treated as sophisticated insiders to the debate, not as cartoon figures, and both should be understood to have live internal disagreements of their own, which the document should flag where they matter.

I want a document with the following parts.

1. A short glossary, self-contained, defining the technical apparatus the two sides share or contest: counterfactual of creaturely freedom, prevolitional, middle versus natural versus free knowledge, truthmaker, grounding, and explanatory as opposed to causal priority. I have no internet access while reading this, so the document must stand on its own, and it must contain no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations of any kind. Do not name works, dates, or authors as authorities; where a view is usefully identified, describe the view rather than its pedigree. You may note in one or two sentences how the modern dispute differs from its late-scholastic ancestor — Molina's Concordia, the Dominican theory of physical premotion, and the Roman commission that Clement VIII convened to adjudicate the quarrel — but the document should not depend on that history.

2. A numbered list of agreed claims: what both parties can sign, stated so that neither is signing something that quietly concedes the other's position. Include what they agree about God's knowledge, about the compatibility of foreknowledge with libertarian freedom, about the logical (non-temporal) character of the ordering of God's knowledge, about the syntactic form of counterfactuals of freedom, about what middle knowledge is and is not needed for, about whether the dispute presupposes incompatibilism, and about what the disagreement is not about. Aim for claims that are substantive rather than truisms; a claim both sides accept only because it is vague is a failure.

3. A disagreements table. Each row is one point of residual disagreement, with columns for: the question at issue; the Molinist's answer, phrased as the Molinist would phrase it; the critic's answer, phrased as the critic would phrase it; and what each side thinks is at stake in the row.

4. A cruxes section. For each crux — a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — give the case, argument, or thought experiment that both parties agree bears on it, followed by each side's prediction about how it comes out. I want at least these four cruxes, and you should add any others you think are genuinely load-bearing:

   (i) the scope of the grounding requirement: whether the critic's demand for truthmakers is a general thesis or one restricted to counterfactuals of freedom, and what the critic says about truths that look ungrounded elsewhere — necessary truths, negative existentials, and truths about merely possible persons;
   (ii) the asymmetry between God's own free acts and creatures' free acts: God's free decisions are not themselves objects of middle knowledge but of a knowledge that follows his will, and both sides should say whether that asymmetry is principled or ad hoc;
   (iii) indeterministic processes: whether counterfactuals about genuinely chancy physical processes have determinate truth values, and what that implies for counterfactuals about undetermined free choices;
   (iv) the direction of fit between God's intellect and its objects: whether God's knowledge is measured by what it knows or is itself the measure of it, and whether either side can state its view without begging the question.

   For at least one crux, use the case in which Jesus says that if the miracles done in Capernaum had been done in Sodom, Sodom would still be standing. For at least one crux, use a case in which an agent is undetermined between two options she regards as equally supported by reasons. For at least one crux, use a case involving a truth about a possible person who never exists.

5. A diagnosis of verbal dispute: which parts of the disagreement, if any, dissolve once the parties say what they mean by grounding, by freedom, and by knowledge — and which parts survive even after the terms are fixed. If you think a row of the table is verbal, say so there and explain what would have to be added for it to become substantive.

6. A short closing section recording, for each side, the argument or finding that side would count as evidence against its own position, and anything both sides agree would actually move the debate.

Two things about how I want you to handle the assignment. First, be willing to push back: if you conclude that these two positions cannot both sign a document worth signing — for example because the critic's view is defined only negatively, or because the joint statement would come out vacuous once the equivocation in grounding is removed — then say so plainly and tell me what I would have to change, rather than manufacturing a document that papers over the problem. Second, be self-critical about the result: at the end, tell me which formulations you are least confident are faithful to a live position, and why. Aim for something between 1500 and 2500 words, written in the register of a document two professionals would actually sign.
