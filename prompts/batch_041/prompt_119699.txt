I'm a TA for a second-year course on Marx and social theory, and I'm mid-way through grading the midterm. Students kept recycling the same three lecture examples (the factory, the gig economy, the warehouse), so for the main short-answer question I made up a case they couldn't have seen before and asked them to apply the machinery to it. The question was: "Couriers United is a delivery platform owned by its 40 member-drivers, who each hold a share, vote at quarterly meetings, and split the year-end surplus. It also employs 60 non-member couriers on hourly wages, and they are not eligible for membership. Routing and pay rates for everyone, members included, are set by dispatch software licensed from an outside vendor, which the members may inspect but not modify. Members and non-members do the same work. (a) Would Marx regard the non-members as exploited? Would he regard the members as exploited? (b) Would he say alienation has been overcome at Couriers United? Use Marx's own distinctions, not your own moral intuitions, and say what would have to change for your answer to flip. ~400 words."

One student's answer is below. The prose is confident and well organised, which is exactly why I don't trust my own read of it at 1am with fifty more of these to go. Here's what they wrote:

"The central move in Marx is the distinction between labour and labour power. The worker sells not his labour but his capacity to work for a day, and the wage is the price of that capacity, fixed like the price of any other commodity by what it costs to reproduce it. Exploitation is not a matter of a wicked employer cheating his workers: the wage contract can be a perfectly fair exchange and exploitation still happens, because a day's labour produces more value than the labour power that was bought. The difference, surplus value, is what the capitalist appropriates. Alienation is a seperate matter: the worker is estranged from the product, which belongs to someone else and confronts him as an alien power; from the activity of labouring, which is not his own and is forced on him; from his species-being, the free conscious activity that is distinctively human; and from other people.

So the non-member couriers at Couriers United are exploited. They sell their labour power for an hourly wage, they produce value beyond what that wage represents, and the surplus goes into the members' year-end payout. Their exploitation is worse than an ordinary wage worker's, because the dispatch software is constantly driving rates down, which means they are paid below the value of their labour power — exactly what Marx's iron law of wages predicts under competitive conditions. The members, by contrast, are not exploited, since they are not selling their labour power to anyone; the surplus they generate comes back to them. And because the surplus is redistributed to the people who produced it, no surplus value is really extracted at Couriers United at all — that is the whole point of a co-op.

Alienation is a different story, and here ownership is the crux. Because the members own the platform, the product no longer confronts them as an alien power; and because they can inspect the dispatch algorithm and vote on major decisions, the labour process is theirs rather than something imposed from outside. So alienation has been overcome for the members, though not for the non-members, who are still separated from the product of their labour."

I need a grade out of 10 — my rubric is 4 points for part (a), 4 for part (b), 2 for precision in using Marx's distinctions, and tell me if that rubric is badly designed — and then the comments, point by point, keyed to specific sentences: what's right, what's wrong, and why it's wrong, not just "this could be clearer." The part I actually need help with is the middle ground. If a sentence is wrong in a way that a fast grader reads straight past because it sounds fluent, I want it named and the error explained, since the whole point of the question was the distinction and not the vibe. But I also don't want to mark something down that's genuinely defensible — if a claim that looks shaky to you is a legitimate reading, say so and say what it turns on, because this class appeals everything.
