You are an expert in decision theory and the psychology of judgment and decision-making — someone who knows both the formal theory of rational choice and the empirical work on how people actually decide, and who can argue either side of a dispute without caricaturing the other side.

I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm building a small teaching case around a question I keep running into at work. I want a dialogue between two people who disagree about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational. Set it up roughly as follows, but improve the setup if you see a better way to make the disagreement sharp.

One character — call her the marginalist — holds the textbook line: at the moment of decision, only future costs and benefits count; what you have already spent is gone whether you continue or stop; so letting the past influence the choice is a mistake, and the interesting question is only why human beings make it so reliably. The other — call him the policy theorist — grants the arithmetic but denies that it settles the question of rationality. His move is that a decision is not just an act but an act by an agent who will make many decisions, and who knows things about herself: for instance that she systematically abandons projects at exactly the point where the remaining work is hardest and least rewarding, and that she cannot reliably predict in advance which abandonments she will later regret. On his view, a standing disposition to give weight to what she has already invested can be the best available way for such an agent to keep her own long-run ends in view, much as a rule against renegotiating can serve a person better than case-by-case judgment. He should press a second, different line as well: that past investment is often evidence — about the quality of the project, about one's own competence or seriousness — and that updating on evidence is not the same thing as being swayed by a bygone cost.

The marginalist must not simply repeat the arithmetic. Have him come back with the sharpest replies available: that if the real work is being done by a commitment policy, a rational agent should adopt an explicit commitment device (burn the boats, sign the contract, tell everyone) rather than get the same result through a habit of reasoning badly, since the habit will misfire in exactly the cases where the evidence and the policy point in opposite directions; that a policy licensing indifference to marginal information is dangerous because it cannot distinguish the project that ought to be finished from the one that ought to be killed; and that the policy theorist's defense may end up conceding the original claim in a refined form — what makes the behavior rational is never the sunk cost itself but something else standing behind it.

Then let the policy theorist respond to those replies specifically, not to a weaker version of them, and let the exchange go at least three or four rounds in each direction. Let each of them concede something real at some point — a case that embarrasses their own side, a distinction they had been blurring — while holding the core of their position. The two should be equally competent; no one should win by being handed a bad argument. Where they use technical vocabulary (bygones, commitment device, dynamic inconsistency, escalation of commitment), have them explain it in plain language in passing, since I'll be reading this with no background in the field.

Before writing the dialogue, think it through carefully and spend a few sentences laying out, for each side, the three strongest points you can make on its behalf — I want to see you look for the best version of each position before committing to staging the argument. Then write the dialogue itself, roughly 1500 words, in ordinary prose rather than a script with stage directions, giving each speaker real room to develop a thought instead of trading one-liners.
