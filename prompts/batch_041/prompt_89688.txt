I am a novelist midway through a second book, and I am putting together an application for a one-year fellowship at a humanities institute whose writing fellows are expected to pursue a parallel line of conceptual inquiry; what they want from me is a two-page research proposal, and I would like you to draft it. The novel is set in a grand hotel that refuses to stop unfolding: a concierge with a ledger that never closes, a guest who is forever being moved one room further along a corridor that is somehow both endless and fully staffed, a night porter who insists the building was full before anyone arrived. I had thought of all this as atmosphere, but over the past few months — largely through arguments with two friends, one a mathematician and one a stubborn Aristotelian — it has hardened into a genuine philosophical quarrel, and I now suspect the book is secretly an argument with a finitist that I have not yet learned how to make. My half-formed idea is this. The familiar reductios against completed infinities — the hotel that can always take one more guest, the whole that is no larger than its proper part — are usually presented as proofs that an actual infinite is impossible, or at least unintelligible; but every one of them, as far as I can tell, leans on a single representational strategy, namely the demand that a collection be exhibited by matching its members off against one another one at a time. What I want to explore is whether the alleged absurdity is a fact about the world at all, or only an artifact of forcing an infinite structure into that one finite-bred habit of representation, and whether an alternative device — a finite, recursive specification of an infinite structure, of the kind a narrative can actually embody rather than merely describe — shows that the finitist's charge of unintelligibility fails. The catch, which I think is the real crux, is that recursive representability may turn out to be far too cheap: if it will equally "vindicate" structures that are plainly incoherent, then it is no test of coherence at all, and the whole framing collapses. I want the proposal to face that possibility head-on rather than hide it.

So please write the proposal, in my voice and at the register of a serious application. It needs a precisely stated question — one that has a determinate answer, not a vague theme — an account of why the existing positions (the Cantorian, the Aristotelian potential/actual distinction, the various finitist and constructivist refusals of completed totalities) leave that question open rather than settling it, and a clear statement of the method: what I would actually do over the year, month by month, given that I am also drafting the novel and cannot treat the philosophy as a full-time occupation. Concretely, I imagine the method involving a taxonomy of the representational strategies available for infinite structures (enumeration, rule or recursion, narrative iteration, diagram) and then a test of whether any of them, and specifically the recursive one, can bear the weight I want to put on it. Say what a result would look like in each of the two directions, and be honest about what would count as the project failing — including the failure mode I just described, in which recursive representability proves too permissive to do any work. Give a realistic scope for one year: better a small, finishable piece of conceptual work with the novel as its laboratory than an overreaching system. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; speak of positions and arguments rather than of who said what when, since I will fill in the references myself once the shape is settled. Begin the reply directly with the proposal itself — title, statement of the question, and onward — with no preface, no restatement of this message, and no commentary about what you are doing.
