State a claim precisely enough to have a truth value: no physically realizable device — not merely no device we currently know how to build, but none permitted by the actual laws of physics — can compute a function that no Turing machine computes. Call this the physical Church–Turing thesis, PCT. I want your credence in PCT.

Why I am not asking the textbook question. The live route to the denial of PCT, as I see it, is not quantum computing, which by general consensus remains within the Turing-computable, but the appeal to continuous quantities. A device whose internal state is a real-valued physical magnitude — an idealized analog computer, or a network whose units have real-valued weights — can in a single step depend on infinitely many bits, and on some formalizations such a machine decides sets that are not recursively enumerable. So the question is not whether quantum mechanics helps; it is whether physics contains anything that behaves like a genuine real number, as opposed to a finite-precision approximation of one that could in principle be represented in a bounded number of bits.

Give me a single number for PCT, then the decomposition that produces it: for each sub-question the number depends on, a rough probability and one line of justification, then how the terms combine. You should feel free to revise my framing of the sub-questions, but candidate terms include: the probability that physics is fundamentally finite — that any bounded region carries only finitely much information, or that continuous-looking magnitudes are always finite-precision in fact; the probability that, given genuine continuous quantities, they can be harnessed to compute a non-Turing function rather than being swamped by noise, decoherence, or the measurement problem; the probability that any such harnessed computation still counts as "physically realizable" rather than a mathematical idealization; and the probability that PCT is not truth-apt at all — that it functions as a definition or a stipulation about what we will call "computable," so that no empirical discovery could count against it.

Then: which term your number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it most. Say briefly what would count as resolving the question one way or the other.

Finally, a calibration aid. My own credence in PCT is about 0.9; a colleague's is about 0.4. Given your decomposition, which sub-question are the two of us most likely actually disagreeing about, and is there a way of stating that sub-question that would let us see whether we differ over a fact, a definition, or a value?
