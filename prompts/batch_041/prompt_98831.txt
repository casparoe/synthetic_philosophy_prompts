I'm a philosophy of science student, and my final paper is a test case for the rival interpretations of probability. We've spent the term on the frequentist reading (probability as long-run relative frequency), the subjectivist or Bayesian reading (probability as degree of belief), and the propensity reading (probability as a disposition or tendency in the physical setup itself). For the paper I want to run one real episode through all three: the failure of the quantitative risk models in the run-up to the 2008 financial crisis — the banks' value-at-risk estimates and the rating agencies' models for mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations. I know the broad shape of the crisis, so you needn't retell it; what I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions, theory onto case and case back onto theory.

First, tell me which interpretation was actually doing the work. When a bank reported a value-at-risk figure at a 99 percent confidence level, or when a rating agency assigned a triple-A rating on the strength of a model that compressed the dependence among mortgages into a single correlation parameter, what conception of probability were the practitioners implicitly relying on? I suspect it was a thinly disguised frequentism — a long-run frequency read off a short and unrepresentative historical sample — but argue it out, and say what each of the three interpretations would have licensed and what it would have ruled out.

Second, use the episode to test each interpretation, and be specific about where each one strains:
- the reference class problem: what was the frequentist's reference class, and what made it the wrong one?
- the single-case problem: what could it mean to assign a probability to the default of one particular tranche, and can any of the three interpretations make sense of that?
- the propensity reading: is there a defensible sense in which a pool of mortgages had a propensity to default, or does the reading quietly collapse into subjectivism here?
- the risk/uncertainty distinction: which interpretation, if any, honors the difference between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty, and does the crisis show that the distinction was doing real work?
- the collective-credence problem: if the probability is a degree of belief, whose belief is it — the modeler's, the bank's, or the market's? And does the frequentist's appeal to a long run smuggle in an assumption of stationarity, or ergodicity, that the crisis falsified?

Third, turn it around. Does the episode vindicate one interpretation over the others, or does it suggest that all three were beside the point — that what failed was not the meaning of probability but something else, such as model risk, incentive structures, or the plain fact that the future need not resemble the past? Say honestly where each interpretation is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and tell me if any of my framing above is wrong or oversimplified.
