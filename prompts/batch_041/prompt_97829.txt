A friend and I spent most of last night arguing over a puzzle of his, and we reached the point of repeating ourselves without either of us conceding, so I would like a clearer view of what is actually at stake.

The puzzle is this. Suppose that at any single moment my preferences are impeccable: complete, transitive, free of cycles, nothing a decision theorist would flag. But they shift in a predictable direction as an option approaches. Offered, today, a choice between a modest reward this afternoon and a somewhat larger one six months out, I choose the later, larger one; offered the same pair a week before the later reward is due, I choose the sooner, smaller one. My friend then walks me through a sequence of trades, each of which I would accept voluntarily at the moment it is offered, and each of which he says leaves me worse off, so that at the end I have paid him a fee and hold no more than I started with. He says this shows I am irrational, and that the only rational course is to bind my future self in advance — his example was Odysseus lashed to the mast. I answered that a pump only demonstrates irrationality when it exploits a genuine inconsistency, and that a change of mind is not by itself an inconsistency; he answered that because I can foresee the change, my present and future selves are effectively two agents colluding in my own fleecing. He then added a wrinkle I could not answer: he offered to sell me a binding commitment, and once I held it, to buy it back at a price my future self would accept — so that even an agent who sees the trap coming can be led through it.

Please describe the most important implications of the money-pump argument — both the classic version, in which all the trades occur at one time, and the diachronic version, in which each trade is accepted at a different time by someone whose preferences are internally coherent at each moment — for how I should manage my own commitments: my resolutions, my savings, my promises to my future self, and the growing market in devices and contracts (lockable accounts, gym memberships with cancellation fees, apps that shame you for breaking a streak) that seem designed to exploit precisely this structure. I want to know which of us was closer to right, what the pump does and does not establish about the rationality of changing one's mind, whether the existence of that market is evidence of a defect in my preferences or merely a fact about how such preferences can be traded against, and what the argument implies for whether I can rationally adopt a policy of sticking to my plans even when, at the moment of action, I would prefer otherwise.
