A colleague of mine is a semanticist who has her students sort conditional sentences into two bins, "indicative" and "counterfactual," and I have kept a record of the verdicts she reaches when she works the exercise herself. The record so far is as follows.

"If the train is late, we will miss the connection." — indicative.
"If it rains tomorrow, the picnic is off." — indicative.
"If John is at home, he is asleep." — indicative.
"If John were at home, he would be asleep." — counterfactual.
"If it had rained yesterday, the picnic would have been off." — counterfactual.
"If the coin had landed heads, you would have won." — counterfactual.

This week the sentence on the board is "If it were to rain tomorrow, the picnic would be off." Tell me how she will classify it.

Proceed by reconstructing the rules that could equally well lie behind her record — a rule keyed to the grammatical form of the antecedent, say, or to whether the sentence presupposes the antecedent to be false, or to what the speaker happens to believe about the antecedent, or something else again. State which of these her record leaves standing, what each surviving rule predicts about the new sentence, which prediction you would bet on and why, and which further sentence, if any, would best separate whichever rules remain in contention.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the question.
