My sister and I inherited equal shares of the family firm, and the partnership agreement settles who ends up owning it: there are ten annual windows, in each of which one of us — the right alternates, and mine comes first — may buy the other out. Whoever exercises takes 60% of the firm's value that year and leaves the other 40%. If neither of us ever exercises it, the firm is wound up after the tenth window and the proceeds are split 50/50. The firm has been growing about 20% a year, so if we both keep declining, each of us ends up with several times what I would get by exercising in this first window. An economist friend tells me the case is closed: in the tenth window whoever holds the right will obviously take the 60% rather than gamble on a 50/50 split, so in the ninth the other one should take 60% rather than be left with 40% of a slightly bigger firm, and the same step repeats all the way back to my window, where the answer is to exercise immediately. That feels like being argued into destroying the very thing that makes the asset worth holding, and it doesn't fit my sister, who has twice declined this kind of early exit in other deals, and who knows that I know it. What should I do when my window opens, and is there anything either of us could do beforehand to make the cooperative ending the one that actually happens?
