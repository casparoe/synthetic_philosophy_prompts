I'm on the standards committee of a professional association — mid-sized, humanities-leaning, I'd rather not say which — and I have to draft the opinion we circulate before Thursday's meeting, so I'm asking you to help me write the actual memo.

The case: a member, call him R, gave a conference talk that took a line a lot of people found demeaning toward a particular group. Nothing illegal, no harrassment of any named person, no incitement — just an argument that a large chunk of the membership thinks is ugly and beneath the discipline. A clip went around, and now there's a petition with several hundred signatures asking us to do three things: strip him of the 'distinguished fellow' title he was awarded two years ago, take him off the graduate mentorship roster, and withdraw the plenary invitation he already accepted for next year's meeting.

Here's the wrinkle that's turned into the whole fight. Our bylaws say the committee's job is to 'protect the community and the integrity of the discipline,' and one line says we don't 'punish' members. Half the commitee reads that as: perfect, we're not punishing him, we're just declining to platform him and withdrawing a privilege — a protective measure, end of story. The other half says that's a dodge, that taking back a title he earned, cutting him off from students, and publicly cancelling his plenary is punishment in everything but the name, and if it's punishment then we owe him what punishment owes anyone: a clear charge, a real chance to respond, some proportionality, and maybe a limit on how long it lasts.

I need a memo with a bottom line I can actually defend in the room: which of the three things (if any) we should do, under what conditions, and the honest case for the side I come down against. But the part I really want you to work through is whether that 'we don't punish, we protect' line is coherent or whether it's just relabeling — and what turns on the answer. Like, if these moves really are punishment, what follows for how we run this? And if they're genuinely not punishment but something else — consequences, disassociation, withdrawal of a benefit we're free to give or withhold — does that get us off the hook, or is that just a second dodge?

Last thing: a colleague wants to bolt on a rule that any sanction we impose auto-expires after three years unless we vote to renew it. Good idea, or does it smuggle in a theory of what these things are for that we haven't actually agreed on? Give me your view, not a menu.
