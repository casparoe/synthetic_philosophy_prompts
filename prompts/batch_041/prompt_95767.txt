I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether you can ever come to know that a painting is good by being told so, and we ended up talking past each other, I think because neither of us had said what an adequate theory of the matter would even have to accomplish. Before I read anything else on this, I want the specification. So please act as a philosopher working at the intersection of aesthetics and the epistemology of testimony, and think carefully before you write. I would rather have a shorter, sharper list than an exhaustive one, so begin by brainstorming candidate requirements and then cut the ones that turn out to be restatements of each other or of a single camp's pet prejudice.

Give me the desiderata for a theory of aesthetic testimony — what any adequate account of what can be transmitted, believed, known, or felt on the strength of someone else's aesthetic say-so has to deliver. Attach to each requirement the case or consideration that motivates it. I want at least the following in the mix, and you should add whatever else belongs:

- the ordinary cases on both sides: the friend who insists you have to see the film for yourself, the museum guide, the trusted critic, the wine merchant whose recommendation you act on;
- the intuition that first-hand experience is required, and whether it should be honored, explained away, or confined to a narrower class of properties than people assume;
- the distinction between what can be believed, what can be asserted, and what can be appreciated or felt — the theory should say which of these the skeptic is really denying;
- the asymmetry, if there is one, between non-aesthetic testimony about a work (that it depicts a horse, that it is a forgery) and testimony about its merit;
- the hearer who cannot have the relevant experience at all: someone blind from birth told about the beauty of a sunset, or someone who cannot form mental images told about a novel's imagery. Does the theory predict that nothing is transmitted, or something?
- the critic with no experience to draw on: suppose the review was written by a system that has never encountered the work, or anything. If the reason aesthetic testimony fails is that experience cannot be shared, this case should be irrelevant; if the reason is that the hearer must judge for herself, it should not be. Which is it?
- the distinction between testimony about the work and testimony about someone's response to it: I may be able to learn from you that I will find the film beautiful, or that you found it beautiful, while still being unable to learn from you that it is beautiful;
- secondhand pleasure: a critic's description sometimes produces genuine delight in a hearer who never encounters the work. Is that an aesthetic experience, and must the theory count it?
- the tension between the apparent availability of aesthetic expertise and the thought that aesthetic judgment must be autonomous;
- the parallels with moral testimony, and whether the aesthetic case is a special case of a general problem about subjective matters or a different problem;
- whatever the theory presupposes about the metaphysics of aesthetic properties (response-dependence, expressivism, realism), and whether the account of testimony can be held fixed while that background varies or must move with it.

Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of particular camps, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. Finally, which single requirement does the most to rule candidates out, and how the main positions on the question (the pessimistic, the optimistic, and any intermediate view you think is worth naming) fare against the list. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria are what I am after.
