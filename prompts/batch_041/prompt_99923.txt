I teach a graduate seminar in applied ethics whose closing unit is on the doing/allowing and killing/letting-die distinction, and I am drafting the take-home final. My students can state the distinction in either of its familiar forms: on the causal reading, an agent lets an outcome happen when her conduct is not among the causes of that outcome; on the normative reading, an agent lets it happen when she omits to prevent it in breach of a duty to prevent it. They can also recite the standard cases. What I need is a single essay question that makes them work with the two readings rather than name them. The question should require them to construct two cases of their own, neither drawn from the usual literature, in which the two readings diverge in opposite directions: in the first, the causal reading counts the agent as having allowed the harm while the normative reading does not; in the second, the normative reading counts her as having allowed it while the causal reading does not. At least one of the two cases should lodge the omission in a machine's default behaviour or in a division of labour across several agents, so that the question of who allowed the harm cannot be settled by pointing to a single pair of hands. Having constructed both cases, the student must then say whether the doing/allowing distinction, on either reading, can bear the moral weight that the killing/letting-die debate places on it, and defend that verdict against the strongest objection to it — for instance, that the causal reading covertly imports norms and so collapses into the normative reading, or that the normative reading reduces the distinction to the separate question of when there is a duty to rescue.

Please write the exam question itself; a model answer of about 650 words that makes its case without leaning on named authorities, since students will not have the literature in front of them; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, each carrying its mark out of a total of 20, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. The students will have ninety minutes and may write up to a thousand words.
