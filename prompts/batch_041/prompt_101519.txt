Last night a friend and I argued until well past midnight about Gödel's contention that relativity theory favours an idealistic conception of time. Her objection was brisk: even if we grant that Gödel's rotating cosmos — the solution in which closed timelike curves thread through the whole of spacetime and no global time function exists, so that there is no frame-independent fact about which events are simultaneous or which are earlier — is a genuine solution of the field equations, nothing follows about whether time is real in the actual world. The bare possibility of a universe without a global lapse of time no more shows that our time is unreal, she said, than the possibility of non-Euclidean geometry shows that our space is unreal. I think that is too quick, and I suspect the argument survives in some weakened form, but I cannot say where its weight actually sits, so I would rather have your number than your verdict.

Take the proposition to be a reconstruction of the argument, not Gödel's own words: (i) the Gödel universe is a physically possible spacetime, not merely a mathematical curiosity; (ii) in that spacetime there is no objective, global lapse of time; therefore (iii) an objective lapse of time is not a necessary feature of reality, and our sense that time passes is an illusion of our particular perspective within it. What is your credence that this argument, so reconstructed, is sound?

Give me the number, then the decomposition, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each term: the physical possibility or reasonableness of the rotating solution; whether the absence of a global time function is the right criterion for the absence of objective becoming; the modal step from a possible world without objective time to a conclusion about the actual world; whether, if the argument works at all, it establishes idealism about time rather than merely the relativity of simultaneity; and whether the reality of time is a determinate enough notion for the question to have a truth value in the first place. Then combine them, tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to, and say which single sub-question, settled one way or the other, would move your number by more than 0.2 — and what evidence or argument would settle it.
