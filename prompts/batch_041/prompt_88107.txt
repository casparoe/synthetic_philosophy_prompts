Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole about how courts decide whether a law gets to stand, and theres something that's been bugging me. For an ordinary law — say a state requires a license to arrange flowers, or bans alcohol sales on Sundays — the court basically asks whether the legislature *could* have had some halfway sensible reason for it. And here's the thing, they'll come up with the reason themselves. Like, well, maybe it's about public health, even if nobody in the legislature ever said that, and even if the state's own lawyer in court admitted that's not actually why they did it. The law stands anyway. But if the law touches speech or religion, the court flips completely: now the government has to actually show a real, pressing reason, and if they can't, the law's dead. And one more wrinkle that really gets me — when it's a government agency instead of a legislature, the court says it's not allowed to invent reasons on the agency's behalf. If the agency didn't explain itself, the decision gets thrown out.

So what is that first part committed to? Does it mean the actual intentions of the legislature don't matter at all, and a law is fine as long as some imaginary sensible person might have voted for it? That feels like it presupposes a pretty strange picture of what a legislature even is. And is that picture consistent with the flip in the second case, or with the rule for agencies? Reconstruct the view of law, of legislatures, and of what courts are for that this whole practice only makes sense under, tell me which rival views it quietly rules out, and point out where the practice is actually underdetermined — where several different theories would all fit what the courts do.
