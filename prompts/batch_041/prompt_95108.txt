I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to two decades, long enough to have acquired the habit of keeping a private file of arguments that prove too much — the ones in which every step feels forced and the conclusion is plainly false — because the productive question is rarely 'is this valid' and almost always 'which step is cheapest to abandon, and who pays for it.' I would like your help with the entry in that file I keep returning to.

The argument, reconstructed as carefully as I can:

(1) Set theory as it is standardly axiomatized is a first-order theory over a countable vocabulary — the sole non-logical symbol is the membership relation.
(2) By the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, any consistent first-order theory in a countable vocabulary has a model whose domain is countable.
(3) So if the standard axioms of set theory are consistent, they have a model M whose domain contains only countably many objects.
(4) Those axioms prove Cantor's theorem, and with it the existence of uncountable sets.
(5) M satisfies the axioms, and the axioms entail that uncountable sets exist, so M contains a set a that is uncountable.
(6) But M's domain is countable, and the members of a are drawn from that domain, so a has only countably many members.
(7) So a is both countable and uncountable.
(8) The only way to block the contradiction is to deny that 'countable' and 'uncountable' name anything absolute; they are relative to a model.
(9) Therefore Cantor's theorem establishes nothing absolute about the size of the continuum, and there are no absolutely uncountable sets.

The friend who first walked me through this treats it as a reductio of the thought that set theory describes a single intended universe, and generalizes without hesitation: on his view the same reasoning relativizes 'finite' and every other cardinal notion, and since nothing in the argument is special to set theory, it applies equally to the metatheory we used to run it. He is untroubled by that last point — he says the self-application is the argument's conclusion rather than its refutation — and I am not sure whether that is courage or a tell.

I have sketched two escapes and cannot price either. The first is to insist that the argument uses the word 'countable' in two different senses at two different steps, so that (7) never follows; he replies that this is a relabelling that cannot be made precise without already assuming what is at issue. The second is to keep the argument but add structure — a second-order axiom, or a determinate background notion of 'all subsets' — sufficient to guarantee that the set M calls uncountable really is; he replies that this either presupposes the very notion of set it was meant to secure, or else purchases absoluteness with a logic that has no completeness theorem, so that we can no longer tell which sentences our axioms imply.

Where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? Name the step I should reject, say why it looked forced until now, and tell me what rejecting it costs — and if the honest answer is that nothing is wrong and the conclusion must be swallowed, say that too. I would rather keep (4) than (9), but I want to know what I am buying, and from whom.
