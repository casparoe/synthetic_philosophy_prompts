A friend in my seminar built this argument and now thinks it is a reductio of precommitment, and I cannot see where it breaks. (1) A commitment device works by constraining what my later self may do. (2) Constraining someone's action is legitimate only if the person constrained authorized the constraint or can revoke it. (3) Suppose my earlier and later selves are one person. Then the constraint is authorized by the very person it constrains; but that person can revoke it, and a constraint the constrained party may lift at will constrains nothing. (4) Suppose instead that they are two persons. Then the constrained party neither authorized the constraint nor can revoke it, and a constraint imposed by one person on another, without consent and without recourse, is coercion. (5) So a Ulysses contract is either empty or coercive. (6) Entrenchment travels the same road: a people that may unmake any of its own acts has entrenched nothing, and a people that may not is ruled by its founders. (7) Yet self-binding is the standard illustration of autonomy and entrenchment the standard illustration of constitutionalism, and (5) and (6) would condemn promising and ordinary contracting too. So a step has to go. Which one, why did it look right, and what does giving it up cost — in particular, does the price differ between the personal case and the constitutional one?
