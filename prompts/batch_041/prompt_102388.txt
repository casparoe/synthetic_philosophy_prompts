I have been asked to give the keynote address at the hundred-and-fiftieth anniversary of the incorporation of the town where I grew up. The trouble is that I am a historian, and I know what the town prefers not to: the founding story everyone loves — the hardy settlers, the pact sworn under the old oak, the date carved above the door of the first hall — was assembled almost entirely in the 1890s by a committee of local worthies who wanted a pageant and manufactured one, complete with a founding date the records do not support and ancestors who were conveniently omitted. Write the speech I should give.

The audience will be mixed. Most are townspeople and local officials who have believed the story all their lives and who have come to celebrate, not to be corrected; my mother will be in the front row. Two colleagues from the history department will also be there, and they will see immediately what I am doing and whether I am doing it honestly. The speech has to land for both halves of the room at once.

I want it to think in public, and out loud, with the idea that the traditions and communities we treat as immemorial are typically modern and deliberately made — that a practice repeated with ceremony manufactures the continuity it claims merely to commemorate, and that a community is real precisely as something held in the imagination of its members rather than as anything given by blood or soil. But I do not want the easy move, which is to debunk and then shrug: to observe that all traditions are invented and conclude that our story is therefore no worse than anyone else's. That flattens the difference between the town's genuine, long-standing customs — the things it actually did, year after year, without needing a founder — and the fabricated pageant, and it insults the audience by pretending the fabrication costs nothing. The committee in the 1890s did not merely invent; it deleted people. The speech has to hold both truths at once: the community is real, the invention is real, and the invention did damage.

Some constraints. It should run seven or eight minutes. No citations, no footnotes, no reading list — I cannot look anything up on stage, and neither can the audience. You will not know the town's particulars, so invent a placeholder legend if you need one, but mark it clearly as replaceable (bracketed) so that I can substitute the real names and dates; the structure must not depend on which legend it turns out to be. There should be a genuine turn in the middle, where the speech stops being about what the founders were and starts being about what the town is now. And it should close by giving the audience something to do — a way of holding the story that is neither credulous nor cynical — rather than with a wistful aside about how we all need our myths.
