My cousin's doing a Wittgenstein module and he's got this argument he keeps using on me. I was complaining about a relative who's gone down the flat-earth rabbit hole — he wont let anything count as evidence, arguing with him is like arguing with a wall — and my cousin says, well, that's the point. The stuff you can't doubt, he says (the world didn't start five minutes ago, that's your mum and not a robot, words mean what they mean) isn't really beleived at all, it's more like the rules of a game: you don't argue about the rules from inside the game, you just play by them. Different people are playing different games, so my relative isn't being unreasonable, he's playing by different rules — calling him irrational is like calling a chess player a cheat for not moving like a draughts piece. He says it also explains why the shift from the earth being the centre of things to the sun being the centre wasn't anyone finding out they'd been wrong, it was just the rules changing.

I half buy it, but I want the audit. What maps onto what — the game, the rules, the moves, the players, winning, the referee, who's allowed to change a rule — and which of those bits has no counterpart in the belief case? What does the game picture quietly smuggle in that these can't-be-doubted beliefs don't have? Does the "so nobody's actually being unreasonable" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and does the bit about the earth moving survive along with it? And if the game picture is the wrong one, what's a better comparison for what these beliefs are and how they change?
