I am trying to settle whether the objection to quotas is an objection to the category or to the consequences, and I want the question posed as a pair of designed cases rather than as another tour of the familiar arguments. Please construct them to the specifications below.

**The cases.** Each case is to be set in an ordinary institution allocating an ordinary good — a faculty, a residency programme, a company board, an admissions office — and each is to be given in a single paragraph. Nothing may turn on an exotic stipulation, and no variant may be described as discriminatory, unfair or unjustified in the setup: the verdicts have to be generated by the positions applied to the facts, not read off the description. Stipulate whatever facts you need and mark them as stipulated. Do not cite cases, statutes or empirical studies, and do not name real programmes.

*Case one holds the outcome fixed and varies only the mechanism.* Three variants of the same allocation: (i) seats explicitly reserved for members of a named group, adopted openly with a published rationale; (ii) a facially neutral criterion adopted in full knowledge that it will select the same people, with that knowledge not disclosed; (iii) a blind lottery among all minimally qualified applicants, adopted for reasons independent of the group composition of its result, which in fact selects the same people. The cohort selected must be identical across the three variants, and no worse by the institution's own measure of fitness than the cohort the previous procedure produced, so that no one can be said to have lost a place they would otherwise have held.

*Case two holds the mechanism fixed and varies the outcome.* A single criterion, published in advance, applied without exception, and adopted for reasons having nothing to do with group membership, produces a stark and stable imbalance between groups in the allocation of the good. Identify the cause of the imbalance in the case, and make it non-invidious — an unequal distribution of some antecedent qualification, a difference in the reliability of the criterion across groups, or a difference in the rate at which members of each group apply. The criterion must be genuinely predictive of performance in the role while being, on reflection, not the sort of thing a candidate could deserve credit for.

**The positions.** Apply all five to all four allocations, each treated strictly as a verdict rule:

(a) the wrong lies in the use of the category: no allocation by group characteristic is permissible, whatever the mechanism and whatever the consequences;
(b) the wrong lies in the aim: what is impermissible is pursuing a group-proportional result, however achieved;
(c) the wrong lies in the departure from the desert-relevant criterion: a criterion that merely predicts performance without being deservedly attributable to the candidate is already such a departure;
(d) the wrong lies in the loss to identifiable persons: a preference is impermissible to the extent that a determinate individual is deprived of a good they would otherwise have received;
(e) the wrong lies in concealment or unreason: allocation on ascriptive grounds is tolerable when it is open, stated and answerable, and the same allocation achieved by an undisclosed proxy is worse.

**What the answer must contain.** For each position, its verdict on each of the four allocations and the feature it keys on. Build the cases so that the positions genuinely come apart: at least two must disagree in both directions within case one, and no position may return the same verdict on all four allocations. For each case, name the single feature that does the work, say what the pattern of verdicts becomes when that feature is removed, and say whether the case then collapses into the familiar dispute. If two of the positions turn out to be extensionally equivalent across the pair — one position wearing two hats — say which, and treat them as one. If a position's verdict on some allocation is indeterminate as stated, identify the premise it would need rather than supplying one silently. Say which position has the hardest time surviving the pair and give the strongest reply its defender would make. If any of these specifications cannot be jointly met, say which and why instead of quietly weakening it.

Finally, in two or three sentences each: what changes if the good allocated is a seat in the body that makes the allocation rather than a place held by an individual; what changes if the requirement is imposed by statute on private bodies rather than adopted by an institution for itself; and what changes if the group characteristic is an economic one rather than an ascriptive one, with everything else held fixed.
