My friend Nadia calls herself a Burkean and is definately one of those people who won't shut up about Chesterton's fence — the idea that you shouldn't pull down a rule or an institution until you can say what it was for. She's not a politician, she just has opinions and a newsletter, but she weighs in on basically every local reform fight, so I've ended up with a decent sample of her verdicts. Here's the pile:

- Our city had an ancient bylaw requiring every shop to keep a bucket of sand by the front door, left over from when everything was timber and lit by oil. Council moved to repeal it. Nadia was against, and her reason was basically "nobody in that room could tell me what it's for. If you can't say why it's there, you don't get to take it out."

- Different fight: the state notary system, where you had to be appointed by the governor to notarize anything. A bill would have let anyone with a certificate do it. Nadia was for it. Her line was that the only real function of the appointment requirement was keeping notary work with people who had the right connections, and once you've named the purpose and the purpose is that, the fence comes out.

- At her old university, they proposed replacing the oral qualifying exam with a written portfolio. She was against. The exam's purpose isn't a mystery — it tests whether you can defend your work out loud, and it drags you into the department's culture — and she said you don't swap out something that's been doing that job for decades on the strength of a scheme nobody's run.

- When the library extended its quiet-floor rule to the new annex, she was for it. Extending a thing is different from replacing it, in her book.

- She was against a ballot measure to write a right to housing into the state constitution, even though she complains about rents as much as anyone. Her view was that you don't fix a mess by putting an abstract right in the founding document and hoping the courts sort out the details.

- She was for shortening the notice tenants have to give to break a lease, even though the long notice period plainly does something (it gives landlords predictable turnover). A rule whose function is shielding landlords from mild inconvenience isn't a fence worth keeping, she said.

So here's what I can't work out. Is she actually running the Chesterton rule — oppose only when the purpose is unknown, otherwise just judge the merits like anyone else? Or is she running something more Burkean — a standing presumption in favor of whatever has lasted, so that even a practice whose purpose you understand stays up unless you can point to a real abuse, with the burden always on whoever wants the change? Those two give the same answer on everything above, as far as I can tell, and I can't tell which one she's got.

Now the actual question. I'm on the standards committee of the regional daily I work for. We have a rule that's been in place since the fifties: no story runs unless the central claim is confirmed by two independent sources, and single-source stories are effectively banned. Everyone knows what it's for — it's how the paper avoids printing something false because one smooth talker was convincing. It's not a racket and nobody's abusing it, though it does kill true stories sometimes and it slows us down. The proposal on the table replaces it with a documented-provenance rule: a story can go on one source if that source's track record is written up and a senior editor signs off. It was piloted at three papers our size, a study found somewhat fewer corrections and no more retractions, and it's being pushed by our own standards editor, who's been here twenty years and is not a person who wants to burn the place down. No scandal behind it, no victim, no abuse to point at. The old guard say the three pilot papers run different beats and the study is thin.

Would Nadia be for it or against it? Give me your best guess, and name the rule you think she's actually running. If both rules I described still fit her record, say which one you'd bet on and why, what each predicts here, and which bit of her earlier track record — or which fact about our proposal — would tell them apart. If the record genuinely underdetermines this, tell me which principle she'd be signing up for with each answer.

And be straight with me about how much this is worth. Tell me what would change your mind: what she'd have had to have said, or what about the proposal would have to be different, for you to predict the other way — and where your read of her is most likely to be wrong.
