I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to twenty years, and I am tired of the loop my corner of the internet gets stuck in whenever markets come up: someone treats the no-trade theorem as a conversation-ender, someone else replies that markets obviously aggregate information, and the exchange dies there because neither side has said which claim it is actually holding. I am making a handout for a reading group that has spent far too long trading Aumann references and if-you-are-so-smart-why-are-you-not-rich jokes without ever laying out the space of positions. Please write me a decision tree.

Assume the reader grants the following premise, and do not let your first node re-litigate it: if it is common knowledge that the traders are rational, that they share a prior over the state space, that they know the price, and that none of them is trading for hedging, liquidity, or consumption reasons, then no trade occurs whose only motive is informational, because the offer to trade would itself leak the information that made it attractive. Restate that premise in your own words inside the handout, since I want the handout to stand alone.

Build the tree so that every internal node is either one of the assumptions in that premise or one question about how to read it, and phrase every node as something a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to: concrete cases and commitments, not yes-or-no questions about labels. I want at least these nodes: the common prior; where the disagreement lives, whether in private evidence, priors, likelihoods or models, or preferences and endowments; whether the result is a claim about the impossibility of trade or only about the impossibility of both parties expecting to gain; whether the theorem is meant descriptively, normatively, or as a benchmark; whether the price is common knowledge because the market makes it so; and whether prices are cameras or engines, that is, whether a price describes a state that exists independently of it or partly constitutes that state.

The leaves must include, each named and each carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment:

- the reading on which disagreement is always in principle a matter of pooling evidence, the agreement-theorem reading;
- the reading on which the theorem is a reductio of the common prior rather than of trade;
- the deflationary view that markets aggregate preferences and endowments rather than beliefs, so the theorem is a non-result for market epistemology;
- the microstructuralist view that the antecedent never holds, because volume is life-cycle, hedging, and index flow;
- the adverse-selection view that any offer to trade is itself evidence, so quotes are self-undermining;
- the interior-equilibrium view, what gets called the Grossman-Stiglitz paradox, on which fully informative prices are impossible because information is costly, so efficiency is an interior fixed point rather than a corner;
- the performativity view on which prices are engines rather than cameras and the theorem's fixed state space is what fails;
- the mechanism-design view that accepts the limit and substitutes scoring rules, subsidies, or conditional markets;
- and at least one position of your own that the standard menu omits.

I especially want one inversion in the tree, defended or dismissed as you see fit: read the theorem backwards, as a baseline, so that observed volume becomes a thermometer for how far the traders are from common knowledge, a measure of heterogeneity rather than a puzzle to be explained away. I also want the thought that a market manufactures the common knowledge of its own price, so that the antecedent is an output of the institution rather than an external constraint on it, to appear somewhere in the tree.

Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say why. I suspect at least one pair collapses: if the common prior is a joint distribution over states and signals, then disagreeing about likelihoods just is failing to share a prior, and the different-priors and different-models leaves may turn out to be the same leaf; if you can keep them apart, say what the residual disagreement is about. Note the combinations nobody occupies, and diagnose each one as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

No citations and no reading list, please; I will take the blame for the attributions, and I want the handout to stand on its own. Aim for something a reader finishes in ten minutes: six to eight internal nodes and nine to twelve leaves.
