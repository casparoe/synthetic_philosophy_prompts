Ok so I'm putting together the take-home final for my upper-level "limits of formalism" course — it's a mixed room, half philosophy students half CS, and the essay section needs one question about what Gödel's theorems actually do and don't show once you drag them outside pure math. Every single year I get a stack of essays that open with some version of "Gödel proved there are truths reason can't reach" and then gesture vaguely at consciousness, or at science never being finished, or at AI never being able to know everything — and it's obvious they've memorized a vibe, not the theorem. I want to kill that.

Here's the shape I want. The question has to force them onto the preconditions, because that's the whole ballgame and it's exactly what the vibe-memorizers skip. So I want it built around two cases that look like they should be counterexamples to Gödel but aren't, and the student has to say which precondition each one quietly drops:

- first, arithmetic with addition but no multiplication at all — turns out that theory is complete and decidable, no Gödel sentence anywhere
- second, take the whole set of sentences that are true of the standard natural numbers, all of them, and treat that collection as your "theory" — that one is complete too; the catch is just that it isn't the kind of thing you could ever hand to a machine as a finite rule for generating axioms

If they can explain why neither of those sinks the theorem, they've actually understood what the effective-axiomatizability / recursive-axioms condition is doing. Then I want a third part where they evaluate one non-mathematical application and are honest about what would have to be true for the theorem to even get traction. Give them a couple to pick from — something like "no fully written legal code can ever be complete, so there will always be legal truths the code can't derive," or the AI chestnut ("Gödel guarantees no machine will ever capture all the truths, so no machine matches the mind") — and make them spell out what the code or the AI would have to be for the theorem to bite, and why in the realistic case it doesn't.

So what I need from you: the exam question itself, a model answer around 700–800 words, and a marking scheme out of 25. For the scheme, break the marks down by part so it isn't all-or-nothing, and be specific about what an A answer has that a B answer doesn't — name the actual moves, not "shows deep understanding." Also list the specific wrong moves that should cost marks, and I mean the real ones I keep seeing: confusing omega-consistency with plain consistency, treating "not provable in F" as if it meant "not true," sliding between incompleteness and undecidability as if they were the same thing, and the whole move of assuming the relevant system is fixed and knowably consistent without arguing for it. Its a take-home so no time limit needed, but I do want the parts weighted so the preconditions bit carries the most.
