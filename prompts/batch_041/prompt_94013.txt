A friend of mine, Nadia, is finishing a PhD in philosophy, and for the better part of a year we've been circling the same disagreement: when does an action have what she calls *moral worth* — her shorthand for an action being not merely the right thing to do but done for the right reasons? I don't have any formal philosophy training (I write backend services), so I've mostly been trying to reverse-engineer the rule she's running from the verdicts she hands down, the way you'd infer a function from its outputs. Here is her track record on the cases we've actually argued through. (1) A team lead who can't stand one of her reports recommends him for an award he plainly deserves, and does it because it would be unfair not to; she takes no pleasure in it, gains nothing from it, and is like this with everyone. Nadia says the recommendation has moral worth. (2) A second lead makes the same recommendation for the same award, equally deserved, but does it because he's fond of the man and enjoys being the one to deliver good news; he isn't otherwise a principled person. Nadia says no moral worth — being fond of someone, she says, isn't a reason that makes an action right. (3) A woman gives anonymously to a food bank because the thought of people going hungry distresses her too much for her to do otherwise; she's been like that since we were students, and she says she'd stop if the feeling ever went away. Nadia says that one has moral worth. (4) A colleague helps finish a project that genuinely needs finishing, but only because he expects it to get him promoted. Nadia says no moral worth. (5) And in a case I put to her as a trap: someone does the right thing both because she's convinced it's required and because she badly wants to, with no inner struggle at all. Nadia says that has moral worth too.

Now the new case, which is the one I actually care about. A teammate of mine, Marcus, has a reputation for cutting corners and looking out for himself; nobody would call him principled, and there's no pattern of this sort of thing anywhere in his history. Last sprint, though, he refused to ship a change he'd built because it would have quietly misled users, even though shipping it was exactly what he'd been asked to do and refusing cost him a bonus. When I asked him why, he said the change was wrong and that was the end of it — not that he felt bad, not that he was worried about getting caught, just that it was wrong. Then he went back to being Marcus.

What I want is Nadia's verdict on that refusal, but I'm far more interested in the rest: the rule she seems to be running, whatever other rules fit her five verdicts just as well as that one does, what each of those candidate rules predicts about Marcus, which prediction you'd actually bet on and why, and what further case would separate the candidates. If her verdicts genuinely underdetermine Marcus's case, I'd rather be told that than handed a confident answer — and if they do settle it, I'd like to see the closest precedent named and the reasoning that gets from it to Marcus made explicit.
