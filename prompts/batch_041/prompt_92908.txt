I am a high school student writing a long essay for a philosophy competition, and I have settled on the following conceit: Charles Darwin, who died in 1882, is brought back and given a careful but non-technical briefing on what has happened in biology, moral psychology, and moral philosophy since his death, and is then asked to give his verdict on four debates that did not exist in his lifetime. I want the essay to be a serious exercise in philosophical and historical inference rather than an exercise in ventriloquism, and I would like your help in building the case.

Before I set out the four debates, here is the picture of Darwin's own position that I am working from, so that you can correct me if I have it wrong. In his later writing on human evolution he argued that the moral sense is continuous with the social instincts of other animals, that sympathy is strengthened by habit and by the approval and disapproval of our fellows, and that an important part of the explanation is selection acting between groups rather than within them: tribes whose members were more sympathetic and mutually loyal would tend to prevail over rival tribes and to absorb them. He also worried, in a way that sits uneasily beside this, that civilised life — medicine, care for the weak, the softening of the harshest competition — had blunted the operation of selection among humans. And he firmly rejected the view of his co-discoverer Alfred Russel Wallace that natural selection could not account for the higher human faculties and that something extra, of a spiritual kind, was required.

The four debates I want his verdict on are these.

First, the gene-centred reformulation of evolution that took hold in the second half of the twentieth century, on which selection is understood as acting on genes rather than on groups or individuals, and cooperation is explained by the fact that interacting organisms often share copies of the same genes. Would Darwin have accepted this reformulation, or would he have defended his own between-group explanation of the moral sense? And if he accepted it, would he have thought it deflates the authority of the moral sense, or left it untouched?

Second, the debunking argument: the claim that if our moral beliefs are the output of a faculty shaped by selection for cooperation, then we have reason to doubt that those beliefs track any independent moral truth. Would Darwin have been a moral realist, an anti-realist, or someone who thought the question was malformed? Bear in mind that his own religious belief was in retreat over the last decades of his life.

Third, the modern dispute about adaptationist storytelling. Many critics hold that any trait whatsoever can be fitted with a plausible-sounding adaptive story after the fact, and that such stories are therefore not genuine science; others reply that they are legitimate hypotheses that simply need to be tested properly. Would Darwin have seen this as a continuation of his method or as a corruption of it, and what standard of evidence would he have demanded? Note that his own argument about tribes prevailing over other tribes was itself a selection story he had no way to test.

Fourth, moral bioenhancement: the proposal, defended by some contemporary philosophers, that because our moral dispositions are biologically limited and were shaped for life in small groups, we should consider using drugs or genetic technology to strengthen them. Would Darwin, who wrote at length about the breeding of domestic animals and about the effects of civilisation on human selection, have welcomed this or resisted it? And does his unease about the protection of the weak commit him to one answer rather than the other?

For each of the four, I would like (a) a predicted verdict, stated as a verdict rather than as a survey of opinions; (b) the specific commitments in his actual work from which you are inferring it, and how tight that inference is; (c) the strongest reason to think the prediction is wrong; and (d) where it applies, a distinction between what Darwin would probably have said had he read the modern literature and what his own views, developed consistently, would commit him to. Please also tell me where the counterfactual is so underdetermined that any verdict would really be a projection of the predictor's own preferences, and what kind of evidence would have settled the matter.

One constraint: please give me no citations, no reading list, and no footnotes. I have no access to a research library and the competition forbids them, so argue from what you know rather than from what you would look up, and keep the discussion at the level of the arguments rather than the level of scholarly reception.
