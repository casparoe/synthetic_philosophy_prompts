Does the following asymmetry have an established name? If it does, I want to know what work that name is doing and how the thing it names differs from its neighbours; if it does not, I want a coinage, with the boundaries drawn, because the label is the least of what I am after.

Consider two junior employees, each of whom takes a moral verdict from the same trusted mentor without ever examining the mentor's reasons. The first does something that turns out to be seriously wrong; she would not have done it had she thought the matter through, and she never asked why. The second refuses to take part in something that turns out to have been entirely permissible; again, she simply did as she was told. What I keep observing is that the first is held responsible regardless: no one says of her that she was merely following advice and is therefore not at fault. The second, by contrast, is denied any credit for her scruple: the act was right, we say, but it was not her judgment. The borrowed verdict thus appears transparent to blame and opaque to praise — it can carry fault downstream, but it cannot carry merit. And what seems to be tracked is not the truth or the warrant of the borrowed verdict but its provenance: credit is withheld even when the borrowed judgment is correct, and blame assigned even when it is reasonable.

This is not, I think, the familiar puzzle about moral testimony, which concerns whether a deferred-to verdict can count as knowledge, or whether the deferring agent can be said to understand what she does. Nor is it the general asymmetry between praise and blame, since that asymmetry concerns how much we demand of people in each direction, whereas here the very deference that lowers the bar for praise leaves the bar for blame untouched. Nor is it moral luck, since nothing about the outcome is in question and both agents act on the same kind of input.

If the pattern has no name, I would want the coinage to answer the following. Is the asymmetry supposed to rest on the thought that one owes it to a person to let her make her own moral mistakes but not to let her borrow another's merits, or on something else? Does it survive when the advice-giver is an institution, a text, or a machine rather than a person? And is the right description really that fault transfers while merit does not, or is it rather that fault attaches to what one does whereas merit attaches to what one has judged for oneself — and if the latter, what becomes of the agent who never judges for herself at all?
