I spent thirty years doing bench chemistry and have gotten into philosophy of language in retirement, mostly the indicative conditionals (the "if it rains the game is off" kind, not the counterfactual "would have" kind). Here's a pattern that keeps biting me and I don't know if it has a name.

I want to say my confidence in "if it rains, the game is off" is just my confidence that the game is off given that it rains. Fine for one sentence, and it matches how I actually reason. But the moment I try to turn that into a general rule covering all conditionals at once, the thing seems to eat itself. If I build the rule into a whole probability assignment, the assignment can't survive learning anything new — condition on some unrelated fact and the rule quietly stops holding, so I either keep the rule and give up on updating, or I keep updating and give up the rule. And when people try to save it by restricting the rule to certain conditionals — the ones where the antecedent doesn't tell you anything about the consequent, something like that — the restriction strikes me as either arbitrary or so narrow that the rule ends up saying almost nothing.

Is there a name for this specific failure? I know it sits near the family of results people call triviality results, and near the distinction between the probability of a conditional and a conditional probability, but I can't tell whether the thing I'm describing — the collapse under updating, plus the way the restricted versions deflate — is one named phenomenon, two, or just the same theorem said twice. I don't want a bare label. I want the carving: what exactly fails, what the minimal assumptions are, which assumption people actually give up and at what cost, and whether the "save it by narrowing the scope" move has a name and whether it's treated as a genuine rescue or as a concession that the original idea was wrong. If there's no established name for the pattern as I've described it, coin one, and tell me the boundaries — what would count as an instance and what wouldn't.

Answer in American English please.
