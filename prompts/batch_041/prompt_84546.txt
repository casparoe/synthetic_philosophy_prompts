I am a third-year undergraduate in a course on nineteenth-century German philosophy, and I have been asked to lead the seminar meeting on Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. There are about eighteen students and seventy-five minutes; the group has read the essay once and can list its three kinds of history and say what each is for, but has no feel for why any of them is dangerous or why the essay ends so uneasily. I would rather they run into the problem themselves than hear me summarize it, so I am looking for a runnable activity rather than a lecture.

The idea I want them to feel is the essay's standard that history is healthy only where it serves life: the contrast between a culture with enough capacity to absorb its past and still act, and one so saturated with the past that it can no longer decide anything. I want the second condition to occur in the room, not to be described. Two further things should be at stake without being named: that each of the three uses of history — monumental, antiquarian, critical — has its own way of both feeding and poisoning the present, and that forgetting is as necessary as remembering, so that the power to let a thing go is part of the same power that lets a culture act.

A few constraints on the design. Do not announce the three modes, and do not build a three-team debate in which each team is handed a label; the point is that participants should be using history in three different ways before anyone tells them those are three things. Prefer a single shared task in which the different uses emerge as moves within one game.

The seed I have in mind, which you should keep, replace, or improve as the design requires: the group is a town council that must commit, before a fixed deadline, to one of three plans for a present problem. The past reaches the councillors only through a dossier of invented, unglamorous fragments — an entry in a rate book, a line in a local paper, the caption of a photograph, a name on a memorial — which you should write yourself, keeping them morally mixed and deliberately undramatic. Any councillor may invoke the past to support or attack a plan, but every invocation is paid for out of a shared pool, and if the pool empties before the council commits, the council has failed to act at all.

Please give me the whole activity: rules, roles, materials, the dossier, how the pool is spent, a timeline for the seventy-five minutes, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the three modes and onto the health-and-paralysis contrast. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the essay's idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
