I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty-five years in statistical mechanics and low-temperature physics — who has spent the last four years reading philosophy in earnest, mostly metaphysics and the philosophy of probability, with considerably more appetite than training. I run a small fortnightly seminar, and two of its members have now spent five sessions talking past one another about the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles. One is a younger philosopher who holds the Principle false and takes the case below to be its decisive counterexample; the other is a logician of an older school who holds the Principle true and who, in my younger colleague's view, keeps changing what it says. I want you to write the joint statement the two of them could put their names to. I do not want a survey or a reading list — I cannot consult the literature and would not trust a list of it if you produced one. I want the document itself.

The case, as we have been running it, together with the wrinkle I want included. First: a world whose only contents are two spheres, perfectly alike in size, shape, colour, mass and every other intrinsic respect, at rest, with no observers, no other matter, and no absolute space or time — nothing that would let one say where either sphere is except in relation to the other. Call it W2. Every intrinsic property of the one is an intrinsic property of the other. Now the wrinkle. The defender of the Principle always answers that the spheres are after all discernible, because each stands in a different relation to the other — this one at such-and-such a distance from that one, and conversely. That answer does not seem to me to survive the following. Second case: a world whose only contents are three spheres, again perfectly alike, at the vertices of an equilateral triangle, all at rest, with the same absence of absolute space and time. Call it W3. There the relational profile of each sphere — its distance from the other two, the angles, everything — is identical to that of every other. So far as I can see, any reply that saves the Principle in W3 saves it in W2, and if it cannot be saved in W3 I want that said without euphemism. I should also like the document to say whether W3 does any independent work, or whether it is merely the old case with a third sphere bolted on.

The one place where I suspect my own subject may bear on the matter — though I cannot tell whether it bears on the Principle or only on my grasp of it — is the treatment of identical particles in quantum mechanics. There the theory appears to supply no fact of the matter as to which particle is which even when there is more than one of them; the formalism counts states rather than individuals. Whether that is a case of the Principle's being false, or of its being about something else altogether, I would rather have the two of them tell me than guess.

What I want written:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign — about what W2 and W3 do and do not establish; about what the Principle asserts on a weak reading, where the properties in question are restricted so that it comes out trivially true, and on a strong reading, where they are not; about what, if anything, turns on whether identity itself counts as a property; and about what would have to be true of the world for the counterexample to go through.

2. A statement of the residual disagreement, each side's position written in wording that side would accept — not a compromise formulation that neither would sign, and not a formulation that wins by definition.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side.

4. For each crux, the case, argument or consideration both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — what each expects to be true of W3, of the quantum case, or of some further case you devise.

5. If part of the disagreement is about words rather than about the world, say which part and why, restate the disputed claim so as to remove the ambiguity, and say whether either side's substantive position changes once it is restated.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements section stating each side's position in its own wording, then a cruxes table with a column for each side's prediction. Write it as a working document a small department could use: precise, no throat-clearing, willing to say of one claim that it is settled and of another that it is not.
