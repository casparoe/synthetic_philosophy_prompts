I am a third-year philosophy student currently enrolled in a seminar on the ethics of emerging technologies, and in three weeks I will be giving the best man's speech at the wedding of two close friends. Both of them are committed to what is sometimes called the hedonistic imperative: roughly, the view that we have a serious moral obligation to use biotechnology and other means to eliminate suffering throughout the living world, not merely to manage or redistribute it. The reception will have a split audience. About half are graduate students and researchers in philosophy, effective altruism, and neuroethics, who will know the position and its internal debates; the other half are the couple's families, including a Catholic grandmother, a stepfather who thinks transhumanism is a cult, and several cousins who mainly want a warm and funny toast. I need a five-minute speech that is genuinely moving and appropriately light in places, but that does not treat the couple's deepest commitment as a quirky hobby. The hedonistic imperative should do real structural work in the speech, not serve as decoration. Because they regard suffering as something to be abolished rather than ennobled, I cannot rely on the usual wedding consolation that love is worth the pain or that hard times will make them stronger, and I do not want to imply that their marriage is valuable because it will involve sacrifice. I want the speech to articulate a vision of love as a joint project of reducing suffering—perhaps even as a small-scale instance of the larger project they believe in—without turning the reception into a lecture. To give you something concrete to build around: they have a private, semi-serious practice they call the no-net-suffering ledger, a notebook in which each of them records moments when they think they have added to or subtracted from the other's suffering, and they review it on their anniversary. It began as a joke after a bad argument about whose turn it was to do the dishes, but they have kept it up for four years. I would like the speech to use that ledger, and the idea of a life devoted to the abolition of suffering, as its spine—not as a punchline, though a few jokes are welcome.

Please take the role of a philosopher with expertise in utilitarian and transhumanist ethics and a professional speechwriter. Before drafting, think carefully and brainstorm three or four possible structures for the speech, considering how to make the hedonistic imperative intelligible to the non-philosophers without flattening it, and how to handle the tension between a wedding's conventional celebration of love's endurance through hardship and the couple's rejection of suffering as a good. Then write the final five-minute speech. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to particular books or authors; the speech should stand on its own. Do not use quotation marks unless you are quoting the couple's own invented phrases. The speech should not mention that it is written by an AI or that it is an assignment, and it should sound like something a close friend would actually deliver. After the speech, add a short paragraph, clearly separated and not part of the toast, explaining which structural choices you made and why, and noting one thing the speech cannot do—one aspect of the hedonistic imperative that a wedding toast is structurally unable to convey.
