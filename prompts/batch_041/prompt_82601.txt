The investment sub-committee I chair is split down the middle, and I've got to bring a written recommendation to the full board on the 14th, so I could do with a hand drafting it.

Setup: it's a small charitable foundation — about £40m, mostly funds philosophy research — and our newly hired investment advsier wants us to put 5% into a trade she describes as basically free money. Some thinly traded inflation-linked bonds she reckons are mispriced because the institutions that would normally arb that away are constrained from doing so, and she's built a model that spots the gap before it closes. She's done real work and she's plausible. Half the committee wants in; the other half thinks it smells.

My worry, and the thing I want the memo to actually wrestle with rather than just mutter "diversify", is this. There's an old result in the economics of information — the no-trade theorem, I think it's called — that says roughly: if two agents are both rational, both know the market's already efficient, and neither is trading for hedging or cash-flow reasons, then they can't both gain from trade, because the other side's willingness to take your bet is itself a signal about what they know. Related to that is the Grossman–Stiglitz tension: if prices already reflected every scrap of information, nobody would get paid for digging information up, so an edge can't be simultaneously large enough to bother with and common knowledge. (I'm told the no-trade result really turns on *common* knowledge — everyone knowing that everyone knows — rather than mere mutual belief, which is part of what makes me unsure how hard it actually bites in a messy real market.)

So the crux for the memo: does the adviser's pitch survive that argument? If the mispricing is as big and as persistant as she says, who's on the other side of our trade, and what do they know that we don't? And is the bare fact that a strategy is being pitched to us — a small foundation with no particular skill at this — itself evidence that we're the mark?

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate: a clear recommendation (go, no, or go-with-conditions, with a time frame), the reasoning behind it, and a fair statement of the strongest case for the adviser's side — she'll say the theorem's assumptions are hopelessly idealised, that real markets are stuffed with noise traders and constrained institutions, and that if nobody's willing to be the informed trader then prices never move at all. Give that its due, don't strawman it.

One secondary thing to flag briefly, without letting it swamp the main question: we're a foundation that exists to fund philosophy, so *if* we genuinely have an informational edge, is it right for us to monetise it?

British English please, and keep it to something I can read aloud in about ten minutes. Thanks!
