I am a high school junior and the student chair of the committee overseeing our school's new AI advising chatbot, which will answer students' questions about course selection, extracurriculars, conflicts with friends, and family expectations. The committee has to decide whose values the chatbot should serve when students, parents, and teachers disagree. We have no AI specialists, no budget for outside review, and a weekly meeting that lasts an hour; the principal has final sign-off, but the procedure has to be defensible to parents and students. Please turn the principle of liberal neutrality -- refusing to treat any one family's, religion's, or club's values as the school's official values -- together with the practice of value aggregation -- adding up what people say they want -- into a decision procedure my committee can actually run.

Start from the distinction between aggregating preferences and remaining neutral among contested conceptions of the good. The procedure should tell us, in order: how to identify the values at stake in a query; when the chatbot may answer from a settled school policy; when it must present a menu of defensible options rather than a recommendation; when it must defer to a human counselor; and when it must decline to answer at all. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, and mark the points where the step is a value judgment the committee, not the chatbot, has to make. Build in a way to handle students who are minors and whose parents may have different values, and a way to keep the procedure from collapsing into either majority rule or a veto for the loudest objector. Also say how the procedure should treat the gap between a student's stated preference and what the student would endorse after reflection, since the chatbot may know the first but not the second.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a student asking whether to take an advanced course that their parents oppose; a student asking how to handle a friend who has confided that they are self-harming and asked for secrecy; and a student asking whether to attend a political protest that some families would find objectionable. At least one of these should end in an answer the committee will not like -- perhaps that the chatbot must stay silent or hand the matter to a person -- and you should say plainly why the procedure requires that. Finally, tell us what the procedure cannot do: which conflicts it will merely relocate rather than resolve, and which parts will look like neutrality but are actually a substantive choice about whose values count. Write it as a one-page checklist with a short rationale for each step, not as an essay.
