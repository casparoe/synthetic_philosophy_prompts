My grandad died three weeks ago, he was 84, and I've ended up being the one doing the eulogy at the crematorium on Friday and then a few more words at the wake in the social club where he drank every night for about fifty years. The complication is that he was a communist his whole life — joined up in his twenties, sold the paper outside the gates, had the books on the shelf with the spines all broken — and he was dead set against what he'd have called mumbo jumbo. No heaven, no looking down on us, no saying he's still here in spirit. He told me once that he didn't believe in souls, he believed in wages and rent and who owns the machines, and that if you want to understand why a person turns out how they turn out you look at the conditions they lived under, not at their character. So half the room on Friday will be old comrades from the branch, and they'll be listening for exactly that, and they'll wince if I start going on about his beautiful soul or his journey or what he taught us about love. The other half is family: his sister, who's fairly religious and wants to hear that he's at peace; my mum; my cousins; an uncle who's a mortgage broker and thinks the whole communist thing was a phase he never grew out of; and my nan's side, who never really forgave him for the politics. There's an extra thing hanging over the day as well. He left the house to be split between the three kids and there's already a proper row brewing about selling it, and after the crematorium everyone's going back to stand around in the front room of that house, which he bought in 1964 with money from the plant. The row is about money but nobody will say so, they'll all say it's about what he would have wanted.

What I keep going round in circles about is how to say anything true. I want a eulogy he wouldn't have thought was rubbish — so nothing that treats his decency as a free-floating quality of his soul, everything tied back to the life he actually lived, the job, the strike, the years on the sick, the way he was with us kids. But I don't want to do the other thing either, where you explain a man entirely by his circumstances, because my mum will hear that as me saying he had no choices, that he was just a product, and it'll hurt her — and honestly I'm not sure it's even true, since the fella next door had the same job and the same money and turned out a mean old bastard. Then a mate of mine who's in a reading group said something that's been stuck in my head. He said that in the Marxist way of thinking the family and the household aren't just the soft bit sitting on top of the economy — that some people argue the home is actually part of the base, because that's where the worker gets made and fed and washed and sent back out to work every morning, and where the next lot of workers get raised. Which, if you take it seriously, means the house and the marriage and the way he was with us isn't decoration on top of the economic story, it's the same story. I don't know if that's right and I don't need the history of the argument or who said it, I just want to know how to use it.

So what I'm asking for is the eulogy itself, a real draft I can stand up and read, about five minutes, that works for both halves of that room. The base and superstructure thing has to be doing the actual work in it — shaping what I can and can't say about him, and letting me talk about the house and the money and the row without either turning his life into a lecture or pretending the money isn't the reason everyone's standing in that front room — and it has to do that without the words base or superstructure ever coming out of my mouth, because the family will switch off and the comrades will think I'm showing off. Don't give me quotes or who-said-what, I'd only get the attribution wrong and then stand there looking stupid, and don't send me off to read anything, I've got until Friday. Just the speech, and then a short paragraph at the end telling me the hard calls you made — where you decided to keep him explained by his conditions and where you let him be something more than that, and which bits of the room you were aiming at with which bit.
