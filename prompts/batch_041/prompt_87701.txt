I recently read a short explainer on Newcomb's problem — the kind that presents itself as having settled the matter — and before I repeat any of it I would like a careful audit of how much of it I can trust. Here is the gist, reproduced as faithfully as I can manage:

Newcomb's problem is a test of decision theory. A being called the Predictor, whose track record is excellent, shows you two boxes. Box A is transparent and contains a thousand dollars. Box B is opaque and contains either a million dollars or nothing. You may take either both boxes or only box B. The Predictor has already acted on her prediction of your choice: she put a million dollars in box B if she predicted you would take only box B, and nothing if she predicted you would take both. The evidential decision theorist says to take only box B, because doing so is evidence that the Predictor has rewarded you. The causal decision theorist says to take both boxes, because your choice cannot change what is already in them. The two-boxer's case rests on the sure-thing principle: whatever the boxes happen to contain, taking both leaves you a thousand dollars richer, so taking both is better. And that dominance argument settles the matter, which is why evidential decision theory is simply irrational. Newcomb's problem is, at bottom, a test of whether you believe in free will: if you think your choice is genuinely up to you, you take both boxes, and if you think it was determined all along, you take only box B. It is really the same puzzle as the toxin puzzle, where a rational agent must acquire an intention to do something she has no reason to do.
