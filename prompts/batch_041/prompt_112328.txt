I am a teaching fellow in a combined history-and-film seminar, and next month we screen Miloš Forman's Amadeus (1984) — Peter Shaffer's play brought to the screen, itself descended from Pushkin's Mozart and Salieri — as the closing case study in a unit on hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation. My students have already met the experimental literature, including the finding that once we know how a thing turned out we systematically overrate how inevitable it was and quietly credit the actors with a foresight they could not have had, a pattern sometimes called creeping determinism. Two of them have also read the sharper philosophical point, associated with Danto, that history may have no other option open to it: the historian's basic unit, the narrative sentence, characterises an earlier event under a description that only later events make available, so that retrospection is not a bias to be scrubbed out but the very form of historical understanding. I should like you to take on the role of a philosopher of history who also writes criticism, to start by sketching two or three possible readings before settling on the one you can actually defend, and then to give me a reading of the film against both of those ideas — one that treats the film as thinking about historical explanation rather than merely illustrating it, and that says plainly where the reading strains.

In particular: does the film dramatise the narrative fallacy from the inside, locating the pressure to narrativise not in the observer who knows the outcome but in the participant who lived through it? Salieri recounts his life in old age to a young priest as a story whose whole point is his own mediocrity, and the confessional frame imposes a shape on the past before a word of it is told; is the film showing us that a plotless account of a life is unlivable, or merely that Salieri is an unreliable narrator and the frame is a licence to invent? Does the film trade on our hindsight — we know about the Requiem, the early death, the poisoning legend that the historical record does not support and that reaches the film through Pushkin — and if so, is that exploitation of foreknowledge an instance of the fallacy or a legitimate use of the audience's position outside the story? And where does the lens fail? Salieri's envy, and his quarrel with a God who hands out talent so unevenly, is a psychology of meaning rather than a miscalibration of probability; and the film's Salieri is a fiction twice over, since the real man was a successful court composer who taught Beethoven and Schubert, not the mediocrity the legend requires. I should add that two of my students have staked out opposite positions — one says the film is the narrative fallacy in its purest form, the other that it is a refutation of it — and I want to referee them with something better than my own impressions. So tell me which three or four scenes to put in front of the group and what question to ask about each; say where the film resists the reading I have sketched and what an honest critic has to concede; and end by telling me what the film contributes to this debate that neither the experiments nor the point about narrative sentences supplies. I do not need citations or a reading list, and please do not quote dialogue back at me — I know the film well enough; I want the argument. Write in British English, and finish with a single line beginning Verdict: stating in one sentence whether the film finally indicts retrospective narrative or shows it to be inescapable.
