Theres a chapter on his geometry that I'd like checked against someone who actually knows the period. Heres the gist of it:

Hobbes came to Euclid late — he was around forty, sitting in a gentleman's library, and by his first biographer's account he was converted on the spot. From then on he held that any real knowledge had to be set out the way Euclid sets out geometry: a few definitions and axioms, then one step after another, each forced on you by the last. That's why his political books read as deductive systems — he takes a handful of definitions of human nature and derives the necessity of an absolute sovereign the way a geometer derives a theorem. The trouble is he was no good at the mathematics itself. He became convinced he'd squared the circle; when John Wallis showed him he hadn't, Hobbes spent two decades in a pamphlet war, goaded mostly by wounded vanity, refusing to concede. Wallis eventually buried him in print and Hobbes never lived it down. The failure of his geometry is why his political philosophy was dismissed in his own lifetime, and it stands as a warning about trying to do ethics and politics geometrically.
