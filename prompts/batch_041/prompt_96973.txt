Hey — I'm deep in the five modes material in Sextus (the set people usually call the Agrippa trilemma, though he gives five and the three-horn framing is a later distillation) and I keep tripping on something that I suspect is doing more work than anyone admits. I dont have the Bury translation with me and I'm not going to trust my Greek, so here's my rough rendering of the argument as I understand it — paraphrase, not a quote, so don't hold me to wording:

To decide whether something is true you need a criterion. To know the criterion is the right one you need a proof. That proof needs a criterion. And so on. So for any claim put forward as established: either you demand a further justification for it and get pushed back forever; or you let the justification run around in a circle, so that the thing being supported is also doing the supporting; or you simply posit it as a starting point and grant it without proof. But positing something without proof is exactly what someone who claims to have knowledge isn't allowed to do, and the other two options are no better. Hence suspension of judgment.

Here's what I can't get straight. Horn three is standardly read as the anti-foundationalist horn. But the foundationalist doesn't say stop wherever you like, it's fine — they say some beliefs are justified non-inferentially, so the stopping is supposed to be epistemically okay, not merely stipulated. So does the third horn actually touch them, or does it only refute someone who thinks any stopping point is arbitrary? And if the skeptic needs that horn to rule out non-inferential justification, hasn't the argument just assumed its conclusion? I keep feeling like the hypothesis mode and the infinite-regress mode are the same complaint at different lengths — either you stop or you don't — and that all the force is meant to come from the thought that a stop is always a gap, which is precisely the thing in dispute.

Second thing, related: I'm suspicious that the circularity horn in Sextus is really about circular argument at all. My sense is that the diallelus in that text is more about the mutual dependence of the criterion and what gets judged by it — you need the criterion to certify the proof and you need the proof to certify the criterion — which is a structural priority point, not the coherentist's whole-web-supports-itself point. If that's right, then the modern trilemma has smuggled in a horn the ancient version doesn't have, and the coherentist is answering a question nobody had asked yet.

Can you help me sort out (i) whether the third horn is genuinely a distinct horn or just the terminus of the regress horn, and (ii) whether the ancient dialectical context makes the conclusion weaker than the modern no-belief-is-justified reading — i.e. is it about the dogmatist's inability to present a completed justification, rather than about the epistemic status of beliefs themselves? And please say plainly which reading you think is right, not just that there are readings.
