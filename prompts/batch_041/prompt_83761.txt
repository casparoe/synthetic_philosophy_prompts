I am a planetary scientist who has drifted into the philosophy of biology, and I am drafting the "what counts as life" section of a life-detection protocol for an icy-moon mission. Before I commit it to paper I would like an audit of my own position: I suspect it does not hang together, and I cannot see where the fault line runs. This is what I hold.

(1) Aliveness is a fact about a thing, not a decision we make about it. If we found a second, independent origin of life, it would be life whether or not our concept happened to cover it.

(2) The core of the concept is a self-maintaining chemical system: something that takes in energy and material from its surroundings, holds its own organisation together, repairs the damage it accumulates, and produces further systems like itself.

(3) Viruses are not alive. They are biological — the same stuff, part of the history of life, subject to evolution — but they are not living things. They are nearer to escaped genetic machinery.

(4) Sterile organisms are alive. A mule is alive, a worker bee is alive, and the last member of a vanishing species is alive until the end. A definition that denies this has made a mistake, not a discovery.

(5) The one feature that genuinely unites living things is that they are products and vehicles of Darwinian evolution — variation, heredity, selection. An account of life that leaves that out has missed the point.

(6) There is no sharp line. "Life" is a cluster concept with clear cases in the middle and a genuine grey area at the edges, and the vagueness is in the world rather than merely in our words.

(7) For the mission I need something a robot can apply: observable, unambiguous, and decidable from a sample within a few hours, because we shall not get a second look at the plume.

Two cases keep me circling back.

The Ganymede artefact. Suppose a lander finds a field of mineral structures on the sea floor that copy themselves using the local chemical gradient. Copies inherit their parent's lattice defects, and those defects affect how faithfully the next copy is made, so the population varies and the more faithful copiers come to dominate. Nothing, however, metabolises: there is no uptake of material for self-maintenance, no repair, no internal energy economy. Each structure is a passive template that either is copied or is not, and it crumbles when the gradient shifts. I want to say that this is not alive — a mineral process, however elegant — but I cannot say why without sounding like a carbon chauvinist.

The Lazarus organism. Suppose instead the sample holds a single organism, the last of its line, with no conspecifics anywhere in the galaxy. It metabolises, repairs itself and maintains its own organisation indefinitely; it never reproduces, and nothing else of its kind exists. I want to say that it is plainly alive.

I have been calling the distinction I am groping for the two-ledger view — one ledger for organisms, one for lineages — though I suspect it may just be a name for having my cake and eating it.

So: which of (1) to (7) actually pull against one another, which merely look as though they do, and what is the cheapest way to restore consistency? Rank the real clashes by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the package, including which of my verdicts on the two cases would have to change. Do not tell me merely that the concept is vague — I already believe that — and reason it out rather than pointing me at the literature; I do not want a reading list. Please write in British English, in about 900 words, under the following headings:

Verdict (~150 words): which commitments are genuinely in conflict, ordered by severity, and which of my verdicts are the casualties.

Real clashes (about 200 words each, most severe first): for each, the commitments involved, what forces them apart, what has to give, and the least costly version of that concession.

Clashes that dissolve (about 100 words each): the apparent conflicts that are not real, and why not.

Cheapest repair (about 150 words): the single revision that clears up the most, what it costs elsewhere, and which verdicts it changes.

Residual worry (about 100 words): what remains unresolved after that repair, and the one question I should settle next.
