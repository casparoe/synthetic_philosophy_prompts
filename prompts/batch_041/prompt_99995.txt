I'm a novelist, about 200 pages into a book set in a crumbling apartment building where the tenants organize a rent strike, and I've gotten stuck on a passage I can't crack. It's Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics Book IX, Ross translation:

"But who is to fix the worth of the service; he who makes the sacrifice or he who has got the advantage? At any rate the other seems to leave it to him. This is what they say Protagoras used to do; whenever he taught anything whatsoever, he bade the learner assess the value of the knowledge, and accepted the amount so fixed."

and a bit further on:

"For most things are not assessed at the same value by those who have them and those who want them; each class values highly what is its own and what it is offering; yet the return is made on the terms fixed by the receiver. But no doubt the receiver should assess a thing not at what it seems worth when he has it, but at what he assessed it at before he had it."

What I can't get my head around is what "worth" is even doing here, and why the receiver gets to set it. My landlord character says the flat is worth whatever someone will pay; my tenant character says its worth what she can afford and what it was worth before she got desperate. Aristotle seems to be saying something that touches both of them and lands on neither.

Please take the role of a classicist who also knows the economics and ethics of price controls, and think carefully before answering. Start by brainstorming three or four genuinely different readings of the passage — don't just grab the obvious one — then tell me: what work does "worth" do for Aristotle, is the rule that the receiver fixes the terms meant as a claim about justice or just a practical default, and does it actually cut for the tenant or the landlord? Then say where the analogy to rent control and minimum wage breaks down, because I suspect it does somewhere and I don't want to put a bad argument in a character's mouth.

No reading lists or citations needed, I just want the idea sorted out. And sorry in advance, this is probably a half-baked way to come at it.
