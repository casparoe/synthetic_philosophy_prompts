I'm a doctoral student in a seminar on bargaining and distributive justice, and I've been asked to open next week's session, which turns on the choice between two solution concepts for two-party bargaining. I'd like to frame the disagreement with a dialogue, and I'd like you to write it.

Here is the setup the session assumes. The Nash solution selects the feasible agreement that maximizes the product of the two parties' gains over their disagreement point; it is characterized by Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, and independence of irrelevant alternatives—roughly, the outcome should not change when you delete feasible options that were not chosen. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution instead equalizes the parties' gains as proportions of what each could hope for: it finds the largest fraction λ such that each party receives its disagreement payoff plus λ times the gap between that payoff and its ideal payoff, and picks the largest feasible point of that form. It is characterized by Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, and individual monotonicity—if the feasible set expands in a way that raises one party's best possible payoff without lowering the other's, that party should not end up worse off. The two concepts come apart: the Nash solution honors independence of irrelevant alternatives but can violate individual monotonicity, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution does the reverse, and given the other axioms no concept can do both.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent proponents, one defending each concept, arguing over a concrete case. In the case, two parties are dividing a surplus; the feasible utility pairs form the convex hull of (0,0), (2,0), (0,1), and (0.8,0.8), and the disagreement point is (0,0). Two complications should figure in the argument. First, before bargaining begins, the party with the larger ideal payoff can make an irreversible investment that raises its own disagreement payoff while shrinking the total surplus; the characters should disagree about whether their favored concept gives that party the right incentive to invest or to refrain. Second, the parties disagree about how large the other side's true outside option is, and each character should argue that this uncertainty tells in favor of their own concept.

The two should clash directly over independence of irrelevant alternatives versus individual monotonicity: whether deleting unchosen alternatives can ever be irrelevant to fairness, whether a solution that can pay you less when your prospects improve is defensible, and what each axiom is really tracking about the situation. Let them also contest the fairness rationales—the Nash solution's product-maximization and its reading as a fair split, and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution's idea of equalizing proportions of aspiration. Neither should be a strawman: each should answer the other's strongest point rather than restate their own view, and each should concede only where an argument forces it. Do not let them drift into agreement or trade vague mutual concessions.

Finally, I want a committed response, not a survey. After the dialogue, add a short closing note in which you take a definite position: which concept you would adopt for this case and why, and which of the two characters made the stronger case. State your view plainly rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.

Please write in American English.
