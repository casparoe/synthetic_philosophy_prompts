I teach a second-year metaphysics unit and I've got one 90-minute session with about 18 students to do Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism — the view that nothing created has any causal power of its own, and that what we call a cause is just the orderly habit in which God keeps producing one thing after another. On the page it lands as a quaint medieval oddity; they nod, they write it down, they leave thinking no-one could really believe it. What I want is for them to actually feel the vertigo: that the necessity they're so sure they see between cause and effect isn't seen at all, that it's a projection, and that once you drop it the world turns into a sequence of free-standing events rather than a web of powers — and, weirdly, that "miracle" stops being dramatic, because if there was never any necessity there's nothing for a miracle to violate.

Please design me a runnable in-class activity that gets them there through what happens, not through me explaining it. Crucially I don't want them to just re-run the Hume lecture they've half-remembered — I want the Ghazālī flavour: a voluntary habit that could be otherwise, and events that are decreed fresh each time rather than pushed along by anything inside the things themselves. So here's the thing I'd like you to build the whole activity around, because it's what makes it not-Hume: one of the students secretly plays the Sustainer, and everything the rest observe is just the Sustainer's ongoing decree. The investigators watch a run of pairings (this lever, then that lamp lights; this powder, then that colour) and naturally start attributing powers to the objects and betting on the next outcome. The Sustainer is told to be mostly consistent — I was thinking a written "habit ledger" they fill in each round — but with a limited number of times they're allowed to break their own pattern, and when they do, the investigators' "laws" fail without any law having been broken. I'd like the discovery that there was an agent behind the whole thing to come near the end, and for them to feel the shift from "these objects have powers" to "these events are just regularly paired."

Give me: the roles and how to assign them, the materials (nothing expensive — cards, a torch, a few household bits), the rules, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, a scoring scheme so it stays fun without real money, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto bits of the position. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of occasionalism the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. Write the whole thing in British English please.
