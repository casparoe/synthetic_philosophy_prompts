I'm in a reading group that's been chewing on futarchy — the idea that you vote on values and bet on beliefs, so a policy only gets adopted if conditional prediction markets say the world will be better with it than without it — and I've been asked to bring in a case that really stresses the mechanism. I want to build one myself rather than rehash the ususal objections, and I want it to bite for a reason that isn't thin markets, manipulation, or dumb traders. Specifically: a realistic local setting (a city council, a school board, a hospital trust, whatever), where the conditional market on the policy returns a confident and honestly well-supported yes, every trader is stipulated to be at least as well infomed and as well calibrated as the officials, nothing underhanded happens, and yet adopting the policy makes things go worse than not adopting it. The failure has to be structural — it has to come out of what the bet is actually about, not out of anybody's mistake.

One more thing I want to insist on, because it's where I think the interesting version lives. The easy version of this is feedback: traders see a yes and act on it, the prediction partly causes its own truth, and you can arguably fix that with better market design. I don't want that version. I want a case where the conditional being traded isn't well defined in the first place — where there's no settled fact about how things would have gone had we chosen otherwise, because the counterfactual world's policy is itself a function of a decision procedure (or a price) that only exists in this one. If you think that can't be done without cheating, say so and build the closest thing that can, but I'd rather you try. Constraints: no exotic tech, no time travel, no perfect predictors, nothing that couldn't plausibly happen in a real municipality in the next five years, and I want to be able to run one test on the finished case — delete that single structural feature, change nothing else, and the market's verdict should come out correct. That's how I'll know the case is isolating the right variable.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no wrap-up. (1) The scenario, with the market's question stated precisely enough that I can see exactly what's being bet on and who's betting. (2) What the futarchy procedure says to do here, and the reasoning that gets it there. (3) What the right decision actually is, and why the market lands on the wrong one without anyone having erred. (4) The one feature doing the work, and what happens to the case when I remove it. (5) The best defence available to someone who thinks futarchy survives this, and whether that defence saves the case as stated or quietly changes the mechanism.
