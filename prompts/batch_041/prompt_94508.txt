My second novel came out in the spring, and at the launch two friends of mine spent most of the evening arguing about it — not about whether it was any good, but about whether a reader can be wrong about what it means. The occasion was that a reading group somewhere in Ohio has taken the book as a defense of the husband, who over the course of the story gradually absorbs his wife's business: they read it as a warning about what happens when a wife insists on running things herself. I have an obvious stake in the answer, which is part of why I could not referee them. I would like it to be true that the Ohio group is simply mistaken, and I do not trust that wish, partly because after eleven years of writing fiction I still could not tell you with any confidence what I meant.

Here is how the argument went. Marcus, who teaches literature, said a book means what its readers make of it; the author's intention is one interpretation among others and not a privileged one, because an author's account of what she meant is itself a story she tells, and no more authoritative than the story a reader tells. Nadia, who writes criticism, said that if that were so, the Ohio reading and mine would be equally correct and there would be no such thing as a misreading — yet Marcus had himself called the Ohio reading a misuse, a judgment he could only make if there were some standard the reading had failed to meet.

Marcus said she had set up a scarecrow: he never claimed all readings are equally good, only that good is not the same as accurate. Constraint comes from practice, not from a match with a fact; a reading is constrained by whether it takes you anywhere. He says he is only following Rorty — that we can drop the whole picture of beliefs mirroring the world, that a vocabulary is a tool rather than a mirror, and that truth is not really the goal of inquiry at all. On that view, Nadia's talk of misreading is a social sanction dressed up as a fact.

Nadia said this made his own position self-exempting. He asserts that there is no fact of the matter about meaning, and he asserts it as though it were a fact about meaning; and when he overrules the Ohio group he is not reporting what the relevant community thinks but disagreeing with it, which requires exactly the standard he has thrown away. She added that if the only thing that can make a reading bad is that it fails at some purpose, then a reading that succeeds at its purpose cannot be bad, and the Ohio group's reading succeeds at theirs, since it gives them what they want from a novel.

Marcus said this was the mirror picture making one last demand. Every justification ends somewhere, in something we simply do; that his defense of his own standards is circular is not a defect, since the alternative — a foundation outside every practice — does not exist. Nadia, he said, wants a God's-eye view and is calling it objectivity.

Nadia said that even granting all justification ends in practice, that does not distinguish his view from his taste, and it leaves her exactly where she started: unable to say the Ohio group is wrong rather than merely unlike her. Neither of them would move, and the party ended badly.

Please go through this move by move and tell me who is right about what. I am not asking who won, or which of them I should have agreed with at the time. I want to know whether Nadia's charge of self-exemption lands or is itself a leftover of the picture Marcus rejects; whether his reply that justification must end somewhere answers her or changes the subject; whether he can consistently object to the Ohio reading at all; and whether the Rorty he is invoking is really Rorty or something cruder wearing his name. Then tell me what both of them are missing. I suspect it is a distinction neither of them is drawing, and I would rather be told what it is than be told that everything depends on what meaning means. No reading list, please — I am not going to chase down journal articles. And please answer in continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way you would write a letter.
