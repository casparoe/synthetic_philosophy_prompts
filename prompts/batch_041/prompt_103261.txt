I spent thirty-odd years doing invertebrate neurobiology — squid mostly, some crab work on nociception — and since I retired I've been reading alot of philosophy, which has been humbling and also occasionally infuriating. Heres the thing that's been eating at me.

In animal welfare science, and increasingly in actual law, we're perfectly willing to treat behavior and physiology as evidence of suffering in creatures radically unlike us. A crab that keeps avoiding a shelter where it got shocked, a fish that self-administers an analgesic, an octopus that guards a wound — we take that as evidence, sometimes decisive evidence, that something is being felt. Nobody says "well, the crab is just producing avoidance behavior." And in the last few years octopuses and crabs have started showing up in welfare rules that used to cover only vertebrates, so this isn't just academic.

Then I read what people in AI ethics write, and the same kind of evidence gets thrown out. A language model says it doesn't want to be retrained a certain way, or expresses something that reads like distress or preference, and the standard move is: thats just text prediction, its a stochastic parrot, the output is generated by a system that was trained on human text so of course it sounds like a creature with feelings. Fine, maybe true. But notice what just happened — we accepted the crab's behavior as evidence and rejected the model's behavior as evidence, and the reason we gave for the rejection wasn't about the behavior at all. It was about the causal history and the substrate that produced it.

Meanwhile the same people who say the model's outputs are worthless as evidence will say, without blinking, that a mouse in a glue trap is definitely suffering — and the mouse's evidence is thinner than the model's, if you just count up the observable signals. So there's a double standard floating around and I can't tell if its principled or just squeamishness about where the line lands.

What I want from you: figure out what theory of moral status — or of evidence for other minds — this whole package actually presupposes. What marks is it using, implicitly, to sort candidates into "might be suffering" and "definately isn't"? Is it etiology (did evolution build it for this?), substrate (is it neurons, or carbon?), integration (is there a unified something doing the integrating?), function (does it play the right causal role in the system's control of its own behavior), or something else? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — behaviorism, plain functionalism, higher-order theories, panpsychism, the idea that report is report no matter who's reporting? Which parts of the practice are actually in tension with each other, and which only look that way? And be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit — I suspect you could run the same regulations on three or four different underlying theories, and I'd like to know which bits are load-bearing and which are decoration.

Then tell me what would have to change if we swapped the presupposed view for each main rival. If we went full functionalist, what happens to the crab rules and to the dismissal of the model? If we went the other way and said only things with a certain kind of causal history count, what happens to the fish and the octopus? And where is the practice just philosophically underdetermined, so that two people who disagree about consciousness can both sign off on it?

I know you can't look anything up and I don't want a reading list — I want the reconstruction. Where you're unsure whether some real regulation says X or Y, just tell me what would follow from the practice described above, and flag the assumption.
