I run a graduate reading group on self-reference and rational agency that is jointly enrolled by philosophy and computer science students, and I am setting the take-home final. The unit has been about the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents, and by now everyone can produce the summary sentence: an agent whose reasoning is formalized in a theory extending arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, so it cannot verify the reasoning of a successor that reasons in the same way. That sentence is exactly what I want the exam to get past. The question should force students to see that the obstacle is not about the consistency statement in particular but about every instance of self-trust, and that the usual escape routes are not free. Please write one exam question, together with a model answer of roughly 750 words and a marking scheme out of 25. The exam is closed-book and seventy-five minutes long: students may cite Löb's theorem and the derivability conditions by name without proof, but nothing in the question may depend on their having read any particular paper, and no part of the answer should require citations, dates, or attributions.

The setup I want the question to fix is this. An agent A0 reasons in a consistent theory T0 extending arithmetic, with an ordinary provability predicate; A0 contemplates constructing a successor A1 that reasons in a theory T1, and A0's construction rule says that it will build A1 only if A0 can prove, working within T0, that A1 is trustworthy. The question should then require three things. First, the case T1 = T0, with the student carrying out the Löb-style derivation that makes the required trust statement unprovable, and drawing out the sharper consequence that a single instance of self-trust is provable exactly when the underlying claim is already provable outright, so that trusting a successor's decision is no easier than making that decision oneself. Second, an assessment of two repairs that students propose every year. The first repair is that A0 simply adopts the trust schema as new axioms of T0, on the reasoning that the added axioms concern the successor rather than A0 itself and therefore leave T0's consistency untouched; the student must determine whether the resulting theory is consistent and, if it is not, exhibit the derivation of the contradiction. The second repair is that A0 should reason in a theory strictly stronger than T1, so that vouching for the successor becomes ordinary mathematical reasoning about a weaker system; the student must say whether this delivers the trust the agent actually needs and what it costs, in particular whether a chain of such agents can be self-similar in strength or must strictly decrease. Third, and this is the part I expect to separate the A answers from the B answers, the student should be asked whether the whole difficulty is an artefact of all-or-nothing deductive trust, and what would change if the agent's attitude toward its successor were graded rather than certain.

For the marking scheme, I want the marks broken down across those three parts, each must-hit point named as a specific move rather than a topic, and a one-line note attached to each point describing the mistake that typically costs candidates the marks — for instance substituting a sketch of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem for the Löb derivation, or treating the axiom-adding repair as merely inelegant rather than inconsistent. The model answer should be written as a script a marker could hold next to a student's script, and it should make explicit the steps the derivation has to contain.
