Right, so I've been turning this over for a while and I can't work out whether I'm seeing a real pattern or just three unrelated things.

First: juries. Twelve people at random off the electoral roll, service is compulsory (skip it and you get fined), they get no training in law at all, they hear a case for a few days and then they decide — and their decision is basically final. You can't appeal on the grounds that the jury got the facts wrong, and you can't haul the jurors back in afterwards to explain or justify how they reasoned. If they acquit someone everyone thinks is guilty, nothing happens to them. We've deliberately built it so that nobody can hold them to account.

Second: citizens' assemblies. Ireland ran one that fed into the abortion referendum, France ran one on climate, and in the German-speaking bit of Belgium there's one that just sits there permanently now. Randomly selected people, paid, briefed by experts, they deliberate for a few weekends and produce recomendations. But — and this is the bit I keep tripping on — the recommendations go to the elected parliment, which then decides. So random people can set the agenda and write the report, but they don't get to make the call. Same method of selection as a jury, totally different amount of power.

Third: term limits. Everyone hates career politicians, presidents are limited to two terms, lots of US states limit their legislators. Meanwhile federal judges get appointed for life and central bankers get long fixed terms with no elections, and we do that on purpose, because we want them insulated from public opinion. So we distrust the people who answer to us and we protect the ones who don't.

What I want to know is what this combination presupposes. My guess is there's a line running through it between two kinds of judgement — the kind that supposedly only needs an ordinary person's common sense (guilty or not guilty; what should we do about climate) and the kind that needs years of training (law; interest rates) — and that we've quietly filed legislating under the first kind, which is why we're happy to hand it to amateurs and also why we don't think it's a skill worth letting anybody get too good at.

But is that right? Reconstruct the view of legitimacy and competence that these practices only make sense under, and tell me which rival views they quietly rule out. Does sortition presuppose that there's a common good a disinterested random person can actually find, as opposed to just a clash of interests that elections aggregate? Is the judge/banker half of the picture in tension with the jury/assembly half, or does one theory cover both? Does the gap between juries and assemblies — one decides, the other only advises — presuppose something about whether there's a right answer in each domain? And tell me what's actually forced by these practices versus what they merely happen to fit with, i.e. where the whole thing is underdetermined.

Then the payoff question: if you swapped the presupposed view for a rival — pure expertise, say, or politics as straightforward interest-group bargaining — what concretely would have to change about how these institutions are designed? And do I have to give up something I like here, because I suspect I can't keep both 'ordinary people are the best judges of what's fair' and 'defer to the people who've studied it for twenty years'.

I don't know the vocabulary for any of this, so name the positions as you go.
