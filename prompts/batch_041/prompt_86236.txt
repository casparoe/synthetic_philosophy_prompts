My grandad has Alzheimer's and my dad and my aunt are barely on speaking terms right now because of a form my grandad signed about six years ago, back when he was still himself — no feeding tube, no antibiotics, no hospital if it's pneumonia, "let me go" basically. He's got pneumonia right now. And here's my problem, he's... okay? He doesn't know my name most days, but he's not miserable, he eats if you sit with him and put food in front of him, he laughs at the dog. When the doctor asked him if he wanted to get better he said yes. My aunt says that's not him talking, thats just the disease being agreeable. My dad says a promise is a promise and a man should get the death he asked for.

So I tried to actually write the argument out, because I wanted to know which side I think is right, and now I've got something I can't get out of. (1) The form is about his life, not somebody elses — thats why he was allowed to write it in the first place. (2) It's his life because the man in the bed and the man who signed are the same person. (3) Normally when the same person changes their mind, the newer mind wins — we don't make people stay married, we don't hold someone to a decision they've dropped, and if he'd told a doctor last week he wanted treatment and told a different one today he didn't, today would win. (4) But if (3) is right, then the form loses to "yes, I want to get better," and then the form was never worth the paper it's printed on, because the only time it ever matters is when the person who signed it isn't around anymore to mean it. (5) So the form has to win even though he now says the opposite, which means the earlier him gets to overrule the later him. (6) But (2) said they're the same person, so the later him has exactly as much claim to be the one deciding as the earlier him does. So whatever (5) is leaning on, it isn't sameness — it has to be rank. The earlier him was competent and the later him isn't. (7) But being competent isn't what makes my decisions about my own life binding on me. If it were, a competent me could sign anything: I could sign away my savings at 70, promise never to see my kids again, agree to be somebody's property for the rest of my life — and my later self, the one who actually has to live it, would just have to lump it, because I was of sound mind when I signed. (8) And thats wrong. You can't sell yourself into slavery no matter how sane you are when you sign, and if you change your mind the day before, we let you walk. So if (7) is the only thing holding up (5), then (5) falls, and with it (1) — the form means nothing, and my aunt is right.

I'd rather keep (1) and (8) than (5). The form has to mean something or we're torturing a man to death, and slavery has to stay impossible, I'm not budging on that. So where does this go wrong? Which step do I drop, why did it look so obviously fine when I wrote it, and what does dropping it cost me? I want the actual price, not a fudge — if the answer is that there's some line between the wishes he has now and the ones he had then, tell me where the line is drawn and why the line can't be drawn the other way round, because my aunt will draw it the other way round the second I hand it to her.
