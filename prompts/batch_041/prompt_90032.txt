Question. Ulysses has himself tied to the mast; constitutional designers entrench things so that later majorities can't easily undo them. Consider a device I'll call a ratchet clause: any simple majority may tighten it, but it can be loosened only by two-thirds majorities in two consecutive legislatures. (a) What exactly makes the Ulysses contract work — what does it change about the agent's situation, and what has to be true of the later self for tying yourself down to be rational? (b) Is the ratchet clause the same trick at the scale of a polity, or does the analogy break? (c) Does whatever licenses the earlier self's authority also license the earlier majority's? 900 words max.

Student's answer. A Ulysses contract is a case of precommitment: the agent foresees that his later self will be moved by something he now judges he shouldn't be moved by, so he deliberately changes the options available to that later self. The mast doesn't change Ulysses' desires, it changes what satisfying them would cost him. So the device is a constraint rather than a conversion: the later self's preferences are left intact and the actions that would satisfy them are just taken off the table. That's why you don't have to win the argument with your future self — you only have to make sure he loses it.

For the arrangement to be rational, though, the later self has to be able to endorse it. That's what separates a Ulysses contract from mere compulsion: if tomorrow's Ulysses looks at the ropes and thinks good, leave them on, the earlier decision is vindicated, and if he thinks get me out of this, then all we have is one time-slice imposing on another and there's no reason to call it rational.

Structurally the ratchet clause looks like the same move. A majority now, worried about what a future majority might do under pressure — panic, war, a demagogue — raises the cost of undoing the arrangement. And it does this in the binding way rather than by trying to change anyone's preferences: nobody has to be brought to love the clause. So I'd say it's the same trick at a larger scale, and in one respect stronger, because the mast needs rope and loyal sailors, whereas the ratchet clause runs on the incentives of future legislators and is more or less self-enforcing.

On the identity problem, I think the analogy actually gets easier rather than harder. Parfit showed that the self is not what we think it is — there's no further fact of the matter about personal identity over time — so the Ulysses contract is a bit of a fiction: the you who ties the knot and the you who strains against it aren't strictly the same person, and precommitment is really a relation between different people. A polity doesn't have this problem, because a majority is just the same citizens voting at a different time. The people are the same, only the numbers changed. So the ratchet clause does what the mast does without needing the metaphysics.

The one asymmetry worth flagging is that tightening is cheap and loosening is expensive, so a majority that is only temporarily in power can raise the bar permanently. That seems like the price of the device, and it's the same price Ulysses paid.
