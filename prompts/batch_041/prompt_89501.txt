I run a fortnightly session for our department's undergraduate philosophy society — around twenty students, mixed years, ninety minutes, no reading assigned in advance, and a standing expectation that whatever we do has to land on its own without a lecture up front. This term I've drawn the futarchy slot: Hanson's proposal, as I recall it from the old blogosphere, that we vote on the values we want and then let conditional prediction markets tell us which policy will actually deliver them. I've argued about decision markets for years and I'm tired of the usual outcome, where the room either nods along at the slogan or decides it's obviously unworkable and retains nothing. What I want is for the students to feel the actual shape of the thing in their hands: the moment where it looks like it solves a genuine problem, and the moment where it quietly relocates that problem instead of solving it. So the activity has to be one where they run into the trouble themselves, in the doing, before anyone says the word futarchy.

Here's the decision I'd like to hang it on, though you should feel free to adjust it as long as the structural features survive. Our society has a fixed pot of discretionary money and must choose between subsidizing a peer-tutoring programme and paying for a late-night study space; there is a real disagreement about what the money is for, and I want that disagreement to be about *values* while the factual question — which policy actually produces the good outcome — is genuinely uncertain and dispersed across the room. The sequence I've sketched: first the group has to fix a single scalar metric by vote before any trading is allowed (and I want them to feel how much of the fight has already been settled, silently, at that step); then open a decision market on whether the metric will come out higher under one policy than the other, with play money or points, a small number of privately distributed information slips (someone knows the tutoring coordinator is about to leave; someone knows the study space sits next to a construction site), and a resolution procedure they have to agree on in advance. Then, once a price has formed and the room is feeling pleased with how cleanly it aggregated what it knew, I want a second and third round that break it: one where a participant or two are quietly given the ability to influence the very measurement the market resolves against, so that betting on the metric becomes betting on something the policy itself perturbs; and one where the metric is redefined after the fact, or where the question is posed not as a threshold but as a bare comparison between two worlds only one of which will ever exist.

Design me the runnable activity: the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, the wording of the private information slips, and the resolution protocol. The debrief matters as much as the game — it should map specific moments in the activity onto the corresponding pieces of the idea (the vote-on-values / bet-on-beliefs split, the fact that a threshold market prices a question the threshold-setter chose, the counterfactual problem of resolving on a world that doesn't happen, the endogeneity that turns measurement into a target, and the thin-market and manipulation worries), and it should be honest about which of those the activity genuinely reproduces and which it can only gesture at, with a note on what I'll have to supply myself. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I'd rather hear the failure modes now than discover them live — and how to handle each without rescuing the game so thoroughly that the difficulty disappears.
