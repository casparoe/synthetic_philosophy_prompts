I'm a retired physicist — magnetic resonance, mostly, forty years of it — and since I packed it in I've been reading philosophy and annoying my former colleagues with it. I've built an argument that I'm now suspicious of, because I genuinely can't tell whether it's clever or just a muddle.

(1) If two things are alike in every respect, then they're one thing, not two. What could possibly make them two? You'd need some difference between them, and by hypothesis there isn't one.

(2) So take the two-sphere world (I think this is Max Black's, from a paper in the fifties): a universe containing exactly two iron spheres, same diameter, same mass, same temperature, same everything, two miles apart, and nothing else at all anywhere.

(3) Whatever is true of one sphere is true of the other. Each has mass m and diameter d; each is two miles from the other one; each is one of exactly two spheres; and so on down the list.

(4) So by (1) there's really only one sphere in that universe.

(5) But that's plainly wrong. That universe has twice the total mass of a universe containing one such sphere, twice as much matter in it, and two objects instead of one. Those are different worlds if anything is. So (4) is false.

(6) Therefore (1) is false: two things can be alike in every way, and their twoness is a bare fact with no basis in how they are.

Here's what bothers me. (6) seems to prove too much. If distinctness doesn't have to show up anywhere in how things are, then the world I'm in now and the world with everything shifted a metre to the left — or with every particle swapped for an exactly similar duplicate — are different worlds. I always thought Leibniz used precisely that point to argue that absolute space is a fiction, that the shifted world is just our world under a different label. Give up (1) and I seem to lose that, and I'm left with an invisible extra ingredient in the universe, 'which sphere is which', that makes no difference to anything — which is the sort of thing I stopped believing in when I stopped going to church. On the other hand, in my old trade we'd have said the two spheres differ by a symmetry: swapping them isn't a different state of the world at all, the way swapping the two electrons in a helium atom isn't a different state. That looks like it supports (1) rather than refuting it. But if I keep (1) I'm stuck with (4), and I can't have (4). I'd definately rather keep (1) than (4) if there's a way, but I want to know what it costs.

Which step do I drop, and what's the bill? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question.
