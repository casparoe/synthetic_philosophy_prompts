I'm in a fourth-year seminar on rationality, we've just done two weeks on Dutch book arguments, and the more cases I turn over the less my gut verdicts hang together. My flatmate, who's a statistician, says I'm overthinking it, but I want to lay the whole pile out and have someone find the principle behind them, if there is one. Case 1: someone whose credence in P is 0.6 and whose credence in not-P is also 0.6. A bookie who buys and sells at her own stated odds can assemble a set of bets that leaves her down whatever happens. I say she's flatly irrational, and -- this is the part I care about -- I'd say it even if she were the last person on earth and no bookie could ever exist to do it. Case 2: someone whose credences at every moment satisfy the probability axioms, but who is certain that tomorrow, with no new evidence, she'll rate some proposition P at 0.9 when she rates it 0.5 today. There's a two-step trade that takes her money for sure. Irrational, I say, even though at no single moment is she in a state you could point at and call inconsistent. Case 3: someone whose credences are perfectly coherent but who prefers a sure $1 to a 50-50 shot at $3. A bookie can bleed her by offering her trades she likes. I don't think she's irrational at all -- that's just risk aversion, and if anything is wrong, it's wrong with her tastes and not with her beliefs. Case 4: someone whose credences are genuinely vague -- her credence in P is somewhere between 0.3 and 0.6 and she means it, it's not that she hasn't got round to thinking -- and she has a fixed policy of pricing every bet you offer her by the sharpening of that range that's least favourable to her. I'm told no book can be built against her. I want to say she's fine. But what saved her was vagueness, and vagueness doesn't feel like it should be a virtue.

Case 5: exactly Case 1's woman, except she has decided she will never make a bet with anyone, ever. The bookie can do nothing at all. My gut still says irrational, but my whole reason in Case 1 was the existence of the book, and here there's no book. Case 6: someone with cyclic preferences -- she'll pay a little to swap A for B, a little to swap B for C, and a little to swap C for A -- and she cheerfully makes each swap when it's offered. You can walk her round the loop and finish holding her original A plus some of her money. This one I honestly can't place. She ends up worse than she started, but only because of what she does, and at every single step she's getting exactly what she prefers. Is that irrational in the same sense as Case 1, a different sense, or not at all? Case 7, which I only thought of last night: someone who passes all the usual coherence tests but assigns credence exactly 0 to a proposition she cheerfully agrees could be true -- she says she's certain it won't happen and that it might happen. No book seems to touch her. I don't know whether to call her irrational or just epistemically unpleasant.

So: what's the principle? My own guess is that it has to be about what's true of her at a time versus what she'd be led into over time, or maybe about whether her credences could be read as anybody's honest estimate of anything -- but that's a gesture, not a principle, and it definately doesn't tell me what to say about Cases 4, 5 and 7. I'd like it stated precisely enough that I can apply it myself: which case motivates each clause, what it says about something I haven't listed (say, someone whose only vulnerability is to an infinite book -- countably many bets, each one fair by her lights, no finite subset of which costs her a penny -- or the Case 7 person, if you'd rather), and honestly which of my seven verdicts it forces me to give up. I'd rather loose a verdict than keep a principle with an epicycle for every case, and if the true answer is that there are two different notions of incoherence doing the work here, say that instead of fusing them. I don't need to know who said what first, just the principle and its verdicts.
