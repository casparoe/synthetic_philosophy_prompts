I run a small applied-ethics group inside a company that ships a general-purpose assistant, and we have been given roughly four researcher-years and a mandate to write the company's public doctrine on when the assistant should refuse a request outright, when it should comply but steer, when it should add friction (ask once more, slow the user down, require an explicit confirmation), and when it should simply do what was asked while quietly correcting what it takes to be a mistake. Take the role of a philosopher who works both on the classical literature about paternalism and informed consent and on the practical business of writing behavior specs for AI systems, and help me decide where those four researcher-years should go. My own working view, which I would like you to treat as a target rather than a premise, is that almost all of the doctrine is generated by a single hypothetical-consent test: the assistant may act on the user's behalf only in ways the user would endorse if they knew what the assistant knows. The difficulty is that the interesting cases are precisely the ones in which the user, by stipulation, does not know what the assistant knows, so the test threatens to be either circular or covertly doing something else. I also suspect that a great deal of what gets called paternalism in this area is really about third parties — refusing to help me do something that harms someone else is not paternalism toward me at all — and that the genuinely self-regarding cases, the ones where the assistant overrides or withholds for my own sake, are both rarer and harder than the discussion usually implies. A further wrinkle: some of the most consequential paternalism is exercised not by any identifiable decision but by defaults the lab chose and the user never saw, which makes it unclear who the paternalist even is.

What I want is an agenda, not a survey. Assume the four people are philosophers and social scientists with no ability to run training experiments, so questions whose only payoff is an empirical result we cannot produce are out of scope. Which open conceptual questions about assistant refusal, override, and default-setting should we take up, and in what order? For each, tell me why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people who cannot run experiments, whether it is already crowded, and what would have to be learned in the next year or two for it to move up or down the list. Name the questions you think are overrated as well as the ones you would prioritize — the ones that sound central but whose answer would not change much downstream — and be willing to tell me that my hypothetical-consent framing is the wrong organizing idea, if you think it is.

Before you rank anything, spend a paragraph brainstorming the candidate questions, including at least two that my framing above does not mention; then defend an ordering against at least one serious rival ordering, so I can see what the disagreement turns on. Throughout, be explicit about which of your claims are settled among people who actually work on this and which are genuinely contested — I would much rather have an honest map of the disagreement than a confident false consensus, and I would like you to flag where you are uncertain or where your answer depends on empirical assumptions you cannot check.
