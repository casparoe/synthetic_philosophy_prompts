I am an undergraduate taking a course on democratic theory, and I have become preoccupied with a cluster of institutional practices that tend to appear together, in varying combinations, in a number of countries, but that the course treats under separate headings. The cluster is this. A body of citizens chosen by lot, rather than elected or self-selected, is convened to study one question, hear evidence from experts and advocates, deliberate over several weekends, and publish a recommendation; the question itself is framed by that body rather than by the government. The same question is then put to the whole electorate in a referendum, and the state funds and circulates an even-handed statement of the arguments on both sides, prepared by a body forbidden to advocate either answer. Finally, if the assembly's recommendation and the referendum result diverge, nothing follows from the divergence: the result binds and the recommendation is filed.

I would like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this composite practice presupposes, rather than summarising the existing debate about deliberative democracy. Specifically:

1. The convening of an assembly suggests that the popular will has to be formed; the binding force of the referendum, together with the indifference to divergence, suggests that it is simply counted. Can both be held at once, or is the practice incoherent? Does it, perhaps, presuppose two different subjects of legitimacy rather than one?
2. What does selection by lot presuppose about the kind of representation appropriate here? Descriptive resemblance, statistical representativeness, and the superior quality of small-group deliberation are three quite different justifications, and I want to know which the practice actually needs and which it merely fits.
3. What does a state-funded, even-handed statement of the arguments presuppose about the possibility of presenting a case without weighting it? That the merits of an argument are separable from its persuasive force, or only that voters are to be treated as judges rather than parties?
4. Does the practice distinguish between what people now want and what they would want after better deliberation? If so, which of the two the referendum is supposed to measure, and why the first should bind when the second is what the assembly was convened to discover?
5. Which of these commitments does the practice strictly require, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Could someone who regards voting as the aggregation of given preferences, with no interest in deliberation, run exactly these institutions without inconsistency? Conversely, if one abandoned the view that preferences are transformable, would the assembly collapse into an expensive opinion poll, or could it still be defended on other grounds?
6. Finally, tell me what would have to be redesigned if one located legitimacy in the deliberating body itself rather than in the vote, and whether the resulting arrangement would still deserve to be called democratic.
