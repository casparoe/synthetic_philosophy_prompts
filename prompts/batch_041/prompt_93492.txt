I teach a unit on definitions and borderline cases in an interdisciplinary course on the philosophy of biology, and for the final session I've decided to hand my students a satirical text rather than a piece of secondary literature, so that they have to diagnose the confusion for themselves instead of being told about it. What I want from you is the text itself: a deadpan internal handbook — eight hundred words or so, formatted as numbered sections — issued by the (invented) Bureau of Biological Classification, the agency charged with maintaining an official register of what counts as living. The handbook instructs field registrars on how to determine whether a given entity should receive a Certificate of Life. It should read like genuine bureaucratic prose: confident, procedural, entirely unembarrassed, and never once winking at the reader. The comedy has to come from the substance rather than from jokes laid on top of it.

The definition the Bureau operates under has been amended eleven times, and I'd like the amendment history to do the philosophical work. Each revision should have been introduced either to capture a case the previous wording excluded or to expel a case it wrongly admitted, and each should open a fresh difficulty that the following revision closes — so that the reader watches the line being redrawn around viruses, prions, sterile hybrids such as the mule, frozen tardigrades, mitochondria, a laboratory cell line descended from a long-dead donor, self-replicating synthetic RNA, and a self-replicating computer program, with at least one earlier draft having admitted fire. Let the committee be split between a faction that treats self-maintaining metabolism as the mark of life and a faction that treats the capacity for evolution by natural selection as the mark, with a third faction quietly arguing that the register is a filing convenience rather than a discovery about nature; let the handbook dispose of this by asserting, in a footnote, that the definition is descriptive in intent and stipulative in effect, which the registrar is instructed not to worry about. Somewhere in there the origins problem should surface as a procedural question: at what point in a continuous chemical process the registrar is to record that the first registered entity came into existence, and whether the certificate attaches to an individual or to a lineage.

Please also include an appendix of frequently determined cases with one-line rulings, a short appeals decision in which a petitioner who cannot reproduce is nonetheless granted a certificate on the ground that he belongs to a kind that usually can, and a dissenting footnote from one committee member (invent a name) who has begun to suspect that no single criterion will do. Don't let the satire curdle into a sneer: a philosopher of biology ought to read it and recognize the real disputes — metabolism-first versus replication-first, the sterility objection, the problem of biological individuality, the difference between a natural kind and a stipulation, the sorites of abiogenesis — while a reader who has never opened a biology textbook should still be able to follow it. Keep the humor deadpan and legible. Any citations or document references should point to the Bureau's own invented internal papers rather than to real works.
