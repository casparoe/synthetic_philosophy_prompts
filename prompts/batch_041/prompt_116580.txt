I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on the two-envelope paradox, and I would like the handout to be a decision tree rather than a digest of the literature. Assume the reader has met the standard setup exactly once: two envelopes, one holds twice what the other holds, you choose one, open it, and see an amount; the argument that you should now swap, since the other envelope holds either twice or half your amount with equal probability and so has an expected value of 1.25 times what you hold; and the objection that the same argument would have told you to swap before you opened anything. Half the room finds the argument plainly valid and half finds it plainly question-begging, and I want the tree to sort them by what they say about particular cases rather than by the diagnosis they would profess if asked outright. Accordingly, no node may be phrased as a question about a named position, and no node may be settled by announcing a general methodological allegiance of the sort one might have before hearing any of the cases. The reader has no access to the literature and cannot look anything up, so nothing on the handout may depend on a citation, an author's name, or a result the reader is expected to have memorized. Where a leaf corresponds to a position that has a standard name, give the name; where it does not, mark the leaf unnamed and characterize the position in a sentence.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located within that space, with seven to nine leaves. Each branch node should be a question about a concrete variant of the setup, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing where it leads. I would like the following variants to appear somewhere in the tree, because they are the ones that split the room:

(a) the plain version, in which you are told only that one envelope holds twice the other, you see an amount, and you know nothing about how the envelopes were filled;
(b) a version in which a fair coin first selects between the pair (10, 20) and the pair (20, 40), after which you are handed one of the two envelopes at random; you open it and find 20;
(c) a version in which the smaller amount is drawn from a proper distribution over the positive integers that is disclosed to you, so that seeing a very large amount makes it likely that you are holding the larger envelope;
(d) a version in which the amounts are 2^n and 2^(n+1) for an integer n that is, in the intended informal sense, equally likely to be any integer at all, so that whatever amount you see, the other envelope is equally likely to be double or half;
(e) a version in which the amounts are capped at a known maximum, so that seeing the maximum settles which envelope you are holding;
(f) a version in which you are offered the swap before opening anything at all.

Two distinctions matter especially for my purposes, and I want the tree to be able to place a reader on either side of each: first, whether the reader locates the trouble in the probability assignment or in the rule for choosing under uncertainty; second, whether the reader thinks the correct verdict depends on the amount seen.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or unnamed), one sentence stating where it says the error lies, and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — in particular, what it must say about the variants in which swapping is plainly correct, or in which the equal-probability step is plainly sound. Some combinations of answers will correspond to no position anyone holds; note them, and say briefly whether they are empty because the view would be incoherent, because it would be unattractive, or because no one has bothered to explore it. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, rather than papering over the gap with a distinction that does no work.
