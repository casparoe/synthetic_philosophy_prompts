I'm midway through a political philosophy seminar on the concept of liberty, and our instructor has set us an exercise that I'd like to work through properly before the next session. Instead of asking us to argue that one conception of liberty is the right one, she has circulated six short cases and asked us to score each of them three times over: once under a negative conception (freedom as the absence of interference), once under a positive conception (freedom as self-mastery, or as the effective pursuit of a life one has reason to endorse), and once under a republican conception (freedom as non-domination, understood as the absence of another agent's arbitrary power over one, whether or not that power is ever actually exercised). Each score is to be a number between 0 and 1, where 0 means the person is not free at all in that sense and 1 means they are as free as anyone could be. She then wants us to rank the six cases by how far the three scores diverge from one another, taking divergence to be the gap between the highest and lowest of the three numbers, and to explain what drives the divergence in each case.

The cases are these. (1) A resident of a company town who is legally free to leave, but whose employer owns her housing, runs the only store, and effectively appoints the local officials. (2) A person whose preferences were shaped from childhood by relentless advertising, so that she wholeheartedly endorses and pursues a life of consumption she would not have chosen had the shaping never occurred. (3) A debtor who is legally free and unmolested, but who knows that his creditor could ruin him at any moment and refrains only out of whim. (4) A subject of an absolute monarch who is entirely benevolent, never interferes with anyone, and grants every request put to him. (5) A citizen bound by a rule she herself helped write, which she can amend only through a process she does not control and cannot exit. (6) A monk who has freely taken a vow of obedience to her abbot, may leave the order at any time, and sincerely wants to obey. For each case, please give me the three scores in a compact table, then the ranking by divergence, then a short paragraph explaining where the three traditions part company and which of my scores a defender of each tradition would most want to contest. I would also like you to flag any case in which you think scoring the three conceptions separately is itself a mistake -- where one of the conceptions would deny that the question so posed even makes sense -- because that is the part of the exercise I am least confident I understand.
