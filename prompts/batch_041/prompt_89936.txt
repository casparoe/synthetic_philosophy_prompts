I teach a seminar on early Christian moral thought that ends with a unit on Augustine's two treatises on lying, and every year the same thing happens: my students, most of whom are headed into law, compliance, or public administration, try to convert his position into an office policy, and every year the exercise collapses the same way. The absolute prohibition turns out to need a vast apparatus of distinctions — concealment versus misdirection, the double heart, the ranking of lies by gravity, the exemption for jokes and figures of speech — simply to remain livable, and the students end up producing either a rule no institution could survive or a rule that has quietly gone consequentialist. This year I want to hand them a finished specimen of the genre instead of a sketch, so I am asking you to write, in the flat register of an internal corporate handbook and with no signal anywhere that the document is a joke, the Communication Integrity Policy of a mid-sized firm whose ethics office has adopted Augustine's position wholesale. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,100 words, formatted as a numbered policy document with a brief covering note from the Director of Communication Integrity, and, if it fits naturally, an appendix laying out the schedule of violations by tier. The humor must come entirely from the collision between the doctrine's absolutism and the bureaucratic form that has to administer it — the drafting should stay so deadpan that a reader could plausibly mistake the document for a real policy, and a specialist in the period should recognize that the author knows exactly what is being satirized.

Several features of the position need to be in the document, and they should be load-bearing rather than decorative. The definitional core comes first: a lie is not a false statement but a mismatch between what the speaker holds in mind and what he utters, together with the intention of deceiving, which means that good-faith error is outside the policy's scope while a true statement uttered by someone who believes it false is a violation — a wrinkle the handbook should handle with visible administrative discomfort. Then the tiered schedule, running from the lie about religious teaching at the top down to the lie that harms no one and is told to protect someone's chastity at the bottom: every tier is a violation, none is ever permitted, but the tier determines the sanction, and the policy should say so without irony. The concealment exemption matters too — employees may decline to answer, change the subject, or state that they will not tell, but may not misdirect — along with the exclusion of jokes, hyperbole, and figurative speech, on the grounds that these carry no intent to deceive. The absolutism must bite: no exception to spare a colleague's job, no exception to avert a serious harm, and a specific refusal of the undercover operation the security department keeps requesting, in which staff would pose as members of an outside group in order to expose wrongdoing; the policy should decline this on the principle that a lie cannot be used to prosecute lies, and should acknowledge that the refusal will cost the firm and that the cost is accepted. Add a clause on employees compelled to speak falsely under legal or professional pressure, who are expected to accept the consequences of refusing, and, if you can manage it, a case note on the awkward precedent of a respected figure in the tradition who appears to have staged a public rebuke of a colleague for the audience's benefit, which the tradition has sometimes excused and sometimes condemned — handle it in a way that does not depend on the details being right. The document may begin with this epigraph, from Augustine's Against Lying: "How then by a lie shall I rightly be able to prosecute lies?"

Beyond that, three constraints. Do not put any quotation, real or invented, into the mouth of Augustine or any other historical person, and do not include citations or reading lists; the policy can refer to "the tradition" or "the received teaching" without naming sources. Do not let the policy drift into a position it would not hold — it may not license a helpful lie under pressure, may not weigh consequences against the rule, and may not resolve the tier system into a permission. And keep it legible to a reader who knows nothing about the period, while making sure that someone who does will find the satire fair: the target is the casuistical impulse that an absolute prohibition generates, not the prohibition itself, and by the end the reader should be genuinely unsure whether the firm's policy is monstrous or admirable.
