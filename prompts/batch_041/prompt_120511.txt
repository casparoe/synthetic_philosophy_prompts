I am a retired physicist — statistical mechanics, forty years of it — who has spent the last four years teaching himself philosophy of mind, and in three weeks I am giving a seminar paper to an audience I find rather intimidating: philosophers of mind who take intractability results to establish that ideal rationality is unavailable to finite agents, cognitive scientists who build models in which the mind optimally allocates scarce computation, and one complexity theorist who will be the sharpest person in the room. My thesis is that the appeal to computational intractability in this literature carries no weight. The theorems are asymptotic, worst-case claims about problem families; an organism only ever confronts particular instances, drawn from a distribution that its own behaviour partly generates, and nothing in those results licenses the step from 'this problem is NP-hard' to 'this mind must be heuristic rather than optimal.' Please prepare me for the question period: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, phrased in their own vocabulary, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is softest; what I should concede at the outset rather than defend; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I will add the references myself, so there is no need for citations.
