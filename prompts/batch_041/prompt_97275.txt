A friend of mine is a historian who works on why big things fall apart — empires, currencies, regimes — and she has a really particular, kind of homemade way of doling out causal blame that I've never seen written up anywhere. I want to know what she'd say about a case I just made up, and honestly I mostly want to reverse-engineer her rule.

Her schtick: she refuses to ask "what caused X" flat. She says you have to fix a contrast — why did it fall rather than just weaken, or rather than stay standing — and then she sorts the factors into what she calls the backdrop and the trigger. Only the trigger gets to be "the cause"; everything else is backdrop, no matter how big it looks. Exception: if two factors both pass what she calls her swing test, she'll name both, grudgingly.

Her verdicts so far. In every one of these, each factor on its own would have been enough — she knows that and says so, so shes clearly not running the "would it have happened without it" test:

1. A city falls. A faction inside opens the gates; separately, the fever season that comes every summer had already thinned the garrison to the point it wouldve fallen anyway. She says the betrayal caused the fall. The fever is backdrop.

2. A currency collapses. The harvest was a bit below average, the way it is most years; the central bank also made a deliberate, unprecedented call to stop defending the peg. Either one would've done it. She says the bank's decision caused the collapse, the harvest is backdrop.

3. A famine. A drought the region gets most years; a brand new export policy nobody had tried before. Both sufficient. She names the policy.

4. A revolution. A bad winter, ordinary for that place; a general deciding to fire on a protest, which nobody had done before. Both sufficient. She names the general's decision.

5. A coup. Two factions each independently had the votes to bring the government down, and both moved. She names both.

New case, tell me what she says. An empire falls. A once-in-a-millennium earthquake flattens the imperial grain depots. And in the same week, a provincial governor signs the same routine grain requisition order he signs every single harvest without thinking about it — which, on its own, would also have emptied the depots. He wasnt scheming, he was on autopilot. The earthquake obviously wasn't a choice at all. Both are enough; the depots end up empty either way.

So what's the trigger and what's the backdrop here? I care way less about the answer than about the rule. Give me the two or three rules that fit her five verdicts equally well, say what each one predicts for the empire case, tell me which you'd put money on and why, and name one further case — something you make up — that would actually separate those rules.
