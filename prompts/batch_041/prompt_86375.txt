I teach an upper-level seminar on social choice and democratic theory, and I have just finished a unit on Arrow's impossibility theorem — the conditions, the proof strategy, the standard interpretations — alongside Condorcet's jury theorem and the recent literature on judgment aggregation. To close the unit I screened Sidney Lumet's Twelve Angry Men, and I now have a pedagogical problem I would like your help with. My students' consensus is that the film refutes Arrow: here, they say, is a body of twelve people with clashing convictions who arrive, by talking, at a unanimous and (they insist) correct verdict — no dictatorship, no cycling, no agenda manipulation. I think the inference is too quick, but I do not want merely to tell them so. I would like you to act as a philosopher of social choice who also reads films closely, and not as a reviewer, and to think carefully about the film and the formal results together before you commit to a reading.

Please begin by brainstorming what a mapping from the film onto a social-choice framework would even look like: who or what plays the role of the individuals, the alternatives, the preference or judgment profile, the aggregation rule, and the agenda. Then note the complication that the jurors do not rank alternatives; they make judgments about sub-questions — whether the switchblade was as unique as it was taken to be, whether the disabled witness below could have reached his door in the time he claimed, whether the woman across the elevated tracks could have seen anything at all through the windows of a passing train, whether the downward angle of the wound is consistent with the assailant the prosecution describes — and the verdict is supposed to follow from those judgments by inference. So: does the film ever stage the discursive dilemma, in which a majority of the jurors can accept each premise while a majority rejects the conclusion that follows from them? Does the unanimity requirement mean that each juror holds a veto over the outcome, and what does that do to Arrow's non-dictatorship condition — is a unanimity rule a dictatorship of each over the status quo, or a case the framework cannot represent? And does the film's dramatic logic amount to a defense of independence of irrelevant alternatives as a norm of deliberation, given that the jurors who must be argued out of their positions are the ones whose verdicts rest on considerations that have nothing to do with the evidence — the man relitigating his own estrangement from his son, the man whose prejudice is doing the work, the man who wants to catch the ballgame? I want to know whether the film's insistence that such considerations be excluded dramatizes the condition or quietly evades it.

Then the part I care most about: where the reading strains and where the film resists it. The film's resolution requires the conversion of the last holdout, so it never depicts a stable, principled minority; the jurors' judgments are not independent inputs but are revised under the pressure of reasons and of one another, which is exactly what Condorcet's theorem assumes away and what Arrow's fixed profiles cannot model; and the film's warm ending may be doing work that the argument is supposed to do. Tell me honestly whether the film illustrates Arrow, refutes him, or changes the subject from aggregating preferences to pooling evidence, and where the honest critic has to concede that the film will not bear the reading I am proposing. Close by saying what the film contributes to this debate that the formal literature cannot, and give me two or three discussion questions I can put to the seminar.
