Since retiring after thirty-odd years in geochemistry I have been teaching myself philosophy, and I have gotten stuck on the cluster of questions about what one is to do with evidence about one's own evidence: the sort of thing that arises when a competent colleague disagrees with you, or when you learn something about how reliable your own reasoning happens to be. A philosopher I have been corresponding with, Professor Lena Marsh, has kindly walked me through a series of puzzle cases, and I have been trying to reverse-engineer the rule she is actually running, because I suspect it is not the rule she believes she is running. Her verdicts, as faithfully as I can report them, are these. First, two equally competent geochemists working from the same data, with no reason for either to doubt the other's competence or honesty, reach opposite conclusions about whether a sample was contaminated; she holds that each is required to move most of the way toward the other's credence. Second, the same pair, except that one of them has just realized she did the work after thirty-six hours awake; she holds that the sleep-deprived one must abandon her conclusion, while the rested one may keep hers essentially untouched. Third, a colleague reads a small and methodologically shaky study suggesting that people who reason about contamination in the way she has just reasoned are somewhat more often wrong than they realize, with nothing in it bearing on her particular sample or her particular chain of inference; Professor Marsh holds that she may keep her belief. Fourth, an agent who, after a disagreement like the first case, reports that she still believes the sample was contaminated but accepts that her evidence no longer supports that belief; Professor Marsh holds this to be permissible, remarking that belief and credence answer to different standards. I gather that last one is what people here call epistemic akrasia, and I confess I cannot tell whether she is licensing it or merely redescribing it.

The case I would like a verdict on is this. An agent reaches a conclusion about a sample by an ordinary and competent route; her first-order evidence is decent but not overwhelming. She then learns of a large, carefully conducted study, the sort of thing she has no particular reason to doubt, showing that people who reason exactly as she has just reasoned, under exactly those conditions, get it wrong about eighty per cent of the time. Nothing in the study concerns her particular sample, her particular inference, or any particular colleague's disagreement; it is wholly about the class of reasoners she belongs to. I would like you to predict what Professor Marsh will say about her. May she keep her belief, must she substantially lower her credence, both, neither? But I am after more than the prediction. I want the rule she appears to be running, the other rules that fit her four verdicts just as well, what each of those candidate rules predicts about this new case, your best guess at which one she in fact holds and your reasons for preferring it, and the further case that would best separate the survivors from one another. I find the third verdict and my new case hard to distinguish, and I would like to know whether the difference I am sensing is real or something I have invented.

Please do not simply hand me a tidy reconciliation. I am an outsider to this field and it is entirely possible that I have misdescribed her positions, or that the four verdicts I have reported cannot all be sustained by any single rule, or that the new case is incoherent as I have set it up. If any of that is so, say so plainly rather than patching over it. Where you are genuinely uncertain which rule she is running, tell me so, and give me some indication of how confident you are and what further consideration would change your mind.
