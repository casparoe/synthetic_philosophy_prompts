I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar that sits between philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I want to pair a literary text with the literature on concept learning from sparse examples. The text is Borges's "Funes the Memorious," the story of Ireneo Funes, who after being thrown from a horse and left paralyzed acquires a memory so retentive that he can reconstruct an entire day in a day, who picks up Latin, French, Portuguese, and English from a dictionary and a grammar, and who is said by the narrator to be incapable of general, Platonic ideas — the narrator's own diagnosis being that thinking requires forgetting differences, generalizing, abstracting. The philosophical apparatus I want to bring alongside it is the tradition of Bongard problems: puzzles in which twelve figures are divided into two sets of six and the solver must identify the rule that separates them, where any number of rules will fit the visible data and the real task is to guess which concept the designer intended. Mikhail Bongard introduced these in an appendix to his 1967 book on pattern recognition, and Hofstadter later made them a touchstone for thinking about analogy, fluid concepts, and the kind of intelligence that cannot be captured by storing more data. My students enjoy solving them but cannot articulate why solving them is hard, which seems to me exactly the philosophical question worth asking.

What I would like from you is a reading of Borges's story through this lens, at a level of detail I could teach for ninety minutes. I want to know whether Funes is best understood as a limit case of a Bongard solver — a being with unlimited access to the instances but no capacity to entertain the intended rule — or whether that analogy distorts the story, since Funes's affliction is a surplus of particularity rather than a shortage of examples, and since Bongard problems are, after all, solved by ordinary people from only twelve glimpses. Where does the story dramatize something the puzzle literature leaves implicit, for instance the role of a shared sense of relevance, or of a community of solvers who can confirm that one has found the designer's concept rather than a merely extensionally adequate one? Where does the story resist the reading? Does the narrator's thesis that thought is abstraction actually follow from what happens to Funes, or is it a claim the story quietly ironizes, given how much the narrator himself enjoys the spectacle of a mind he declares useless? And what does the story suggest about whether concept formation requires something like a capacity for seeing-as that cannot be reduced to storing and comparing more instances?

Finally, I would like you to say where the pairing breaks down as pedagogy. The Bongard literature treats concept induction as a problem solved against a background of shared human salience, and the story treats it as a catastrophe of too much salience; I am not sure those are two versions of one problem or two different problems wearing the same name. Please write in American English, and feel free to close by saying what the story contributes to this debate that the puzzle literature does not.
