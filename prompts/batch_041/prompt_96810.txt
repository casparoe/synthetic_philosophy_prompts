I'm a journalist — I write about philosophy and ideas for a general audience — and I've spent alot of the last few months sitting in on a workshop series about material constitution, the statue-and-the-clay stuff, for a piece on why some philosophical debates never end. I've got my quotes and I don't need the who-said-what; what I need is a diagnosis.

Here's the state of play as I understand it. You've got a lump of clay; a sculptor shapes it into a statue. Either the statue and the clay are one and the same thing, and the apparent differences are an artifact of describing it under different sortals, or of what's essential to it as opposed to what's true of it for a while. Or there are two things in exactly the same place at the same time, one of which can survive being squashed and one of which can't. Or there are no statues at all, just particles arranged statue-wise, and "the statue" is a useful fiction. And then there's a fourth camp who say that "is the statue the same thing as the lump?" and "how many objects are on the pedestal?" are malformed questions until you specify a sortal — that identity and counting are sortal-relative, and there's no further fact about how many objects are there.

Nobody in the room has moved in years. The Leibniz's law argument — statue and lump differ in what they can survive, so they aren't one thing — gets answered with "that difference is just an artifact of how we're describing it," and that answer gets answered with "then describe it without the description," and around we go. Honestly I could write the whole piece off the transcript.

But heres the thing that's actually eating at me, and it's a case someone dropped in week six. A kid leaves a lump of clay on a radiator, it slumps, and the slump happens to be a dead ringer for a famous sculpture. A passerby photographs it, a gallery puts the photo in a show, and now everyone calls it a sculpture. The clay gets squashed later. Was there ever a statue? If yes, when did it start — when the clay took that shape, or when someone treated it as a work? If the shape alone did it, statues seem to pop in and out of existence whenever clay happens to look like something, which nobody in the room wanted to accept. If the treating did it, then being a statue is conferred by us, and the whole debate starts to look like it's about social kinds rather than about matter and form. If neither, the gallery's got nothing on the wall.

My hypothesis — and I'd much rather be told I'm wrong than have this smoothed over — is that the accidental-statue case shows the real disagreement was never about the clay and the statue. It's about whether "statue" is a sortal that tracks something in the world or a label we apply; and if it's the second, then the coincidentalists and the one-object people are fighting about the count of things under a description we chose, which would make the dispute verbal in a way that's interesting rather than trivial. The other possibility is that I've smuggled in a premise about artifacts needing makers and the case is a red herring.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is the case doing any work, or does each camp just say what it always says — and if that's the answer, is the answer that this is a clash of starting intuitions no argument can reach? Is there a hidden question doing the work, empirical or formal? And what would actually count as progress here — some distinction, some case, some proof — or should I write that no progress is coming and explain why? If my framing is confused, say so plainly and tell me where. Don't just map the positions for me; I've got that. I need the diagnosis.
