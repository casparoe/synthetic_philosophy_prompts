Something keeps bugging me about how we build these systems. We train a model by scoring its outputs with a reward model built from human ratings, and then we insist the model should be aiming at what the rater meant, not at the score itself. When it finds some trick that scores high but that we didn't want, we call it reward hacking and count it as failure, even though by the training signal's own lights it did great. Related: we bolt an off switch onto the system and expect it to let us press it, while also training it to pursue whatever goal we handed it. And we write a hidden system prompt telling it to follow the user's intent but to defer to us when the two conflict.

What does this bundle presuppose philosophically? I care about three things. (1) What it assumes about the link between what a system wants and what it does — can you train in the disposition to be switched off without touching the models objective, or does that quietly presuppose a clean belief/desire split? (2) What it assumes about intent: it treats 'what the rater meant' as a fact the model could in principle get right even when the raters are a disagreeing crowd, so what theory of interpretation is being smuggled in, and does it survive the crowd? (3) What it assumes about authority — when user and operator conflict, who's the principal and why, and does the practice actually need an answer there or can it stay silent?

Then say which of these the practice strictly needs versus which it merely fits well with, whether the parts pull against each other, and what would break if we dropped the score-isn't-the-goal bit and just optimized the score honestly.
