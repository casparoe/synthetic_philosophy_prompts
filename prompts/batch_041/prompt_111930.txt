I've been taking a course on classical Indian epistemology, and something in the Nyāya material has been nagging at me, so I'd like you to help me think it through carefully. Please take the role of a philosopher who works across both contemporary analytic epistemology and the Nyāya tradition, and who is especially good at reconstructing the implicit commitments of an everyday practice. Here is the practice I keep returning to: the way we actually treat expert consensus. When a layperson -- say, me -- concludes that a vaccine is safe or that the climate is warming, they almost never inspect the studies; they take the word of people they have never met and cannot check, and they treat that as a genuine reason to believe. The same thing happens inside science itself: a researcher builds on results she has not reproduced, and a citation in a footnote is treated as doing real epistemic work rather than as a promissory note. Yet the very culture that runs on this tells students to do their own research and to question authority. I want to know what view of knowledge this whole pattern presupposes, and the Nyāya school seems to have a distinctive answer that the modern Western tradition mostly does not. Nyāya counts testimony (śabda) as a basic source of knowledge -- a pramāṇa in its own right, alongside perception, inference, and comparison -- and it holds that one can know something simply by understanding the assertion of a reliable speaker, without inferring anything about that speaker's track record; Hume and Locke, by contrast, treat testimony as at best an inference from past reliability. So which view does the practice of deferring to expert consensus actually presuppose, and does the practice need the Nyāya view, or can a reductionist story be made to work for it?

Reconstruct the implicit epistemology as carefully as you can, using Nyāya's own machinery: what makes testimony a source of knowledge rather than a mere conduit for someone else's knowledge, what conditions it places on the speaker and on the hearer, and why it insists on keeping testimony separate instead of folding it into inference. Then distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it equally. Finally, suppose we replaced the presupposed view with the reductionist one -- testimony as inference from reliability -- and say what would have to change about how we teach, how we cite, and how we defer, and whether the practice would survive the replacement at all.
