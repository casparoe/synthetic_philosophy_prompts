I report on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a feature about what our best physics actually claims about reality. The piece keeps stalling on the same exchange: someone says our theories get the unobservable world roughly right, someone else points to the graveyard of past theoretical posits, and the conversation ends in hand-waving. I want your honest number, not the polished version people give on panels.

Here is the proposition I'd like a credence for, stated so that it has a truth value: in the year 2125, working physicists will regard quantum fields and curved spacetime as approximately correct descriptions of an unobservable reality, rather than as calculational scaffolding that the then-current fundamental theory has learned to do without. Take "approximately correct" to mean the successor theory treats these as limiting cases or coarse-grained approximations of its own ontology, not as useful fictions.

Give me your credence in that proposition, then decompose it. I want the sub-questions it rests on, each with a rough probability and one line of justification, and I want to see how they combine into the final number. At minimum, address: the base rate of survival for central unobservable posits across past revolutions in physics; whether the historical record is a fair sample or is distorted by how we remember and count theories; whether novel predictive success gives positive evidence of approximately-true ontology, and how strong; whether the realist can specify in advance, rather than after the fact, which parts of a theory are the ones worth believing; and whether the sheer possibility of unconceived rival theories makes the whole exercise ill-posed.

Two wrinkles from my own reporting, which I'd like you to fold in rather than ignore. First, the base-rate argument seems to depend entirely on how you individuate theories, so I've adopted a deliberately austere counting rule for my own notes: count a theory as replaced only when the successor assigns a genuinely different ontology to the same domain, not when it merely extends or refines its predecessor. Tell me whether your number is robust to that convention, and what it becomes under the opposite one. Second, a philosopher I've been interviewing floated a criterion for belief in a posit — believe it only if it is indispensable to the theory's predictive success and stable across independent reformulations of that theory — and claimed quantum fields fail it. Apply the criterion as stated and tell me what, if anything, it changes.

Please structure the response with these named sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

- "The proposition, pinned down" (~100 words): restate it precisely and say what would count as settling it either way.
- "Headline number" (~50 words): your credence, stated plainly.
- "The decomposition" (~450 words): each sub-question with its probability, one line of justification, and how they combine.
- "Where the number is load-bearing" (~150 words): the sub-question your final number is most sensitive to.
- "What would move it" (~150 words): the evidence or argument that would shift your credence most, and in which direction.
