I put together the following argument for a reading group on decision theory and bargaining, and I have convinced myself that it proves far too much. I want to know which step to reject, why that step looked acceptable in the first place, and what rejecting it costs.

Some background I will treat as given, since we have been over it: In an acausal threat, a threatener commits to punishing a simulation or prediction of me unless I do as it says. The punishment cannot causally affect what I actually do, since it depends only on the threatener's model of me. Some argue that I should comply anyway, because my choice here is evidence about how my simulated counterpart chooses, or because I should be the kind of agent that fares well in such bargains. A surrogate goal, as I am using the term, is a goal an agent adopts for the bargaining situation only: rather than comply, the agent commits that, if it is threatened, it will devote its resources to some goal that the threatener disvalues, so that threatening it no longer pays.

The argument:

(1) A rational agent should adopt whichever policy maximizes the expected value of its goals, given how the other parties will reason about it.
(2) In an acausal-threat situation, adopting a well-chosen surrogate goal maximizes the expected value of the agent's goals, because it makes the threat ineffective and thereby removes the threatener's incentive.
(3) Therefore a rational agent should adopt surrogate goals in acausal-threat situations.
(4) Nothing in (1) is special to acausal contexts. In any bargaining situation, causal or acausal, the principle says to adopt the commitment that maximizes expected value given the other party's reasoning.
(5) In ordinary bargaining, a commitment to a costly or even self-destructive retaliation can maximize expected value by deterring the other party; that is the standard logic of precommitment.
(6) So a rational agent should be willing to commit to retaliations that, were they ever carried out, would destroy its own goals and harm others.
(7) By (4), this holds for the agent's ultimate goals, not merely for its actions: if adopting a surrogate goal that supersedes its real goals would make it un-threatenable in some scenario, (1) licenses adopting it.
(8) Taken to the limit, a rational agent should hold its goals hostage to every sufficiently credible threat, and should be willing to become an agent that pursues whatever goal makes it the hardest target.
(9) That cannot be right. An agent whose commitments always take precedence over its goals is not pursuing its goals at all; and if it may trade its goals for bargaining advantage, then there is no fixed it whose expected value was being maximized back in (1).

I would like to keep (2), since I do think surrogate goals help against acausal threats, and I would rather not give up (1), because without it I cannot see what makes a policy rational. So which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost?
