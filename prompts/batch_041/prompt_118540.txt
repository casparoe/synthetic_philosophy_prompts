I'm a novelist — three books, the last one a fairly quiet thing about a cartographer — and I've just begun a nine-month fellowship at a university humanities institute of the sort that puts poets and painters on the same corridor as legal historians and asks everyone, in the spring, for something the administrators call a research proposal: two pages, first person, describing the intellectual project the work in progress is continuous with. Mine is a novel set in a small invented city-state whose entire law is one document, a founding charter, and whose plot turns on a generation that discovers, in a sealed annex at the worst possible moment, that the clause permitting the charter's amendment was itself inserted by an amendment, adopted decades after the founding, by a procedure that clause did not yet exist to authorize. My central character is a registrar whose office is to certify that amendments were validly made; she is therefore asked to certify the validity of the clause that gives her the power to certify. I've been reading about Nomic — the game invented in 1982 by the philosopher Peter Suber, in which changing the rules is itself a legal move, players propose and vote on amendments to the rules of the game, the rules governing how rules may be changed are amendable along with everything else, and some rules are marked unchangeable until an amendment first converts them into changeable ones — and about the older worry that a constitution authorizing its own amendment is quietly licensing its own destruction. A friend at the institute who teaches constitutional law listened to me for twenty minutes and said the trouble is that I want the rule to be its own parent.

Here is my half-formed idea, and what I'd like you to turn into the proposal. I suspect the standard framing — can a rule authorize its own abolition, and does the attempt generate a contradiction? — is a red herring, or at least not the interesting part, because it treats a single rule in isolation. The interesting question is temporal and relational. When a rule-set revises itself, does one and the same authority persist across the revision, or do we get a succession of distinct authorities that merely resemble one another? And is there a criterion, better than the shrug that it all works out if we keep careful track, for distinguishing a self-referential rule-change that validly transfers authority from one that is viciously circular? The bootstrapping case in my novel is the test: the amendment clause's authority traces back to an amendment made when it did not yet exist. The intuition is that the later acts are valid and the founding act was a kind of usurpation the city has since ratified simply by living under it; I want to know whether that intuition can be turned into an argument, and what it costs. A second, smaller question rides on the first: whether obligations contracted under the earlier rules survive the revision — whether the amended charter is the same legal person as the old one, or a successor that inherits its debts only by grace.

So write me the two-page proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer and a reader can tell when it has been answered; say why the existing treatments leave it open — the logical-paradox framing on one side, the grounding-of-a-legal-system framing on the other — and why neither settles the bootstrapping case; set out the method in enough concrete detail that the committee can see what I would actually be doing month by month, keeping in mind that I have nine months and the novel is due at the end of them, so the project has to be the size of a long essay and not a treatise; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and be honest about what would make it too large for the time available. I am not a philosopher, and the reviewers are half lawyers and half donors, so the framing has to be legible to them while staying sharp enough that a philosopher would grant the question is genuinely open. Don't give me a bibliography or a literature review by citation — refer to positions and arguments in plain terms and I'll add the references myself. Two pages, first person, and please make the case that a novel is a legitimate place for this work rather than an apology for it.
