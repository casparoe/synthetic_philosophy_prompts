Third year in a classics program, and our source-criticism reading group has basically ground to a halt on something I genuinly can't tell is a real disagreement or a category mistake, and it's currently eating my thesis chapter, so I'd like an outside diagnosis.

Here's the shape of it. We keep hitting the same wall with texts that are late, tendentious, and demonstrably wrong about the big things but oddly specific about small ones — a chronicle written a century after the fact that gets a king's line of descent backwards but somehow knows how many days the siege lasted and which gate the grain came through. Every period has one of these; call it the tendentious-but-detailed source.

Camp A, the older faculty: if an author is caught inventing or distorting where we can check, you can't then go cherry-picking the bits you happen to like, because whatever pressure produced the distortions probably shaped the incidental stuff too. On this view reliability is a property of a source, not of a sentence. Camp B, basically all the grad students plus the visiting scholar: no, each claim carries its own evidence, and some details are exactly the kind of thing you wouldn't bother to invent, or are against the author's interest, so they stay usable even out of a liar. On this view reliability is a property of a claim, not of a source. And then there's the visiting scholar's line, which is what really split the room: he said we should stop asking whether the text is true at all and treat it purely as a trace of the world that produced it — the distortions are the data, not the noise. Half the room thought this dissolved the whole dispute; the other half thought it quietly changed the subject and gave up on history.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to either sharpen or kill: this isn't a disagreement about the past at all. It's a disagreement about what the object of 'reliability' is — sources versus claims — smuggled in under one word, plus a buried disagreement about what we're actually trying to reconstruct, the events or the beliefs and institutions of the people who recorded them. If that's right, the two sides are answering different questions under a single name and there's no fact of the matter waiting to settle it.

So diagnose it. Is this a verbal dispute, or is there a substantive methodological arguement underneath — for instance a hidden empirical claim about how ancient invention actually works that nobody in the room has stated, let alone tested? Do the two camps differ about facts, about the burden of proof, about what counts as a datum, or about starting intuitions neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result (would a Bayesian treatment of correlated rather than independent errors just settle this one way or the other?), a change of question, or nothing at all? I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it, not a reading list.
