I recently began teaching a first-year course in formal reasoning, and grading has made a philosophical problem feel urgent rather than academic. Some students have clearly grasped the rules we are working with; others are extrapolating from the examples they have seen and will diverge as soon as the examples run out. I am confident about the difference, and I cannot say what it consists in. I have drafted notes toward an essay on this and would like your comments, especially on whether my reconstruction of the problem is accurate and whether my proposed solution earns its keep.

The notes are these.

1. As I understand the problem as Kripke presents it, the sceptic asks what fact about me now makes it true that I mean addition by a certain sign, rather than an operation that agrees with addition on every case I have ever considered and then diverges, returning say 5 for every larger sum. My past answers and my present habits are finite, and every finite pattern is compatible with both readings, so neither can be the fact in question.

2. Kripke's own response, as I understand it, is not to deny that we mean things but to give up on the idea that correctness consists in matching a meaning-fact. Instead, correctness is fixed by the community: an answer is correct if the community accepts it and incorrect if the community would correct it. This is offered as a sceptical solution, one that concedes the sceptic's premises and relocates the notion of correctness rather than vindicating it.

3. My dissatisfaction is with the communal move. A community's responses are just a larger finite pattern; if my finite pattern cannot determine my rule, neither can theirs. I take the reply to be that the community need not be infallible, only the thing we in fact defer to — but that looks like trading a normative question (what I ought to say) for a sociological one (what I will be permitted to say).

4. The part I most want scrutinised is my own proposal, which I call the apprenticeship criterion. What shows that someone has grasped a rule is not their output on fresh cases but their competence within the practice of training: they can be corrected and can take the correction as warranted, they can correct others, they can say why one extrapolation is right and another wrong, and their responses are answerable to reasons rather than merely predictable. On this view, meaning is not a state of a speaker at a moment but a standing capacity exercised in teaching and in being taught. My summary slogan is that a rule-follower is someone who can be apprenticed and can apprentice.

5. The objection I expect is that this is circular: correcting someone presupposes the grasp of a rule that the criterion is supposed to explain. My reply is that the circle is not vicious, because the practice of correction can be described without appealing to meanings, and because the criterion can be stated as a condition on whether the practice reproduces itself across generations of learners.

Please reply under these four headings, in this order, keeping each roughly to the length indicated.

1. Fairness of the reconstruction (about 150 words): Have I stated the sceptical problem and the communal solution accurately, and have I missed a standard reply that would embarrass me?

2. The weakest premise (about 250 words): Which single step is most vulnerable, and what is the strongest objection to it?

3. The apprenticeship criterion (about 300 words): Is this a genuine advance on the communal answer, or a relabelling of it? If it is genuine, what work does it do; if not, say precisely where it collapses.

4. Verdict (about 100 words): Is the essay worth writing, and what is the one change that would most improve it?
