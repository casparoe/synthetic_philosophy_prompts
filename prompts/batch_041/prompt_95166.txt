My reading group has spent the last several months on self-locating belief, and one of the regulars has settled into a mantra he repeats more or less verbatim at every session: 'You should reason as if you were a random observer.' He treats it as close to self-evident, and he makes it do a great deal of work. It is what he offers when he wants to run the Doomsday argument on us: we are, he says, a random draw from the set of all humans who will ever live, so we should expect to find ourselves somewhere in the middle of the story rather than near its end. It is also what he offers on the other side, when I press him with the presumptuous philosopher: he says the slogan blocks the inference that a hypothesis on which the universe contains vastly more people is correspondingly more probable, and with it the armchair cosmology that comes out of that inference. And in our most recent session, on the ethics of creating very large numbers of digital minds, he began saying the slogan tells him something about what he should expect to be -- that he may have to take seriously the thought that he is one of them -- and I could not tell whether that was a new consequence of his principle or a straightforward misuse of it. I suspect the same nine words cannot be doing all three jobs at once, but I cannot say precisely why.

What I want from you is the menu of distinct precise principles that one sentence could be expressing. For each reading, I want to know exactly what is being sampled and from which set -- all observers who actually exist, all observers who possibly exist, observer-moments rather than observers, all humans who will ever live, all observers across all worlds -- and whether the sampling is weighted by the probability that the sampled individual exists at all. I want to know whether the choice of reference class is a substantive commitment or a bookkeeping convention, whether the principle is a genuine constraint on rational credence or a trivial consequence of applying indifference to a partition someone has already chosen, and what the principle may be conditioned on, given that I already know a great deal about which observer I am. Then, for each reading, say whether it supports the Doomsday argument, whether it supports or undermines the presumptuous philosopher, and what if anything it implies about expecting to be a digital mind; a reading that yields no verdict on one of the three should be marked as yielding none, rather than patched up so that it does.

Give me the menu as a JSON object with this shape: a top-level array "readings", each element containing "label" (a short name for the reading), "precise_claim" (the principle stated exactly, in one or two sentences, including what is sampled, from what, and with what weighting), "reference_class_status" (whether the reading forces a particular reference class and whether that choice does real work), "status" (one of "platitude", "substantive and defensible", "substantive but contentious", "false or incoherent"), "supports_doomsday_argument" (yes, no, or only under stated additional assumptions, with a clause saying why), "verdict_on_presumptuous_philosopher" (one clause), "upshot_for_digital_minds" (one clause, or "none" if the reading is silent), and "main_objection" (the strongest objection in one sentence); followed by the top-level fields "reading_most_likely_intended", "reading_actually_defensible", "can_one_reading_do_all_three_jobs" (yes or no, with the reason), and "bottom_line". Please take a side rather than holding the ring: after the menu, commit to which reading you would defend, which one you think my friend actually means, and which of his three uses survives contact with the reading he means -- and say so plainly even if the choice is a close call. Assume I know the standard material; there is no need to explain what the Doomsday argument is, and no need for citations.
