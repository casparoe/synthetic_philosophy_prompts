I want to know whether the DNA–Gödel isomorphism is a substantive structural claim or a piece of intellectual decoration, and I want your credence in it rather than a survey of the literature. To fix the target, consider three systems.

(1) A template replicator: a seed crystal in a saturated solution, or a clay sheet that catalyses further layers in its own image. It propagates structure, but it contains nothing that deserves to be called a description of itself. Copy the arrangement and you have copied everything there is to copy.

(2) An artificial string-rewriting world of the kind sometimes called typogenetics. A strand is a word over a small alphabet; a fixed code translates letters into enzymes, meaning short sequences of operations such as cut, splice, delete and insert; the enzymes then act on strands, including the strand that encoded them. Self-reference here is built in by construction, in the way that a quine, or the fixed point guaranteed by the recursion theorem, is built in.

(3) The cell. DNA is, as a molecule, little more than a string that gets read; the reading is done by an apparatus assembled from the products of that reading; the apparatus is itself encoded in the string it reads; and copies of the string are made by machinery that the string also encodes.

Call P the claim that the relation between (3) and (2) is explanatory rather than decorative: at least one non-obvious claim about (3) follows from the recursion-theoretic structure, or would have been substantially harder to reach without it, and does not follow from the chemistry alone. Treat P as resolved in favour if you can produce such a claim, and as resolved against if every candidate turns out to be false, trivially true of any replicator whatsoever, or already a theorem of biology.

Please give me your credence in P, decomposed. I want a number and one line of justification for each of at least these sub-questions: whether a non-vacuous mapping exists at all, given that any physical system equipped with a decoding convention can be Gödel-numbered; whether the self-reference sits at the right level, since a Gödel sentence borrows its self-reference from an interpretation fixed outside it, whereas the cell's loop is spread across an entire cell rather than localised in the molecule; whether the mapping does predictive or explanatory work, the obvious test case being von Neumann's argument that open-ended evolution requires inherited information to be copied separately from the replicating machinery, made before the structure of DNA was known; and whether the apparent payoff survives the deflationary reading, on which the isomorphism amounts to no more than the observation that both are physical systems with decodable maps.

Then combine the terms into a single figure, tell me which term you are least confident in and which one your final number is most sensitive to, and say what would move it most: a worked example of the isomorphism earning its keep in a biological explanation, an argument that no such example can exist, or something else entirely. I am at roughly 0.3 myself, mostly because I cannot shake the triviality worry; tell me where you think that number is wrong.

End with a final line of the form \answer{0.xx}, containing only your credence in P.
