Next term I am presenting a paper to a mixed colloquium, and what I need from you is not a précis of my argument but a question-and-answer preparation sheet for the discussion that will follow it.

The position I am defending is that creativity and authorship come apart, and that they come apart in opposite directions. Creativity, I argue, is a structural property of a process: a search that is at once novel and apt relative to the norms of a domain, and nothing in that description requires a conscious mind, so a generative model can be creative in the only sense of the word that does any explanatory work. Authorship is a different matter. It is the relation of being answerable for a work — of being the one who can be asked to justify it and to whom credit and blame accrue — and it requires an agent. On my account the model is never the author; the person who prompts may be; and the widespread habit of settling disputes about rights, praise, and liability by asking whether the machine is creative is a category error, because creativity was never the property those disputes needed.

I defend this with two proposed criteria. An output is creative, I claim, when a competent practitioner in the domain, shown only the output and the domain's norms, would treat it as a contribution — call this the counterfactual-practice test. A party is an author, I claim, when it can be asked for reasons and can answer in its own voice — call this the answerability test. The paper argues that generative models pass the first and fail the second, and that this is not a paradox but exactly what we should expect.

The audience is the difficulty. It will be four groups with almost nothing in common. First, analytic aestheticians who work on definitions of art and on the creative process; they will want to know whether novel-and-apt is sufficient, and they have a stock of counterexamples involving accidental novelty. Second, practising artists and an art-school tutor, who treat creativity as bound up with lived experience, intention, and the studio, and who will hear my view as an attempt to award a machine something that was earned by people. Third, a copyright and AI lawyer, for whom authorship is a legal construct rather than a metaphysical one, and who will object that my tidy separation collapses the moment rights attach to a work. Fourth, two machine-learning researchers, who will say that a generative model does not search a structured space at all but interpolates a learned distribution, and that this makes my first criterion either trivially satisfied or plainly false.

Please prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this audience will actually raise — not the ones I would get from a room of computer scientists alone, and not the ones a general audience would raise. Order them by how much damage they would do, combine any that are really one objection in different clothes, and phrase each one the way its source would put it: the aesthetician's version of a worry is not the lawyer's version. For each, tell me what to concede up front, what to hold, a reply that fits in about two minutes, and where that reply is soft. Mark the one or two objections that have genuine force against me rather than against a caricature of my view. Identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Write your answer in British English.
