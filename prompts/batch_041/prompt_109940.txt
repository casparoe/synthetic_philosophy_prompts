My colleague in the department defends a naturalist moral realism, and his central move is an analogy. The naturalistic fallacy, he says, is not a fallacy; it is an artefact of a pre-scientific vocabulary. 'Water' once named a superficial kind of stuff — the clear, drinkable liquid — and chemistry then informed us that this stuff is H2O. Nobody thinks the chemists committed a fallacy in making that identification, and nobody thinks 'but is water really H2O?' remains an open question once the chemistry is settled; the feeling of openness is a residue of the old concept, exactly as it would have been for a Greek. So too, he says, 'good' and 'right' pick out some natural property — he cheerfully declines to say which, and treats that as a matter for empirical inquiry rather than for conceptual analysis — and the fact that 'is this really good?' still sounds like an open question is a fact about our word-learning history, not about metaphysics. The upshot he draws is that the is/ought gap is an illusion: if 'ought' names a region of the natural world, then an ought can be derived from an is, and philosophers who think they can settle the matter by reflecting on the meaning of 'good' are in the position of a philosopher trying to settle the composition of water by reflecting on the meaning of 'water'.

I want to press him, but in the right place. Audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence first — what plays the role of the pre-scientific concept, the surface properties, the reference-fixing, the division of linguistic labour, the a posteriori identity, the open question, and the eventual discovery — and say plainly which of these has no counterpart on the moral side. Then the disanalogies, ranked by how much argumentative work they do, separating the picturesque ones from the ones that break the argument. I am especially interested in three things: whether the analogy survives the fact that water has a causal-structural essence that explains its surface behaviour, whereas any candidate for the property 'good' names would have to be action-guiding and would have to explain why we care; whether the existence of many unrelated candidate properties (hedonic tone, fitness, what an idealized adviser would approve of, whatever) is fatal to the parallel with a unique chemical essence; and whether a true identity claim could by itself yield a derivation of an ought, or whether the gap is a fact about inference that no identity can close. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion — that the naturalistic fallacy is a fallacy only about definitions, and that the is/ought gap is an illusion — survives the audit or whether the analogy was carrying it all along, and whether there is a better analogy for what he is actually entitled to claim.
