I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have gotten in over my head in a reading group that keeps circling the hinge of history hypothesis: the claim that there is a real chance — someone in the group puts it around ten percent — that we are living in the era when the long-run future of civilization gets settled, so that what humanity does in the next few decades matters astronomically more than anything that happened in any century before it. A friend who is a much better mathematician than I am put the following argument on a whiteboard last week, and I genuinely cannot tell whether it is a serious problem for the view or a sleight of hand. (1) There is a ten percent chance that we are at the hinge. (2) Conditional on that, the difference between the future going well and going badly is worth 10^40 in some common unit — lives, well-being, whatever; the exact figure does not matter so long as it is enormous. (3) The expected value of an action is the sum, over the possibilities, of probability times value. (4) So the expected value of anything I do now is at least ten percent of 10^40, since if we are at the hinge, then what we do now is what settles it. (5) I objected that what I do is a drop in the bucket compared with what all of us do collectively; he agreed, multiplied by a guessed 10^-8 for the chance that anything I personally do is the decisive factor, and got 10^31. (6) The strongest ordinary reason I could have to keep a promise, protect a friendship, or spend a Saturday with my family comes out to maybe 10^3 in the same units. (7) So whenever an action of mine has any nonzero chance of improving the odds of a good hinge, it beats every ordinary reason I have by a factor of 10^28. (8) Therefore I should be willing to break promises, deceive people, and sacrifice the interests of the people close to me whenever the arithmetic is favorable — and if I am consistent, the same reasoning licenses things I would otherwise call monstrous, since in every case the downside is finite and the upside is not. (9) But a rule that reduces every ordinary decision to a rounding error, and that any stranger with a sufficiently large number can turn against me, cannot guide action at all; so at least one of the steps above must be wrong.

I have already been told that this is just the mugging problem and that the fix is to reject expected value whenever the payoffs are unbounded, but that reply seems too quick to me, because the mugger's number is invented on the spot while my friend's is supposed to come from a real argument about how history works — and I would rather not give up expected-value reasoning, which I use every day at work for capacity planning. If anything, I would rather give up the claim that we are at the hinge than give up the arithmetic. He also insists that the hinge has to be concentrated, that there must be some small set of decisions that tips the whole thing, since otherwise it would not be a hinge at all; I suspect the word might only mean unusually high-stakes era, in which case the outcome is the sum of millions of small contributions and I cannot see what follows. Please tell me which step to reject, why it looked plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it costs me — including what it costs if I insist on keeping both the expected-value framework and the claim that the future is astronomically valuable.
