Roughly three months ago I would have told you that when two options seem to be on a par, the failure is mine — a shortage of imagination or nerve — and not a fact about the values themselves. Then I spent most of a spring deciding whether to take a job on the far side of the country or stay within an hour's drive of my father while his health declined, and I came out of it unable to say that either option was better, unable to say that they were equally good, and unwilling to call the whole thing a coin flip. I have been trying since then to work out whether that state of mind was tracking something in the world or only reporting the limits of my own deliberation.

Please give me your credence in one proposition, stated so that it has a truth value: that there are pairs of options of the kind we actually face such that neither is better than the other, neither is worse, and they are not equally good — and that this is so not because the chooser is ignorant or the predicate vague, but because the usual trichotomy of better, worse, and equal fails to exhaust the relations that hold between values. If you think the proposition is not well formed as it stands, say what would have to be fixed, and give your credence in the repaired version.

Then decompose it. I want the sub-questions the number rests on, each with a rough probability, one line of justification, and then how they combine. I would expect at least these:

- the small improvement argument: take two options neither of which seems better than the other, improve one of them slightly, and observe that the improved version still seems neither better nor worse than the rival — which, if better, worse, and equal were all the options, would make it equal to the rival, and therefore make the rival better than the original, contrary to hypothesis. Does that argument work, and does it establish anything about value as opposed to about our judgments?

- whether the trichotomy is a conceptual truth about comparative value or a substantive claim that could fail, and what the failure would consist in.

- the choice between bare incomparability (the relation simply does not hold in either direction, and there is nothing further to say) and a positive fourth relation — call it parity — with a logic of its own; in particular, whether parity is transitive, and whether an option can be on a par with a second while a small improvement on it is better than the second.

- whether the cheapest rival explanation absorbs the same phenomena: an error theory on which there is a fact of the matter we cannot access, a semantics on which the comparative predicate is indeterminate in these cases, or a view on which the options are comparable along several dimensions but the resulting ordering is incomplete.

- whether the first-person phenomenology of a hard choice — settling on one option and afterwards neither judging the choice a mistake nor believing the other would have been just as good — is evidence about the structure of value at all, or only about me; and whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal.

Two things I especially want. First, tell me which single term the number is most sensitive to, and what would move it most — a conceptual argument, a case, or a formal result. Second, my own credence is around 0.5 and a friend who works in decision theory puts hers near 0.1; tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in, and whether one of us has a mistaken picture of what the question is rather than of the answer.
