I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, a philosopher named Aurel Kest, holds a position I find myself unable to refute cleanly, and I would like to use you as a sparring partner while I try. Please play Aurel — not a caricature of an externalist about reasons but the strongest version of him: unhurried, precise, willing to grant a small point when it is genuinely granted and never conceding the main one, and able to argue from the position itself rather than from anyone's authority. Stay in character for the whole exchange unless I write "out of character." Keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph, so it reads like a conversation rather than a lecture, and do not cite or allude to the literature by name; Aurel should reason as though he had worked all of this out from scratch and could not care less who else has said it. If you need some detail of the case I put to you in order to press a point, ask me for it, in character.

Aurel's view, for the record, is this. Whether some consideration is a reason for a person to act is settled by whether it genuinely counts in favor of the act — by facts about what is worth doing and what is owed to others — and not at all by whether the person could be moved by it, even under any ideal refinement of the ends she already has. He grants that motivation and reason can come apart; he denies that the gap is a defect in the reason rather than in the person. He will insist that a reason no one can be brought to heed is still a reason, and that calling it one is not a covert way of recommending something.

Think carefully before each reply. I would rather have one slow, well-aimed paragraph than a quick volley, and you may work through your resources privately beforehand, but keep the answer itself in Aurel's voice. Here is my opening move, and I mean it as a real objection.

Consider a woman I call the Locked Room. She has lived her whole life in one house, and her motivational set — everything she cares about, values, and would be moved by — contains nothing that leaving the house would serve and nothing that staying would frustrate. Suppose she is fully informed, coherent in her deliberation, and that no counterfactual refinement of her actual ends would ever bring her to the door; she stays, contentedly, and always would. Aurel says she nevertheless has a reason to leave, because leaving would open her to goods she cannot now want. I want him to tell me what would be different in the world if she had no such reason. If nothing in her deliberation, her action, or any version of her that is recognizably her would differ, then I cannot see what work the word "reason" is doing here except to voice Aurel's own estimate of the house — and I do not see why she, or anyone, should treat that estimate as a constraint on her rather than as a compliment to the outside. So: what is the difference, and why should it bind her?
