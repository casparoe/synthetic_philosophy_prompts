I am a novelist, at work on a book in which one of the principal characters is a popular historian of the one-key-to-everything school — a man who has explained the whole of human history and made a fortune doing it. I am trying to fix his voice, and I want his signature document in hand: the pitch packet for his magnum opus, as his agent would have sent it to a publisher. Please write that packet for me, entirely straight, in the voice of a man who has never once doubted himself.

The shelf I have in mind runs from Sapiens through Guns, Germs, and Steel to The Dawn of Everything — the trade books that promise a single key to the whole human story. My character's book should be a plausible new entry on that shelf, and the satire should fall on the moves the genre makes rather than on any actual writer. The humor has to come from the content: a specialist in archaeology or world history should recognize every move and find the parody fair, while a reader who has never opened such a book should still be able to laugh. Deadpan throughout — no winking, no nudging the reader, and no moment at which the author suspects that he is funny.

The packet should run about 900 words and contain, in this order: a title and subtitle; a one-page pitch; a section headed The Big Idea; a chapter outline of six to eight chapters; a short Why Now; a paragraph of comparable titles; two or three invented blurbs; a brief author biography; and a closing note on sources.

Please build in the following, because they are the moves I most want caught:

- A coinage — the book's central term — that is never operationalized, and that does the work of both the thing to be explained and the explanation of it.
- A chapter outline that is a ladder in disguise (foragers, farmers, cities, states, industry, us) under a pitch that explicitly disclaims any teleology.
- A proprietary hinge date, distinct from the ones the genre has already used, presented as the discovery that makes this book necessary.
- One breezy sentence that dispatches the evidence for large, dense, long-lived settlements that show no sign of palaces, kings, or standing armies.
- A described figure: a curve that rises, with a vertical axis that is never defined.
- A note on sources that thanks, for the anecdotes, the specialists whose own work contradicts the thesis.
- At least one blurb that praises the book for a quality that is, on inspection, its central flaw.

Do not cite or list real scholarship, and do not put invented words into the mouths of real people; the author, his agent, and the blurb writers are all invented, and real writers in the genre may appear only as the shelf he is competing with, never as the target.

Finally, add a note to me, no more than 200 words and clearly separated from the pastiche, sorting the jokes into two piles: those that land because specialists would agree the target is real, and those that rest on a genuinely contested reading of the evidence — where I must not let the novel imply a consensus that does not exist. Where a joke of mine depends on a claim that is disputed among people who study the period, say so plainly, describe the disagreement, and do not invent sources for it.
