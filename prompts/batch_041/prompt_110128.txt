I am assembling a reading for a graduate seminar in decision theory, and I want a critical essay on Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead read through the theory of imprecise probabilities. The play opens with the two of them flipping a coin that comes up heads over and over, far past the point at which a fair coin could be trusted; Guildenstern treats the run as a breach of the law of probability and casts about for explanations — a trick coin, a world that has stopped, the suspicion that they are inside a game someone else is running — while Rosencrantz keeps flipping and pocketing the winnings. Around that scene the play is built out of material the theory ought to find familiar: two characters who cannot be told apart, who have been summoned by a plot they never see, who carry a sealed letter that turns out to name their own deaths, and who spend the play in the company of a Player who appears to know how the thing ends. I want the coin scene, and that architecture, treated as a hard case in the theory of credence under ambiguity rather than as an illustration of it.

Address the following, in whatever order serves the argument.

1. Two ways of modeling the run. On one, the agent's credence is precise and the coin's bias is simply an unknown parameter with a prior, so the run is ordinary evidence and Bayes does the rest. On the other, the agent's credence is genuinely imprecise — a set of probability functions, a credal set — together with the decision rules that come with it (maximality, E-admissibility, gamma-maximin, that is, maximin against the lower probability, and minimax regret). Which of the two does better justice to the scene, and where does each distort what Stoppard is doing?

2. Whether the imprecision belongs to the characters or to the world. If it is merely an artifact of an incomplete information set, an omniscient being would have none of it; if it is a feature of the situation itself, no one could. The play's premise that the two are embedded in a plot with an author gives it, unusually, something like a God's-eye modeler in the person of the Player. Say which reading the play supports, and whether it has the resources to tell the difference.

3. Whether the scene is better read through the principle of indifference and its failures — the demand that a coin of unknown bias be assigned one-half, and the paradoxes that follow — than through imprecision at all, and whether the run of heads dramatizes the failure of indifference or something else.

4. Dilation: the phenomenon in which conditioning on evidence leaves an agent's credences about a related proposition more imprecise than before. If there is a case of it in the play, identify it; if not, explain why the coin scene only resembles one.

5. What the play adds that the formal literature does not. That literature is mostly about a single agent choosing among bets; this play is about two agents who cannot be distinguished, who share one fate, and who hold their credences while waiting to be killed. The game of questions they play along the way, in which the point is to keep asking and never to commit to a statement, may also be worth a paragraph as a picture of what refusing to be precise costs.

Close by saying honestly where the play resists the reading. It is a comedy about death and about being a minor character, and it is possible that the coin scene's real subject is not credence at all; if so, say what it is.

Work from the play's plot and its well-known scenes. You cannot consult the text, so do not quote it and do not invent lines. Assume your reader knows both the play and the decision theory.
