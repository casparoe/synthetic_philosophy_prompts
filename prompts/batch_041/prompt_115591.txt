I've been going through the Anti-Federalist essays signed "Brutus," the ones that ran in a New York paper while the Constitution was up for ratification, and I've stalled on the fifteenth, where the writer argues that the proposed federal judiciary would end up the most dangerous branch because nothing can check it. A friend of mine says the essay is just a straightforward attack on judicial review, and I don't think that's right — it seems to me the target is narrower and stranger than that, having to do with independence and accountability rather than with the power to strike down statutes as such. But when I try to say what the argument actually is, I bog down in the middle of it. Here is the stretch I keep rereading (I've kept the original spelling):

"The judges in England, it is true, hold their offices during their good behaviour, but then their determinations are subject to correction by the house of lords; and their power is by no means so extensive as that of the proposed supreme court of the union. — I believe they in no instance assume the authority to set aside an act of parliament under the idea that it is inconsistent with their constitution. They consider themselves bound to decide according to the existing laws of the land, and never undertake to controul them by adjudging that they are inconsistent with the constitution — much less are they vested with the power of giving an equitable construction to the constitution.

The judges in England are under the controul of the legislature, for they are bound to determine according to the laws passed by them. But the judges under this constitution will controul the legislature, for the supreme court are authorised in the last resort, to determine what is the extent of the powers of the Congress; they are to give the constitution an explanation, and there is no power above them to set aside their judgment. The framers of this constitution appear to have followed that of the British, in rendering the judges independent, by granting them their offices during good behaviour, without following the constitution of England, in instituting a tribunal in which their errors may be corrected; and without adverting to this, that the judicial under this system have a power which is above the legislative, and which indeed transcends any power before given to a judicial by any free government under heaven.

The great reason assigned, why the judges in Britain ought to be commissioned during good behaviour, is this, that they may be placed in a situation, not to be influenced by the crown, to give such decisions, as would tend to increase its powers and prerogatives. ... But these reasons do not apply to this country, we have no hereditary monarch; those who appoint the judges do not hold their offices for life, nor do they descend to their children. The same arguments, therefore, which will conclude in favor of the tenor of the judge's offices for good behaviour, lose a considerable part of their weight when applied to the state and condition of America."

What I can't reconstruct is the inference in that last paragraph. He grants that judges in Britain ought to hold during good behaviour, and he identifies the reason for the arrangement as keeping the bench from being used by the crown to inflate the royal prerogative; then he says that because we have no hereditary monarch, those same arguments lose much of their weight here. That reads to me like a non sequitur — in a republic the danger that judicial independence is supposed to answer is pressure from the legislature or the executive of the day, and the absence of a king does nothing to remove that danger. So either he is assuming something he never states, or the paragraph is doing rhetorical work rather than argumentative work, and I'd like to know which. I'm also unsure how much weight to put on his contrast between deciding "according to the existing laws of the land" and being vested with the power of giving "an equitable construction to the constitution" — I take it "equitable" is doing something technical here that a reader in the 1780s would have caught immediately, and I want to know what that is and why it matters to the contrast he is building between the English bench and the proposed one. And when he says the judges "will controul the legislature," I genuinely can't tell whether that is a claim about how a court will read a text, a claim about which body's judgment is final, or a claim about institutional incentives.

Could you walk me through the passage closely — paragraph by paragraph, saying what each claim is doing and where the joints of the argument are — and then tell me squarely whether my charge of non sequitur lands? If it does not, I want to see the premise he must be assuming and whether it is defensible; if it does, I want to know what the paragraph is still good for. Two further things while you're at it: whether his reasoning about the absence of a monarch would survive the substitution of a term-limited executive with its own interest in aggrandizing federal power at the expense of the states, and which of us is closer to right about the essay's real target. Mark clearly which of your claims are textual and which are your reconstruction, don't tidy away the places where he is plainly scoring political points rather than arguing, and please write in American English. I'd rather have connected prose than a bulleted outline — the point is to see how the thing hangs together.
