My reading group has gotten weirdly obsessed with a Hofstadter idea — the one from I Am a Strange Loop where he says the "I" isn't bolted to a single skull. A person, on this picture, is a pattern (he calls it a strange loop), and patterns can be instantiated in more than one place at once, so when somebody dies a big chunk of them is still running in the heads of the people who loved them, and in that sense my late grandmother's "I" is partly still going, distributed across me and my cousins and her friends. Half the group finds this profound, the other half thinks it's a metaphor wearing a lab coat, and nobody can say whether the claim is even well-formed. So before we argue about whether it's true, I want the boring groundwork done first: give me the desiderata a theory of personal identity would have to meet for a claim like that to even get off the ground. Not the theory — the spec. What cases does it have to get right (someone dying in pieces while their pattern lives on in others, the person who loses their memories, the person whose friends carry a version of them that they themselves would disown, conjoined twins, fission, the copy-or-teleport case, whatever else you think earns a place), which distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse (numerical versus qualitative sameness, a self genuinely being shared versus merely being represented, "part of her is in me" as literal versus as sentiment, and how much of a pattern has to survive before it still counts as the same I), and what it has to stay consistent with about how brains actually work — no little homunculus parked in there, no single privileged spot where the I sits, no ghostly stuff, whatever contemporary cognitive science already rules out.

For every item, tell me whats behind it — the case or intuition that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp — and then, most importantly, tell me which requirements I should be ready to drop if it turns out they can't all be held at once, and flag the pair or triple you think is jointly unsatisfiable. Thats the part I actually want, since the list itself is the result. If you have room at the end, do a quick one-line-each pass over how the usual contenders fare against it — psychological continuity, bodily or animalist continuity, the narrative-self view, and the no-self or bundle view — but keep that short, the criteria are the deliverable. Dont send me reading, I just want the spec.
