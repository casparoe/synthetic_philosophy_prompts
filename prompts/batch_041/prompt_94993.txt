A colleague in our data-ethics seminar has come away from reading about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness with a conclusion I think is too strong, though I have trouble putting my finger on exactly what is wrong with it. Her view is that these results amount to a proof that accuracy and fairness stand in fundamental tension: the more accurate a classifier becomes, the more it must end up disadvantaging some group, and the fairer we try to make it, the less accurate it must be. She treats this as a limitation of machine learning as such, one that more training data, more informative features, or a more expressive model would eventually dissolve. Pending that, she proposes that we can sidestep the whole difficulty by letting the model abstain on borderline cases and hand them to a human reviewer, so that whatever the model does decide will be both accurate and fair. I suspect there is a confusion running through all of this, but I would like a careful diagnosis: what do the impossibility results actually establish, and is her reading of them correct — that they reveal an accuracy-fairness trade-off which better data or bigger models could overcome, and that a reject option would let us escape the dilemma?
