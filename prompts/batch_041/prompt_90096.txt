For the past few months I've been sitting in on a philosophy of religion reading group that keeps circling back to Molinism — the view that before God settles anything about the actual world, God already knows what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible circumstance, and that this middle knowledge is what lets God arrange providence without thereby determining anyone's choices. The objection we keep re-litigating is that there is nothing to make those counterfactuals true. God hasn't decreed anything yet, the creature doesn't exist yet, and if the choice is free in the libertarian sense then it can't be fixed by the creature's nature or essence either, so the counterfactuals appear to float — true of nothing at all. The Molinists among us insist that this objection simply assumes a contentious theory of what it takes for a proposition to be true, or that it mistakes an order of explanation for an order of causation, or that God's knowledge need not be grounded in anything prior to it; the objectors reply that this is just relabeling the difficulty. We've now spent a whole term adding analogies and distinctions without either side moving an inch, and I've lost confidence that one more analogy will do it.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than another round of the argument — I already know the positions and I don't need them surveyed again. Is this a substantive metaphysical disagreement, say about truthmaking or about what grounds a proposition's truth, or a verbal dispute about the word knowledge, or two groups quietly answering different questions under a single heading — one asking how God could arrange a world, the other asking what makes a counterfactual true? Is a hidden formal or metaphysical premise doing the real work? My own working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a settled view, is that the whole exchange is downstream of two commitments neither side makes explicit — a libertarian conception of freedom, and the assumption that God's knowledge must be grounded in something logically or causally prior to it — so that the disagreement is not really about middle knowledge at all but about whether those two commitments can be held together, in which case the impasse is a symptom of an inconsistent package rather than a merely hard problem. Tell me whether that is right, and if it isn't, what the real structure of the disagreement is. Then say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the standoff, a result about grounding or truthmaking, a decision to abandon one of the background assumptions, or nothing at all, with reasons.

Please write this as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic for emphasis, no tables — just a few flowing paragraphs of the sort you'd put in an email to a friend in the group. And don't give me citations or a reading list; I can't look anything up, and I don't want guesses at who said what. Argue from the ideas themselves.
