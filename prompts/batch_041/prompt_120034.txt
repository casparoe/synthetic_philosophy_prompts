Over the weekend I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about the color phi phenomenon and what Daniel Dennett makes of it, and I have come away without a clear sense of how much of his case to accept. He is a careful reader and not given to inventing claims, and he was more confident than I could match, so I would rather audit his position than wave it away. What I want is not a general essay on consciousness, and not a verdict on Dennett, but a claim-by-claim assessment of whether my friend has the material right.

His account, as close to his own words as I can reconstruct:

Take the color phi phenomenon. You flash a dot at the top of a screen and then, a fraction of a second later, a dot of a different color at the bottom. At the right spacing and timing you do not see two dots; you see a single dot travelling from top to bottom, and you see it change color part-way along the path. The curious part is that people report the color change as occurring in the middle of the journey, before the second dot has even appeared. In essence it is just the familiar phi phenomenon with a color change added, which is why it became a stock example.

Dennett's interest in it was to show that the brain has two ways of producing the illusion, and that the difference between them is invisible to the person having the experience. On the Orwellian story you genuinely see the dot travel in its first color, and then your memory is quietly rewritten afterwards so that the color change lands in the middle, like the way records of the past are altered in Orwell. On the Stalinesque story the brain holds everything back, stages the whole thing in advance, and hands you a single, already-resolved experience in which the color change was always in the middle, like a show trial whose verdict is settled before anyone takes the stand. Dennett argues that the Orwellian story is the correct one: the phenomenon shows that the brain retroactively edits what you were conscious of, and so that your conscious experience of an event can occur after the event itself. He ties this to the Libet experiments, which he says are the same result, the brain getting going before you are aware of having decided, so that the feeling of deciding in real time is a post-hoc construction. This is why Dennett is sometimes described as holding that consciousness is an illusion, and his target was Cartesian dualism: the point is that there is no little theater in the head where it all comes together, no Cartesian theater. And because the two stories make exactly the same predictions about what people will say they saw, the matter had to be settled by looking at the brain, and the imaging work has since come down on the Orwellian side. So we now know that experience, strictly speaking, is never in real time.

That is the substance of it. I suspect he is right that the phenomenon bears on the timing of experience and wrong that Dennett would draw the conclusion he draws, but I cannot locate the mistake, and I do not know how far it infects the rest.

Please give me a claim-by-claim audit. For each substantive claim, place it in one of three categories: something a philosopher of mind working on this would accept as stated; a standard simplification a competent popularizer might make that does no real damage; or a genuine misrepresentation that Dennett or a defender of his would reject. Where he attributes a position to Dennett, say whether it is Dennett's actual view or a familiar distortion of it, and identify the move in Dennett's argument that the attribution gets wrong.

I have a few specific questions. Does his bottom line follow from the parts of his account that survive correction? Are the two hypotheses really supposed to differ in the way he assumes, and if not, what is the actual difference between them, and why did Dennett put the pair to work at all? Is he conflating this with some other result or debate that tends to be mentioned in the same breath but makes a different point? Is any part of what he and I were disagreeing about merely verbal, a case where he and Dennett are using a word such as "experience" or "consciousness" in different senses and talking past each other? And is there anything in his summary that sounds wrong but is in fact correct, so that I do not accuse him of an error he did not make?

Close with the two or three corrections that would most change his overall picture, in order of importance, and with what a defender of Dennett would single out as the most serious mistake. I do not need references or a reading list; I want to know what to trust and what to push back on.
