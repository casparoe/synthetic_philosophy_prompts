I'm in a tiny reading group that's spent about two months crawling through the wrong kind of reasons problem, and two of us have basically stoped speaking to each other philosophically. Quick orientation in case it helps: a demon threatens to torture you forever unless you admire him; everyone agrees that's no reason to admire him, and yet it looks like a reason of exactly the same shape as the reasons that make admiration fitting, i.e. that it would be good or required to have the attitude. So either value can't be analysed in terms of fitting attitudes, or there's a principled line between reasons that bear on whether an attitude is correct and reasons that bear on whether it's good to have it — and that line has to be drawn and defended rather than gestured at. My friend, the Repairer, thinks the line is real and can be spelt out in independent terms: something about what the attitude is directed at, or the direction of fit, or which facts its correctness depends on. I'm the Abolitionist, mostly for the sake of argument: I think every attempt to say what makes a reason 'of the right kind' just redescribes the phenomenon in fancier vocabulary, and if that's the best we can do then fittingness can't be the fundamental normative notion people want it to be. (There are usually biscuits and we never get past four pages, so we're not getting anywhere fast on our own.)
