I host a small podcast on decision theory and practical rationality, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a distinctive line on the absent-minded driver problem. The standard setup, for context: a driver will pass two intersections and then reach home; exiting at the first pays zero, exiting at the second pays four, continuing past both pays one; and whenever he reaches an intersection he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. My guest holds that the driver's optimal strategy is to randomize, exiting with probability one-third at each intersection, for an expected payoff of four-thirds, and that this result shows ex ante optimality and sequential rationality can come apart in a way standard decision theory has not adequately captured. I want to interview him in a way that goes well beyond having him restate the calculation. Please draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, organized around the following pressure points.

First, the dominance argument: at the second intersection, exiting guarantees four while continuing guarantees one, so continuing is strictly dominated there; how does his view avoid the conclusion that the driver should simply exit whenever he reaches an intersection?

Second, the status of the randomization: is the mixed strategy a genuine option for the driver, or does it presuppose a commitment or policy choice that the absent-minded driver, by construction, cannot make at the moment of decision?

Third, the evidential question: does the fact that the driver finds himself continuing at an intersection carry information about which intersection he is at, and does his view require an evidential rather than a causal reading of the driver's choice (that is, treating the choice as evidence about which intersection he is at rather than as a cause of his location)?

Fourth, the relation to the Sleeping Beauty problem, in which a subject is woken once or twice depending on a coin toss and cannot tell which waking she is in: both problems involve imperfect recall and self-locating uncertainty; does his treatment of the driver commit him to a particular position on Sleeping Beauty, and if so, which one?

Fifth, a novel variant I have devised: suppose the driver has partial memory, so that with probability q he can tell whether he has already passed an intersection, and with probability one minus q he cannot. What does his framework predict about the optimal payoff as a function of q, and does the four-thirds result survive only in the limit case where q is zero?

Sixth, a variant with three intersections, where exiting at the first pays zero, at the second pays two, at the third pays four, and continuing past all three pays one: what is the optimal policy, and does his account of the two-intersection case generalize in a principled way?

Finally, a closing question about what would count as a decisive objection to his position, and whether he thinks the problem is a genuine paradox for decision theory or an artifact of a particular formalization of imperfect recall.

For each question, include a follow-up that anticipates the most likely reply and presses further; for example, if he says the mixed strategy is available because the driver can commit ex ante to a policy, ask whether that commitment is itself something the driver can rationally make given that he knows he will be unable to recognize the second intersection, and whether the same move would vindicate other seemingly irrational policies in problems with imperfect recall.
