I'm a phil professor, and I've been teaching a seminar on Sartre and bad faith. I keep circling a distinction I've started calling the alibi and the vertigo. The alibi turns me into a thing: I'm just a coward, just a waiter, just someone with this temperament, so the choice was never really mine. The vertigo goes the other way: I'm pure freedom, nothing fixed at all, so none of my particular life is really me -- it's just contingency I could disown. Both dodge the same uncomfortable fact, that we're a mix of facticity and transcendence, and bad faith lives in the oscillation between them. Write me a villanelle about this. The first and third lines of the opening stanza should become the two refrains -- one in the voice of the alibi, one in the voice of the vertigo -- and they should keep taking turns until the closing quatrain, where they finally sit side by side, so the reader sees they're two faces of the same evasion rather than opposites. It should be accurate enough that someone who's read Sartre would recognise the structure, not just the buzzwords, and good enough as a poem that someone who hasn't would still get something from it. No epigraph, no title, no notes. Nineteen lines max, under 180 words. And no quotations from Sartre -- I want it in your own words.
