I'm taking a seminar on scientific realism as an outside elective — my own field is epidemiology — and I'm trying to write a term paper about a habit of my professor's that I can't quite formalize. Her recurring move is to take two theories that she agrees are both false and say which one is closer to the truth, and she does it both with famous pairs from the history of science and with toy cases she invents for the class. Her verdicts so far: the Ptolemaic system versus Copernicus, Copernicus closer; phlogiston chemistry versus Lavoisier's oxygen chemistry, Lavoisier closer; the caloric theory of heat versus the kinetic theory, kinetic closer; Newtonian mechanics versus general relativity, relativity closer. Then, in one of her made-up cases, she stipulated the facts herself — an illness caused by a bacterium that lives in the blood, spreads through water, and incubates for about two weeks — and compared Theory A, which said it was a bacterium, spread by water, and incubated for a week, with Theory B, which said only that it was caused by something living, that it could spread, and that it took more than a day to appear. She called A closer to the truth than B, even though A's incubation claim was false and everything B said was true. She has also insisted, more than once, that two theories can make exactly the same predictions about everything we could ever observe and still differ in how close they are to the truth, so that counting observable consequences alone gets things wrong. I gather the technical name for what she is ranking is verisimilitude, though I may be misremembering.

Next week's pair is another invented one, and this time she refuses to tell us the facts until after we have committed to an answer. Theory P says the phenomenon in question has five components, identifies four of them correctly, and describes the fifth as a thousand times larger than it actually is. Theory Q says the phenomenon has the same five components, but misdescribes all five, each by roughly a factor of two and none of them wildly. I want to predict what she will say, but I care much more about the rule she is running than about the answer itself.

So: (a) what will she say about P versus Q, and why; (b) what rule do her earlier verdicts commit her to, and which other rules fit her track record just as well — I can see at least one rule that measures how much of the truth a theory captures and another that measures how far its claims sit from the truth, and I suspect there are more; (c) what does each of those rules predict for P versus Q, and what single further case would force them apart; and (d) can closeness to the truth be made precise at all, or is the whole business a metaphor — and if it can, how is anyone supposed to apply it when, as in real science and in next week's case, nobody yet knows what the truth is?
