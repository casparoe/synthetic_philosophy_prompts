I run the graphics desk of a municipal statistics office. Every year we produce about a dozen large pictorial wall charts — printed for libraries, schools, clinics and welfare offices, frequently reproduced in newspapers, and sometimes lifted by political campaigns we have no control over — for a city in which many residents read the local language poorly, so the pictures have to carry much of the message. My team is three people, we get two weeks per chart, we work only from tables our statisticians have already certified, and we draw on a library of some two hundred pictograms.

I want to rebuild our house style on the principles of Otto Neurath's Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics, later renamed Isotype, developed at the Social and Economic Museum of Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s. The essentials as I understand them: a quantity is shown by repeating an identical symbol, never by enlarging one, because readers cannot reliably compare areas or volumes but can count; symbols are drawn flat, without perspective; colours carry fixed meanings; and a distinct role, the transformer, mediates between the certified table and the artist's sketch to produce a chart a reader can decode without instruction. Neurath held that this was not a full language but a helping language always accompanied by words — his slogan was "Words divide, pictures unite" — and the museum's own formula was that it is better to remember a simplified picture than to forget an accurate figure.

Write me the decision procedure my team should follow, from receiving a certified table to signing off a poster. It should be ordered steps a junior designer can run without me; for each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer and what to do when the answer is no. Say where the procedure stops and hands the matter to a person, and name that person. Include a rule for when a new pictogram may be added to the library and how a colour may be given a new meaning. Deal explicitly with the data we actually receive, most of which are not counts of countable things: rates, averages, index numbers, and money figures spanning decades of currency change. And state plainly which claims the visual system must refuse to carry, and how we should answer someone who asks us to make a chart more forceful.

Then apply the procedure to three cases, one row each in a table: (a) new dwellings completed last year, by district; (b) average net household income, by district; (c) a request from our communications director for a campaign poster showing that our city's vaccination coverage is two percentage points above the neighbouring city's. For each, give the verdict, the chart if any that the procedure yields, and the step that decides it.

Close with a short section on what this procedure cannot do and where it will misfire. Open the whole response with one sentence stating the rule that the rest of the procedure elaborates, and keep the response under 1,200 words.
