I teach a 90-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in epistemology (14 students; they have had a week on credences, but no formal decision theory). I want them to feel the force of the Reflection principle — the demand that my present credence in a proposition A, given that my later credence in A will be x, should be x — before they ever hear the name or the formula. Design the session.

What I need in the answer: a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes with slack built in; the exact words I should say, written out, for the framing, the rules, and every transition; a materials list with quantities and any printed sheets in full; the debrief; an inventory of what will predictably go wrong the first time and what to do when it does; and one frank paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. No citations, no reading list, no bibliography — write it so that I can run it cold, from the page, with no background reading.

Constraints:

1. Setting and materials. One facilitator (me), one room, a whiteboard, index cards, sealed envelopes, poker chips or tokens. No real money, no laptops, and no confederate who has to lie convincingly for more than a minute.

2. The idea must arrive through the rules, not through me. Nothing I say before the debrief may name or gloss the principle; the words "reflection", "coherence", and "Dutch book", and van Fraassen's name, stay out of my mouth until the last twenty minutes. At most four minutes of framing at the start, and that framing should be about the game, not about the idea.

3. First movement — the pull. A two-stage trading game with me as the house, bound by a rule I read aloud and post on the board: at each of the two stages I may trade with any participant at the price they have themselves just quoted for a ticket on A (their stated credence is the price), and I may choose which side of the trade to take. Between the stages I run an evidence event whose direction I already know. I want each participant to watch their own two quotes turn into a loss for them and a margin for me, with the arithmetic done on the board, so that nobody can suspect sleight of hand. One constraint on the design: my edge must come only from their own quotes and from my knowing which way the evidence moves them — never from my knowing anything about A that they do not. If, under the rules as I have stated them, the house cannot be sure of a margin, say so, change the rules as little as possible, and tell me exactly what you changed and why.

4. Second movement — the break. Then a case in which the later credence is produced by something that is not evidence about A at all: the later self is drunk, drugged, flattered by a confident stranger, or simply worn down. I want them to feel the very principle that just booked them now instructing them to adopt that corrupted credence in advance. The pull must come strictly before the absurdity, in the same session, with the same participants, and neither movement may be announced as such.

5. Third movement, short and droppable if the clock runs out: they write an instruction to their later selves and I either honour it or do not, so that the difference between what they should believe and what they should do about what they will believe has to be acted on rather than discussed.

6. The debrief must map moments onto parts of the idea — the house's rule onto the thought that your own inconsistency across time is exploitable in the way a current inconsistency between credences is; the initial deference intuition onto the thought that a better-informed later credence is evidence about A; the corrupted later self onto the limit of the principle. I expect at least four failure modes, and one of them should be risk aversion: a participant who will not take a bet they consider fair makes the whole book evaporate, and I need to know what to do about that when it happens in the first ten minutes. Another is the participant who announces the answer early.

End with a single final line of the form \answer{...} naming the one moment in the activity that does the load-bearing work — the thing I should protect if I have to cut for time.
