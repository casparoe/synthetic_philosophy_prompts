I would like a careful explanation of a conceptual issue that arises when Chesterton's fence is used in political argument. The issue is that the fence argument appears to require two incompatible things at once. On one reading, it says that we should not remove an institution until we know why it was put there, which treats the institution's function as something recoverable by historical inquiry. On another reading, it is defended in a Burkean spirit according to which the institution embodies accumulated practical wisdom that no individual reformer can fully articulate, which treats its function as partly tacit and therefore not fully recoverable. I want you to explain whether these are really two different arguments, whether one is stronger than the other, whether either can be separated from a general presumption in favor of existing arrangements, and whether either is better understood as a substantive moral presumption or merely as a heuristic for decision-making under uncertainty.

Then apply the distinction to a concrete case. A professional licensing requirement was introduced a century ago mainly to exclude a disfavored group. That purpose is now gone. The requirement also has an unintended effect: it slows the spread of a dangerous but profitable practice. A reformer wants to abolish the requirement. A defender says the fence argument blocks abolition. I want a committed answer, not a survey. Does the fence argument apply here? Does the bigoted origin matter? Does the unintended current function count as a reason to keep the fence even if no one can give a full account of it? In your answer, distinguish the historical-origin version of the argument from the tacit-function version, say which one the defender should rely on, and take a definite position on whether the requirement should be abolished.
