Fourth year in philosophy of physics here, and I keep getting into an arguement I lose in a way I can't diagnose, so I want to hand it over.

The setup: Bell's theorem plus the experiments, including the ones that finally closed the detection and locality loopholes, tell us the correlations can't come from a locally causal story of the usual kind. Everyone in the room agrees on the theorem, the statistics, the data. Nobody agrees on what that leaves us with. One camp: the world is nonlocal, that's the end of it, and the only remaining question is which nonlocal story you prefer. Second camp: what the experiments actually force you to give up is the assumption that the measurement settings are independent of the hidden state, the freedom-of-choice assumption, and since that was never an empirical result the theorem doesn't rule out a local deterministic world, it rules out a certain kind of statistical independence. Third: let influences run backwards and the spacelike seperation stops doing any work. Fourth: the derivation assumes a single definite outcome, so if there isn't one the inequality simply doesn't apply. Fifth: the theorem's premise is a formal condition on a joint probability distribution, which was never the same thing as the physical notion of locality, and quantum mechanics famously allows no signalling, so calling the world nonlocal is at best misleading.

What made me start writing this is that the loophole-free experiments landed and nobody moved. Nobody switched sides. So I've stopped thinking of it as an empirical disagreement, and I don't think more data will fix it.

My hypothesis, and I want you to tell me where it's wrong: the impasse is three things stacked on top of each other. (1) A definitional dispute about what locality should mean — a formal condition on models versus a claim about causal influence — where the two sides are using one word for two things and neither is obviously misusing it. (2) A real disagreement about which assumption is worth giving up, which isn't a factual matter at all but an evaluative one: it's about priors and explanatory cost, about which remaining option is least bad. (3) A hidden formal question about whether the escape routes are actually distinct, since if some of them collapse into each other the choice is smaller than it looks.

So diagnose it for me. Which parts are verbal, which are disagreements about evidence or about what an explanation has to do, and which are bedrock — say, intuitions about whether backwards causation is coherent at all, or about whether the settings-could-have-been-otherwise assumption is a real physical assumption rather than a methodological one you can't do physics without? Is the freedom-of-choice assumption empirical, methodological, or metaphysical, and if it isn't testable even in principle, what does that do to the debate? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a classification theorem that tells us what the escape routes are and which are genuinely distinct, a clarification of locality that makes the question empirical again, an experiment, or nothing? I'd rather be told it's stalled for a boring reason than get a tour of the interpretations.
