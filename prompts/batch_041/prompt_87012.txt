I write about science and ideas for a general audience and I've managed to get myself into a bar argument with a friend who actually does decision theory for a living, so I'm outsourcing the tiebreak. Its a known puzzle, I know, but I've bolted a twist onto it that I haven't seen anyone talk about, and I want numbers rather than vibes.

The game: flip a fair coin until it lands heads for the first time. If it lands heads on toss n, you're paid (-1)^n times 2^n divided by n. So heads on the first toss costs you 2, heads on the second pays you 2, heads on the third costs you 8/3, heads on the fourth pays you 4, and so on — the payoffs grow without bound and alternate in sign. Now do the obvious thing and weight each payoff by its probability, which is 1 over 2^n. The terms become (-1)^n over n, the alternating harmonic series, which adds up to minus the natural log of 2 if you take the terms in order. Trouble is that series only converges conditionally, so rearranging the terms lets you make the total come out to any real number you like, or to plus or minus infinity. So there's no well-defined expected value in the sense that matters. My friend's position is that this means the game simply has no fair price, done, end of story, and anyone who says otherwise is smuggling in a convention.

Here's my twist. Same game but with a dial on it: on toss n you get (-1)^n times 2^n divided by n to the p. Call it G(p), so the original game is G(1). For p above 1 the expectation series is absolutely convergent and you get one unique number. For p between 0 and 1 the series still converges but only conditionally, so the rearrangement problem is still their. At p of 0 or below the terms of the expectation series don't even shrink, so the thing diverges outright. So there are two candidate sharp lines in the math — one at p=1 where absolute convergence kicks in, one at p=0 where convergence of any kind dies — and my question is whether either of those lines shows up as a sharp line in the philosophy.

What I want, and please give me an actual number instead of a tour of the literature: your credence that the Pasadena game, the p=1 case, has a determinate fair price — that is, that there's a fact of the matter about what it's worth which doesn't depend on our choice of how to order the sum. Decompose it. List the sub-questions the number actually rides on, give each a rough probability and one line of justification, and then show me how they combine into the total. I'd expect to see some of these in the list, though add whatever you think belongs: whether fair price just means expected value or is a broader notion; whether the failure of absolute convergence is a fact about the game or only about one way of keeping the books; whether G(1.01) really has a determinate price or just looks like it does because we stopped poking; whether the right answer for p=1 is a set or an interval of admissible prices rather than a single number; whether unbounded payoffs make the game unpriceable for reasons that have nothing to do with convergence; and whether rationality only ever requires a ranking between options and never a cardinal value, in which case my whole question might be malformed.

Then tell me which term is carrying the most weight and what evidence or argument would move it. And is there anything — a theorem, a thought experiment, a decision procedure — that would count as settling the p=1 case, or is this one where the question just dissolves?

One sanity check while you're at it. If the fair price of G(p) ought to be continuous in p, what happens to it as p drops down to 1 from above? Does it approach minus the natural log of 2, or something else? And what does that tell us about where the real threshold is, if there is one.
