I keep a running list of cases where I think it's fine for one person to just go ahead and do the thing alone, no permission, no committee, and cases where it clearly isn't, and the list has started to eat itself. What I want is whatever principle is behind it, if there is one.

Fine, in my book:
- A guy driving past a wreck late at night stops and starts hauling someone out of the car by himself. Nobody asked him to.
- Me paying out of pocket for an unproven treatment for my own cancer after my oncologist tells me it's a bad bet. My body, my odds.
- A researcher posting his preprint the same week two other labs are about to post basically the same result.
- A lone cop deciding not to disperse a protest he's been ordered to disperse, because on his read of the crowd it will go worse if he does.
- One guy seeding clouds over a drought region because he thinks the regional models are wrong.

Not fine, in my book:
- A hobbyist dumping his engineered bacteria into a lake to 'clean it up', even though he's thought about it for a year and reckons it will probably work.
- A guy with access to a big collection of dangerous pathogen recipes putting the whole thing online, saying transparency is the safer default.
- One boat going far over quota on a stock that's already collapsing, on the grounds that one boat's catch is nothing.
- One lab running a small geoengineering test by itself because the international process is too slow.
- A company shipping a model it knows can be used to manipulate people, because its competitors will ship worse ones.

Two stories in there don't line up. Story one: what matters is whether your act, on the margin, makes things worse given what everyone else is going to do anyway. That story says the fisherman is fine, and that the hobbyist is fine too, because someone else would have dumped something eventually. I dont accept either. Story two: what matters is whether you're well placed to know — if you're the only one who can do it, or the only one who's actually looked, back your judgment. That story makes the pathogen guy fine, since he's the only one holding the list, and I don't accept that either.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these? If it needs a clause for the pathogen guy and a different one for the fisherman, is it still doing any work, or is it just my list with extra words? And what does it say about the case I actually care about — a friend who found a hole in a piece of safety software half the world runs on, and is thinking of just publishing the exploit rather than telling the vendor, on the grounds that vendors sit on these things for months. Also, if the simple version is the right one, tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up, because I'd rather know than keep the list.
