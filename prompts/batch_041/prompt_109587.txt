For the past three years I've been the junior analyst at a small forecasting outfit, and this month I have to write the memo that decides whether we keep or retire the framework the whole place is built on. Our director made his reputation with a long-run prediction: that as demand for a certain resource grows, supply will fail to keep up, costs will climb, and the economy built on it will stall. He first staked this out in the late 1990s. It hasn't happened. Prices rose, then substitutes appeared, then the whole sector reorganized around something the model never anticipated. When clients push back, he says the forecast was right and the calendar was wrong — that we're watching a delay, not a refutation. A colleague of mine insists the prediction was never really tested, because the conditions it assumed no longer hold; that sounds to me like a way of never being allowed to lose. Last month the director added a lag term that pushes the crisis out another twenty years, and I honestly can't tell a genuine refinement from a patch. I don't know how to distinguish a theory that's simply early from one that's been rescued into unfalsifiability, and I need to decide what to recommend. What should I do, and what would actually count as evidence one way or the other? Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just write it out the way you'd explain it to me in a letter.
