Six weeks ago I finally sat down with two years of brokerage statements, and I have been unable to stop thinking about what I found. Over nineteen months I made three trades. I began in Fund B and paid a fee to move into Fund A, because at that moment I preferred A to B; I then paid a fee to move into Fund C, because I preferred C to A; and finally I paid a fee to move back into Fund B, because I preferred B to C. Three trades, three fees, and I ended exactly where I began, roughly $3,800 poorer. A friend in a decision theory program told me, with what I chose to read as admiration, that I was the textbook illustration of a money pump, and that the example exists precisely to show what a patient counterparty can do to someone whose preferences run in a circle. What unsettles me is not the money. It is that I cannot locate the step at which I was irrational. Each trade was, at the moment I made it, the best move available to me.

I write a short humor column for a general-interest magazine, read mostly by people with no philosophical training, though a few of my old professors subscribe. I would rather turn this into a piece than let it sit. Please draft that column for me, under the following conditions.

1. Form and voice. Write it as a self-help or advice column in the voice of an invented wellness or money guru who dispenses practical guidance. The irony is sustained and deadpan: he never breaks character, never signals that the piece is a joke, and never states the objection in a way that turns him into a strawman. Include a headline, an invented byline and the name of his method or program, one invented reader letter, his reply, and a closing list of three or four steps or practices for readers. Make the reader letter a close cousin of my own case — someone who has cycled through three of something and landed where he started, minus a few thousand dollars — so that the column reads as an answer to me without naming me.

2. Target. What is being satirized is his position: that the only thing rationality can demand of you is that each choice, considered on its own, be the best one available at the moment you make it; that a past self has no authority to veto a present self; and that anyone who tries to bind himself in advance is simply exhibiting a failure of self-trust. He should be an articulate, even appealing, proponent of this, not a fool.

3. Where the humor comes from. The jokes must come from the decision theory being right, so that a specialist laughs rather than winces. Every trade should genuinely improve the chooser's position at the moment of choice; the fees are what make the arrangement work; the sequence leaves the person worse off even though no single step does; and the guru's advice should be exactly the thing that makes the exploitation possible. The obvious resolution — deciding in advance and then refusing every subsequent trade no matter what one prefers at the time — should be described fairly and in plain language before he waves it aside. You may let him mention the term "money pump" once and dismiss it, deadpan. Optional punchline: a footer of fine print disclosing, in flat corporate prose, that his program collects a fee on every trade.

4. No citations. Nothing may quote, cite, or name a real book, paper, or author, and there should be no reading list or bibliography. I cannot verify attributions and will not run invented ones dressed up as real ones. Invent the guru and every other figure.

5. Length and register. About 750 words, American English throughout, polished enough to run as written. A reader who has never encountered the idea of transitivity should be able to follow it; a reader who has should find it fair.

6. What it should leave behind. By the end I want the reader to feel the real question rather than be told it: whether it makes sense to call a life irrational when every choice within it was rational, and whether the person who binds herself in advance is being rational or quietly changing the subject.
