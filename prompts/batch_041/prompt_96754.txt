For the better part of a year I have been caught in a dispute among three of us in my department about the family of arguments in population ethics that proceed in small steps -- from a mild pain to a slightly more severe one, or from a life barely worth living to a much larger population of lives only slightly less good -- where each step appears to be an improvement and yet the endpoint appears clearly worse than the start. None of us has any difficulty stating the structure of the argument, and none of us disputes the arithmetic or the logic of the steps. What has become frustrating is that the discussion has hardened into two camps that cannot move each other. One camp holds that the argument is valid and that every premise is at least plausible, so the lesson must be that 'better than' is not transitive, or that our assumption of transitivity is a prejudice we should abandon. The other camp holds that transitivity is a conceptual truth about comparatives, so at least one premise must be false, and our intuitions about the small steps must be mistaken, vague, or confused. A third position, which some of us find tempting, is that the argument equivocates: the small steps are 'better' in some local or pro tanto sense, while the conclusion requires an all-things-considered sense, and the two cannot be combined.

I have a hypothesis I would like you to test rather than assume: perhaps this is not really a disagreement about value at all, but a disagreement about what kind of thing 'better than' is -- a relation among states of affairs, a relation among preferences, or a relation of choiceworthiness -- and the two camps are answering different questions under a single word. One of my colleagues, who works in social choice, says the whole dispute reminds her of Condorcet cycles: when majority preference cycles, we do not conclude that 'more popular than' is meaningless or that the voters are irrational; we conclude that this is a domain in which transitivity cannot be assumed, and we ask what weaker structure the relation has. Another insists that the real issue is a distinction I do not fully grasp, between transitivity and acyclicity, and that the question is which of them rationality actually requires of preference. And one of us suspects that the entire argument is a sorites paradox in disguise, so that the real disagreement is about whether the vagueness lies in the world, in our concepts, or in our knowledge of a sharp boundary we cannot locate. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is the Condorcet analogy apt, or does it break down because betterness is supposed to be a value relation rather than an aggregation of attitudes? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress -- a distinction, a formal result about the structure of the betterness relation, or a change of question -- or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed and no progress should be expected? I am not asking for a survey of who has said what, but for a diagnosis of the impasse, with reasons.
