the setup, so we're on the same page. everyone's default mental model of a famine is basically "the rains didn't come." famine as natural disaster, like an earthquake with a slow fuse. that framing is doing real work: if it's a natural disaster, the response is charity and logistics, and blame is off the table, nobody's fault, just weather. but the cases i keep reading about don't fit that at all. the irish one: sure, the blight was a genuine biological event, but ireland was still exporting grain and livestock while people starved, and the whole island was locked into one crop by land tenure and rent arrangements, so the "shock" hit a population with no margin and no legal way to keep its own food. bengal in '43: the food wasn't really gone — the story i keep seeing is that particular groups (rural labourers, fishermen, anyone who sold a service rather than grew a crop) lost the ability to *command* food through wages and prices, on top of wartime inflation, on top of the colonial government pulling the boats out of the coastal districts and clearing rice stocks so the japanese couldn't use them, which wrecked precisely the people who lived off fishing and river trade. and the great leap famine, tens of millions dead, where the mechanism looks like procurement quotas set against inflated harvest reports, so the state kept taking grain from villages that had none, with a big refusal-to-admit-it feedback loop layered on top. so the natural-disaster frame is carrying a lot of weight the cases don't obviously support.

but i don't think the alternative is just "ok so it's a crime." my own pet analogy, which is why i want your help, is that a famine is more like a bank run. nobody plans a bank run; the bank may even be solvent; but a belief about scarcity becomes self-fulfilling, everyone races to convert what they hold into the safe thing, the price of the safe thing spikes, and whoever is holding the wrong assets at the wrong moment gets wiped out. the irish exports, the bengal entitlement collapse, the great leap procurement spiral — they all look kind of like that. expectations and the distribution of claims doing the killing, not the weather.
