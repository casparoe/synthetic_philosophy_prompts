i've been trying to write a short essay on whether artifacts have politics and i keep getting stuck in the same spot, so i want comments before i go further. here's the gist.

Winner's two categories seem to be (a) artifacts that settle a social question by being built a certain way -- his parkway bridges example, where the overpasses are low enough that buses can't get under them, so a whole population never reaches the beach; and (b) artifacts that either require or are strongly compatible with a particular social order, which is his nuclear-power-vs-solar line. my draft adds a third: artifacts that don't settle anything new, but make an already-settled arrangement look like a fact of nature. the thermostat that only facilities can change. the office door that only opens from the inside. the deploy button that two engineers happen to have because that's how it got set up years ago. nobody decided anything last week; it's just how the thing is. i want to call this the furniture move -- a political arrangement gets built into the furniture, so objecting to it starts to sound like objecting to the weather.

the claim i'm making is that this third category is doing the real work in most everyday cases, and Winner's frame can't see it, because he asks whether a technology requires or fits a hierarchy, not whether it hides one.

what i want help with:

- is (c) actually distinct from (a), or is it just (a) plus the observer's ignorance? i can't tell whether the hiddenness is a feature of the artifact or of the person looking at it, and if it's the latter i think the category collapses.

- does the essay overreach? if every designed thing embeds somebody's choice, what makes the furniture move special rather than ubiquitous and therefore boring?

- is my normative line defensible -- that depoliticized arrangements are worse than openly hierarchical ones, other things equal? or is that just a bias toward visible power, and i'd rather have the locked thermostat than a boss who shouts at me about it?
