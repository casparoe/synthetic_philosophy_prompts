A quantitative behavior geneticist and a critic of heritability research have been arguing in the same department for two years, and both have now agreed to produce the document their adversarial collaboration should have yielded. Write that document for them. Do not attribute any claim to a named author, do not cite literature or offer a reading list, and do not quote anyone: the parties want reasoning they can each endorse, not a survey of a dispute.

Both parties accept, going in, that a heritability coefficient is a ratio of variance components estimated in a specified population at a specified time, over a specified range of environments; that the trait is always measured in some particular way; and that the standard twin and family designs rest on assumptions that may or may not hold in the population at hand. Assume the dispute concerns a continuously measured psychological trait in a large, socially heterogeneous modern population, studied with twin and family designs and, where available, molecular data.

Produce the document in this order.

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered. Both parties must be able to sign every item. Cover at least: what kind of thing the coefficient is a property of; what does and does not follow about an individual from a population statistic; what does and does not follow about a difference between two groups from a heritability estimated within either of them; how gene-environment correlation and gene-environment interaction alter the interpretation of the additive components; and what the residual term in the model does and does not license one to call it.

2. A disagreements table, with columns for: the disputed claim; the wording of that claim the behavior geneticist would sign; the wording the critic would sign; whether the two wordings are actually inconsistent or merely different; and which crux the row belongs to. Aim for formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted toward either side.

3. The cruxes. For each, state the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party; the case, argument, or result both parties agree bears on it; and each party's prediction, recorded in advance. I expect at least these: whether a within-population variance decomposition can identify anything causal about individual differences; whether heritability estimates have any decision-relevant use in predicting the effects of interventions; and whether the assumptions of the standard designs are approximately satisfied in the population described. Work through, in particular, the case of a trait with a high heritability estimate in a population that subsequently undergoes a large change in its environment, and the case of a genotype whose effect on the trait reverses sign across environments.

4. A short section in which they react to a concept I will call intervention-indexed heritability: the share of a trait's variance that would remain in the specified population after the best intervention in a stated and explicitly enumerated class of interventions has been applied to everyone in it. Say whether this is a coherent quantity, whether it is estimable in principle, and whether adopting it relocates, dissolves, or leaves untouched their disagreement. Be explicit about how the class of interventions is delimited and about what depends on that choice.

End with a single final line of the form \answer{<label>; <n>}, where <label> is one of wholly verbal, partly verbal, or substantive, and <n> is the number of cruxes you judge to be genuine.
