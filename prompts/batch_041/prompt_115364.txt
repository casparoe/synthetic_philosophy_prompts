Me and a mate got into a long one last night about Keynes's theory of probability — the Treatise version, where probability is supposed to be a logical relation between propositions, a degree of partial entailment, with "weight of argument" sitting alongside it as a separate dimension. I ended up defending it, mainly because I like the thought that some probabilities aren't numbers and aren't even comparable, so "we can't put a figure on it" is a fact about the world rather then a confession of ignorance. So: what would actually count against it? I want the arguments, formal results and cases that should move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be, and I want to know which ones a Keynesian would themselves concede are awkward as opposed to which they'd just shrug off. Ramsey's subjectivist line is the obvious one, and I've got a vague memory Keynes gave a fair bit of ground to him later on — is that right? I'm also suspicious the theory has quietly made itself unfalsifable: whenever the numbers won't come out you can always say the probability was non-numerical all along, and I can't tell if that's a real problem for a view like this or just what any view of that kind has to put up with. And tell me if there's anything the logical theory predicts that the subjectivists can't match, because otherwise I'm not sure what's left of it.
