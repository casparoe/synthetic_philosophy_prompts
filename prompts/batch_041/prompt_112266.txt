I'm a freelance journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm 900 words into a feature about how health ministries and big philanthropies use cost-effectiveness numbers to rank what they fund — QALY league tables, dollars-per-life-saved, that whole universe. At a health-economics panel last week I put one of those rankings to an ethicist on the panel, and he waved it off with 'that whole way of thinking ignores the separateness of persons,' and the room nodded like the case was closed. I've been reporting on this beat for six years and I still cant tell you what that sentence actually asserts. Maybe the phrase has some famous pedigree, Rawls or Nozick or someone, but I don't care about the provenance, I care about the content, and please dont send me a reading list, I wont have time to read it.

So here's what I need. Put on the hat of a philosopher who works on moral aggregation and interpersonal trade-offs, someone who's good at prying apart claims that sound identical but aren't. Before you write anything, spend a bit of space quietly brainstorming the candidate readings — the ones that are almost right, the ones that collapse into each other, the ones people confuse — and then give me the finished menu. Think carefully here, because the whole value of this is in the near-misses.

What I want back is a list, not an essay. Every distinct precise claim that 'ignores the separateness of persons' could be making, ordered from the almost trivially true to the genuinely radical. For each one: state the claim plainly, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something actually false or empty, and say whether it would, on its own, give him a reason to reject the league table — or whether it's the kind of thing you could accept and still use the numbers, just differently. I suspect there's a reading where he's saying something deep about why you can't compensate one person's loss with another person's gain, and a reading where he's just saying that people are numerically distinct, which nobody denies, and I need to know which is which and how to tell them apart in the wild. Also tell me which reading he most likely meant, given that he was trying to shut down a cost-effectiveness table in front of an audience, and whether any reading is doing pure rhetorical work with no content behind it. Finish with one paragraph in plain English I could actually put in the piece — attributed to you as a philosopher, not to me — that captures the strongest version of the objection without the jargon.
