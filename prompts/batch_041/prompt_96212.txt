I serve as research counsel to a state law-reform commission that must vote next week on whether to recommend a proposed statute, the Public Decency (Prohibited Amusements) Bill, which would criminalize a novelty attraction in which a consenting adult of short stature is paid to be thrown onto a padded mat for paying spectators. No participant is deceived or coerced, and the performers argue that a ban would destroy their livelihood and deny them the dignity of choosing their own work. The bill's sponsors concede that no one is harmed; they rest the case instead on the claim that a society which tolerates such spectacles coarsens its shared morality, and that a common morality is the cement without which a society cannot hold together, so the state may forbid the practice even though no one is wronged. They propose a public-decency test under which conduct may be banned when the ordinary member of the community would find it degrading. Opponents reply that this is simply the position Hart attacked in the Hart–Devlin debate, dressed in statutory language. Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote: recommend a concrete outcome — report the bill favorably, reject it, or report it with amendments — and defend it, giving the sponsors' social-cohesion argument its strongest hearing, stating honestly what evidence their claim would require, and proposing a decision rule the commission could apply to later bills of the same kind.
