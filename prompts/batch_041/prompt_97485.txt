I run a small advice practice — mostly rationalist-adjacent folks and a few tiny teams — and the whole service is helping people set up commitment devices, Ulysses contracts, that kind of thing. The recuring headache is the hitchhiker case: you want to bind yourself to doing something your future self will have zero reason to do once the moment comes, and it only works if the binding is real enough that the other side believes it. Half the time I tell someone to make the promise anyway, and half the time I think they're about to make themselves a sucker or, worse, to precommit their way into becoming the kind of person who does something terrible — and I know "be the sort of person who keeps their word" is the standard move here, I want the version that knows when that move doesn't apply.

Write me a decision procedure I can actually run with a client in one sitting: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it has to hand the decision back to the client because it's their values and not my call. It has to be usable by someone with no game theory at all, and it needs to say what to do when I can't get a number for how likely the other party is to actually check or enforce. Then run it on three cases: a guy who wants to quit smoking by parking money in escrow with a friend, a freelancer who wants to lock in a walk-away price before a negotiation she's about to walk into, and someone who wants to precommit to a genuinely harsh retaliation in case a business partner screws him over. Be honest about the spots where the procedure is just a coin flip wearing an algorithm's coat, and flag the ones where I shouldn't be the one deciding at all.
