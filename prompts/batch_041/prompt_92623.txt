For the back-of-book column I write on ideas and their discontents, I'm assembling a short piece on the comedy of self-binding, and I need one joke — a setup and a punchline I could actually deliver at a dinner party full of people who have read a little too much about precommitment. The domain is Ulysses contracts and their constitutional cousins. I don't want the obvious version, in which someone lashes himself to a mast or vows to quit smoking on Monday; I want something that turns on the machinery rather than the imagery. The machinery, as I understand it, runs like this. A precommitment holds only if the enforcer is deaf to the bound party's later, sincere, and possibly quite good reasons. In the old story the crew's ears are stopped with wax, and the standing order is that if the man begs to be released they are to bind him tighter still — so the contract is enforced by an agent who cannot hear the argument for breaching it. Call that the wax requirement. Its constitutional analogue is a rule that binds a future majority and is applied by an interpreter obliged not to weigh the current majority's reasons, and who cannot be released from that obligation by the very authority it constrains. Then there is the regress, which I find genuinely funny: the crew's obedience is itself a commitment that no one made on their behalf, so every act of self-binding leaves a residue of unbound enforcers somewhere further down the line.

The joke should be tellable in under a minute, should not require a footnote or a citation, and should not lean on the reader having any particular book in hand — Elster's name can come up in the column around it, but the joke must stand alone. So invent a small contemporary scene rather than re-telling the Odyssey: a startup, a wellness app, a homeowners' association, an ethics committee, a family. It needs a clear turn. The laugh should come from the wax requirement, or from the regress, or from the recognition that the only enforcer who can be trusted to hold you to a commitment is one who is indifferent to you or cannot understand you at all. A revealed-preference economist makes a serviceable foil — for that character every breach is simply a preference revealed and every contract therefore redundant — but keep the economist in a supporting role and out of the punchline. Give me two versions: one at the personal scale and one at the constitutional scale, where the joke has to survive the awkward fact that the bound party is a majority that never signed anything. And tell me briefly which few words are doing the work in each, so I can trim them in the edit.
