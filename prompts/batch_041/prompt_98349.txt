I've been lurking on rationalist blogs and forums for years now and I keep bouncing off the money-pump stuff. I get the basic trick -- you find someone whose preferences run in a circle, offer them a series of trades that each look good to them by their own lights, and they end up exactly where they started, minus a fee every time; same deal if their preferences flip between when you make the offer and when they actually act on it. What I can never keep straight is what the argument is supposed to prove. My background is RL rather than philosophy: I train agents, I think in reward functions, discount factors, value functions, policies, Bellman backups, and the fact that the reward function is fixed and exogenous -- that's basically the whole game for me.

So here's what I want. Translate the money-pump argument, and the surrounding debate about diachronic rationality (whether your earlier and later selves have to agree, whether it's rational to bind yourself, all that), into the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can manage. Then give me the residue report. Which parts go across cleanly? Is having intransitive preferences just having a policy that's suboptimal for any fixed reward function? Is a preference reversal over time just a non-stationary reward? Is a commitment device just an action that changes the state so your later policy is forced? And then the part I actually care about: what doesn't survive the trip? My suspicion is that the whole notion of diachronic rationality -- that there's a single agent who owes consistency to a past or future self -- just evaporates in my vocabulary, because there's no self there, only one optimizer and a reward signal, and if the reward changes that isn't irrationality, that's just a seperate problem. But tell me whether that suspicion is right or whether I'm smuggling in an assumption the RL framing can't actually support.

One more thing: don't just hand me the clean translation and call it done -- I want the places where the mapping has to be fudged, and I want you to say how confident you are in each claim you make, singling out the one you're least sure of. I'd rather have a calibrated answer than a confident one, especially on the residue, since that's where I suspect the interesting philosophy is hiding.
