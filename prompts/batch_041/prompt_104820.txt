I have been a potter for about a dozen years, and this past term I began sitting in on an introductory metaphysics course, mostly out of a vague curiosity about whether any of it would touch what I actually do in the studio. I did not expect the old puzzle of the statue and the lump of clay to be the thing that kept me up at night, but it has, because I now see that I hold a set of opinions about my own work that I cannot get to line up. When I take a lump of clay and throw it into a vase, I am inclined to say there is just one thing in front of me — I bought one bag of clay and never acquired a second object — and yet the vase and the clay seem to differ in what they can survive: knock the vase over and the vase is gone, while the clay remains, a flattened heap I can wedge up and use again. If two things can differ in their properties, they cannot be numerically one thing. So I seem forced to say either that there were two objects there all along, occupying exactly the same place, or that one of my two judgments is simply mistaken.

Here are my verdicts, such as they are. (i) The vase and the lump it is made of are two distinct things that happen to coincide. (ii) If I leave the clay unworked as a shapeless lump, there is only one thing — the lump — and no additional lump-shaped object shadowing it; the mere having of a shape does not create a second item. (iii) If I make a piece that is a vase when upright and, turned over, serves perfectly well as a bowl, it is still one object, not a vase and a bowl that coincide. (iv) If I smash a finished vase and glue the shards back together, the vase survives; but if instead I melt the clay down and throw an exactly similar vase from the same material, the new vase is a different vase, though it is the same clay. (v) If I replace the clay of a vase pinch by pinch with a different clay until none of the original material remains, I still think I have the same vase. (vi) My cat and the object that is exactly my cat except for one missing whisker occupy almost the same region, and I am certain there is only one cat there, not a legion of near-duplicates; the same goes for my house and the house-minus-one-brick.

What I want is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that actually generates this pattern — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again. My own tentative proposal is that two material things are distinct when one can survive a change the other cannot, and identical otherwise; but that seems to give me two objects in cases (v) and (vi) as well, which I do not want, so I suspect it is wrong or at least incomplete. Please tell me which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to give up, and why that verdict is the one to sacrifice rather than another; whether the surviving principle is a form of constitution-is-not-identity, or of the view that there is only ever one object and the apparent differences are differences in how we describe it, or of the view that identity itself is relative to a kind or sortal; and what each clause of the principle is doing, with the case that forces it. Finally, tell me what the principle predicts for a case I have not mentioned: a sandcastle that the tide is gradually eroding, where at every moment it seems arbitrary to say exactly which grains are the castle. If two of the principles I have gestured at disagree about that case, say which one you would keep and what that choice costs elsewhere.
