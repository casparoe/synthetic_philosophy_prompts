I need something I can actually defend if the student appeals, so tell me which sentence does the work and what's wrong with it, rather than a general impression.

The question (10 points): "We've looked at two inversions of reasoning, one associated with Darwin and one with Turing. (i) For each, say what assumption gets inverted and what replaces it. (ii) Are these the same move made twice, or two different moves — and does either one depend on the other? (iii) A critic objects: if competence can come without comprehension, then nothing really comprehends anything. Does that objection follow from the inversions? Explain."

The student wrote:

(i) Darwin: the usual assumption is that design has to come from a designer — if something looks purpose-built, then something with purposes built it. Darwin inverts this by showing you can get design out of a process with no purposes at all: variation plus selection, neither of which is aiming at anything, and the fit between organism and environment shows up anyway. So the explanation runs from mechanism up to purpose instead of from purpose down to mechanism. Turing: the usual assumption is that a machine can only do what we've told it to do, and that to do arithmetic or logic you have to know what arithmetic and logic are. Turing inverts this — a machine can be a perfect computing machine without knowing what arithmetic is, because following a rule isn't the same thing as understanding the rule you're following.

(ii) I think these are the same inversion twice. In both cases something that looked like it had to be the source — a mind with purposes, a mind with understanding — turns out to be downstream of something mechanical, which means purpose and understanding are really just names for the patterns of behaviour the mechanism produces. There's no extra ingredient and no leftover fact of the matter. On whether one depends on the other: Turing's does depend on Darwin's, I think, because the minds that built the computers were themselves products of the mindless Darwinian process. If minds were exempt from that, Turing's move would just push the question back a step and wouldn't really invert anything.

(iii) I'd say the objection does follow and we should just accept it. If comprehension is competence all the way down, then comprehension isn't picking out anything that competence wasn't already picking out, and calling something a comprehender is just shorthand. Dennett's slogan is about how competence gets produced, not about some extra ingredient added on top — so I don't see how he avoids the conclusion that nothing comprehends anything, including us.
