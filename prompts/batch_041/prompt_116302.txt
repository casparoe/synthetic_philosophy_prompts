I am an undergraduate taking a module on historical methods, and we have spent the past few weeks on archives, record-keeping, and the politics of what survives. During a seminar, one of my classmates kept returning to an analogy that I can neither fully accept nor fully dismiss, and I have since heard versions of it from a couple of other students. She says that an archive is like a museum: somebody decides what is worth keeping, the collection reflects the values and interests of the institution that houses it, and the gaps in the record are simply what the curator did not choose. On this view, the silences in the sources are not a scandal but the ordinary signature of selection, and the responsible historian should acknowledge them and get on with the analysis. I can see the appeal, and I can see that it captures something about appraisal, acquisition, cataloguing, and the way institutions shape what counts as worth preserving. But it also seems to flatter the archivist, and perhaps to excuse the state, the colonial administration, the firm, or the ordinary bureaucratic habit of recording some lives and not others. I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply agree or disagree with it.

Could you set out the correspondence explicitly—what plays the role of the curator, the acquisition committee, the donor, the object, the display case, the visitor, the storeroom, the catalogue, the conservation lab, and so on—and then say where the mapping breaks down? I am especially interested in which disanalogies actually matter for her conclusion that silences are just curatorial choices, and which are merely decorative differences. What does the museum picture quietly import—public display, aesthetic value, unique objects, a visiting public, deliberate acquisition—that the archive does not have, or has only in a different form? Does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion all along? I have also been trying to distinguish three kinds of silence: silences of omission, where no one thought to record something; silences of commission, where records were destroyed, withheld, or never created because someone preferred them not to exist; and silences of form, where the very categories of the record make certain experiences impossible to register. Does that threefold distinction carve the problem better than the museum analogy, or does it simply re-describe it? If the museum analogy is not the right one, what would be a better analogy, and what would it license that the museum analogy does not? Please be concrete about the archival practices and historical situations you have in mind, and do not just give me a list of abstract caveats.
