I spent a decade working on decision theory before leaving academic philosophy six months ago for a firm that designs long-horizon savings and commitment products, and until recently I would have said the money-pump literature was a tidy corner of the subject with no bearing on anything. Two things changed my mind. I used one of our own products, exited it early at a penalty, and then re-entered it three months later — a shape of behaviour I had previously encountered only in textbooks. And I have now been asked to run a ninety-minute session on money pumps and diachronic rationality for about fifteen colleagues — actuaries, product designers, two lawyers — none of whom has read any decision theory. I do not want the explanation; I want a plan for giving it.

First, the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit: which claim has to be in place before the next can even be formulated, and in particular where the distinction between a preference that is unstable within a single act of choice and one that is stable at each moment but changes over time must enter, and why it can neither come earlier nor later than that.

Second, the one case the session should hang on, with an account of what that case must have and must lack for the later material to land. I had assumed I would use the three-commodity cycle everyone uses, but it seems to me to display the trick and nothing else; tell me whether I am right about that, and if so propose a case that does the work, drawn from the world my audience actually inhabits.

Third, the misconceptions to expect, each located precisely in the sequence, and a judgement about which are errors to be corrected and which are defensible positions I shall have to argue against rather than dispatch. I anticipate at least these: that the pump merely shows the agent to be foolish; that an agent who anticipates the pump is thereby immune to it; that intransitivity is a harmless fact about tastes rather than a constraint on what can coherently be chosen; and that the whole matter is a curiosity about hypothetical agents with no bearing on our customers.

Fourth, one question to put to the room at the end that separates someone who has seen why the pump is disturbing from someone who has merely followed the steps.

Fifth, what I should say I am deliberately not covering at this level, and why.

The pivot I am least sure how to stage is the point at which the pump stops being a curiosity: it appears to bite only against an agent who does not see the whole sequence coming, so that the live question is not whether intransitive preferences are irrational but what rationality requires of an agent across time — whether the fault lies in the preferences, in the agent's inability to act on them as a whole, or in whoever is willing to trade against them. If the plan can make that fork feel open rather than a choice between two textbook positions, it will have done what I need. There is a whiteboard and no slides.
