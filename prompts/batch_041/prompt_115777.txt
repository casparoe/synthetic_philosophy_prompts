"Machine ethics is the project of getting computers to follow the right rules when they make moral decisions. There are two ways to do it. Top-down machine ethics programs a moral theory directly into the system — utilitarianism, Kantianism, a list of duties — while bottom-up machine ethics lets the system learn morality from data and examples instead. Top-down approaches all run into the frame problem, since a system cannot be handed in advance all the facts that will turn out to matter in a new situation, so the field has largely moved to bottom-up learning. This is why machine ethics and AI alignment have become the same project: both are trying to make sure the values a system ends up with match human values. Within the field, the important distinction is between operational morality, where the machine just follows the rules its designers gave it, and functional morality, where the machine actually understands right and wrong — that is, where it is conscious of what it is doing. The field's central thought experiment is the trolley problem, and a large online survey that asked millions of people what a self-driving car should do in a crash found no universal answer, which shows that any machine we build will simply encode the values of whoever trained it. The first serious attempt at machine ethics was Asimov's three laws, and their failure shows that rule-based approaches cannot work."
