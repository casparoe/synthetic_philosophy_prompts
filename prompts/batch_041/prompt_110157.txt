Write a mock regulatory bulletin, of roughly 750 words, in the deadpan register of a national standards body announcing the successful conclusion of a long inquiry. The conceit: a fictional Standing Committee on Orthographic Integrity, after years of consultation, certifies that it has isolated the essence of the lower-case 'a' — the form shared by every 'a' and by nothing that is not an 'a'. The Committee holds, in the manner of the essentialist view of letterforms, that each letter has a 'spirit' distinct from the style in which it happens to be rendered, and its bulletin is the report of a body that believes it has finally written that spirit down.

Format it as a numbered bulletin, with a document reference and a title, containing at minimum: a statement of purpose and jurisdiction; the certified essence itself, described in whatever terms the Committee adopts (a geometric template, a set of topological invariants, a checklist of components), precise enough to sound authoritative; a conformity-assessment procedure built around an invented quantitative instrument — a tolerance band, a fidelity coefficient, a deviation quotient — which the Committee applies with complete confidence and which should be visibly doing normative work that no measurement could do; adjudications of at least three hard cases, each with a deadpan ruling and reason; a brief quoted statement of dissent from an invented objector, reported without comment; and a closing note on forthcoming business that the framework is unlikely to survive.

The hard cases should include (i) a single-storey 'a', of the kind set in geometric sans and in italics, against the double-storey form most serif faces use; (ii) an 'a' from an older or regional hand — blackletter, uncial, a scribal form — where the Committee must decide whether history is a reason for exemption or a reason to be strict; and (iii) a form visually indistinguishable from a Latin 'a' that nonetheless belongs to another script, forcing the Committee to explain what its shape-based criteria are actually tracking. A fourth case is welcome if it earns its place, ideally a mark that passes every stated criterion while being plainly not a letter at all.

The humour must come from the substance, not from winking. Get the real difficulties right: that an essence extracted from exemplars is then used to judge those exemplars; that style and essence cannot be cleanly prised apart, since what counts as the same style is itself a matter of degree; that letter identity may depend on the surrounding system rather than on shape alone; and that a body which begins by describing forms will end by licensing them. No character may explain the joke, and the bulletin must never concede that anything has gone wrong. The target is the definitional impulse itself — the conviction that a category must have a specifiable essence, and that classification is therefore an administrative task. A typographer or a philosopher of categories should find the result fair rather than soft; a general reader should finish it able to see what is being satirised.

Invent the Committee and its instruments; do not model them on a named real institution, and do not cite real literature. Write throughout in British English.
