I'm on a panel next week arguing that global history's dream of telling the past from everywhere at once isn't just hard, it's self-undermining. My line: the fight over Eurocentrism has mostly been a fight about content -- whose discoveries, whose empires get airtime -- but the deeper problem is formal. Any actual narrative has to rank events by significance, and significance is always significance-for-somewhere, so a story told from everywhere at once either flattens into a chronicle where nothing counts for more than anything else, or it smuggles in a center and pretends it didn't. My proposal isn't "add more voices"; it's to drop the single synoptic story and write what I'm calling layered, standpoint-declared histories that stop pretending to a view from nowhere.

The room: mostly world and global historians, on board with the anti-Eurocentrism critique and also pretty attached to narrative as a genre; two early modernists who think "global" is an anachronism for their period; and one philosopher of history who will go straight at my definition of narrative.
