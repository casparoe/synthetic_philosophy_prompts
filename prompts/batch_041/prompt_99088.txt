Hey — I'm a second-year PhD in classics (late-Republican prose), and I have to present a draft essay at a seminar on Cicero's De Senectute next week and then take comments on it. I'd rather get a hard read from you first, before I'm standing up in front of people. Could you give me comments? I care most about (a) whether I'm actually getting Cato's arguments right, and (b) whether the central distinction I'm drawing is real or just me being clever. Honest beats kind here.

The gist of the draft. Cato the Elder, age 84 at the dialogue's dramatic date (150 BC), runs through the four standard charges against old age: that it pulls you out of public life, that it weakens the body, that it strips away the pleasures, and that it sits right next to death. He answers all four.

My claim is that every one of those answers is a consolation and not a vindication. A consolation says the alleged bad is bearable, or compensated, or not as bad as it looks. A vindication says old age is itself a good, or that a life is better for having this stage in it. I say Cato only ever does the first. Take the pleasure charge: he replies that the old can still enjoy the pleasures of the mind, and of farming. But the young can enjoy those too, so nothing there is specific to old age — he's shown the loss is coverable, not that the loss is a gain. Take the death charge: he offers either that the soul survives or that death is natural and timely. Again that's a reason not to fear it, not a reason to want the stage that brings it. So the whole performance is defensive, and being defensive is compatible with old age being, on net, a bad thing we should minimize. Which is a strange thing for a text everyone reads as a hymn to aging.

Where I might be wrong, and I say so in the draft: the one place Cato looks like he's doing more than consoling is authority — the old man's standing, the deference he gets. If that's a good the young don't have, then he really is vindicating. But then the thesis turns social: old age is good when a society happens to honor it. That's a claim about us, not about aging. I treat this as the strongest objection and try to absorb it rather than dodge it, but I'm not sure I've earned it.

Two things I fold in that I'm nervous about. First, the frame: Cicero wrote this in 44 BC, aged 62, freshly out of the game politically, and he hands the defense to Cato the Censor — the least mellow old man in Roman memory, the guy who wouldn't stop saying Carthage had to be destroyed. Choosing him to announce that old age is calm and pleasant seems almost like a joke, or a symptom. Second, I bring in a modern parallel: the finding that older adults often report emotional well-being as high as or higher than the young — Carstensen's socioemotional selectivity line, where people reprioritize toward emotionally meaningful goals as their time horizon shortens. I say this looks like Cato's structure all over again: it might be evidence that old age is genuinely fine, or it might just be adaptation, a consolation dressed up as a result. And then I more or less leave it there.

So: am I reading Cato right, or does he somewhere actually claim old age is a positive good and I've missed it? Is consolation-versus-vindication a distinction that survives contact with the text, or am I building a house to live in? And is that ending — the Carstensen parallel — doing real work, or is it the kind of move a seminar will rightly tell me to cut? I've be staring at this too long to tell anymore.
