I teach a 90-min upper-level seminar on social choice, about 16 students, and I want an in-class game that makes them feel Arrow's theorem as a trap they walk into themselves rather than a proof they nod along to. Not the usual "look, a Condorcet cycle, weird right" thing -- they've all seen a cycle. What I want them to actually feel is the IIA pressure: that a rule which looks scrupulously fair gets caught flipping its answer when an option nobody ranks first gets added or dropped, and that every patch they try to bolt on ends up handing some single person a veto. So have them build a decision rule together as a committee (give them roles, a few competing proposals, something at stake so they care which rule wins), then run a staged "constitutional review" where they test their own rule against demands they voted for themselves -- pairwise consistency, unanimity, nobody dictating -- and have to find which one they're forced to give up. I need the actual preference profile to seed, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each round onto the axioms; don't let them hear the name of the theorem until the end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce -- I'm guessing the induction to n voters, but say what else. And where is this design most likely to fail as a lesson? If it does fail, what result or argument would convince you to scrap it and rebuild?
