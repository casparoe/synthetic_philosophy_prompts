I teach a 90-minute session in a graduate seminar on social norms and cultural evolution — eighteen students, movable chairs, a whiteboard, no devices, and a co-facilitator. Everything has to run on paper, tokens, or points; nothing that requires real money. I want a game that makes the group feel the trade-off that talk of "tight" and "loose" cultures is supposed to capture, rather than hear me summarize it afterward.

The thing I want them to collide with is this. A group that has settled on some arbitrary convention should discover, when the environment shifts so that the convention is now a bad one, that it cannot simply abandon it — someone has to pay a personal cost to punish the first person who moves. A group that never settled on anything should discover the opposite failure: unanimous private recognition that a new option is better does not get that option adopted, because there is no mechanism to make anyone's move stick. I want both failures to come out of the same mechanism, enforcement, so that the punishment which makes coordination possible in one phase is the punishment that blocks adaptation in the next. And I want enforcement to be genuinely costly to the enforcer, so they feel the second-order problem — the monitor is paying for a public good and has every private reason to stop.

Two further things I care about, because they are what I find missing in most classroom treatments. First, the strength of a group's norms and its tolerance of deviance should come apart in the activity, not be fused: I want at least one condition where norms are strongly held and enforced but the group is genuinely permissive about harmless variation, so students see that these are two variables rather than one. Second, I want them to notice, at some point, that they are conforming only because of the fine — that the game has no way to produce the internalized conformity real cultures rely on — and I want that noticed inside the activity if possible, not just asserted in the debrief.

Constraints on the design: four or five people per group, with a role that rotates, so that everyone spends at least one phase as the enforcer; at least two phases and a shock between them; rules a facilitator can explain in under five minutes without using the words tight, loose, norm strength, or tolerance of deviance — those should not be spoken until the debrief. Materials should be printable from a single page.

Give me: the rules and setup, the payoff scheme with the actual numbers, a facilitator timeline with the shock placed in it, and a debrief script organized in the order the moments happened, mapping each moment onto the concept it is doing work for. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — where groups will coordinate too easily for any deviance to appear, where an enforcer will refuse to fine a friend, where someone will defect for entertainment, where a group will quietly invent its own enforcement and collapse the condition I set up — and what to do in each case without steering the outcome.

Then be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. I expect real tightness to be unchosen and to emerge from threat and subsistence pressure over timescales no seminar can simulate, and I expect participants to experience enforcement as imposed from outside rather than as something they own; tell me which features are like that and what I have to supply myself.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the design: the argument or the pilot observation that would lead you to rebuild it — for instance if norm strength and deviance tolerance turn out not to be separable, or if enforcement cost turns out not to distinguish the regimes — and say where the mapping from your mechanics onto the theory is weakest. I don't need citations or reading suggestions; I need the plan.
