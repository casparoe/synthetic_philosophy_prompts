I spent thirty-five years as a phylogenetic systematist and paleobiologist, and since retiring I have been reading intellectual history and the history of science with the enthusiasm of a late convert. I keep meeting the charge of Whig history — the habit of narrating the past as a story whose point is the present, sorting past actors into forward-looking heroes and backward-looking obstacles, and treating what happened as what had to happen — along with the now-standard objections to it. I grasp the objection in outline and do not trust my grasp of it. I would like it restated in the vocabulary I actually command.

Treat me as professionally fluent in phylogenetics, paleobiology, and the statistics of evolutionary inference: use the technical terms without glossing them, and assume I will notice if you reach for a loose analogy where an exact translation is available.

The task. Restate the Whig interpretation of history, and the principal objections to it, as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary. Then give me a residue report: for each element of the interpretation and of the critique, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Draw on the following resources, and be explicit when they fail to fit:

1. The tree and the ladder. A tree is a statement about relationships; a ladder is a statement about rank. A Whig narrative that designates one line of descent as the trunk and everything else as a sideshow is doing the ladder move on a tree. Say precisely what has to be assumed for that designation to be more than a choice of viewpoint, and whether the historical record could in principle supply the assumption.

2. Uniformitarianism, in the form I use it: past processes are inferred from present ones, so the present has a privileged epistemic role in reconstruction. Note that this anchors inference in the present without making the present a terminus. Ask whether the Whig historian's use of the present is an inference rule of this kind or a claim about where the sequence was heading, and whether that distinction can be drawn sharply or only in retrospect.

3. The anatomy of a progress claim. As I would put it, any claim that a sequence is progressive requires four separate commitments: a state space; a scalar defined on it; a claim that the scalar is monotone along the actual trajectory; and a reason to prefer higher values of the scalar. Perform this decomposition on the Whig claim and identify where the Whig historian and the critic must each supply something the record alone cannot.

4. Apparent trend without a driver. A random walk confined by a reflecting boundary will, sampled at the end, look directional, and whichever lineage happens to survive will look like the culmination of a trend even if nothing drove it. Ask whether the Whig pattern is a property of the process or an artifact of where the sampling stops, and whether the question is even well posed for a single unreplicated history.

5. Contingency and convergence. State the replay thought experiment plainly. If the same sequence recurs on independent runs, that is evidence for constraint or direction; if it does not, the appearance of progress is one draw. Ask which diagnosis the evidence available to historians could in principle support, and which of the two the Whig reading actually needs.

The residue. My own guess is that the evaluative element — progress as a standard rather than a direction — is what will fall out of the translation, but I would rather be corrected than confirmed; if the real failure lies elsewhere, say so. End with a verdict on the residue: is it a defect of my framework, which cannot express something real, or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked?

I am not asking for a literature review; I want the argument, not the scholarship.

Begin with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence — what survives the translation and what does not — and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question. About a thousand words.
