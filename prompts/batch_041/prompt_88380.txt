Im a software engineer at a small game studio, nine of us, I do the engine and the tools. No philosophy background, so if I use the wrong words for any of this thats on me.

We're making a time travel puzzle game, and before we started we put one thing in the trailer and in every interview: one timeline, no parallel universes, no do-overs, you can't branch history, whatever you do is what always happened. It started as a marketing line and now its a promise we can't walk back. The trouble is the writers and level designers keep pitching scenarios that I'm pretty sure break it, and when I push back we just argue in circles, because nobody can say what the promise actually commits us to. My lead asked me to write the rulebook so we stop having the same fight every week.

What I need is a procedure, not an essay. Something a level designer can run on a pitch in about fifteen minutes with no physics: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question turned out to be about story or taste rather than logic. It has to survive the pitches we actually keep getting, which are:

- the player goes back and kills their own ancestor, and the designer wants the gun to jam because the timeline refuses. Another designer says jamming is a cop-out and wants the player to succeed, with the game just continuing in a present where they were never born.
- the player's whole motive is to stop the disaster in the last act, and the designer wants the reveal that the attempt is what starts it.
- a puzzle where the code you need is left for you by your future self, plus a variant where nobody ever invented the code and it has no origin at all.
- the player meets themselves, and their future self sets up the puzzle the player solves, which is also what taught the future self to do it.
- a pitch where the only version of events that holds together requires the player's action to fail, but the player is holding a gun with full control of it. You can't render impossible.

Then run it on two of them for real. One: the ancestor one. Two: our flagship level, the one the whole game got pitched on, where the player goes back and actually prevents the disaster. I have a bad feeling your procedure kills that level, and if it does I want you to say so plainly and tell me the cheapest rewrite that keeps the level, not the cheapest excuse to keep it.

Two more things I need from it. The narrative lead signs off on story and I sign off on anything that touches the engine, so mark where the procedure is finished and where a person has to sign. And be straight with me about which steps are just our taste wearing a logic costume, and what the procedure can't settle at all. Related: my lead writer and my systems designer have been going at each other about this, writer says if there's only one consistent timeline then the player never actually chose anything and the whole game is a puppet show, designer says no, being consistent only rules out the actions that contradict themselves and leaves everything else free. I don't know which of them is right, and the rulebook needs to either take a side or be honest that for our purposes it doesn't matter. Don't send me a reading list or a list of films, I won't get to them.
