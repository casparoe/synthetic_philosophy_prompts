Quick background: I got into a dumb fight at a family thing last month — my uncle said something, I said something back, and it turned into a whole argument about whether we've actually gotten more civilized or just found politer ways to run the same old status dance. Since then I've been reading about honor cultures vs dignity cultures vs this newer "victimhood culture" idea, and I built an argument to get clear on it, and now the argument scares me because it seems to eat itself.

The typology I'm working with: honor culture = your worth lives in other people's recognition, so a slight really does injure you and you're expected to answer it yourself (duels, vendettas, "what are you looking at"). Dignity culture = your worth is intrinsic and doesn't depend on anyone's recognition, so you're supposed to shrug off insults and let institutions handle real harms. Victimhood culture = slights are treated as serious injuries, but you don't answer them yourself; you appeal to third parties (authorities, audiences, platforms) to make the wrongdoer account.

Here's the argument.

(1) Honor culture is defined by: a slight is a real injury to your worth, and it calls for a response.
(2) Dignity culture is defined by: a slight is not an injury to your worth, and needs no response.
(3) But look at how dignity-culture institutions actually work — courts award damages for defamation, employers fire people for harassment, reputations collapse over a bad post. All of these treat slights and status-attacks as real injuries that demand a response.
(4) Anything that treats a slight as a real injury demanding a response just is honor culture (from (1)).
(5) So dignity culture, as actually practiced, is honor culture.
(6) And victimhood culture is the same thing again — third parties instead of seconds, but the same logic of injury-and-redress.
(7) So there's really only one culture wearing three costumes, and the typology is an illusion.
(8) If there's only one culture, then none of them is morally superior to the others — you can't rank a thing against itself.
(9) But that puts a blood-feud society and a society with courts on the same moral footing, which is absurd.
(10) So one of (1), (3), or (4) has to go.

I don't want to give up (3) — it seems obviously true about how courts and HR work. And I don't want to give up (1), because then I don't know what "honor culture" even means. So I feel like I'm stuck with (4), but (4) looks like it just falls out of (1), so I can't see the seam. Tell me where it actually breaks, why that step looked airtight, and what each option costs — if I give up (4), do I lose the ability to say a duel and a lawsuit differ in kind, or only in degree, and does that cost me the ability to call one culture better than another? And be honest with me: if the whole three-way typology is mush, or if the argument is really just a relativism I should own up to, say so plainly instead of smoothing it over. I'd rather be told my framing is confused than get a tidy answer that quietly assumes the typology is sound.
