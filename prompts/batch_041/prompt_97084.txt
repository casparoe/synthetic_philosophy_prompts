I've been going to a philosophy discussion group since I retired (I spent thirty-odd years doing spectroscopy, so I'm used to arguments that terminate in a number, which may be why this one drives me up the wall). Last week two of the regulars, Marcus and Dee, went at each other for alot of the session over something called the reflection principle, and I genuinely couldn't tell who was making sense. I'd definately like you to walk through the whole exchange and tell me who's right about what, point by point.

Here's the principle the way I understood it (paraphrasing — if I've mangled it, say so): if you're sure that at some later time you'll be, say, 90% confident in some claim, then you should already be 90% confident in it now. Your future opinion is itself evidence about the claim, so you should take it into account in advance. I think it's named after a philosopher of science, van Fraassen, but don't quote me on that.

Marcus defended it like this. (1) If you're certain you'll end up at 90%, then you're certain your future self will have looked at the matter properly and landed at 90%. But that future self is you — same standards, more thought. So being at 60% today means you're already treating your own future judgment as a broken instrument, and you have nothing to check it against except that same judgment. The only consistent position is to be at 90% now.

Dee borrowed a story of mine for her reply. (2) When I was a grad student I kept a calibration chart for late-night work: on the nights when I'd been up past two and felt 90% sure a result would hold, it held about 70% of the time. So take a night like that. It's 11 p.m., I know the feeling I get at 3 a.m., and I know that at 3 a.m. I'll be 90% confident in H. The principle says: be 90% in H now. But I have the chart. The chart says 70%. So the principle is simply false.

(3) Marcus said that isn't a counterexample, it's a case the principle doesn't cover. The principle is about trusting your future self as a thinker; it doesn't say you must copy down whatever number a tired brain produces. If you know the process that will generate the number is broken, you aren't required to defer to its output. It only binds when the future credence is your own careful response to evidence.

(4) Dee said that's either circular or empty. Every time I know what my future credence will be, I also know something about why — and if the why is bad you exempt the case, while if the why is good the principle just tells me to do what the evidence already told me to do. She added: suppose I know that tonight's run will give a decisive result and that tomorrow, reading it, I'll be 90% confident in H. Does the principle really tell me to be 90% in H tonight, before the data exists? That isn't deference to a better thinker; that's getting the experiment's answer without running it.

(5) Marcus said no — there's a difference between 'I expect tomorrow to bring evidence that will move me' and 'I'm certain that once I've settled this I'll be at 90%.' The first is just ordinary reasoning about the future; the second is a verdict I'm committed to, and I can't both own the verdict and refuse to hold it now.

(6) Dee said that's the same escape hatch every time: whenever the number is bad, the future self turns out not to be 'really me,' and whenever it's good, the principle has nothing to add. So it's either false or it's empty.

So: go through moves 1 through 6, rate each one, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing. One thing I kept wanting to raise but didn't want to wade in on: is the principle supposed to be the strong claim that I should match my future self's number exactly, or the weaker claim that I should match my best estimate of what that number will be? Those two came apart in Dee's chart example, and neither of them ever seemed to notice.
