For a 90-minute session with sixteen philosophy graduate students in a course on set theory for philosophers, I want an in-class activity — not a lecture — that makes them feel why Zorn's lemma has the hypothesis it has. Most of them can recite the lemma and have used it once or twice, but they treat it as a machine that hands over a maximal object, and they have never felt that the chain condition is doing any work.

By the end, they should have felt all of the following in the doing, and I would rather they run into each one before I name it:

- that the interesting step is the limit step: extending one element at a time is trivial, and everything hinges on whether the object built so far, taken all together, is still the kind of thing they were allowed to build;
- that the chain condition is a substantive hypothesis that can simply fail, so that a group playing by one set of rules grinds to a halt and ends up with a maximal object, while a group playing by another set of rules can extend forever and arrive nowhere — and that when the hypothesis fails, the conclusion fails with it;
- that maximal is not maximum: what they arrive at need not contain what they wanted, and a table that made different arbitrary choices arrives somewhere else, equally legitimate, with nothing in the rules preferring either;
- that maximality is relative to the collection they chose to work inside: a table that imposes an extra restriction at the outset gets something maximal among the restricted things that could still have been extended had the restriction not been there — the standard way the lemma gets misapplied;
- that existence is not construction: at each step someone had to pick among several legal moves for no reason, and nothing in the procedure guarantees any particular outcome.

The rules, materials, and exact card or rule content should be concrete enough that I can prepare them tonight without a textbook, and the activity should avoid the words poset, chain, upper bound, and maximal until the debrief, where the group should assemble the lemma's statement themselves and say what breaks if the hypothesis is deleted. Note that any finite board runs out of cards, and running out of cards is not the same thing as maximality: build in whatever stipulation is needed so the group can feel the difference between stopping because nothing more is permitted and stopping because the supply ended, and tell me plainly how much of the infinitude you are stipulating rather than producing.

Give me the rules and materials, the timeline, the roles (including whatever referee or adversary role the design needs), the debrief that maps what happened onto each bullet above — including at least one genuine application where the chain condition is verified by hand, so they see what that verification costs, and at least one plausible-looking choice of collection where it fails, so they see that the lemma simply cannot be applied there, plus a plain statement of what kind of principle they have been using (one of a family of equivalent maximal principles, not something the axioms they had before can supply) — the things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle them, and an honest note on which features of the lemma the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
