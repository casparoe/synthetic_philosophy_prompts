I travel a good deal for work, and for the past couple of years I've been keeping a notebook of my gut reactions to the small local rules I run into, mainly because a colleague insists my reactions are incoherent and I want to see whether he's right. Here is the list as it stands. In the United States I tip twenty percent, and I would feel I had wronged the server if I didn't, even though I think the whole arrangement is unjust and would vote tomorrow to abolish it. In Britain I queue, and cutting in strikes me the same way the tip does: not merely rude but a wrong done to the people behind me. In Japan I take my shoes off at the door of a private home and would feel I had done something wrong to leave them on, although shoes on a floor harm no one and my host might not even notice. By contrast, in places where guests are expected to bring wine or flowers to dinner, I often arrive empty-handed, feel mildly awkward, and do not think I have done anything wrong. On an empty London escalator I will happily stand on the left if the mood takes me, and it never crosses my mind that this is a wrong, even though standing on the right is the local rule and I observe it scrupulously when the escalator is crowded. In Munich, on the other hand, I stand at an empty crossing at midnight and wait for the green man, and stepping off the kerb with no car anywhere in sight feels wrong to me, not merely illegal. In New York I cross against the light in the middle of the day with traffic visible two blocks away and feel nothing at all. And when I was told, in a country I would rather not name, that having my permit processed in reasonable time would mean a small payment to the clerk, I refused, and the fact that this was simply how things were done there seemed to me to have no claim on me whatever, even though refusing cost me three weeks and the clerk's family presumably needed the money as much as any waiter does.

The visitor question is the part that actually nags at me, because I am a guest in all of these places and I cannot tell whether that makes my obligations stronger or weaker. I keep the queueing rule in Britain out of something like gratitude to my hosts, and I keep the tipping rule in America although I am not a party to the wage bargain that is supposed to make the tip owed, and an American friend tells me I am simply being exploited by it. Yet in countries where haggling in the market is expected, I pay the first price offered, my local friends laugh at me, and I feel only that I am bad at it rather than that I have failed in a duty. I cannot find the difference between the cases where being a guest seems to bind me and the ones where it leaves me free.

I have tried to write down the rule I am actually following and it keeps collapsing. My first attempt was: follow a local convention when breaking it would harm someone or would be taken as contempt for the people who keep it, and ignore it when it is a mere coordination device with no victim. That gets the tip, the queue and the bribe roughly right, but it makes the shoes and the wine come out the same way and they plainly do not, since the shoes are harmless and binding while the wine is harmless and not; and it makes the empty escalator and the midnight crossing come out the same way when they do not either, though both are harmless bits of coordination with nobody watching. So either I need two principles where I thought I had one, or one of my reactions has to be surrendered, or something I have not noticed is doing the work throughout — how much I like the people, how recently I was scolded, how badly I want to be thought a good guest. What I want from you is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that actually generates these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which work and which case forces each clause. Then tell me what it implies for cases I have not listed — a visitor in a country whose conventions are morally odious to him, someone who emigrates permanently and keeps the conventions of home, a native who has stopped believing in the point of a convention but still follows it, a convention that has decayed so that nobody observes it but everyone expects visitors to — and say where I would have to change a verdict in order to keep the principle intact. Finally, tell me honestly which of the reactions above the principle cannot accommodate; I would rather be told that one of them has to go than have it quietly fudged.
