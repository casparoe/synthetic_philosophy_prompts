I spent most of last night in a long, circular argument with a close friend about phenomenal consciousness, and I cannot let it go. She is an illusionist: she holds that the felt, what-it-is-like character we seem to find by introspection is not a genuine feature of the world but a systematic misrepresentation the brain generates about itself, and that once this is accepted the hard problem dissolves rather than gets solved. I could not accept it. If there is no phenomenal feel, I asked, what exactly is doing the misrepresenting, and what is the illusion an illusion of? She said the question presupposed the very thing under dispute. We went back and forth for hours, neither of us moved, and I came away convinced that we were really fighting over a cluster of questions that no one has settled, and that I do not even know how to rank.

Please help me triage. Set aside for now the question of whether illusionism is true; assume I want to make a genuine contribution to the debate over the next few years. I have roughly three years, a solid background in philosophy of mind, and enough cognitive science to read the empirical literature. Which open questions in and around illusionism most deserve attention, and in what order should someone work on them?

I want the reasoning more than the list. For each candidate, tell me why it is upstream of other work (what else becomes easier once it is settled), how tractable it is for someone working mainly with conceptual tools, whether it is currently neglected, and how central it is to whether illusionism stands or falls. Among the candidates I can already see: whether the illusion is a doxastic illusion, that is, a false belief about experience, or a phenomenal one, and whether any formulation avoids covertly presupposing the phenomenal; whether illusionism can explain the apparent transparency of introspection without helping itself to what transparency seems to reveal; the self-stultification worry, that is, whether a wholly non-phenomenal system can sincerely report on its own seeming-states; whether the view is a form of eliminativism or instead a re-description of realism, and whether that dispute is merely verbal; what would count as empirical evidence for or against it; and the meta-problem of why creatures like us would represent ourselves as having phenomenal experiences at all. Feel free to add questions I have not named and to tell me which of mine are distractions.

Rank them, defend the ranking against the obvious objections (tractability versus importance, for instance), and tell me what developments over the next couple of years would change the order. Please write your answer in American English.
