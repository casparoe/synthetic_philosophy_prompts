I am a torts practitioner. I have a working command of negligence doctrine — misfeasance and nonfeasance, the general absence of a duty to rescue and its recognised exceptions, breach, but-for and proximate causation — but no formal training in moral philosophy. I want to understand the philosophical debate about the doing/allowing distinction, and specifically the cluster of questions about killing versus letting die and acts versus omissions, by having it restated in the vocabulary I actually use.

Take the principal families of position in that debate one at a time: first, the view that the distinction between doing and allowing marks a morally basic difference; second, the view that it marks no intrinsic moral difference at all and that our reactions to it are explained by something else; and third, the intermediate view that the distinction is real but derivative — that it matters only because of what it tracks, such as intention, the violation of a right, or the alteration of a course of events that would otherwise have run its course. Translate each of these as faithfully as you can into the doctrinal categories of tort: the act that triggers a duty versus the omission that does not, the special-relationship, undertaking, creation-of-risk and reliance exceptions to the no-duty-to-rescue rule, the line between breach and a mere failure to confer a benefit, and the causal apparatus of but-for and proximate cause. Do not assume in advance that the philosophers are really talking about legal duty; where a position resists the translation, resist it visibly rather than bending it into doctrine.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims carry over cleanly, which survive only in a distorted form, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I am most interested in three questions. First, whether the philosophers' distinction corresponds to anything more than the law's distinction between an act that creates a duty and an omission that leaves the pre-existing state of affairs alone — or whether, once the translation is done, the moral asymmetry turns out to be doing no work beyond the conventional allocation of duties. Second, what becomes of the clinical case in which a treatment already begun is withdrawn rather than never initiated: the doctrinal ideas of undertaking, reliance and creation of risk seem to make the withdrawal the graver of the two, whereas the clinical consensus, as I understand it, treats them as morally equivalent, and I want to know whether that equivalence can be stated in my vocabulary or only denied in it. Third, whether the residue is best read as a defect of the doctrinal framework — something it cannot see — or as evidence that the philosophical distinction is less determinate than it presents itself as being.

I do not need citations or a reading list; what I need is the mapping and, more importantly, the places where the mapping fails. And do not let the doctrinal categories quietly settle the philosophical question: if a translation succeeds only by presupposing that duties are conventional, say so explicitly.
