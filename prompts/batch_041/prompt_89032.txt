I am midway through a seminar on population ethics, and I keep running into a one-liner that my classmates treat as self-evident while using it to do very different work: 'you cannot benefit someone by bringing them into existence, though you can certainly harm them.' The last time was during a discussion of pronatalist policy — a classmate had argued that generous child benefits are justified because of the good lives they would produce, and a friend, who is otherwise the most careful person in the room, closed the exchange with that sentence, evidently taking it to show that the argument fails at its first step. I have since heard the same formula deployed to establish that there is no duty to reproduce, that procreation is at best morally neutral and possibly a wrong, that the never-born cannot be wronged, and that the so-called procreation asymmetry is merely the non-identity problem in different dress. I cannot tell whether these are four distinct claims, one claim restated four ways, or one defensible claim buried under three confusions, and I would like a map of the possibilities.

So please treat the sentence as a slogan and separate out the precise theses it could be expressing. Among the distinctions I would want drawn: whether it is an axiological claim about the comparative value of existing and not existing, or a deontic claim about what we owe and to whom; whether the operative notion of benefit is a welfare comparison, the satisfaction of an interest, or something impersonal; whether it presupposes that nonexistence can be evaluated as a condition at all, or instead denies that any comparison is available; whether it is a claim about the interests of the never-born person or about the reasons and permissions of the prospective parent; and whether it requires some person-affecting restriction on what counts morally. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and note whether the reading that does the work also carries commitments the speaker would likely disown — for instance, that a life well worth living was nonetheless not worth starting, or that it would have been better had no one ever existed. Most important, say which reading my friend must have had in mind for the sentence to accomplish what he wanted, namely to defeat the claim that child benefits are justified by the good lives they produce, and flag any reading on which the pronatalist argument survives untouched. I do not need the history of the idea or a reading list, only the conceptual anatomy.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. State how confident you are in each verdict you give, and single out the one point about which you are least sure.
