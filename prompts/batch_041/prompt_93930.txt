The question the students were given reads as follows. Scanlon's contractualism is often said to have an aggregation problem. In lecture we distinguished three places where aggregation might enter a framework of this kind: (i) summing the strengths of individual complaints, so that a principle can be rejected because of the total burden it imposes even when no single person's complaint would be decisive on its own; (ii) counting the number of people who have complaints of equal strength, so that numbers decide when no one's complaint is stronger than anyone else's; and (iii) counting the number of people whose complaints are individually weaker, so that the sheer number of complainants outweighs one stronger complaint. Consider two cases. In the first, you can save either one person from a slow and agonising death or five people from a quick and painless one; everyone dies otherwise, each of the five faces a lesser harm than the one, and the five harms together still exceed the one. In the second, you can save either one person or five, and all six would suffer exactly the same fate. For each case, say which of (i) to (iii), if any, is needed to reach the answer, and say whether Scanlon's formulation can accommodate it. Then say whether the three loci are genuinely distinct or whether some collapse into others; if you think any of the distinctions are merely verbal, say which are real and which are not.

The answer I am looking at reads as follows. The reasonable rejection test asks whether a principle for the general regulation of behaviour could be reasonably rejected by anyone it would apply to, and an act is wrong when no set of principles permitting it survives that test. The test is not a weighing of benefits against burdens; it asks whether the principle could be justified to each person who has to live under it. The individualist restriction follows from this. In assessing a principle we look at each person's complaint on its own and we do not add complaints together, because adding them up would let the good of many outweigh the loss of one, which is exactly what contractualism denies. In practice the restriction amounts to the maximin rule: since the person with the largest complaint has the strongest objection, a principle is acceptable only if the person who would fare worst under it could not reasonably reject it, which is why the view is often described as close to Rawls's difference principle. The aggregation problem is that this seems to give the wrong answer when numbers are involved. If we save the one, each of the five can reasonably reject the principle permitting that, because each of them dies; if we save the five, the one can reasonably reject the principle permitting that, because he dies; so the test seems to have no verdict, or the wrong one. The restriction is best understood as a version of the veil of ignorance: the parties choose principles without knowing whether they will turn out to be the one or one of the five, and that is what stops them from choosing principles that sacrifice a minority. Case one: one slow painful death against five quick painless ones. Here the complaints are unequal, since the one's complaint is stronger than any single one of the five's. The restriction tells us to compare complaints one at a time, so we compare the one's complaint with the complaint of one of the five, and the one's is stronger. Contractualism saves the one. That is the right answer, and it shows that the restriction is not a defect but the point of the theory. Case two: one against five, all facing the same death. Now the complaints are equal, so the tie-break applies, and this is the second of the three loci. Contractualism can save the five: each of the five has a complaint equal to the one's, so the five complaints together are greater than the one's, and the one cannot reasonably reject a principle that saves the greater number. This shows that the second locus is really the first locus in a special case, since aggregating complainants is just aggregating complaints when the complaints are all the same size. So the restriction only forbids aggregation where the complaints are unequal, which is to say that contractualism does aggregate, as long as it aggregates like with like. It never has to weigh a stronger complaint against a weaker one, so the individualist restriction survives intact and the third locus is never needed.
