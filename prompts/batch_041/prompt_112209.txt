I'm in a grad seminar on early modern women's philosophy and we keep circling back to Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz — specifically her Reply to Sor Filotea, the 1691 letter where she answers the bishop of Puebla, who had published her critique of a Portuguese Jesuit's sermon and then, writing under a woman's name, told her to quit the secular learning and stick to theology. I've read the letter and I know the backstory, so don't summarize it for me. What I want is to treat her as a theorist of women's education and run her theory against a real episode she obviously never anticipated: the exclusion of Afghan girls from secondary school and then from university after the Taliban's return to power in 2021. The salient facts, which you can just take from me since you can't look anything up: the ban came in stages, applied to girls past roughly the primary level and later to universities and to most vocational and health training; officials have defended it with a shifting mix of justifications — security, curriculum reform, "Islamic" norms, waiting for the right conditions — while some clerics inside and outside the country have publicly argued it has no basis in Islamic law; families and former teachers have responded with secret home schools, online classes, and small unregistered study circles, often with girls studying in hiding and under real risk. I don't need a bibliography or citations, and I don't need you to tell me what the Taliban says it wants — I need the theory-to-case mapping and the case-back-to-theory part.

You're a historian of early modern Hispanic thought who also works on philosophy of education, so bring that double expertise. Before you write the analysis, spend a short section thinking out loud: lay out Sor Juana's position as a set of separable arguments rather than one blob. The pieces I keep coming back to, roughly, are: (1) her claim that the intellect has no sex and that a God-given inclination to learn can't be something she's forbidden to act on, so it can't be sinful to follow it; (2) her scriptural argument — Paul bars women from the public pulpit, but scripture also has women instructing women and Christ letting a woman sit and learn, so she reads the prohibition as about teaching authority, not about study, and concludes women must be educated precisely because they're the ones who will teach other women; (3) her catalogue of learned women from scripture and the Fathers as precedent; (4) her autobiographical bit — reading from age three, wishing as a girl she could pass as a man to sit in the university, being ordered by a superior to stop reading and finding she couldn't stop, so she studied everything God made instead of books, and her crack that one can philosophize while cooking dinner and that if Aristotle had done the cooking he'd have written more; (5) the rhetorical shell around all of it — she keeps insisting she's obedient and submits her judgment to the Church while continuing to argue; and (6) the modesty of the actual demand: she never asks for the pulpit, for public office, or for coeducation, only to be allowed to study, and mostly in private. Feel free to refine, split, or correct that list — say if I'm lumping things that pull in different directions.

Then do the analysis in both directions. First, run her arguments against the Afghan case: who the relevant actors are (the ministry issuing the decrees, the clerics who endorse or reject them, the families, the girls themselves), which of her six arguments actually gets a grip on a state decree backed by force rather than on a bishop's pastoral pressure, what would count as evidence for or against each, and which ones the case simply walks past. Second, be honest about where she's silent or uncomfortable: her deference to religious authority looks like a problem when the ban is itself justified religiously; her strongest argument may be about one gifted individual's vocation rather than about schooling for a whole population; she has no vocabulary of rights or autonomy to fall back on, and it's not obvious her framework condemns the ban rather than just negotiating with it; and her insistence on studying privately, without any institutional demand, may be either admirably minimal or a fatal concession depending on how you read it. Third, the reverse direction: what does this episode show about her view — does it make her look more radical than she presents herself, does it show her theologically framed defense is hostage to whoever holds interpretive authority, and would a plain liberal rights argument do better or worse here than hers? Finally, tell me where the analogy breaks and where you'd resist it — the colonial Inquisition-era context versus a nationalist insurgency, a pastoral letter versus a state ban, one celebrated nun versus a mass exclusion of ordinary girls — and whether any of those disanalogies are load-bearing for the conclusions you drew. Be disciplined about the mapping, don't force her to have an answer where she doesn't, and flag clearly which claims are hers, which are your reconstruction, and which are your own view.
