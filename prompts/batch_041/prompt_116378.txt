Last night my friend Tom and I went at it for like three hours about this view he's been working himself into. He calls it tranquilism — he might of picked the word up somewhere, but the position is his own and I want you to just take it at face value. Here it is: the only thing that makes a life go well is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. Pleasure isn't a separate good on top of that, it's just the absence of a lack, so once you've got zero craving and zero suffering there's nothing left to add — no amount of love or achievement or understanding makes that life any better than it already is. He says if you could park someone in a coma with no suffering at all, that would be the best possible life, and everything the rest of us do is just fussing around the edges of not hurting.

I don't buy it, but I can't shake it either. I don't want a summary of the view and I definately don't want a lecture about how it's just Buddhism with the serial numbers filed off. What I want is the most important implications. If he's right, what actually follows? Concretely: what should I do differently about my own life and my work — do love and curiosity and getting good at something have any value left, or are they just painkillers? What does it mean for how I raise my kid, and for how I sit with a friend who's grieving? And the part I really want: what does the view commit him to that he hasn't noticed yet? Where does it get weird or start eating itself — like, is craving supposed to be a kind of suffering already, in which case the whole thing is true by definition and says nothing, or is craving not suffering, in which case why is it bad? Give me the consequences that actually bite, not a reading list.
