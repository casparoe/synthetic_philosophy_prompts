I am a novelist, and I have agreed to open a summer workshop for working fiction writers with a ninety-minute session on the problem of old evidence. The room will hold twenty novelists with no philosophical training, a few of whom say Bayesian when they mean sensible and most of whom assume that evidence is by definition whatever arrives after the fact. I do not want the problem explained to me. I want a plan for how to explain it: what has to come first, what carries it, where the predictable misunderstandings form, and what I should refuse to attempt.

I am fairly sure that nothing about the puzzle will land until they have felt that confirmation turns on a ratio of likelihoods rather than on the novelty of a fact — that a fact's being old is, by itself, no reason for it to support a hypothesis less — and only then the collapse: that a proposition already certain cannot be moved by conditionalizing on it, since updating on what one already knows leaves the credence exactly where it was. So the sequence must put credence, likelihood, and conditionalization in place before I am permitted to say that general relativity was regarded as strongly confirmed by a residual in Mercury's perihelion that had sat on the books for half a century. Tell me what else has to precede that sentence, and what inside it must wait.

For the carrying example I would rather not use Mercury, for two reasons: it is overfamiliar, and I am told the theory was built with that residual very much in view, which makes it an awkward specimen for the very distinction I need. I have been experimenting with a scene of my own: a woman has known for thirty years that her husband cannot bear the sea — an unremarkable fact of the marriage — and it becomes evidence only when she forms the hypothesis that he never left the country in the summer of 1987. Can that carry the session? If so, what does it have that Mercury lacks, and what does it risk smuggling in (that she had forgotten the fact, that she had never noticed its bearing, that the fact is new to the reader)? If not, what should carry it, and what must that example be careful not to be?

I also want the map of misconceptions, with the point in the sequence at which each forms and the sentence I say to head it off: that this is the problem of priors under another name; that it is a complaint about logical omniscience; that the repair is simply to conditionalize on the old fact together with the proposition that the theory entails it; that the counterfactual credence — what she would have believed had she never formed the theory — is easy to specify; that the difficulty shows the Bayesian apparatus to be refuted rather than an idealization doing quiet damage; and that old evidence means evidence that has stopped being surprising. Then the one question to put to the room at the end that would show me whether they have the problem or merely the vocabulary, and the two or three things I should announce that I am not going to explain at this level — among them, I think, whether any of the proposed repairs actually works.
