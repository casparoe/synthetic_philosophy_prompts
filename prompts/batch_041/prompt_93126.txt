I am taking a course on the Scientific Revolution and have run into something about Kepler that I cannot get straight, and I would like you to explain the conceptual issue to me rather than simply summarize his opinions. My difficulty is this. Kepler is usually presented as the man who took the machinery out of astrology: he had no patience with the signs of the zodiac, the twelve houses, or the planetary rulerships treated as real powers; he rejected the idea that the planets act on us through hidden or occult qualities; and he was contemptuous of the sort of astrology that claims the stars determine a person's character or fortune. Yet he went on casting horoscopes throughout his life, and he defended a pared-down astrology built on the aspects, the angular relations between the planets, especially the sextile, square, trine and opposition. As I understand the rationale, the aspects are supposed to work through harmony: the soul — and not only the human soul but the soul of the Earth as well — is innately furnished with the same geometrical archetypes that structure the heavens, so that when the planets stand in a given configuration the soul is moved by the correspondence between the two. Light and rays are doing genuine physical work elsewhere in his natural philosophy, but the aspect itself seems to be a purely relational, geometrical fact, and I cannot see how a relation could be a cause.

Here, then, is the puzzle I would like explained. If the configuration has no causal power of its own, then whatever the soul does in response must come from the soul's own innate geometry, and in that case the heavens look like a mirror in which the mind reads back its own structure; it is then hard to see why the reading should tell us anything about what is happening in the world, let alone about a particular person's temperament or fate. If, on the other hand, the configuration does carry a real influence, then the occult qualities he threw out of the front door appear to be coming back in through the window, merely relabelled as a formative or configurational power. What I want is an account of whether his reformed astrology is a stable position or an unstable compromise between these two horns, and if it is stable, exactly what has to be true of the world and of the soul to make it so. Does the appeal to the soul's innate harmony actually dissolve the tension, or does it merely relocate the mystery from the sky into the soul? Does he settle on one horn, or does he try to have it both ways?

I would also like you to say whether the move of treating the aspect as a sign of something rather than as a cause of it is a genuine third option or a way of changing the subject, and whether the harmony he invokes is supposed to be a fact about the world or a fact about us. Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would explain it in a long letter to a student — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — and please argue it out rather than handing me a list of things to read; I do not need citations and will track down the sources myself.
