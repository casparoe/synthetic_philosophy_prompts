Ok, so I volunteer a couple days a month at our state archive and something about how we decide what to keep has been bugging me. Roughly how it works: agencies send us their records, and archivists like me go through them and keep maybe 2 to 5 percent. The rubric says to keep records that show how the government organized itself and what it did, records that document people's rights and entitlements, and records with lasting evidential or informational value, and to get rid of the rest. In practice that means we keep the governor's schedules, the bills, the org charts, and the police files on a big protest from the 90s, but we shred the welfare office's case files, and last month we turned down a box of the protest group's own newsletters and meeting minutes because they weren't state records and we don't have the room. There's also a seperate rule that personal info about private individuals gets destroyed after 30 years unless they're public figures. So we keep the file the state built about you and destroy anything you left about yourself, we keep the powerful person's day and drop the poor person's, and nobody here thinks any of this is political. They think it's just good records management. That's what gets me.

I want to know what we're actually presupposing when we do this. What view of the past, of value, of who even counts as a subject of history does a rubric like that only make sense under? Which parts are genuinely required by the practice and which are just habits a different theory would allow just as well? And if you swapped the underlying view for something like "the archive exists to preserve the records of the governed, not the governing," what in the actual practice would have to change?

Please give me:

1. What we're doing, in your words (about 75 words) -- restate the practice and name the tension I'm pointing at.

2. The implicit theory (about 300 words) -- the view of the past, of value, and of who counts that the practice presupposes.

3. Required vs. just fitting (about 200 words) -- what the practice strictly needs versus what it merely accommodates, and where it's philosophically underdetermined.

4. Rival views (about 200 words) -- which rival theories the practice rules out, and which it turns out to be compatible with.

5. If we swapped it out (about 150 words) -- what concretely changes if we adopt the alternative I floated.
