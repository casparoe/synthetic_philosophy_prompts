My freind from our reading group has an arguement he calls the squeeze, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a real problem for the cosmological argument or a cheap trick. Context: I'm writing a chapter defending a contingency-style cosmological argument — the universe might not have existed, so its existence calls out for explanation, and only a necessary being could do the explaining. The engine is the principle of sufficient reason: every contingent truth has a sufficient reason, some fact which makes it the case and which couldn't have left things open. Here's his argument. (1) Every contingent truth has a sufficient reason. (2) Let W be the one big contingent truth — the world being exactly the way it is, i.e. the conjunction of every contingent truth. (3) W is contingent, so by (1) it has a sufficient reason, R. (4) R is either contingent or necessary. (5) Suppose R is necessary: since R is sufficient for W, if R obtains then W obtains of necessity, so W is necessary — which contradicts (3). (6) So R is contingent. But then R is one of the conjuncts of W. And W entails R, because W entails each of its conjuncts. So R and W obtain in exactly the same worlds; R is a dressed-up part of the very thing it's meant to explain, and nothing gets explained by a fact that is simply a fragment of its own explanandum. (7) There's no third option for R to be, so either (1) is false or (3) is false — meaning there are no contingent truths at all and everything, the universe included, is necessary. (8) But if everything is necessary then the universe never needed explaining in the first place, and my whole chapter has nothing to run on.

So the principle that powers the argument either fails or proves so much that the argument's own starting point — that the universe is contingent and therefore cries out for explanation — dissolves. I'd sooner keep the principle than give up contingency, but I want to know what I'm paying for it. Which step should I reject, why does that step look fine on a first pass, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, does the repair leave me with a principle strong enough to run any cosmological argument at all? Keep it under 550 words, prose rather than a bulleted list, and in British English please.
