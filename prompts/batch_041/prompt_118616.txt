Here's my model, spelled out:

1. We pick a metric that stands in for the public good. GDP per capita is the obvious one, or maybe median income, or a happiness index if we can get survey data.
2. We set up prediction markets on what that metric will be five years out.
3. Departments and outside groups propose budget policies. Traders bet on what the metric will be under each proposal.
4. The market converges on the true expected value of the metric for each policy, and whichever policy has the highest number gets funded.
5. Since prediction markets are efficient, the market's number is basically correct, so we can trust it.
6. The beautiful part is that this takes the politics out of budgeting. Nobody has to argue about what's worth wanting anymore — the market just tells us which policy actually produces more of the good thing.

Now, my questions:

(a) Is it right that the only genuinely hard problem here is choosing the metric? Once you've got the metric, is everything else just engineering — liquidity, traders, resolution, that kind of thing?
(b) What metric should we use? GDP feels crude but at least it's measurable. Is there something better that's still tractable for a mid-sized city?
(c) Am I right that this lets us skip the part where we vote on what we value? Because if the market just tells us which policy produces more of the good thing, then there's no value disagreement left to have, which is the part of politics I find most exhausting.
