I teach a grad seminar for about a dozen MFA students — fiction writers mostly, one poet, a playwright who argues with everything — and I've got ninety minutes with them next Thursday, and I want to run a game about Hempel's ravens, the paradox where a white shoe or a green apple is supposed to tell you something about whether all ravens are black. Here's the backstory, because it's why I'm being fussy: I'm writing a novel with an ornithologist in it and I keep needing to know what she'd actually believe, and last week my brother-in-law and I got into it at dinner — he says the green apple thing is a trick, obviously it tells you nothing, anyone who says otherwise is doing math at you — and I couldn't defend the other side in a way that felt like anything other than a magic trick. So I don't want an explanation. I want them to feel the thing happen. Specifically I want a round where three of them each pull one object out of a bag and get a black raven, a black shoe, and a green shirt, and I want the group to discover — from their own bets, not from me — that the shoe is worth nothing at all, the raven is worth barely anything, and the shirt is the strongest evidence in the room. That's the moment I'm building towards, so please lead with the one-sentence version of the game — the core mechanic and the moment it's designed to produce — then give me the whole design: setup, materials, rules, roles if any, the payoff scheme (no real money, chips or candy is fine), a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety, and the debrief.

The one design constraint I'm fairly sure about, from a napkin I filled up at the bar: the world has to be small. If the bag holds twenty-some objects, almost all of them ravens, and only three or four of them non-black, then drawing a green shirt should move a person's confidence by something like a third, which is way more than a black raven moves it — there are so many ravens that one more black one is no news, and there are so few non-black objects that ruling one of them out as the possible odd raven is real information. Blow the world up to thousands of objects and the whole effect evaporates and the game is dead. So I'm imagining several teams, each with its own bag and a card stating the counts — how many objects, how many ravens, how many non-black — but not which is which, blind draws only, and a scoring rule that pays out in chips for calibration, rigged so that the teams who refuse to budge on the shirt end up poorer than the ones who move. Do the numbers for me so I know the thing actually works before I stand up in front of them.

Also, and this matters as much as the game: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My guess is everyone sits frozen at their priors and stares at each other, and one draw per round won't bite hard enough for the scoring rule to teach anybody anything, but you'll know better than me. Then a debrief that points back at specific moments in the game and names the parts of the idea they map onto, no lecture up front and no names until the end — I want them to have the fight first. And finish with an honest paragraph about what the game can't reproduce. My suspicion is that the real reason the green apple feels like a joke is just that the actual world has a staggering number of non-black objects in it, so the confirmation is genuinely there but far too small to ever feel, and if that's right I want them walking out holding both halves of it at once: yes, it's evidence, and no, you will never in your life notice. Don't leave that part out to make the activity tidier.
