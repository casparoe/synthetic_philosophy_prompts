I'm on the economic development board for a mid-sized city (we're not huge, maybe 200k people). We're in a bidding war with a rival metro to land the new headquarters of a regional company. Here's the mess: our consultants figure the HQ is worth maybe $60M in net benefit to us over 15 years, tops. We've already put $45M in incentives on the table, spent about $6M on site prep, lawyers and consultants, and signed earnest-money contracts on three parcels that we forfeit if we don't close. The company just came back asking us to go to $60M, and word is the rival city is at $52M and will probably match whatever we do. If we lose we eat the $6M plus the forfeited deposits and the mayor looks like a chump; if we win we're basically at breakeven at best and maybe underwater. One of our analysts keeps calling this a "dollar auction," another says its really a war of attrition, and honestly I'm not sure either label tells me what to do Thursday.

I have to circulate a written opinion before the meeting. Draft it for me. It should land on a concrete recommendation — raise the offer to some number, hold firm at what we've already bid, or walk, and by when — with the reasoning spelled out, and it needs to take seriously the strongest case for the other side, which is basically that folding now wrecks our reputation for resolve in every future negotiation we ever have.

Couple of things I specifically want you to work through rather than hand-wave:

- Whether our escalation is just sunk-cost irrationality or whether its actually rational given the rules of the game we're stuck in, and why that distinction changes the fix.
- Whether this is closer to a dollar auction or a war of attrition, and what actually differs between them here.
- The proposed fixes floating around: publicly precommitting to a hard ceiling before the next round, telling the company we'll submit one sealed final offer and no more, offering non-cash stuff (land, workforce training) instead of more money, begging the state to cap municipal incentives, or quietly getting the rival city to coordinate with us — and flag anything legally dicey about that last one.
- Whether an announced ceiling is even credible, given the other side can just bid one dollar under it.
- And a standing policy for future contests, so we're not back here in two years.

Keep it to something I can actually read aloud. No need for footnotes or reading lists, just the argument and the call.
