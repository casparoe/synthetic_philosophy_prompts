I am preparing the final session of an undergraduate seminar on the Scientific Revolution, and the topic is Hobbes's philosophy of mathematics — specifically the strangeness of his position that geometry is the one branch of knowledge we can be said to possess with certainty, and for a reason that has nothing to do with self-evident axioms: because we make the figures ourselves, we know them from the inside, in the way a craftsman knows the thing he has built, whereas the natural world, being God's artefact and not ours, can only be conjectured at. I have been trying to explain to the students why this makes him so hostile to the algebra that John Wallis and the Oxford mathematicians were developing, which he took to be a screen of shorthand that hides the constructions it is supposed to display, and why he believed his geometrical method gave him a purchase on the subject that a mere manipulator of symbols could never have — a conviction that led him into a decades-long quarrel he lost badly, including over his own attempts to square the circle. At the same time I want the students to notice the tension inside his own system: the same author who insisted that reasoning is nothing but a reckoning, an adding and subtracting of the consequences of general names, is the author who despised the most powerful calculus of names anyone had yet devised.

What I would like from you is a serious attempt to work out what Hobbes would have said about the mathematics that came after him, given a competent modern briefing — the content, not the notation — on three developments. First, the discovery of geometries that deny the parallel postulate, and with it the loss of any guarantee that the axioms of geometry describe the structure of the space we inhabit. Second, the complete triumph of the symbolic and algebraic methods he spent his last decades denouncing, up to the point where the objects of mathematics are now largely defined by their formal behaviour rather than by any construction one could draw. Third, computer-assisted and machine-checked proofs — the four-colour theorem, the Kepler conjecture, the formalisation projects that are currently underway — in which a result is certified by the execution of a machine rather than by a human being's insight into why it must be so. For each development, I want a verdict, and where possible I want it delivered in his idiom, as he would have argued it: where he would see his own position vindicated, where he would have to concede that his critics were right and on what grounds he would concede it, and where his materialism, his nominalism, or his conception of knowledge as the knowledge of causes would push him somewhere surprising. Then step back and tell me which of his commitments the subsequent history of mathematics genuinely refutes, which it leaves entirely untouched, and whether a recognisably Hobbesian account of mathematical knowledge is still available to someone who knows all of the history — or whether the whole position was doomed from the start. Please argue it out rather than merely narrating what happened in the seventeenth century, and do not give me a reading list; I know the literature and am only interested in the reconstruction and the assessment.
