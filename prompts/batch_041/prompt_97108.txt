A regulator has been persuaded by Daniel Dennett's warning about "counterfeit people" — AI systems that pass for persons while lacking whatever grounds the moral status of real ones — and must now choose the single criterion by which the law should separate genuine persons from counterfeits. Rank the candidates below into tiers, from the one you would most recommend to the one you would least recommend, and give a sentence or two of justification for each placement. Assess each candidate both for its enforceability at scale (will it still work against the next generation of models?) and for how well it tracks what actually matters morally, and say plainly where those two demands pull apart.

(a) Behavioural indistinguishability: the system fails if it cannot be told apart from a person in sustained conversation.
(b) Provenance: the system fails if its outputs carry no verifiable mark of their artificial origin.
(c) Accountability: the system fails if it cannot be blamed, cannot acknowledge fault, and cannot bear consequences.
(d) Welfare: the system fails if it has no interests that can be set back.
(e) Diachronic commitment: the system fails if it cannot bind itself across time — make a commitment now and remain answerable for it later.
(f) Developer liability: the system fails if no one can be held legally responsible for what it produces.

If you think the list omits a criterion that belongs in the top tier, name it and place it.
