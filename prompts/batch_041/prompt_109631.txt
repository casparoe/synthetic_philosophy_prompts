Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is finishing a doctorate in philosophy, and I have been chewing it over ever since. He maintains that the old puzzle of the statue and the lump of clay — the case in which a sculptor makes a statue out of a lump of clay, and we seem forced to say both that there is one thing on the pedestal and that it has two incompatible histories and two incompatible fates — is not a genuine problem at all, and that anyone who takes it seriously has confused metaphysics with grammar. I want you to audit his case for me, claim by claim: what a competent metaphysician would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. I should declare an interest: I suspect there is more to the puzzle than he allows, but I cannot yet say where his argument fails, so please do not treat my suspicion as a reason to vindicate me.

His position, as nearly as I can reconstruct it, runs as follows.

(a) There is one object on the pedestal. 'The statue' and 'the lump of clay' are two descriptions of that single object, not two things.

(b) The apparent differences between them — that the lump existed before the sculptor began but the statue did not, that the lump would survive being flattened and the statue would not — are differences in how the descriptions work, not differences in the object. A predicate can apply to a thing under one description and fail to apply under another without there being two things. The morning star and the evening star are one body, and the fact that we say different things about it under the two names tells us about our descriptions rather than about Venus.

(c) The view that there are two objects here — the statue and the lump, sharing all their matter while differing in their modal and historical properties — is therefore unmotivated, and its defenders owe us an account of what distinguishes the two. The only candidate, that one is constituted by the other without being identical with it, names the mystery rather than solving it.

(d) The grounding problem — the question of what makes two such things differ in their properties when they share every part — is an artefact of the two-objects view, and simply vanishes once we admit that there is only one object.

(e) The two-objects view also breeds objects without limit. Besides the lump and the statue there is the clay as it was on Tuesday, the portion of clay that existed before the sculpting, the sum of the lump and the statue, and so on, until we are committed to an infinity of things where we thought there was one. Nobody can believe that, so the view is not merely unmotivated but absurd.

(f) Conclusion: the puzzle dissolves once we stop treating a difference in description as a difference in things.

What I want from you:

1. Taking (a) to (f) in turn, which claims would a philosopher working on material constitution accept, which are common simplifications that have gone slightly wrong, and which are straightforwardly mistaken? Be specific about where the slide happens in each case.

2. He moves between 'there is only one object' and 'the statue is identical with the lump' as though these were the same claim, and between 'there is not a second thing' and 'the lump is not a thing at all'. Are these the same? If not, does his conclusion survive their separation?

3. Is the comparison with the morning star and the evening star doing the work he needs, or is it a disanalogy dressed up as an analogy?

4. In (d): does the grounding problem really vanish on his view, or is it relocated rather than dissolved? What would he have to say about the single object's modal profile in order to earn the claim that it vanishes?

5. Does (e) tell against the two-objects view in particular, or against something wider that he would have to accept on his own account?

6. Finally: is there a defensible version of his conclusion — one object, and the puzzle genuinely dissolved — that escapes the objections you raise against his version? If so, say what it costs to hold it.

No citations, please. I know the positions well enough to place them myself, and what I want is the reasoning rather than a reading list.
