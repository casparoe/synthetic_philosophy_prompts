So I'm in this decision theory reading group — we did Savage a few weeks back and now we're into some of the more recent stuff on uncertainty — and after last week's session two of the members, Sam and Rae, got into an argument about Ellsberg's two-urn setup that is basicaly still going over text. I stayed out of it because I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it, and now they've asked me to referee. Here's the gist, as fairly as I can put it.

Sam's line is that the whole thing is a mistake. If you pick the urn with the known 50/50 split over the one where you're only told there are 100 balls and each is either red or black, you're being irrational. You have no more reason to think the unknown urn is red-heavy than black-heavy — you could swap the two color labels without changing a single thing you know — so by symmetry your credence in red has to be 0.5 in both urns. And once it's 0.5, preferring the known urn for a red bet and also for a black bet is incoherent: the sure-thing principle says that if you prefer one act to another no matter which state turns out to obtain, you prefer it outright, so you'd end up preferring a guaranteed $100 to a guaranteed $100. So the preference is just a bias, a fear of the unknown dressed up as caution. Rae's line is that Sam is smuggling in a probability where there isn't one. Symmetry of labels doesn't hand you a number for the unknown urn — that's the same move that lands you in Bertrand's paradox — and sticking a uniform prior over "how many red balls there are" is arbitrary. What you actually have is a set of possible odds rather than an odds, she says, and choosing the urn whose odds are pinned down is a sensible response to that, not a cognitive error. She also says the coherence arguments people trot out assume your preferences have to be complete, and she doesn't see why rationality requires that.

Then Sam threw in a case that I couldn't answer either way. Suppose there's a third urn where you're told the number of red balls was fixed by a fair coin — 30 if heads, 70 if tails, say. Now you have a perfectly good probability over the composition, a real second-order distribution, and yet the urn is every bit as "unknown" as urn two in the sense that you still don't know how many reds are in there. So if you're happy to bet on urn three, Sam says, you've got no business refusing urn two, and the difference Rae keeps pointing to is doing no work. Rae shot back that urn three is just a risk you can compute with whereas urn two isn't, and that this is precisely the difference between a gamble with known odds and one where the odds themselves are unknown, a distinction that matters even if you call both of them "uncertain." She added, as an aside, that people mostly show this aversion when they can see both urns side by side, which she thinks says something about whether it's a stable preference or a framing effect — though she admitted she couldn't say which way that cuts.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? I want each of the main claims rated on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right — the symmetry argument, the sure-thing argument, the Bertrand/indifference point, the risk-versus-ambiguity distinction, and the third-urn twist — with a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Then tell me who, if either, is closer to right overall, and if they're each right about different parts of the puzzle, say exactly which parts and what they're both missing.
