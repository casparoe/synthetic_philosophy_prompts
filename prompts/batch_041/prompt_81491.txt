I keep thinking the 737 MAX is a better test case for the responsibility-gap literature than any killer-robot scenario, and I want you to actually run it through. The setup: MCAS was a deterministic control law, not a learning system; it acted on false data from a single angle-of-attack sensor; Boeing kept it out of the manual to avoid triggering a simulator-training requirement, so pilots were fighting an input nobody had briefed them on; and the FAA had handed a lot of the certification work back to Boeing itself. Two planes, 346 people, everyone aboard killed. And nobody is blameworthy in a way that sticks -- the engineers, the managers, the regulators, the pilots, the airlines all have somewhere to point.

So two things. First, don't let "responsibility gap" stay one thing. Split it: (a) a causal gap, nothing that counts as an agent's action produced the outcome; (b) an epistemic gap, no human could have known; (c) a liability gap, nobody can be made to pay; (d) an accountability gap, no institution can call anyone to answer. Which of these does the MAX case actually have, and which one does the standard gap argument need to be real in order to get off the ground? Second, the part I actually care about: does the gap need autonomy at all? MCAS was dumb automation with opaque authority, and if that's enough to produce the same gap, then the literature may have misplaced the problem -- its not machine agency, it's diffusion plus unreadable delegation. Then run it back the other way: what does the case do to the theory? Is the gap a real feature of the world, or an artifact of a control-based individualist picture of responsibility that this case should make us drop? Write it in American English, say how confident you are in your main claims, and flag the one you're least sure of.
