I am assembling the final paper for a third-year undergraduate metaphysics module on infinity, continuity, and the completion of infinite processes, and I need one long essay question on Thomson's lamp. The students have read Thomson and Benacerraf, so a question that merely asks them to expound either will be answered straight out of their notes. I want a question that forces them to do original work on material they have not seen before: two variants of the lamp, which I have designed for the purpose and which are to be printed on the paper and used as the evidential basis of the answer.

The material to be printed is as follows. Lamp A is Thomson's original: a lamp with two states, on and off, starting off, whose switch is toggled at the times t_n = 1 − 2^−n for n = 1, 2, 3, …, so that infinitely many toggles are completed by t = 1. Lamp B runs on the same schedule, but its switch is a dimmer rather than a two-state toggle: at t_n the brightness is set to b_n = 1/2 + (−1)^n · 2^−(n+1), a continuous quantity between 0 and 1. Lamp C is Lamp A with one clause added to its specification: the lamp is on at t = 1.

The question should present four candidate diagnoses of the trouble in Lamp A and require the candidate to adjudicate among them using Lamps B and C: (1) the trouble is the infinite number of actions; (2) the trouble is that the lamp's state space has only two values, so the process cannot converge; (3) the trouble is that the description is incomplete, fixing the lamp's state only on the interval up to but not including t = 1; (4) the trouble is a tacit assumption that the lamp's state function must be continuous at t = 1, which the description does not license. For each diagnosis the candidate should state what it predicts about Lamps B and C and whether the prediction survives, and should close by saying what, if anything, the case shows about the thesis that supertasks are impossible, using as a control an idealised ball with coefficient of restitution less than 1, which bounces infinitely often within a finite time and then comes to rest. The question should require explicit engagement with the deflationary reply associated with Benacerraf and with Thomson's own conclusion, but it must not ask for anything the candidate cannot work out from the printed material plus the course; no citations, no reading list, no appeal to results the candidate cannot derive on the spot.

Please write three things: the question as it would appear on the paper, together with the time allowance (90 minutes) and the expected length (roughly 800 words); a model answer of about 800 words such as a strong candidate would produce; and a marking scheme consisting of exactly five must-hit points, each with the marks it is worth, totalling 20, and each accompanied by a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates get that point wrong. Order the five points from the most elementary to the most discriminating, and reserve the highest marks for candidates who keep two questions firmly apart: what the description of a process determines, and what a physical system can be made to do.
