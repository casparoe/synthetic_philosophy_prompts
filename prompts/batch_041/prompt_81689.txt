I am a novelist at work on a book set in the last years of the Weimar Republic, and I am applying for a one-year fellowship that supports the research phase of a literary project. I need a proposal of no more than fifteen hundred words, and I have a half-formed idea that I would like you to turn into it.

The book follows a mid-level civil servant, a man of no particular convictions, through the years in which the Republic is dismantled around him. The difficulty is a craft problem that has become, to my surprise, a philosophical one. Every scene forces a choice about where to locate the causes of what happens: in my character's decisions, or in the conditions that make those decisions almost weightless. Readers of the draft have disagreed about which choice the book makes, and I have come to think they are disagreeing about something more interesting than my manuscript.

The question I want the proposal to be about is the one usually argued under the heading of the Great Man and his critics: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? My suspicion — this is the half-formed part — is that the disagreement is not one disagreement but several, and that it is produced by two choices that are almost always left unexamined. The first is the contrast. The question as it stands does not say what Nazi Germany is being contrasted with. An authoritarian regime of the right without the racial programme, a military dictatorship, a Communist Germany, a Germany that merely muddles on: these are different questions, and the evidence bears differently on each. The second is the counterfactual's tolerance for adjustment. When we remove Hitler from the antecedent, may the world rearrange itself so that the same pressures throw up some equivalent figure, or must everything else be held as close as possible to how it actually was? The two settings give opposite answers, and neither is obviously the right one.

There is a further difficulty I would like the proposal to confront rather than avoid. If the historical process is deterministic, then removing Hitler removes everything downstream of him, and the counterfactual is either trivially false or beyond our reach. If it is genuinely chancy, almost any outcome is compatible with his absence, and the counterfactual is trivially true. Either way the question seems to dissolve. I want to know whether it can be repaired instead of dissolved, and whether the repair tells us anything about how a novelist ought to distribute agency in a story about a period like this one.

The proposal should state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions — those that make individuals the engines of history and those that make them its symptoms — leave it open, rather than rehearsing them; and set out the method. I imagine the method in three stages. First, separating the question into the family of contrastive questions that the single sentence conceals, and saying which of them are empirical and which turn on what we are asking for when we ask for a cause. Second, testing the repair strategies against the case, including the one I care about most: whether an explanation of this period has to survive being told from the inside, as a sequence of choices by people who could have chosen otherwise. Third, drawing out what follows for the way a novelist assigns responsibility to characters. Say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — a straight history of the period, or a counterfactual essay in the familiar style. Say what a result would look like, what would count as failure (I would rather be told now that the question is merely verbal than discover it in month nine), and give a realistic scope for a year.

The panel includes two historians and three writers, so the proposal has to be legible to both without being shallow for either. Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I will add references myself, and I would rather the argument rest on positions than on titles. Where you need to name a view, describe it.
