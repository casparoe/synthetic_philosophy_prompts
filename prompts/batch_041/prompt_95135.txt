For a term paper in my AI policy seminar, I want to examine what the current apparatus of pre-deployment AI safety evaluation presupposes about how AI systems could go catastrophically wrong. The practice, roughly: labs evaluate models for dangerous capabilities (autonomy, cyber, biosecurity), run adversarial red teams, publish system cards, and in some cases adopt deployment policies in which crossing a capability threshold triggers additional safeguards. My suspicion is that this apparatus encodes a particular picture of failure -- that it arrives as a discrete event brought about by a system that acquires influence and uses it, that the danger shows up as a measurable capability, and that a misaligned model would betray itself under behavioural testing. Reconstruct that implicit theory as precisely as you can, and distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit. Then say which rival picture it quietly excludes -- in particular the one on which the harm is instead a slow drift, accumulating out of many small delegations of human judgment, with no scheming agent and no moment of takeover -- and what the practice would have to become if we took that picture seriously: what we would start measuring, what we would stop measuring, and which existing procedures are equally licensed by both pictures and so reveal nothing about which one the field actually holds. The point I most want pressed is an apparent tension: capability evaluations and red-teaming assume misalignment is detectable by testing, while the same field treats as live the possibility of a model whose misalignment is built to survive exactly that testing. Can those commitments be held together, and if not, which gives way?
