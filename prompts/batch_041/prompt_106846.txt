I am writing a novel set in the mid-1960s, centered on a public works project that has become a national embarrassment, and I need to get the psychology of the people involved right rather than relying on the received wisdom that they were simply irrational. So I would like you to analyze the Sydney Opera House affair as a case study in the sunk cost problem, and to be genuinely disciplined about the mapping in both directions: what the theory of sunk costs and escalating commitment says about the choices that were actually made, and what this episode reveals about the limits of that theory.

Here are the facts I intend to work from, so that you need not rely on any contested detail: Jørn Utzon, a Danish architect, won the design competition in 1957 with sketches that were closer to a gesture than a building; construction began in 1959 before adequate working drawings existed, and part of the podium had to be rebuilt; the geometry of the shells was not resolved until Utzon hit on a spherical solution some years in. The projected cost rose continuously and the final figure was, in the currency of the day, on the order of a hundred million Australian dollars, with the building opening only in 1973. In 1965 a new state government took office whose public works minister, Davis Hughes, was hostile to the project and to Utzon personally. Hughes withheld payment on a fee claim — I believe around fifty-one thousand pounds — which left Utzon unable to pay his staff; after a heated exchange Utzon wrote to Hughes to say that he had been forced off the job, and the minister announced within hours that the architect had resigned. A local architect, Peter Hall, completed the interiors in a markedly different spirit, and the building was finished under his hand.

What I want from you is not a narrative of these events but a careful application of the sunk cost literature, in the following order.

First, identify the genuine decision points — not the vague sense that "they kept spending," but the specific moments at which someone could have stopped, scaled back, redesigned, or handed the thing over. At each, state what the bygones principle implies: which expenditures were truly irrecoverable, which costs were still prospective and therefore still live, and what a decision-maker reasoning only from the future should have done. Be explicit about the distinction between money already committed and obligations not yet entered into, since I suspect the interesting decisions are about the latter.

Second, say where the standard irrationality verdict actually lands. My working assumption is that some of the persistence is textbook escalation — the refusal to cut losses because of what has already been spent — but that other parts of it are not, and I want you to separate them rather than lumping the whole affair under one fallacy. Pay attention in particular to the fact that continuing to build was not simply "throwing good money after bad": the shells, once up, were a completed asset whose value could not be realized in any other way, and there is a real question whether abandoning them would have destroyed value rather than conserved it.

Third, and this is the part I care most about, examine the ways in which the case does not fit the theory's assumed shape. The sunk cost fallacy is formulated for a single agent with a single, well-defined payoff, deciding whether to persist in a course of action. Here the "agent" is a government answerable to an electorate, the "investment" is public money raised in a way that made the project's wastefulness a permanent political fact, and the payoffs are scattered across parties with almost nothing in common: the premier's need to be seen to finish what was started, the minister's need to be seen to impose discipline, Utzon's stake in the integrity of a design that was his reputation, the public's growing identification with a building it had already paid for. Does the theory survive that dispersal of agency, or does it quietly assume a unified decision-maker that does not exist here? I would like you to say plainly which parts of the analysis the theory can carry and where it goes silent.

Fourth, consider the strongest revisionist case. There are defenses of honoring sunk costs in the literature — that persistence can be rational when it signals resolve or protects a reputation for follow-through, that a decision-maker with private information may rightly ignore outsiders who see only waste, that commitment to a chosen course can be a precondition of accomplishing anything hard. Which of these, if any, has real purchase on the Opera House? And notice the awkward fact that the one figure who refused to persist — Hughes, who stopped paying and drove the architect out — is not obviously the rational one, while the one who insisted on the design's integrity at ruinous cost may have been protecting something the bygones principle has no vocabulary for.

Fifth, confront the trap I most need to avoid in the novel. The building is now regarded as a masterpiece and a World Heritage site, which makes it tempting to conclude that the persistence was vindicated and the warnings were wrong. I take it this is an outcome bias and that the ex post magnificence of the result tells us nothing about the ex ante rationality of the spending. But I want you to say precisely why, and whether there is any residue of the case that the standard "don't reason from outcomes" reply fails to dissolve — for instance, if the willingness to overspend on an unprecedented design is what made the design possible at all, whether that is a reason to revise the bygones principle or merely a reason to note that it was never meant to govern decisions under radical uncertainty.

Finish by telling me what the episode shows about the theory itself: whether it is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated, and what a novelist should understand about the difference between a character who honors sunk costs out of weakness and one who honors them because the alternative would mean abandoning something he cannot coherently abandon.
