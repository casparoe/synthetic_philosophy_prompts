Question 3. In the bucket experiment the surface of the water is flat at the start, when the bucket is spinning and the water is still, and it becomes concave once the water has caught up and is rotating along with the bucket. What does Newton think this shows, and what does it actually show about the substantivalism/relationism dispute? Be specific about what the argument does and does not establish.

Student's answer:

Newton's point is that the surface can't be tracking the water's motion relative to the bucket: the water and the bucket are moving relative to each other most at the start, when the surface is flat, and not at all at the end, when it's concave. So the concavity has to come from the water's rotation with respect to space itself rather than with respect to any body. Since the concave surface is a real physical difference, and the only thing that varies between the two cases is how the water is rotating with respect to space, Newton concludes that space is real, a substance over and above the bodies in it and not just a system of relations among bodies. Even a relationist has to concede that acceleration is absolute in a way that velocity isn't, so the argument stands up as a case for substantivalism.

The strongest reply is Leibniz's: the concavity is caused by the water's rotation relative to the fixed stars, because he held that inertia is produced by the whole body of matter in the universe, so that a bucket spinning on its own in an otherwise empty cosmos would keep a flat surface. That's basically Mach's principle. So I'd say the argument refutes the crude version of relationism, where all motion is just motion relative to some other body, but not a version that makes inertia depend on the distant matter, and even then Newton wins the exchange as stated.
