Every month we get three to six complaints — noise, junk in the hallways, someone's dog, someone subletting without telling us, someone being a menace in the group chat. And roughly half the time the person complaining is themselves not clean. The guy who's furious about clutter has a whole bike-shed situation. The woman who wants us to fine people for late charges was in arrears herself for eight months. So when we try to act, the other person says the magic sentence — you can't say anything, you do it too — and half the time they're right and half the time it's just a way of getting out of it.

Dan thinks the you-do-it-too move is always a dodge: a rule is a rule, if the complaint is true it's true, ignore who's making it. I think it matters but I can't say where the line is. I know there's a real philosophy literature about this — standing to blame, hypocrisy, all that — but I don't have it organised in my head well enough to run a meeting with it, and I'm not going to sit there citing things at my neighbours anyway.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run. Before each meeting I want to sit down with the complaint list, go through each one in order, and come out with a decision. Checklist or decision tree in words: which questions to ask in what order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where it stops at no, and where it has to go to the board vote or to a person because it's a values call and the theory doesn't settle it.

Stuff it has to handle, because these are the ones that keep biting us:

- someone who did the exact same thing vs someone who did a related thing vs someone who's a general mess but not about this particular thing
- someone who did it, stopped, and apologised vs someone still doing it while complaining
- the person who says yeah I do this too, I'm not pretending otherwise, I'm still saying it — that doesn't look like hypocrisy to me at all, so does that person get to complain?
- the difference between me privately thinking less of them and them losing the right to ask the board to enforce the actual rule. Those feel like seperate questions and I keep collapsing them.
- the reverse case, where the board is the hypocrite: we let things slide for people we like and then come down hard on someone we don't, and then the complainer's own record is beside the point and it's us who's the problem.

I also need to know what the person being complained about is entitled to do with all this. If someone lacks standing, does the complaint just die? Or do we still enforce it and only the moralising is off the table? Because in a co-op we have to enforce things regardless of who noticed them, and I suspect the honest answer splits into at least three things — someone's private opinion of you, someone publicly telling you off, and the actual rule with a fine attached — and I don't want that fudged.
