ok so I'm a software engineer, never took a philosophy class in my life, and I've gotten into a long argument with a friend about which sentences are "true by definition" and which are "true because of how the world turned out." I've got strong gut feelings on alot of them and I want to know if there's an actual rule behind my gut or if I'm just making it up case by case.

My gut, going down the list:

- "If someone's a bachelor, they're unmarried." By definition. Easy.
- "If a shape is a square, it has four sides." Same.
- "Every number divisible by 4 is even." By definition too, I think — except I had to do a little work to see it. But I didn't learn anything about the world, so: definition.
- "If a triangle has three equal sides, it has three equal angles." This one bothers me. It feels like a discovery, like something I found out, not something sitting there in the words. But I also can't picture checking it in a lab. No idea where it goes.
- "Water is H2O." Not by definition — people used water for ages without knowing that, and I wouldn't say they had a wrong idea of what "water" meant, just a fact they didn't have yet. But now that we know it, it also doesn't feel like a random fluke of the universe. So it's not-definitional but also not-just-lucky, and I have no slot for that.
- "Nothing can be all red and all green in the same place at the same time." Feels like it has to be true. But is it true because of the words "red" and "green," or because of how eyes and light work? Can't tell.
- "The bar in the vault in France that used to define the metre — that bar is one metre long." Feels trivially true, like I get it from the words alone, but its also about one specific physical object, which shouldn't be a "by definition" kind of thing.
- "A whale is a mammal." I used to think this was by definition. Then I learned people thought whales were fish and it turned out to be a real biological discovery, so now I think it's not by definition at all. But it *feels* exactly like "a square has four sides," which worries me.
- "A correct program does what its spec says." By definition, I'd say — that's literally what "correct" means for a program. Though maybe that's cheating since I defined the term myself.
- Here's one from my own work that messes with me: I write "if the build passes, the tests pass," and then I change my pipeline so the build *includes* the tests. Yesterday that sentence was a real claim about my setup; today it's true no matter what. Did I turn a fact into a definition just by changing what "build" means?

Two half-baked rules I keep reaching for: (P1) it's true-by-definition if you could know it just by understanding the words, without ever checking the world; (P2) it's true-by-definition if there's no possible way it could have been false. But they come apart on me. Water-is-H2O seems to pass P2 (could water have not been H2O? feels like no) but clearly fails P1. The metre-bar sentence passes P1 (words alone) but seems to fail P2 (the bar could've been longer, right?). So they can't both be the rule, and honestly I don't know which one I actually mean.

What I want: is there a principle — even a messy, two-part one — that actually matches most of these? Where does it force me to give up a verdict? Where do the two I can't place (the triangle one and the water one) end up? And is this even a real distinction or just a spectrum — because in code "compile-time vs runtime" is a real split even though people argue about the edge cases forever, so "people argue about the edge cases" doesn't by itself mean there's no line.

And if you can, tell me what your principle predicts for a case I didn't list — say "if you're in pain you're having an experience," or "if a number is prime and bigger than 2 then it's odd." The prime one feels like the triangle one (a bit of work) but I can't tell if that's the same thing or a different thing.

Be straight with me if my gut just contradicts itself and some of these have to go.
