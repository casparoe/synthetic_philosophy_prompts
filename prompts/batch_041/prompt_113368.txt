You are a philosopher of language with a specialist's knowledge of late-twentieth-century American pragmatism and a good comic ear. I want you to write a joke about Rorty's antirepresentationalism — a joke that is actually funny to someone who knows the position, rather than a generic nerd joke about philosophers being relativists or about "everything is just language."

Here is the material the joke should turn on. On Rorty's account, "true" is not the name of a correspondence relation holding between a sentence and a mind-independent world; it is a compliment we pay to sentences that are currently paying their way within a practice, and a vocabulary is better understood on the model of a tool for coping with the world than a mirror that copies it. What the antirepresentationalist says we lose when the mirror metaphor goes is not the ability to prefer one vocabulary to another — he keeps plenty of room for that — but only the ability to say that the preferred vocabulary gets reality right in some sense over and above its working well for our purposes; and the demand for a world-side check on our vocabularies is precisely what he thinks we should stop making. Rorty pairs this with his figure of the ironist, who knows that the final vocabulary she uses to justify her beliefs and actions — the words she would use to state what matters most — is contingent and answerable to nothing outside itself, and who uses it anyway.

Write the joke. It may be dialogue, a short scene, or a narrated setup and punchline; keep it under 150 words. The humor must turn on a real feature of the view, for instance on exactly what is and is not given up when correspondence drops out, or on the ironist's relation to her own final vocabulary. Do not let the punchline be merely the Rortian saying "that is just your vocabulary," and do not recycle the stock gag that pragmatists think true is whatever works. Begin by brainstorming three candidate jokes in a few lines each, then pick the one with the sharpest content-bearing punchline and present it. End with a final line of the form Punchline premise: <one sentence stating the claim the joke turns on>.
