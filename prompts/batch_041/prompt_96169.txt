I am a graduate student who splits my time between a philosophy department and a behavioural economics group, and for the past year I have been having the same argument with the economists across the corridor — always over lunch, never to any conclusion. Their position is that what ordinary speech calls weakness of will is fully captured by present bias: an agent whose ranking of two future options flips as the earlier one arrives, so that she is coherent at every moment and simply ranks the tempting option first at the moment of action, whatever she ranked first before. My side's position is that akrasia consists in acting against one's own all-things-considered judgment about what one should do, held at the time of acting. These are plainly not the same thing, and I suspect the two camps have been talking past each other for decades — one modelling a regularity in choice, the other analysing a species of practical irrationality — without either side ever saying clearly which phenomenon the other's word picks out. I want to see what a good-faith adversarial collaboration would actually produce, so please draft the joint statement that a careful philosopher of action and a careful decision-theoretic economist could both sign.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: the substantial ones, the ones neither side has to hedge — that the empirical regularities of preference reversal are real and robust, that willpower invoked as a primitive explains nothing, that an agent can act against a plan she still endorses without thereby acting against any preference she holds at the time, and whatever else the two could genuinely co-sign. I would rather have eight solid claims than twenty thin ones. Second, a disagreements table, one row per point of contention, with the philosopher's formulation of her own view and the economist's formulation of his, each in wording that side would accept, plus a column marking whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal; I expect at least one row to be verbal, and I want to know exactly which one and why. Third, for each genuine crux, a paragraph naming a case, argument, or test that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance and a statement of what result would move whom.

To keep this concrete rather than a survey, build it around the following material. The first case, which I will call the lucid lapse: at eleven, S judges that she should not have a second drink tonight, and she knows her ranking will flip at midnight; at midnight she pours the drink while still judging that she should not, and afterwards describes herself as having known better throughout. The second case is its revised counterpart: identical behaviour, except that at midnight she genuinely judges that drinking is what she should do, having changed her mind rather than her will. The third is the resolute self-binder: at midnight she still ranks drinking highest and judges it best, but keeps to the earlier plan anyway, because she still endorses the plan and has not come to regard it as mistaken. I want to know whether the first case is possible as described or is a description the economist must treat as incoherent; what each side says about the second and third; and whether the third is the mirror image of the phenomenon in dispute or something else entirely. Press one further point as well: if preference is identified with choice, then no choice can ever be preference-inconsistent, and the economist's framework appears unable to represent the very case under dispute. I want both sides to say what they make of that, and whether the impasse is a real disagreement about minds or a disagreement about how to use a word. No citations, no reading lists, no appeals to who said what — I want the claims, the formulations, the cruxes, and the predictions, argued rather than attributed.
