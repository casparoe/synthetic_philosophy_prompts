Last night I spent two hours arguing with a friend about the regress of justification, and we ended not in disagreement about a doctrine but about a form. She holds that no belief is basic in the sense that would stop the regress -- that the question *why* never becomes inappropriate, and that "self-evident" is only the name we give to the point at which we stop asking -- while I hold that a chain no one could ever traverse cannot be what justifies anyone, so infinitism buys the endless regress at the price of justification for finite creatures. I would like a poem that stages this. Make it a pantoum, four quatrains: the second and fourth lines of each stanza returning as the first and third of the next, and the final stanza closing by bringing back the poem's opening lines, so that the whole thing is a circuit with no privileged line. Let the form make the coherentist's case rather than state it, and let the repetition do double duty -- a reader should be able to hear a returning line both as a claim supporting itself (the charge of circularity) and as a member of a system in which no single line carries the load alone (the coherentist's reply that the charge begs the question by assuming justification must be linear). Somewhere in the poem, one line should speak for the foundationalist, wanting to be the place where the asking stops, and one for the infinitist, refusing to be the last line and treating every *why* as still legitimate; do not name the positions or the philosophers, and let the recurrence carry it. A philosopher who knows the debate should be able to point to exactly where each position speaks and to see that the ending stays genuinely undecided about whether the closing return is a vindication or a trap, and a reader who knows none of it should still find it a good poem. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
