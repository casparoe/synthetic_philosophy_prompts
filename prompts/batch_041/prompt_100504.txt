I am a novelist — four books, twenty years at it, and long enough teaching workshops that the vocabulary of craft is second nature to me: point of view and its varieties, omniscient narration, free indirect style, scene against summary, the ensemble cast, the rotating limited-third chapter, the first-person plural, the unreliable narrator, foreshadowing, and the way a book's opening chapter quietly appoints a protagonist and therefore a center. I have no training whatsoever in history or in the theory of it. I would like to borrow your fluency in that field.

The problem I am writing into is this. My current book is an ensemble novel: five centuries, four continents, a rotating cast, each chapter in close third person, no character appearing in more than two of them. I chose the form because I wanted the book to belong to no one in particular — not to the European characters who arrive by ship, not to the people whose ports they arrive at, not to the descendants who inherit the consequences. The form is meant to make a claim about how history actually happens. It keeps failing. Whichever chapter I put first becomes the origin; the order of the parts becomes a causal chain, so that whatever follows looks like its result; and the characters who left letters become characters while the ones who left nothing become setting. I have begun to suspect the novel is not neutral about where the center is — that a story of any shape must have one, and that a book pretending to be told from everywhere at once is either lying or smuggling a center back in through the arrangement.

I understand that historians have argued about something very close to this: whether the past can be told from everywhere at once, whether a global history can escape treating Europe as its engine and its measure, and whether the attempt to displace that center simply installs a new one under a different name. Restate that debate for me — both the case for decentering and the objections of those who think the project incoherent or self-defeating — entirely in my vocabulary. Not as an analogy, not as a metaphor, and not as an explanation pitched to a layperson: as a systematic translation. Take the historians' claims one at a time and say what each becomes when the medium is the novel. What is the counterpart of a standpoint? Of a periodization? Of a causal claim? Of the assertion that Europe is the engine of the story? Of a source and its silence? Of the difference between the events and the telling of them? Push the translation as far as it will honestly go, and tell me at what point it stops being a translation and becomes a paraphrase that has quietly changed the claim.

Then the part I actually want. Report the residue. Which claims in that debate come through cleanly, which come through only distorted, and which cannot be said at all in the terms I have — where a historian is saying something my craft vocabulary has no slot for. For each item of residue, tell me whether the gap is a defect in the novelist's vocabulary, meaning my medium genuinely cannot say it and my formal trouble is a limit of the form rather than a failure of nerve, or whether it is a sign that the historians' claim was vaguer than it looked and dissolves once you ask what it would mean to write it. Do not do me the courtesy of translating so generously that the historians arrive already dressed as novelists; I want to see the seams. And do not conclude that the two vocabularies are simply the same thing in different words. If they were, I would not be stuck.
