Hi, I'm a novelist — two books, both of which sank without a trace — and I've got a story I can't make work, so I'm outsourcing the hard part.

A philosopher friend explained something to me at a wedding and I've been chewing on it for months. She said there are four different ways a thing can have a quality and still never once show it. (1) The thing really has the quality, but if the moment ever came, the quality would be gone before it could do anything — a genuinely fragile glass, except some sorcerer turns it to rubber the instant anybody swings at it. (2) The quality stays exactly where it is, but something outside gets in the way — same glass, wrapped in six inches of bubble wrap. (3) The thing would shatter when you hit it, and yet was never fragile at all, because a little mechanism inside goes off when you tap it. (4) The thing only becomes fragile at the moment it's struck. She had names for all four and I've forgot which name goes with which, doesn't matter, you get the picture.

What I want is a short story, 2000–2500 words, where all four patterns show up as people. No sorcerers, no magic: the interferences have to be ordinary human ones — a relative who always steps in just in time, an institution that reassigns you the week your nerve is about to be tested, a doctor's note, a shift swap, a well-meaning friend who answers before you can. One character's trait should be the spine of the whole thing — courage, or generosity, something with a moral charge — and the plot should basically be a machine for putting that trait under pressure and never quite letting it fire.

Rules: no philosophy talk inside the story, no narrator telling us which case is which, and nobody says the word disposition. If a reader can't sort the four patterns from the events alone, I've failed. Make one of the four genuinely undecidable between the first kind and the second — I want people arguing in the comments. And don't end on a verdict about whether the protagonist ever actually had the trait; end on a choice that makes the question urgent. Keep the setting small and mundane — a laundromat, a nursing home, a church basement. Not a lab, definately not a startup.
