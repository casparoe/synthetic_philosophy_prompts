For years I've followed the decision-theory and formal-epistemology threads on the rationalist blogs and forums, and I've lately inherited the graduate qualifying exam in decision theory for my department. The two-envelope question I keep setting has gone stale: nearly every candidate clears it with a single sentence — that the switching argument presupposes a uniform distribution over the amounts, which cannot exist — and that sentence tells me nothing about whether they actually understand the puzzle. I'd like you to draft a replacement, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question should be a closed-book essay, answerable in about ninety minutes, in three parts.

In part one, the candidate does not look inside the envelope. I want the ordinary symmetry answer, but I also want them to locate the precise error in the naive expectation argument — not the vague charge of a missing uniform prior, but the exact step where the computation goes wrong and why.

Part two is the crux. Give the candidate a fully specified generative process: the experimenter draws a base amount B = 2^K, where K is a geometric random variable on {0, 1, 2, …} with P(K = k) = (1 − q) q^k, and places B and 2B in the two envelopes; the candidate opens one envelope and sees the amount x. Require them to compute the posterior probability that the other envelope holds 2x, to compute the expected value of switching as a function of q, and to find the value of q at which switching becomes favourable. Then require them to relate that threshold to whether the base amount has finite expected value, and to state and justify the general principle behind what they have found — namely, that a prior can support switching for every observed amount only if its expectation diverges. I care about the argument for that principle, not the slogan.

Part three should ask what the diagnosis amounts to: whether the two-envelope problem is simply an instance of the familiar hazard of reasoning with divergent expected value, and — as the move that should separate the strongest scripts from the rest — whether the same structure appears in self-location reasoning, the pattern familiar from the doomsday argument and the self-indication assumption that treats your position among observers as a random draw and thereby makes a larger population more likely. I want the analogy stated where it holds and its limit stated where it fails. No citations are needed or wanted; the reasoning is the whole of the exercise.

Then supply a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that does the arithmetic cleanly and argues the general principle rather than gesturing at it, and a marking scheme built around five or six must-hit points, each with its mark value (total twenty-five), a one-line note on how candidates typically miss that point, and a short description of what separates an A script from a B — which specific moves the A answer makes that the B answer only names.
