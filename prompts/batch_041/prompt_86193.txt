I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm at work on a long essay about the persistence of the Machiavellian idea that fortune, and not merit, disposes of a great deal of what happens to us. I want the spine of it to be a film rather than a treatise, so please read the Coen brothers' No Country for Old Men through Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù. I don't want the soft fatalism the film usually attracts in reviews — the sense that everyone is simply caught in a current — because that flattens terms I need kept apart. So begin by separating them. Machiavelli's fortune is not fate and not providence but the residual, unmasterable portion of the world, the flood that shows its force wherever no one has built defenses; his virtù is not moral virtue but effectiveness, the capacity to read the times, improvise, and change course when circumstances change, which is why he prizes the impetuous over the cautious. Set against that the older Roman ideal of virtus — constancy and courage before what cannot be altered — which Machiavelli partly abandons.

Then tell me which of these the film actually stages, and be specific about scenes. The gas-station coin toss, where Chigurh spares a man for calling the coin correctly: is that Machiavellian fortune made literal, or something the framework cannot hold — a man who has renounced responsibility dressing up his own agency as chance? Llewelyn Moss, competent and resourceful and a step ahead for most of the film before he is killed offscreen by people he never sees: is he destroyed because fortune finally exceeded any possible virtù, or because he lacked the adaptive prudence Machiavelli demands and was undone by a defect of character that only looks like bad luck? And Sheriff Bell, who withdraws and retires while the violence continues: is his retreat a failure of virtù, or a clear-eyed judgment that the kind of world in which virtù could be exercised has ended?

I need the reading honest in both directions. Don't reduce the film to an illustration of the treatise, and don't bend Machiavelli into a fatalist to make him fit. Where does the film resist the frame — where does it insist on something the framework cannot accommodate, whether that is the sheer moral horror Chigurh carries or Bell's suspicion that the orderly past he mourns was never as orderly as he remembers? And close with what the film contributes to the argument that Machiavelli's prose cannot: what it shows about the texture of living once virtù has been outrun. No citations or quotations, please; I know the film and the Machiavelli well enough that you can argue rather than summarize.
