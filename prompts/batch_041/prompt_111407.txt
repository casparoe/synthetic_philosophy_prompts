Not a philosopher, I'm a backend engineer, so apologies in advance if I mangle the vocabulary. My team does a movie night where whoever picks the film gives a 10 minute talk first, and I picked 12 Angry Men because I figured I could hook it to the one piece of theory I actually know, the Condorcet jury theorem. I know it from the ML side: if you have a bunch of classifiers that are each slightly better than chance and their mistakes are independant of each other, then majority vote beats every individual one, and the more of them you add the closer you get to being right.

So read the film through the theorem, and be straight with me in both directions. Where does that jury actually resemble the theorem's ideal voters, and where does it obviously fall apart? Juror 7 just wants to get to the ballgame, so he's not even voting his belief; they're all stuck in the same sweaty room breathing each other's air, so the independence assumption is a joke; and the guy who ends up turning the whole room around isn't mildly-better-than-a-coin-flip, he's a guy who went out and bought a knife. Also I can't get past this one: the theorem is about a majority getting the facts right, but the jury isn't being asked whether the kid is innocent, it's being asked about reasonable doubt, and I'm not sure the theorem has anything to say about that question at all.

Two more things to fold in, if they're real and not me misremembering. One, a criminal jury has to be unanimous, not a majority, and a single holdout with a veto seems like a different animal from what the theorem blesses — the movie is basically the story of one veto. Two, I have a vague memory of something called a doctrinal paradox, where a group can vote majority-yes on each of two premises and majority-no on the conclusion that follows from them, which sounds suspiciously like a jury voting seperately on the knife, the old man's walk, and the woman's glasses. Tell me if I've got those wrong.

Then give me the payoff. Is the film secretly an argument that deliberation beats aggregation — that what saves the kid isn't 12 independent votes but one stubborn guy making everybody actually look at the evidence — or is it just a story about conformity and pressure, and the math is fine and the movie is about something else? And what does the movie show that the theorem can't model?
