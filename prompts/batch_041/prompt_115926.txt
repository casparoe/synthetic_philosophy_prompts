I am drafting a commencement address for my doctoral supervisor, a philosopher who has spent her career defending four-dimensionalism — the view that persisting things, people included, persist by having temporal parts located at different times rather than by being wholly present at each time. She is, more specifically, a stage theorist rather than a worm theorist, and the difference matters for what she can say. On the worm view, a person is the whole temporally extended object, so the student who enrolled four years ago and the graduate who will cross the stage are literally one and the same thing, related by identity, each of them partly present at both moments. On the stage view, by contrast, the word you names the present momentary stage; claims about your past and future are made true not by your identity with any past or future entity but by a relation of temporal counterpartship holding between stages. Hers is the second view. She will not abandon it, quietly convert it into the first, or turn a commencement into a seminar about it.

The occasion is the graduation ceremony of a small liberal arts college, held in the college's main hall. Roughly half of the graduating class majored in philosophy and has read her work; those students will catch any sentence that silently presupposes identity across time, any sentence that treats the worm view as the plain truth, and any sentence that tries to have it both ways. The other half of the room — parents, grandparents, siblings, a few trustees — has never heard of the debate and must not feel that the ceremony is being conducted in code. She has asked me to make sure the address lands for both.

Two local details she wants the address to accommodate. First, the class has a tradition: each graduate has written a sealed letter to be opened at the tenth reunion, and she has been asked to say something about those letters. Second, the student speaker who follows her wrote a senior thesis defending the worm view and will almost certainly take a friendly swing at her position; she wants her remarks to be robust to that, and fair to her critic, without conceding anything.

What she wants from the draft: an address of roughly 1,200 words, deliverable aloud in nine or ten minutes, that actually consoles — that offers the graduates a picture of what a life is, what a promise is, and what it means to care about someone you will never be — rather than a list of disclaimers about what she cannot say. It has to survive her own standards: no sentence that is false on the stage view, and no sentence whose only defense would be that it is just how we talk. She has banned the phrase in some sense. She is willing to let the address make use of the thought that the people at the reunion are, in a certain respect, other people — but only if that thought can be made to do honest emotional work rather than to produce a clever paradox.

Write it in American English, in the register of a spoken address rather than an essay. Have her cite or name no philosophers, and give me the address alone, with no notes, framing, or commentary around it.
