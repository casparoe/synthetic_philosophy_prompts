Two guys in my reading group, Dara and Owen, got into it last week and I've been chewing on it since. Background: we'd been talking about why induction is hard to justify, and about the no-free-lunch results — the ones saying that if you average performance over all possible problems, every search or prediction method comes out the same, so no method has a built-in edge. Dara had just read a book by Gerhard Schurz arguing that Hume's problem can actually be answered by a theorem about something called meta-induction. Here's roughly how he set it up, and then the fight.

Dara's setup: take a finite list of prediction methods. Say the sequence is a string of 0s and 1s and the methods are: always say 0, always say 1, say whatever came last, say the majority so far, say the opposite of whatever came last. Each round they all predict, the real value shows up, everyone gets scored. Now build a meta-method that predicts a weighted average of the list's predictions, where each method's weight rises the more accurately it's been predicting and falls when it's been wrong, and no weight is ever allowed to hit zero, so a method that only starts doing well late can still get picked up. The theorem, Dara says, is that for any sequence of outcomes whatsoever — adversarial, random, patterned, whatever, with no assumption that the future resembles the past — the meta-method's long-run average score is at least as good as the best method on the list. And that, he says, is a non-circular answer to Hume.

(1) Owen: that's not an answer, it's a consolation prize. It says you won't be beaten by your own tools. If all five of those methods are garbage, the meta-method is garbage too, just the best of a bad bunch.

(2) Dara: but that's more than Hume left us. Hume said no non-circular justification was available. Here's a proof, with no uniformity assumption smuggled in, that the inductive-ish meta-method is optimal in a precise sense. If you want more than that you're demanding a guarantee of predictive success, and no free lunch says that's impossible — averaged over all worlds, every method ties. So this is the maximum on offer.

(3) Owen: that last step is where you lose me. No free lunch averages over all problems under a uniform measure. Your theorem isn't the complement of that, it's a different kind of claim. Yours holds for every single sequence, with no measure at all. Fine. But it also only holds relative to a list fixed in advance. Pick a bad list and you get nothing. So the content is: if you already have the right tools, this combines them well. That's a fact about aggregation, not about the world.

(4) Dara: you don't need to pick the list well. The guarantee holds for any list, including one that contains the true law. If the truth is in there, you'll track it. Which is the most anyone could ask — compare a Bayesian with the right prior, who converges. Nobody calls that circular.

(5) Owen: that's the circularity, relocated. If the truth is in the list, you'll get it — sure, and if the future resembles the past, induction works. Same shape of conditional. And your Bayesian comparison hurts you: everyone agrees the right prior is the hard part. You've just swapped the hard part from 'the world is regular' to 'the world is regular in a way your list can express.'

(6) Dara: no, the difference is that the meta-method doesn't need the truth in the list in order to avoid being beaten. For any list, any sequence, it matches the best member. And the list can be enormous — take every computable predictor. Then the only way to lose is if none of them works, which is a much weaker assumption than uniformity of nature.

(7) Owen: every computable predictor isn't finite, and the theorem is for finite lists. Go infinite and the weights have to sum to something, and then either the guarantee weakens or the best method can end up with vanishing weight. And 'none of them works' isn't so weak — none of them is guaranteed to work on an arbitrary sequence, they can all fail together. Meanwhile the meta-method is itself a method, so no free lunch applies to it too; there's a sequence where it does no better than coin-flipping. You traded a universal guarantee for a conditional one and called the conditional a theorem.

(8) Dara: but the conditional is provable, and it's conditional on your own tools, not on the world. You can't be suckered by a world that rewards a stupid method and punishes yours, because you'll just shift weight to the stupid method. And 'eventually' doesn't bother me — the question was whether science is rational in the long run, not whether it nails next Tuesday.

(9) Owen: an answer to a question nobody asked. Hume asked why we should think the future will be like the past. 'If it is, and if your hypothesis space happens to contain the right pattern, you'll catch on eventually' is not that. And you can't have it both ways — either the long run is what matters, in which case you're conceding that over any finite stretch the method can be worse than useless, or the short run matters, in which case your theorem says nothing.
