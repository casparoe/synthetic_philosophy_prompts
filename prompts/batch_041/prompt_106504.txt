I am a novelist — historical detective fiction, principally — and I have been asked to give a two-hour session at a week-long residency for working fiction writers: twelve of them, none with mathematics beyond secondary school, several of whom will regard any mention of probability as an intrusion on the business of writing, and none of whom will have done any preparation. The session has a whiteboard and nothing else. What I want them to leave with is a working feel for the problem of old evidence as it arises in Bayesian confirmation — the puzzle that a fact already in one's possession cannot raise the probability of a hypothesis, since one's credence in the fact is already maximal, and yet we are all confident that cases are sometimes made out of precisely such facts. I have been calling the session the detective's retroactive confidence, and I would far rather my writers felt the puzzle in their hands than learned to name its parts. So I am not asking you to explain the problem to me; I am asking you to plan how I should explain it.

Give me the order in which the pieces must go, and why each must wait for the one before it — which idea has to be in place before the question can even be posed to this audience, and which apparent preliminaries are in fact traps. Then the example. More than anything else, the session will stand or fall on the single case I build it around, and I have a candidate from my own manuscript which I want you to rule on rather than assume: in the second chapter, casually, and treated by everyone including my detective as a piece of household colour, it is established that the dead man's dog was found shut in the scullery; only in the final act does that detail become evidence — evidence that the caller the dog would have savaged was someone the dog knew well — and my detective's confidence in her theory of the killing rises sharply although she has learned nothing whatever. Tell me whether that case can carry the whole two hours or whether it must be demoted to a subsidiary illustration, and if it cannot carry, supply the case that can — and in either event tell me what the carrying example must have and what it must conspicuously lack, and why.

I also want the misconceptions mapped. I expect at least one writer to insist the puzzle is really about memory — that the detective had forgotten and is merely being reminded, so the evidence is new after all — and another to object that no one is ever quite certain of anything, which I suspect dissolves the puzzle rather than solving it, though I could not tell you why. A third, I am fairly sure, will reach for the thought that the scullery detail is confirmed by being explained, a phrase I use myself and which I suspect is doing work I do not understand. Tell me which of these are real misconceptions and which are legitimate objections I must not head off, where in the sequence each should be met, and what I should say when I meet it. I have also been toying with a distinction of my own — between a fact's arrival and its later promotion to the status of evidence — and I want to know whether that distinction clarifies the sequence or corrupts it, and if it clarifies, exactly where it belongs. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say so from the outset, and give me one question to put to the room at the end that would show me whether they have understood the problem rather than merely enjoyed it.
