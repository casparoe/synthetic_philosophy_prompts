I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy -- my dissertation is actually on algorithmic fairness, but I TA a moral theory course, and I've gotten into a long and increasingly heated arguement with two friends about whether moral principles really exist or whether principle-talk is just case-by-case judgment wearing a lab coat. To settle it I've been writing down my own gut verdicts on a pile of promise cases to see whether any single rule reproduces them. Here's the pile, roughly in the order I thought of them. (1) I promise to help a friend move on Saturday, then cancel because my kid has to go to the ER -- obviously fine. (2) Same promise, I cancel because another friend offered me a ballgame ticket -- definately not fine. (3) I cancel the move to help a third friend move the same day, where the first friend will easily find a replacement; I ask her first and she says go ahead -- I think that's fine. (4) Same as (3) except I just don't tell the first friend, and she'd have said go ahead if I'd asked -- not fine, even though she ends up exactly as well off either way. (5) I cancel because I'm too sick or too anxious to be any use on the day -- fine. (6) I promised my closest friend, who died last year, that I'd finish editing her novel; I'm now spending every weekend on it instead of seeing my family, and I still feel like I owe it to her, though I'd also say I'm allowed to quit if it were genuinely wrecking my life. (7) A promise I made to myself to go to the gym -- breaking it isn't a moral failure at all, it's just me being lazy. (8) I tell a colleague I've sent a check that I haven't sent, to buy a few days -- not fine. (9) I tell my aunt, unprompted, that her terrible casserole is delicious -- fine-ish, and it feels different from (8) in a way I can't quite name.

My tentative rule was going to be 'it's OK to break a promise exactly when keeping it would leave the world worse off, counting everyone equally,' but (3) and (4) seem to kill it -- the first friend ends up identically well off in both, yet the verdicts flip -- and (6) and (7) kill it too, since no living person's welfare is at stake in either, yet one binds and one doesn't. So either there's a much more intricate principle that handles the whole pile, or my verdicts are just a bundle of trained habits and my generalist friends are right to gloat. I want you to actually take a side here and not just lay the options out for me.

Give me the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could test it against new cases -- and be committed: pick the one you think is right and defend it rather than hedging. I need your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no intro and no wrap-up. One item should be the principle itself; one should name, for each clause, the case in my list that forces that clause; one should apply your principle to a case in the same neighborhood that I haven't listed and say what it predicts; one should say honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and why; and the last should give your own verdict on the big question -- whether there's a principle here at all, or whether particularism wins.
