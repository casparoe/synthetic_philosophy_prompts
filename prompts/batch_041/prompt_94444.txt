Many public health systems delegate the question of which treatments the system will pay for to a body that ranks treatments by cost per quality-adjusted life-year. A year of life in full health counts as one; a year lived with a disabling condition counts as some fraction of one, fixed by weights elicited from the general public or from clinicians; and a treatment is funded when it buys enough of these units per unit of money spent, with a threshold that is applied more generously to treatments that extend life at the very end of life.

I would like to know what axiology this practice presupposes. Specifically: (1) The practice will buy a large number of low-quality life-years in place of a small number of high-quality ones whenever the arithmetic favours it. Does that commit the practice to something in the neighbourhood of the repugnant conclusion, or does the repugnant conclusion arise only in comparisons between populations of different sizes, which this practice never has to make? If the practice is silent on how many people there should be, what exactly is the quality-versus-quantity trade-off it does make, and is that trade-off a weaker cousin of the repugnant conclusion or a different thing altogether? (2) The end-of-life exception looks like a departure from straight aggregation. Does it presuppose a rule of rescue, or the view that identifiable lives count for more than statistical ones? Is that consistent with the aggregation used in every other funding decision, or does the body need two principles that cannot both be derived from a single axiology? (3) Which rival views — totalism, averagism, critical-level views, prioritarianism, person-affecting views — would license the practice as it stands, and which does it quietly rule out? (4) The quality weights are elicited rather than derived from any theory of welfare: whose judgments about the value of a life with a disability does the practice treat as authoritative, and what does that presuppose about the objectivity of the scale?

Finally, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me what would have to change — the weights, the thresholds, the readiness to trade welfare across persons — if we replaced the implicit axiology with one that refuses the repugnant conclusion outright.
