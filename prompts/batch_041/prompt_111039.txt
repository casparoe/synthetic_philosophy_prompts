I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. A colleague of mine, who completed a doctorate in philosophy before moving into engineering, and I have spent a good many lunch breaks arguing about which of two possible worlds is better when the worlds differ in how many people they contain. She has given me firm verdicts on three comparisons of that kind, and I now want a prediction about a fourth. She tells me the general shape of these puzzles is well known in the literature; I have deliberately not looked anything up, because I want your reasoning from the cases rather than a label.

The cases share one stipulative setup, which I will state once and then not repeat.

1. Every person's life is assigned a number between 0 and 100. 0 marks a life that is neither good nor bad to live; 100 marks the best life I can imagine. A life at 1 is therefore only just worth living.
2. Within a case, everyone listed is exactly as well off as the number says, and the numbers of people are exact.
3. Nobody's welfare depends on how many other people there are: no resource constraints, no externalities, no effects of one group on another.
4. Unless I say otherwise, the people in one world are not the people in the other. Where I say that the same people appear in both worlds, they are the same individuals.
5. She is asking which world is better in itself — not better for anyone in particular — and she counts everyone's interests equally.

Case 1. World A contains ten billion people at 80 and no one else. World B contains those same ten billion people at 80, together with a further ten billion people at 70 who have no effect of any kind on the first group. Her verdict: B is better than A.

Case 2. World A, against World Z. World Z contains those same ten billion people, except that in Z they are at 2 rather than at 80, together with a hundred trillion further people, all at 2. Her verdict: A is better than Z, and she says it is not close.

Case 3. World A, against World C. World C contains those same ten billion people, unaffected, at 80, together with a trillion further people at 1. Her verdict: A is better than C.

Case 4, the one I want predicted. World P contains ten billion people at 80 and no one else. World Q contains those same ten billion people, but at 20 rather than at 80, together with ninety billion further people at 20; so Q holds a hundred billion people, all at 20 — ten times as many people as P, and every one of them comfortably above the level she counts as a life worth living. Which does she prefer?

Please give me the following, in this order.

First, the two or three rules that fit her three verdicts, stated in plain language and precisely enough that I could apply each one to a new case myself, showing briefly, with whatever arithmetic you use, how each rule fits each verdict. If no single rule fits all three, say so and identify which verdict is the odd one out. (If one of these rules has a standard name among philosophers, mention it in a sentence; I am curious, but I do not want a reading list.)

Second, what each surviving rule implies about Case 4.

Third, your best guess at her verdict on Case 4, with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others.

Fourth, one further comparison of your own construction that would most cleanly separate the rules that survive her three verdicts, and what her verdict on it would tell us.

Fifth, a note on where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and what would change your prediction — a further verdict from her, or an argument showing that one of the rules cannot be the one she is running.

Please write in British English, and end with a single final line of exactly this form: \answer{...}, where the braces contain the label of the world you predict she will prefer (P or Q), a colon, and then your name for the rule you are backing, in no more than eight words.
