I'm not a philosopher by training, but for about a year I've been trying to work out why my own thinking, and the thinking of people close to me, drifts in ways none of us notices at the time. I've put together a list of eight situations I've either lived through or watched closely, and I'd like you to rank them from most to least damaging to the person's ability to figure out what is actually true. I don't mean which are most morally objectionable, or most common, or most politically consequential — I mean which leave the person worst off purely as a knower, with a sentence or two of justification for each placement. Where two of them strike you as the same problem wearing different clothes, say so rather than pretending they're distinct, and where I've described something in a way that quietly begs a question, tell me.

(a) A man reads only news and commentary from sources that share his politics. He has no idea that serious, well-informed people disagree with him; if you told him they existed, he'd say he'd simply never looked. (b) A woman reads an equally narrow set of sources, but she knows precisely what the other side says and believes those sources lie on purpose. When a friend forwards her an opposing article, she treats the forwarding itself as further evidence of the friend's gullibility. (c) A man is embedded in a community where everyone agrees with him and where voicing doubt is a form of disloyalty, so the price of disagreement is social rather than informational. (d) A woman holds unusual views she reached by herself, reading widely on both sides, and is now isolated because everyone she knows thinks she is wrong. (e) A community of hobbyists who agree on nearly everything but who maintain a standing norm of seeking out the strongest available outsider and rewarding whoever argues against them best. (f) A man who agrees with his community on the central question but dissents on a small technical sub-question, and has quietly stopped mentioning it. (g) A person whose sources are diverse in name but all funded by the same interest, so that the apparent disagreement between them is manufactured. (h) A person who consumes a steady diet of opposing views, but only as curated, excerpted and rebutted by his own side, so he has encountered every argument and none of them has landed.

Two things I'd especially like you to address. First, I have a nagging sense that (a) and (b) are different problems and that whatever makes (b) worse isn't just a matter of degree — I'd like to know whether that intuition is defensible or whether I'm romanticising one of them. Second, for whichever three you rank as worst, tell me what an outsider could realistically do to help, and be honest about whether anything at all would work. Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who study this from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested position as though it were the consensus; if the ranking itself depends on a disputed assumption about what knowledge is for, flag that rather than burying it.
