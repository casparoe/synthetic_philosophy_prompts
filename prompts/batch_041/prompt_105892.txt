I'm building a little anthology of deadpan satire about consciousness debates and I need one more piece for it. Write me an agony-aunt advice column, around 600 words, from an in-universe newspaper where illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is just the house position, nobody argues about it, it's the water they swim in. The columnist runs the paper's "Ask Dr. ___" slot and the letter he's answering is from a reader who says she's had a dull, ineffable ache in her chest for years, that it's there when she wakes up, that she can't describe it to her husband, and she wants to know what she's supposed to do about it.

He has to answer the way an actual illusionist would. That means he can never deny that she's suffering, and he can never tell her she's mistaken about anything she reports — the ache is real as a state, the seeming is real, he's just not going to grant it the extra thing she's implicitly asking for. So all his advice has to be about the representation, the attention she pays to it, the vocabulary she uses in her diary, the way the signal gets re-weighted when she's tired. And it should be genuinely decent advice on those terms, the way a competent clinician would talk. The comedy is the gap between what she keeps asking and what he keeps being able to answer: she pushes back three or four times, each time more directly, and each time his reply is technically correct and misses her question by exactly one step. If he ever comes off as the cartoon version who thinks pain isn't a thing, the piece has failed — that's a misreading and the joke dies. Also don't let him wink at the reader, no "one might wonder" nudges, no scare quotes doing the work for him. Play it straight from the first line to the last, so that someone who's read the literature laughs and someone who hasn't still feels that something's being dodged without being able to say what.

Give him a plausible name and a plausible masthead, but don't put words in the mouth of any real person and don't reference real papers. And no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables — continuous prose only, the way a column actually reads.
