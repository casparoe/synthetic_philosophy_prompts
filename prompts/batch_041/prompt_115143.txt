I'm a self-taught letterer — I paint signs by hand, and for the past couple of years I've been slowly drawing out a full typeface of my own — and a question that started out purely practical has turned into one I can't stop turning over. When I push a design far enough to give it real character, there always comes a moment where I look at my 'a' and think: that's a handsome shape, but it has stopped being an 'a'. I can never say what crossed the line. I once read about a computer program that was supposed to invent whole alphabets in one consistent style, and what struck me was that the drawing can't have been the hard part there — the hard part had to be deciding when a shape still counts as the letter it is meant to be. That is exactly the wall I keep hitting, and I would like you to compare the two answers I keep sliding between, and tell me which one actually holds up.

The first answer says a letter is defined by its features. An 'a' is a bowl with a stem down its right side — the two-storey version is that same shape with a second, smaller bowl sitting on top of it — and once those ingredients are present, everything else is style: weight, slant, stroke contrast, how wild the terminals get. The second answer says that gets it exactly backwards, and that a letter has no definition inside itself at all; it is defined by its place in a system of differences. On this view an 'a' is the shape that stands in a particular contrast-relation to the 'o', the 'e', the 'd' and the rest, in something like the way a sound is only a phoneme because of the other sounds it is opposed to — change the rest of the alphabet and you change what counts as an 'a'. I'm drawn to the second because it explains something the first can't: why a brutally distorted 'a' still reads instantly when it sits inside a word, and why the very same shape can read as an 'a' on one page and as a squiggle on another. But it seems to carry a consequence I can't swallow, namely that a lone 'a' on an otherwise blank page, with nothing to contrast with, would not be an 'a' at all. The first answer has its own failures: it can't tell me why an 'a' that has every feature on the list sometimes still fails to read, or why single-storey and two-storey 'a's are the same letter rather than two different ones that happen to be spelled alike.

So what I want is not a survey of what other people have said, but a verdict: which of these is the better account of what makes every 'a' an 'a', what precisely does each one get right that the other cannot, and how much of the losing view actually has to be given up — or is there a way of holding both that isn't just a mush of the two? If you do land on a verdict, tell me what would change your mind: which case, or which consideration about how letters really work, would push you toward the other answer. And say where your own reasoning is thinnest, since I would rather know where the argument is likely to break than be handed a tidy conclusion.
