I am a novelist — fifteen years of practice, a lay reader's acquaintance with the public argument over how judges interpret statutes, and no legal training whatever. What I do have is a professional vocabulary for the relation between a text and its maker: the outline the finished book quietly abandoned, the draft and the revision, the character who takes over, the passage that turns out on a third reading to mean something the author did not know she was saying, the editor's margin note, the director's cut, the letters and interviews in which the author explains herself and which critics tell us to ignore.

Take the dispute between textualism and purposivism — whether a statute means what its words say to a competent reader of the language, or what the enacting legislature was trying to accomplish — and restate it as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary. Treat the statute as a text, the legislature as a body of authors, the judge as a reader, and the canons of construction, the use and abuse of legislative history, the absurdity doctrine, and the counterfactual about what the enacting body would have wanted in a case it never imagined as the machinery of authorship and revision I already understand. Where the mapping is clean, say so plainly and without ceremony. Where you must bend my terms to make them fit — where purpose is asked to do work that no novelist's notion of intention can do, or where the reader's authority in law has no counterpart in a reader's authority over a novel — say that too, and say exactly what was bent. I am not interested in a translation that flatters the source by quietly importing its assumptions.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which claims in the legal debate cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I expect the candidates to include the bindingness of the interpreter's decision, the fact that a statute is an instrument for generating outcomes for parties who never consented to it, the collective and largely anonymous character of the author, and the difference between what a text means and what it is for. For each, tell me whether the gap is a defect in my framework — something about texts and makers I have failed to notice because my trade never forced me to — or a sign that the lawyers are disagreeing about something other than meaning, perhaps about who should be trusted with the power to decide. I would also like to know whether the anti-intentionalist arguments I half-remember from criticism, together with the common novelist's experience of not knowing what her own book means until it is finished, translate into anything usable on the purposivist side, or whether they are a false friend that only sounds like one.

Do not be diplomatic about the residue. If it turns out that the dispute is less about interpretation than about the allocation of authority, and that my vocabulary is missing the concept of authority rather than the concept of meaning, I would rather hear it plainly.
