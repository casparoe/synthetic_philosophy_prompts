Some friends and I have been arguing about whether the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers earns its keep, or whether it is just two words for the same failure mode with the volume turned up. The version of the distinction we have been using runs as follows. A bubble is a social-epistemic situation in which the relevant opposing view simply never reaches you: the exclusion is by omission, and your trust in the sources you do have is, for all anyone knows, perfectly well calibrated. An echo chamber, by contrast, is one in which the opposing view does reach you, or at least sits within easy reach, but you have been supplied in advance with a general reason to discount it — the exclusion is by manipulation of trust, so that the encounter is drained of its power to move you. The natural suspicion is that these are two points on a single spectrum, and that anything worth calling an echo chamber is also, necessarily, a bubble.

I would like a thought experiment that tests whether the two can be prised apart, built to order rather than merely illustrated. Two scenarios, each a few paragraphs at most, set in ordinary contemporary life; no cults, no religious sects, no partisan political tribes, and no reusing the same corner of life for both — a hobby, a workplace, or a health context would all be fine. In the first, the subject is in an echo chamber but not an epistemic bubble: the outside view has genuinely been encountered, at length and in good faith, and yet leaves the subject's belief untouched. In the second, the subject is in an epistemic bubble but not an echo chamber: the outside view has never reached them, but they have not been primed to distrust it, and would take it seriously if it arrived. Hold the content of the belief fixed across the two scenarios — same proposition, roughly the same evidentiary situation — so that the only difference is the mechanism by which the belief is sustained; likewise hold the subject's intelligence and sincerity fixed, since I do not want one of them to be dim and the other clever.

After the scenarios, do the audit. Say which single feature of each case is doing the work, and what happens when you remove it — whether the echo-chamber case collapses into a bubble, or into mere error, or into something else entirely, and whether the bubble case survives as a distinct structure or is merely ignorance with a social flavour. Then tell me which of the two is harder to repair, and whether that ranking follows from the structure you have built or is an artifact of the details you happened to pick. Finally, put on the hat of someone who thinks the distinction is overblown: what is the strongest objection to your pair of cases, and what does a defender of the distinction have to say in reply? If you conclude that the two features cannot in fact come apart, say so plainly and show why — but attempt the construction first.
