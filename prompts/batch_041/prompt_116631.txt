I'm in a small reading group that's slowly grinding through Gödel, Escher, Bach, and last week we hit the Ant Fugue, the dialogue where Achilles and the Tortoise and the Anteater end up talking about an ant colony. Every ant in the colony is doing something you could write down in one line -- follow a pheromone gradient, drop a crumb, bump a neighbour and turn left -- and not one of them has any idea what's going on. But the colony as a whole holds up its end of a conversation: the ants arrange themselves into marks on the ground and the marks turn out to be words, and Hofstadter gives the colony a name, Aunt Hillary, and talks with her like a person. So we ended the session stuck on whether there's anyone home in the colony or whether "Aunt Hillary" is just a handy way of talking about ants. The annoying part is that both answers cost you. Say yes and you seem to owe a mind to every aggregate -- a market, a city, your gut, the traffic on the 101 -- since all of them are also just a lot of dumb parts following local rules. Say no and you owe an account of why the colony fails the test when your brain, which is also just cells doing local things, passes it. I want the poem to make a reader feel that pinch, not hand them a verdict.

So: a Petrarchan sonnet (abbaabba, then whatever you like for the sestet) in the voice of Aunt Hillary. The octave should be pure ant-level business, and I mean pure -- no mental vocabulary, no thinks, knows, wants, decides, no first person. Each line should be one ant doing one trivial thing, and ideally no single line makes sense on its own; the sentence should only assemble when you read the lines together, the way the colony's meaning only exists a level above the ants. Put at least one image in there that does double duty, something that's a meaningless bit of ant business at one level and a word, or a comma, in the colony's utterance at the other -- the reader should be able to catch it working both ways, the way the marks on the ground are just bodies until suddenly they're letters. Then the volta should be the arrival of the first person: no new character, just 'I' showing up at the level of the whole, and the sestet is the colony speaking as one thing. Have her turn and face the obvious objection -- that she's only ants, that there's no extra ingredient anywhere -- and have her point out that the same objection is sitting in the reader's own skull. The closing couplet should say what she's claiming and, just as clearly, what she isn't claiming. Don't let her win the argument; I want her to be right about the pinch. If you know the dialogue, the level-crossing should be exact enough that someone whos read it doesn't wince. If you've never heard of it, it should still be a poem.
