The quarrel among negative, positive, and republican conceptions of liberty has been running in roughly its present form since the 1950s and shows no sign of resolution: the parties largely agree on the same historical examples yet read them differently, and each accuses the others of quietly changing the subject. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than an adjudication of it.

The positions, as I understand them, are these. The negative conception identifies liberty with the absence of interference: a person is free to the extent that no one obstructs, coerces, or forcibly prevents her from doing what she might otherwise do. The positive conception identifies liberty with self-mastery — being governed by one's own rational will rather than by alien impulses or by other people, a condition some of its defenders extend to collective self-rule. The republican conception identifies liberty with the absence of domination: a person is unfree when she stands in a relation of arbitrary dependence on another, even if that other never actually interferes. The case that does the most work for the third view is the slave of a benevolent and unobtrusive master, who is never obstructed and yet is said to be unfree; the negative theorist replies that such a slave is unfree because she is exposed to interference, not because interference is merely possible, and the republican replies that exposure to arbitrary power is not a probabilistic matter of this kind. Meanwhile the positive conception is shadowed by the suspicion that its language of a higher or real self licenses others to coerce a person in the name of her own freedom.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that the dispute is only partly verbal, and that the verbal part is smaller than either side assumes. Gerald MacCallum long ago proposed that every claim about freedom has the same triadic form: an agent is free from some constraint to do or become something, so that the negative/positive contrast marks a difference in which of the three places gets filled in, not a difference in the concept itself. If that is right, a good deal of the apparent disagreement dissolves. What I want to know is whether anything is left, and if so, what kind of thing it is.

So: is the residue a hidden empirical or formal question — for instance, whether non-domination can be reconstructed as non-interference plus some probability of interference, or whether the two ever come apart in a way that matters for action? Is it a disagreement about which cases count as data — a bedrock intuition about the happy slave, or about the contented slave who reports no desire to be free, that no argument on either side can reach? Are the parties answering different questions under one name — one asking what makes a constraint a constraint, another asking how secure a person's position must be before it counts as free, a third asking what work the concept is meant to do in political argument? Or is liberty so essentially contested that the question of who is right is ill-posed rather than merely hard?

I want the diagnosis argued from what each side actually says, not from a survey of the literature, and I want an honest verdict on my MacCallum-style hypothesis — including whether it dissolves less of the dispute than I think. If the disagreement is substantive, say precisely what it is about; if it is partly verbal, say which part and why; if it rests on intuitions that cannot be argued for, say so plainly and identify the case that would expose the dependence. End with an account of what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a formal or empirical result, a discriminating case, or a reason to think no progress should be expected, and why.
