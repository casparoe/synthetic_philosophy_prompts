My case, and my opening shot. Invented community, doesn't matter which one: at dinner parties there, raising your left eyebrow means 'this food is terrible'. It's a settled convention, known to everyone, as fixed as the meaning of a word. Now A and B are at dinner, and A is B's rival. A doesn't intend to communicate anything — it's a tic, or A is frowning about something else entirely and the brow goes up. A knows the convention perfectly well, so A knows exactly what B will take from it. B sees the brow and takes A to have insulted the food. And B has not made a mistake. B recieved the message that act carries in that community; if B later tells me 'A said the food was awful', I think B is right, as right as if A had opened their mouth. (And don't just tell me it's natural meaning, like smoke meaning fire — there's a convention doing the work here, the same kind that fixes word meanings.)

So it looks to me like you've got the order of explanation backwards: the practice makes the act mean that, and the intention is just one more thing that can line up with the practice or fail to. Your view has to say B is wrong, that nothing was communicated at all. That seems like alot bigger a cost than dropping the intention story. So — does A mean the food is awful here, or not?
