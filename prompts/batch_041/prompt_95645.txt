I have been at work for the better part of a year on a novel whose central character — a translator raised in Seoul and educated in Boston — is engaged to render a bundle of classical Confucian family letters into English for a foundation's centennial volume, and who discovers that the moral vocabulary she inherited and the one she acquired keep refusing to line up. Because the book turns on whether she can hold both languages of selfhood in view at once, I have been reading, somewhat haphazardly, in comparative ethics, and I have reached the point where I need someone whose judgment I trust to tell me which of my working assumptions are sound.

At a residency last month a colleague — a novelist, not a philosopher, but an unusually well-read one — gave me his account of the matter over dinner, and it has lodged in my head because he delivered it with such confidence. His view, roughly, was this: the whole dispute between East Asian and Western conceptions of the person comes down to collectivism against individualism; the Confucian tradition treats a person as constituted by his roles and relationships, which is why East Asian societies subordinate the individual to the group and prize harmony over self-assertion, while the Western tradition begins from the solitary, rights-bearing individual who owes nothing to a community he did not choose; the Confucian self has no room for the dissenting conscience, being defined entirely by its place in the social order; and the empirical literature has settled the question, since the cross-cultural psychologists' surveys show that East Asians describe themselves in relational terms and Westerners in autonomous ones — from which it follows that the two sides cannot really argue with each other, only choose.

I would like you to audit that account rather than replace it. Take the claims one at a time and tell me which a philosopher working in the comparative tradition would accept as a fair, if brisk, statement of a real position; which are the ordinary simplifications one makes when speaking off the cuff, and what the more careful version would say instead; and which are simply mistaken — the places where two distinct claims have been fused into one, or a modest thesis inflated into a sweeping one, or a standard objection smuggled in as a refutation. I am especially uncertain about the move from what people report on a questionnaire to what a tradition holds about what a person is, and about whether the classical tradition truly leaves no room for individual moral judgment; if it does leave room, I want the strongest form of that case in plain prose, and I would rather you reason it out for me than send me to the library for it. Finally, tell me what survives of his conclusion — that the two views cannot argue but only choose — once the corrections are in, since that conclusion is the one my protagonist would most like to believe, and I would prefer to learn now, while the manuscript is still soft, that it is an illusion.
