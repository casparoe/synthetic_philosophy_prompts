The question was:

(a) Using the three cases below, distinguish the indicative/subjunctive contrast from the counterfactual/non-counterfactual contrast, and say what this tells us about whether one semantics can cover all 'if'-sentences. [8 pts]

(i) 'If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did' vs. 'If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have.'
(ii) At the bedside, a doctor reasons: 'if he'd been given arsenic, this is exactly the symptom picture we'd expect' — offered as a reason to think he *was* given arsenic.
(iii) 'If you're thirsty, there's beer in the fridge.'

(b) State the hypothesis that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, say what the triviality results do to the truth-conditional programme, and sketch one truth-conditional treatment that survives them. [12 pts]

Student's answer:

"These two contrasts are usually run together, and that's the first mistake. 'Indicative' and 'subjunctive' name the grammatical form of the conditional; 'counterfactual' is a semantic notion, roughly that the antecedent is false. They cut across each other, so a conditional can be subjunctive without being counterfactual. Case (ii) is the illustration: the doctor's 'if he'd been given arsenic' is not offered as a counterfactual supposition but as part of an argument that the antecedent is true. Standard counterfactual (i.e. subjunctive) conditionals like the second sentence in (i) are the ones where the antecedent is assumed false.

(i) shows the classic contrast. The indicative 'If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did' is true — Kennedy was shot, and if it wasn't Oswald it was somebody else. The subjunctive 'If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have' isn't supported by the same facts at all; the plot might have been called off. Same antecedent, same consequent, different truth value, purely because of the mood. This is the main datum any theory has to explain.

(b) The hypothesis is P(if A, then B) = P(B|A). It does well for indicatives: the probability that if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy someone else did just is the conditional probability that someone else did given that Oswald didn't, which is 1. Adams showed this can't be combined with a truth-conditional semantics, since the triviality results prove that conditionals have no truth conditions at all. So accounts on which 'if A, then B' expresses a proposition — Stalnaker's possible-worlds account, for instance — are refuted, and we're left treating 'if' as an expression of conditional probability rather than as a propositional connective.

That said, a uniform semantics is still possible if we follow Kratzer and treat 'if' as a restrictor: the if-clause restricts the modal base of the operator in the consequent, so (i) and (ii) differ only in which modal base is restricted, not in the meaning of 'if'. For subjunctives the antecedent has probability zero, so the conditional probability is undefined and Adams's thesis simply doesn't apply to them.

Case (iii) is the hard one. 'If you're thirsty, there's beer in the fridge' doesn't assert any connection between your thirst and the beer, and it's true whether or not you're thirsty. So 'if'-clauses aren't conditional connectives in the ordinary sense at all, and a truth-conditional semantics is the wrong shape for them; presumably they're a pragmatic effect, though I don't see how to square that with the restrictor view."
