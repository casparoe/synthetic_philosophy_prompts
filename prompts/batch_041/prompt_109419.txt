A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the White Horse dialogue, and I would rather work the argument out of my system in verse than in prose. Her position is that the whole thing collapses into a pun: that the original negative copula is ambiguous between is not identical to and is not a kind of, that the paradox lives entirely on that ambiguity, and that the School of Names is therefore a footnote to grammar. Mine is that the argument survives the ambiguity being cleared up, because its real force is extensional — asking for a horse lets you take any colour, whereas asking for a white horse rules the brown ones out — so the two terms do not have the same extension and the not is doing honest work. Please write me a sestina that takes my side by its form rather than by assertion. Choose six end-words that let the permutation carry the argument (white, horse, name, shape, colour, not are the obvious candidates, but the set is yours to pick), and let the cycle keep re-pairing them the way the dialogue keeps pulling shape and colour apart, so that by the tornada the reader can feel why white horse and horse come apart with no equivocation anywhere in the poem. It has to work as a poem and not as a lecture — no ending that shrugs into it's all just language — recognisably right to someone who knows the dialogue, and intelligible to someone who has never heard of it. Do not quote the dialogue or any translation of it; I don't want a borrowed line doing the arguing for us.
