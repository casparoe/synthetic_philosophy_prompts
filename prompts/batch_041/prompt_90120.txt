The vocabulary I actually think in is the vocabulary of a proof assistant. I work in Lean and Coq, so I am at home with terms and types, inference rules versus axioms, the trusted kernel, definitional versus propositional equality, elaboration, and the distinction between applying a rule and asserting a proposition. Carroll's dialogue in which the tortoise refuses to be moved from the premises to the conclusion has always struck me as a description of a machine I know from the inside, but I want the translation done as a translation rather than as a loose analogy.

Take the tortoise's position -- that accepting the premises and accepting the conditional does not yet oblige you to accept the conclusion, and that any further principle you supply is simply one more premise to be granted before the conclusion follows -- and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary above. Then give me the residue report, in three parts.

First, what survives translation intact: for instance, the collapse of the attempt to convert a rule of inference into an axiom, the regress that results, and the thought that following a rule is not itself a further act of assertion.

Second, what translates only with distortion, and where the distortion lies.

Third, what has no counterpart at all -- in particular, the tortoise's apparent standing to withhold assent, and the sense in which a rule is binding on a reasoner rather than merely being what the kernel does when it checks a derivation. Note that a proof assistant reintroduces something like normativity through the notion of the trusted kernel, and say whether that is a genuine translation of the missing item or a substitute that changes the subject.

Close with the verdict the residue invites: is this a defect of the formal vocabulary, something a proof assistant genuinely cannot say, or evidence that the tortoise's demand was ill-posed, a request for a proposition where only a practice exists? Tell me which verdict a working logician is more likely to defend, and why. I know this vocabulary, so do not gloss the terms. At most 600 words, and lead with the one-sentence verdict.
