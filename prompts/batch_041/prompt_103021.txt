I've been trying to get a grip on superogation by going case by case and seeing where my gut lands, and my gut turns out to be a mess. Wading into a pond and ruining your shoes to pull out a drowning toddler: not optional, you just have to. Giving a stranger a kidney: not required, and I wouldn't think less of someone who didn't. Throwing yourself on a grenade for your squad: also not required -- nobody could be blamed for not doing it -- but I would think less of them, and it's obviously the better act. My colleague covering my courses for a semester while I did chemo: he didn't have to, but it doesn't feel like the kidney. Someone who gives half their salary to anti-malaria charities: I can't bring myself to say it's required, and yet if they spent that money on a third car I'd think something had gone wrong with them. And my aunt sold her house to pay for her nephew's surgery, which wrecked her own kids' college plans -- nobody could have demanded she not do it, but I don't find it admirable at all.

Is there a principle that gets all of this, or close? Don't just tell me it's the paradox of supererogation -- I want the actual principle, and for each clause, the case it's doing the work on. Then tell me what it says about the one I keep flip-flopping on: a friend who left a tenure-track job for aid work abroad and left his daughter with his ex. And if some of my verdicts have to go, which ones?
