I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of mind seminar, and each of us has to design and lead one 75-minute session. Mine is the week on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment: the worry that if experiences are mere byproducts of brain activity, then our judgments about our own experiences are not caused by the experiences they are supposed to be about, and it becomes unclear what makes them judgments about experience at all, or why we should trust them. I have 14 students, a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard, a folding partition I can borrow from the drama department, and whatever stationery I care to buy. Nobody will have done more than the week's survey reading, so the session has to carry the idea on its own.

I don't want a debate and I don't want a lecture. I want an activity in which the rules do the work, so that people run into the problem by playing, before anyone names it. My instinct is a reporting-and-observation game built around a partition: someone in a position to "have" the experience, someone in a position to "read it off," arranged so that the report can be perfectly accurate while nothing ever flows from the experience to the report. Then a later round in which the correlation breaks and the person reporting cannot in principle tell. Build something along those lines, or something better that does the same job; the constraint is that the rule that nothing may flow out of the experience has to be enforced by the mechanics of the game, not announced by me afterwards.

Three things I specifically want the activity to produce. First, that participants discover they cannot, from the inside, distinguish the round where their report was caused by what they saw from the round where it was merely correlated with it. Second, that this applies to the judgment "I am experiencing such-and-such" itself, so the rule turns on the person judging, not only on the person reporting. Third, that by the end the same rule has quietly turned on me and on the class: if no judgment may be caused by an experience, then nobody in the room is entitled to the judgment that anyone is having experiences, including the judgment that the game was about anything. I want that to arrive as a discovery, not as a remark I make at the end.

Please include: rules and materials; the roles, what each role is told in advance, and how to keep a participant from breaking the game by refusing to comply; a timeline for the 75 minutes; a scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't require real money; and a debrief that maps each round onto a piece of the problem — the common-cause structure, the difference between a lawful correlation and a coincidence, the epistemic consequence, and the self-stultification step. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do about it. Finally, be honest about what an activity of this kind cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by talking. I suspect it cannot give anyone a genuine first-person experience with a known causal profile, and cannot distinguish the epiphenomenalist's brute-law story from a parallelism in which the correlation is guaranteed rather than lucky; tell me if I'm wrong about that, and what else is missing.

Keep the whole reply under 900 words. I would rather have crisp rules than exhaustive options.
