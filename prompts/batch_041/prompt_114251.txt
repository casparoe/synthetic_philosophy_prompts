I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question that makes students actually use Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes on a case they can't have read about, because I'm making the case up. Please take the role of an experienced examiner who knows the Lakatos literature well and think the case through before you commit to a verdict, rather than reaching for the textbook summary.

Here's the case I want to hand them, so don't swap in a real one. Call it the solvent programme. Hard core: chronic low-level exposure to a class of industrial solvents causes a specific neurological syndrome. Around that core sit assumptions about dose thresholds, susceptible subpopulations, co-exposures, and how the syndrome gets scored. The history is mixed: the syndrome turns up in exposed workers, then doesn't turn up in a much larger study, then turns up again after the scoring rubric is revised so that two symptoms previously counted separately now count as one; the dose window is narrowed twice, each time with independent physiological argument behind it. There's one real success: from the mechanism the programme predicted the syndrome would be absent in workers carrying a particular enzyme variant, and it was. The rival programme says the syndrome is caused by chronic psychosocial stress at work. It fits the epidemiology about as well, hasn't generated a novel prediction in roughly fifteen years, and it too keeps adjusting its belt (stress measures, coping styles, exposure windows). Each programme has a couple of anomalies the other explains away.

The question should force them to do three things: say which programme Lakatos's criterion actually favours and on what grounds; say whether that criterion can deliver the verdict at the time the evidence comes in or only in hindsight, and what that does to its usefulness as advice; and say what a Popperian and a Kuhnian would each make of the same comparison. The crux I'm after is the difference between a belt adjustment that adds content and one that merely saves the core, so a student who just says "novel prediction, therefore progressive" should not get full marks.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme for 20 marks built on five must-hit points, with a one-line note on each saying how a solid-but-not-top answer typically misses it. If the case as I've set it up is too thin for the verdict to be anything but arbitrary, say so and tell me what to add, don't paper over it — I'd rather fix the question now.
