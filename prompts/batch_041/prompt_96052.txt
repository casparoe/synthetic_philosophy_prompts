I retired from analytical chemistry about six years ago and have been reading philosophy ever since — mostly ethics, whatever I can get from the library — so forgive me if I don't have the right words for any of this. Something about a practice I used to take for granted has been nagging at me and I'd like help digging out what it actually assumes.

Here's the practice. When a government agency wants to justify a new rule — a tighter limit on some solvent, say, or a highway guardrail standard — it runs a cost-benefit analysis, and to put a dollar value on the lives the rule would save it uses a figure usually called the value of a statistical life. The figure is not what a person would pay to save their own skin. It's inferred from choices: mostly from wage studies, where more dangerous jobs pay more, so if you assume workers know the risks and are free to take another job, the extra pay they demand for a little more chance of dying tells you what they'll trade a small risk for. More recently they also just ask people what they'd pay for a small reduction in risk. Then the agency multiplies that figure by the deaths the rule is expected to prevent and stacks it against the cost of the rule.

Three things about this bother me and I can't tell whether they're practical wrinkles or something deeper.

First, the figure is applied to everyone alike. A rule that saves a thousand lives is worth a thousand times the number, regardless of whether the people are rich or poor, old or young. But if the number really comes from what people choose to accept, and someone with money will pay more to avoid a risk than someone without, then we're saying every life counts the same while quietly pricing it by what a person can afford.

Second, when it's a named person in trouble — a miner trapped underground — we'll spend fantastic sums trying to reach them, way more per life than any rule would ever be allowed to spend. Same society, same citizens, completely different arithmetic.

Third, and this is the one that really gets me, the agency will lean on what people choose when the choices come out conveniant (wages, what people pay for safer cars) but overrule what people say and feel when that comes out inconvenient. There's a whole aparatus of expert risk assessment whose job is to tell the public that their fears about radiation or trace chemicals are irrational and out of proportion, and there the expert estimate wins. And the health economists down the hall do their evaluations in quality-adjusted life years, where a year of life is worth less if your health is poor — which looks like a completely different idea of what's good for a person than the wage number is.

So: what does this whole practice presuppose about what utility or welfare is, and about what makes a life go well? I take it the wage studies assume that choice, or preference, is the measure of a person's good and that each person is the best judge of their own interest. But does the practice also need the stronger claims — that a dollar is worth the same to everyone, and that welfare can be added up across people? Those seem to me like they can't all be true at once. Please reconstruct the view of welfare the practice is committed to, say which rival views it rules out and which it merely happens to sit alongside, and be straight about where the practice is underdetermined, where the same numbers could be read several different ways. And if you swapped the underlying view for another one — happiness instead of preference satisfaction, or a list of objective goods, or simply letting people's stated fears count — what would have to change on the ground?

I'm not after a reading list, I just want to see what I've been looking at all these years.
