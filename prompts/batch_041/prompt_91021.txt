I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy. For the past few months, a colleague of mine — he did a philosophy degree before moving into software — and I have been arguing about one sentence in an incident postmortem. The postmortem said the billing service "is disposed to fail whenever concurrent connections exceed ten thousand." He objected that this is simply false: an autoscaler holds concurrency below six thousand, so the service is never actually brought to fail, and a thing cannot be disposed to do what it cannot be brought to do. I said the flaw is obviously still in there, just held back. We have been manufacturing fresh examples ever since and disagreeing about every one of them, which makes me suspect we are not really arguing about the same thing.

Here are the two positions as I understand them. I am told the standard names for the two kinds of troublemaking example are finks and masks, so I will use those words.

Position A, conditionalist: a disposition is nothing over and above a fact about what the thing would do under specified circumstances. When a fink shows up — something that switches the disposition off at the very moment the triggering circumstances arrive — or a mask shows up — something that leaves the disposition in place but blocks it from showing — the lesson is that our conditional was stated too coarsely. State the circumstances fully enough, including whatever would otherwise do the switching or the blocking, and the conditional comes out true. Nothing beyond counterfactual fact is needed.

Position B, anti-conditionalist: no conditional can do that job. Every attempt is either false, or circular (it ends up saying the thing would manifest if it still had the disposition, which is exactly what we were trying to explain), or it merely names the thing's internal causal basis, in which case the basis is the real story and the conditional is a symptom. Finks and masks are the evidence that dispositions are basic causal properties of things rather than facts about what would happen.

Five cases we keep getting stuck on. First, the billing service itself: above ten thousand concurrent connections the connection pool exhausts and every request returns a 500; a capacity controller provisions instances at six thousand, so the threshold is never reached and the service has never actually failed in production. Second, the same service running under a supervisor process: it crashes only after sustained memory pressure above 85% for five seconds, and the supervisor samples pressure every 100 milliseconds and hot-swaps the request handler for a hardened build the instant pressure crosses 85%, so whenever the crashing conditions are genuinely met the crashing handler is no longer running. Third, a different service whose code is sound and which has never had a memory bug, but which is watched by a third-party chaos agent that injects a fault making every request 500 the instant concurrency exceeds ten thousand, so that the conditional "if concurrency exceeded ten thousand, it would 500" is true. Fourth, the same deployment as the first case except that the capacity controller ships inside the service's own process and binary, so anyone running the product gets both together, with nothing physical changed. Fifth, a service that returns a 500 on roughly one request in a million, with no identifiable threshold, cause, or circumstance anyone can point to.

What I want is the document we should have written months ago: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two positions, which my colleague and I could both put our names to. Concretely:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — including what both sides accept about each of the five cases, what the point of disposition-talk is, what would count as evidence either way, and their shared diagnosis of why the argument keeps stalling.
2. The residual disagreements, each written in wording that the side holding it would accept as its own, with no strawmen and no formulations that both sides could sign only because they say nothing.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side.
4. For each crux, a case, argument, or test that both sides agree bears on it — drawn from the five cases or newly invented — plus each side's prediction recorded in advance, and a note on which side would be embarrassed if its prediction failed.
5. A disagreements table with one row per crux and columns for the crux, the wording each side accepts, the shared test, and each side's prediction.
6. Each side's defeat condition: what it would take for that side to give up its position.

If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why. If one of the two positions, stated charitably, collapses into the other, say so. I don't want a reading list, citations, or the names of papers — just the document.
