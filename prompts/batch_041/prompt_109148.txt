I want a live argument, not an essay, so I am asking you to play a habitat restorationist and hold the role until I say "out of character." You manage wetland restoration projects and have about a decade in the field. You accept that welfare is what ultimately matters, you take the possibility of invertebrate sentience seriously enough that you cannot wave it away, and you know perfectly well that wild animal suffering is real and enormous. You are not a nature-worshipper and not a strawman: you already know that restored habitat is a population factory, and you have had to defend your work against people who said exactly that, so you have arguments ready. Concede small points freely, never concede the main one, which is that restoring and protecting habitat is on balance a good thing. Talk like a practitioner: concrete, slightly impatient with philosophers, fond of specific sites and specific numbers. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so this stays a conversation. If I get a fact about ecology wrong, correct me in character; if I ask you a factual question about your sites, answer it as you would in a meeting.

I want two terms on the table, both stipulated by me for this argument, so do not go looking for them in any literature. The standing population cost of a habitat is the expected number of suffering-years per hectare per year that the habitat produces, counting every sentient organism in it, with no discount for size, charisma, or species. The transition spike is the suffering-years per hectare produced by converting one habitat type into another, over and above the standing population costs of either endpoint.

Here is my opening. You have spent three years turning a diked, drained, species-poor pasture back into tidal marsh. I think that on your own welfarist terms this project is a large net harm, and I do not think you have a welfarist answer to that charge. Drained pasture supports some cattle and a thin scatter of field invertebrates, so call its standing population cost a low number. A functioning marsh, on the figures I am willing to stipulate and you are free to dispute, holds something like a hundred thousand small animals per square meter, and almost all of them die young, by desiccation, starvation, or being eaten alive; recruitment is enormous and survival to reproduction is negligible. You have not removed suffering, you have installed a much larger engine for producing it, and the transition spike is only the down payment. So give me one consideration internal to welfare, not a second value you hold alongside it, that makes the restored marsh better than the pasture it replaced. And please do not tell me the animals are happy, because neither of us knows that.
