I have been turning over the problem of causation by omission ever since an argument with my brother-in-law, a plumber, about a flooded basement. His position is that an omission is like a hole. Nobody digs a hole in a pipe; there is simply a place where the metal is not, and that place is what floods the floor, what gets photographed for the insurance file, what gets cut out and replaced. The hole is nothing at all, and yet the whole story turns on it. From this he concludes that failing to do something can be a cause in just the way that doing something can — that the plumber who never checked the joint is as responsible for the water as the one who cracked it — and he thinks the hole proves it, because the water would not be on the floor without it.

I want this audited rather than waved away. Lay out the correspondence: what plays the part of the pipe, the hole, the water, the pressure behind it, and the expectation that a joint gets checked in the first place. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the breakages that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are only decoration. The ones I keep coming back to are these. A hole has a location and a size, it could have been filled, and you can point at it; an omission has none of that until someone has already settled which of the countless things I did not do that day is the one that counts — and the analogy seems to take that settling as given rather than as the very thing that needs explaining. A hole is also there continuously, whereas an omission is tied to a moment and to an opportunity that may or may not have existed.

Then say what the picture quietly imports: whether it assumes there is always a determinate missing thing, whether it confuses our practice of singling out one absence to blame with the question of what actually produced the effect, and whether it can cope with cases where several omissions would each have been enough, or where the same non-action is harmless at one pressure and fatal at another.

And the part I most want: does his conclusion survive the audit? If the analogy can carry only the claim that omissions are the sort of thing we can identify and act on, but not that they are causes in the same sense as actions, say so plainly, and tell me whether some better comparison would carry what he is after.
