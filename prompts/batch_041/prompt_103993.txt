Ok, I need help drafting a memo and I'd rather it not be a mushball. Here's the situation.

I sit on the patient-safety / root-cause committee at a mid-sized hospital. Two years ago we ran a big investigation into a cluster of post-op infections and concluded, fairly confidently, that the driver was a specific lapse in how the sterile field got set up on one surgical line. That finding is now baked into our policy, our training, the whole thing. Fine. Now an outside group has done a re-analysis of our own data and argues the real driver was something else entirely — a contaminated water line in the older wing — and that our original finding was basically an artifact of how we coded the cases.

Here's the part that's bugging me. Our chair wants to formally adopt a standing rule for the committee, roughly: where this committee has established a finding to its satisfaction, later evidence to the contrary shall be presumed misleading. His reasoning is that if we got it right, then any study claiming we got it wrong has to be flawed, so chasing it is a waste of scarce resources. And there's a weird self-supporting feel to it that I can't shake — like, if you genuinely know something, you also get to know that anything pointing the other way is a mirage, which then licenses you to wave off the mirage. But that also sounds like the exact recipe for a body that can never be corrected, which, for a safety committee, seems bad.

I want you to take the role of a philosopher who works on epistemology and the theory of evidence but who also actually understands how committees like this operate. Please think it through carefully before writing anything — start by laying out, in your own scratch notes, the strongest version of the chair's position and the strongest version of the worry, and only then draft the memo. Along the way I'd like you to be precise about a couple of distinctions I keep fumbling: (a) between our knowing the original finding and our knowing that the new analysis is flawed, and whether the second really follows from the first; (b) between the new evidence being *defeated* by our existing grounds and our simply being *entitled to ignore* it; and (c) whether a rule like the chair's, if we adopt it, would end up licensing us to ignore evidence against the rule itself.

Then write the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's vote. It should (1) recommend a concrete course of action on the pending re-analysis — reopen, reject, or reopen with limits, pick one — with a timeframe; (2) say whether we should adopt the chair's proposed rule, and if not, what rule we should put in its place; and (3) give the best honest case for the chair's rule rather than a strawman, so the people who agree with him can actually respond to it. Keep it readable for a room full of clinicians — no jargon dumps — but don't water down the hard part. One typo is fine, I'm not submitting this to a journal.
