I retired last year after forty-odd years of running a wet lab (protein crystallography, if it matters), and since then I've been working my way through philosphy books in the evenings, mostly epistemology, and my wife says I've become insuferable. Here's what's got me stuck: the so-called dogmatism paradox, which I've seen credited to Kripke somewhere though don't quote me on that. The shape of it, as I understand it: if I know that P, then any evidence pointing to not-P must be misleading (otherwise I couldn't have known P in the first place), and if the evidence is misleading then I'm entitled to disregard it — so knowing something licenses me to stop listening. In the lab the whole point was that a result you couldn't be wrong about wasn't a result, so this grates, and yet every time I try to say exactly where the step goes wrong I end up sounding like my old department chair. No bibliography, please — I don't want a reading list, I want the jokes to be right.

So here's what I'm after: a mock advice column, an agony-aunt page called 'Dear Aunt Certitude', running in the in-house newsletter of some invented learned society (make it up, give it a name and a house style) whose members have all read this paradox and taken it to heart. Deadpan throughout, no winking, 600-700 words, three letters with three replies plus a sign-off. The joke has to be that she's a *conscientious* dogmatist: every reply should follow the argument properly, and the humour should come from where the argument actually pinches rather than from her being a crank. So I'd like the three letters to catch it at three different joints — the move from 'I know P' to 'the counterevidence is misleading'; the move from 'it's misleading' to 'I may disregard it'; and the first-person problem, i.e. can I even tell whether I'm in the knowing case, which I suspect quietly wrecks the entire column, since only someone who *doesn't* know would ever write in. Also include a dissenting guest reply from a fictional colleague (invent one — keep real names out of it) who thinks the whole thing confuses knowing with being unable to be wrong, and let Certitude answer him in character. She must not crack. If the piece is funny, it should be funny the way a well-run reductio is funny. Finish with a last line beginning 'Verdict:' naming the one premise you think the column can't survive without.
