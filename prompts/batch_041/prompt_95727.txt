I'm writing a seminar paper on the unity of the proposition, and I've been stuck for a week on an argument a friend of mine reconstructed for me. He works on knowledge graphs, and he uses it to claim something fairly dramatic: that no database, no diagram, and no sentence ever succeeds in recording or stating a relational fact — that what any of these actually contains is, strictly speaking, a list of items, never the fact that the items are related. He says the argument is an old objection to relations, but I had never seen it laid out in this form, and I cannot find the step I ought to reject.

Here is the argument as he gives it. (1) Take the fact that Alice loves Bob; write it aRb. (2) The items a, R, and b, taken one at a time, are not the fact; the fact is one thing and the items are three, so something must hold them together — R has to do its relating. (3) For R to do its relating is for R to be related to a and b; otherwise we are left with a heap of items rather than the fact that Alice loves Bob. (4) But the relation R bears to a and b is itself one of the items in the complex, and it can no more hold the others together by itself than R could, so it too must be related to R, a, and b in order to do its work. (5) That new relation requires the same treatment, and we are off: at each stage the unity of the complex is postponed to a further complex that is just as disunified as the first. (6) No finite stage delivers the unity, and an infinite chain of complexes is not something a finite thought or a finite database can contain. (7) So there are no structured facts, no structured propositions, and no thoughts that something stands in a relation to something else — including the thought he has just expressed and the thought I am now having about it. (8) He adds, with some relish, that the same reasoning disposes of sets: {a, b} is one thing made of two, and nothing in a or in b accounts for their being together in one set.

I can see Alice and I can see that she loves Bob, so I know (7) has to give, but I cannot locate the move that should be rejected. My instinct is to blame the infinity in (6), and I don't trust that instinct. A classmate offered the obvious reply — that R simply relates a and b and nothing further is needed — and he answered that this is only to assert that the unity of the complex is a brute fact, which he says is no answer at all; I can't tell whether that rebuttal is fair. What I want is a diagnosis: which step or steps to reject, what that step was quietly assuming, why it looked like a harmless unpacking, and what each available repair costs. I would rather keep (2), and the fact that I have relational thoughts, than keep (7), so if a premise has to go, tell me where the price is lowest — and if you think the argument is valid and simply forces a choice among unattractive options, say which option you would take and what the metaphysics looks like afterwards.
