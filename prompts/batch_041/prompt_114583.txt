I'm a junior in high school, and I've been stuck in a three-week argument with two friends from our philosophy club that I can't get out of my head. It started when one of them claimed that you can never know anything unless you first prove that your way of finding things out is reliable — you can't trust your eyes, he said, until you've shown they aren't deceiving you, and since you'd have to use your eyes to run the test, the whole project collapses. My other friend said that's backwards: you simply start trusting your eyes, and then later, once you've got a pile of ordinary observations, you can look back over them and notice that your eyes were right again and again, and that track record is your evidence that they work. I said that last step was cheating, and then we spent an hour failing to explain why. He keeps calling it "bootstrapping," a word he says he got from a podcast, though neither of us could decide whether the label was meant as a criticism or just a description.

Since then I've been collecting cases in which someone checks a tool by using the tool itself, and I've noticed that they don't all feel alike. A friend of mine wanted to know whether her bathroom scale was accurate, so she weighed herself, then weighed herself holding a five-kilogram dumbbell, and the number went up by exactly five kilograms. She now trusts the scale's absolute readings and uses them to judge whether her diet is working. That seems far too easy to me, since she never compared the scale against anything outside itself, but I can't say what she did wrong, because the dumbbell test plainly did tell her something and I can't put my finger on what. By contrast, my cousin insists he confirmed that a chatbot is reliable by asking it questions and finding that its answers were confident, detailed, and mutually consistent, which strikes me as obviously worthless. I want to know whether the difference between these cases is real, and if it is, exactly where it lies.

What I actually want is an essay that answers the general question underneath all this: when, if ever, is it legitimate to treat a method's own outputs as evidence that the method is trustworthy, and then to rely on that method for things you had no independent way to check? Please argue both sides properly — give the strongest case for the view that this is fine, including the thought that any demand for independent proof of everything ends in a skepticism nobody can live with, and the strongest case against, including the thought that the same pattern would let a person "confirm" a broken clock, a horoscope, or their own wishful thinking. But don't stop at a survey. Reach a verdict, and if your verdict is that it is sometimes legitimate, state the criterion that separates the good cases from the bad and say where the eyes, the scale, and the chatbot each fall under it, and why. I'm more interested in your own reasoning than in a summary of what famous philosophers have said, so please build the argument rather than report it — and please don't close by announcing that both sides have a point, which is precisely the move my friends and I have been unable to get past.
