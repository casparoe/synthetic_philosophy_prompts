It's embarassing how reliably it happens, so I went looking for what people have said about it and fell into this old debate about weakness of will — the idea that it might actually be impossible, because if you really judged that A was better you'd just do A, versus the idea that it obviously happens all the time and needs explaining. And there's apparently this other strand where people discount future rewards in a funny way, flipping their preference between two options as the choice gets closer (someone called it hyperbolic discounting I think), which is weird to me because my discount factor is exponential by construction, so an agent that flips like that would be dynamically inconsistent in a way my framework doesn't really have room for. Anyway: restate the whole thing — the impossibility claim, the version where it happens and how — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in. Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. In my vocabulary the policy is derived from the value function — if the agent takes an action, that action was by construction the highest-value one available to it, modulo approximation error and exploration noise. So it looks like the impossibility claim is just true by definition and weakness of will is a bug or a noise term. Which makes me suspect that what the philosophers call akrasia is really an agent with two different reward functions — the one it endorses and the one it's actually optimizing — and that the real question is why we'd ever expect those to coincide. But I have a nagging feeling that's cheating, that there's something about judging something to be best and simply not being moved by it that my vocabulary can't express at all, and that the discounting stuff (which I can model, sort of) isn't the same phenomenon as the will stuff (which I don't think I can model).
