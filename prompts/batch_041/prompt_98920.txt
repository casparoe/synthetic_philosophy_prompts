I write about social science for a general audience and I'm trying to do a piece on the old puzzle of why anyone votes, and I keep hitting the same wall. One camp says the act is basicly irrational: the odds your single ballot decides anything are vanishingly small, so the expected payoff is less than the cost of the trip, full stop. The other camp says that's a non-answer, because people obviously do get something out of voting -- it's expressive, it's a way of standing with people, they'd feel rotten staying home -- so the utility calculus was just built wrong to begin with. Then everyone shakes hands and nothing moves, and it's been like that as long as I've been reading. What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. A friend who does decision theory thinks the real fault line isn't expressive vs instrumental at all -- it's that the "it's irrational" camp is doing causal decision theory (my vote only matters if it's pivotal) while the intuition that voting is sensible, expressive or not, is closer to an evidential or rule-based story (what my voting says about people like me, or what would happen if everyone in my position did it). Is she right that the whole thing is a decision-theory dispute wearing a sociology costume? Or is it really a formal question nobody's settled -- how you're supposed to compute pivotality, against everyone else's votes held fixed versus in some equilibrium where my turnout and theirs are pinned down together? Or is it just two groups answering seperate questions under one name, one about the single act and one about why aggregate turnout is so high? Give me your best diagnosis with reasons, and then, given that diagnosis, a straight answer about what would actually move the thing -- a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change of question -- or whether it's stuck for good. No reading list, I need the argument.
