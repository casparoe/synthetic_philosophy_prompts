Draft the joint statement that two camps in the statistical-reform debate could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I coordinate a methods reading group that has spent a term on the replication crisis, and the two most vocal members have been talking past each other. One is a committed frequentist who holds that the crisis is not a defect of null-hypothesis significance testing but of how researchers use, select, and report tests, and who treats long-run error rates as the proper currency of inference. The other is a committed Bayesian who holds that the crisis is at least partly a defect of the frequentist framework itself, on the grounds that a p-value is not the probability that the hypothesis is false and that the framework offers no principled way to fold prior knowledge into the analysis. Produce the document they should have written together rather than the argument they keep having.

Organize it as follows.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive propositions both parties would put their names to: about what a p-value is and is not, about what a Bayes factor is and is not, about what "replication" should mean operationally, and about the roles of selection, publication, and incentives in producing the crisis. Keep these non-vacuous — claims that no serious person disputes do not belong here.

2. Precise disagreements. State each remaining disagreement in wording that its own side would accept as a fair rendering of its view, so that neither side would feel the formulation already concedes the point to the other.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. For each crux, name the argument, case, or analysis both parties agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that evidence would show.

4. A shared test bed. Construct one concrete, fully specified hypothetical replication scenario — original study and replication, sample sizes, observed effect sizes, the resulting p-values, and the resulting Bayes factors under a named default prior — chosen so that the two frameworks visibly pull apart. Then state what each party concludes about whether the original finding replicated, and where exactly their conclusions diverge.

5. A proposed reform. Evaluate the following concrete package as a whole: every empirical paper must report (a) a point estimate with a confidence interval and a p-value, (b) a Bayes factor computed under a prior specified by an independent party and registered before data collection, and (c) a design analysis stating the effect size the study was powered to detect. Say whether both parties would endorse the package, which of the three components each would keep, drop, or amend, and why.

6. Genuine or verbal. If any part of the dispute turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which part and why, and give the reformulation that dissolves it.

The statement should be a text both parties could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than smoothed over.
