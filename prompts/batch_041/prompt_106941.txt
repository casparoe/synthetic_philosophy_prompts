A research consortium has spent three years studying a sprawling, decentralized phenomenon that has no central controller — a continental power grid that keeps rebalancing itself, a slime mold that appears to solve a maze, the informal economy of a port city, or the like. Its members now disagree about whether the regularity they have documented is a feature of the world or an artifact of the questions they brought to it. Write a short story that stages this disagreement.

Let the three stances Daniel Dennett distinguished — treating the system as mere physics, as a designed thing with a function, as an agent with beliefs and aims — be embodied in three characters who must act on their commitments rather than merely argue them. Make the plot turn on a methodological dispute between two of them. One holds that the pattern is real precisely because it compresses: a short description of it predicts the data better than the data predicts itself. The other holds that a pattern is real only if it survives intervention — only if some change one can make to the system alters the pattern while leaving the rest of the structure intact. Let the turning point be a case in which the two criteria come apart, so that the reader sees what each criterion buys and what it costs, and so that the characters cannot simply keep both.

Give the abstract question a practical edge: the consortium's verdict determines whether the phenomenon is something to be modeled, something to be engineered, or something to be negotiated with and held responsible. Do not let any character deliver a lecture longer than a few sentences; the philosophy should emerge from what they notice, what they risk, and what they get wrong. Aim for roughly 1,200 words.
