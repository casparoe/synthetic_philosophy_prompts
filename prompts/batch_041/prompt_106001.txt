After a long career in experimental biology I have spent my retirement reading philosophy, and I have arrived at a settled-looking position on the moral status of sentient beings. Before I inflict it on a seminar, I want a consistency check. Here is where I have landed.

(1) Phenomenal consciousness — there being something it is like to be an organism — is a biological phenomenon that comes in degrees. I see no principled discontinuity in nature between having it and lacking it, any more than there is a first day of summer. Attributions like "sentient" are vague at the margins by nature, not merely because our instruments are crude.

(2) Moral standing — the weight an entity's interests carry when we deliberate — is a strictly increasing function of its degree of sentience. More sentience, more weight; no sentience, no weight. The relation is continuous and monotonic; nothing turns on where we happen to place a label.

(3) Being a member of the moral community — the kind of thing to whom something can be owed, rather than merely a thing we may choose to be kind to — is not a matter of degree. You are either in or out; there is no such thing as being 30% a bearer of claims.

(4) Insects and other invertebrates very probably have some sentience, and therefore some small but genuine moral weight. I would not swat a bee for amusement. I would not claim the same of a thermostat, or of a thermostat-like control system however elaborate.

(5) Since sentience is unobservable and our evidence is always partial, what we can actually act on is our credence. Policy should therefore weight an entity's interests by the probability we assign to its being sentient — an expected-value approach in which uncertainty is folded into the weight itself.

(6) Where law or institutional policy must draw a line — protected or unprotected, permitted or forbidden — that line should be a convention, chosen the way we choose an age of majority, for administrative workability rather than because the moral facts change at that point.

Please audit this set. I want to know which commitments pull against each other, how badly, and what the cheapest way out is — but I do not yet know where the trouble is, so do not assume I would sacrifice any particular item.

Structure the response under these headings, in this order:

- The position, restated: no more than about 100 words, in your own words, to confirm you have read it as I intend.
- Genuine tensions, in order of severity: this is the body of the response, roughly 400 to 600 words. For each, name the two or more commitments involved, say what each is committed to, and exhibit the specific case or inference where they deliver incompatible verdicts. Rank them; do not present them as equally bad.
- Tensions that only appear to be tensions: about 150 words. Say why each dissolves rather than merely softening.
- Cheapest repairs and their cost: roughly 200 words. For each genuine tension, the least revision that removes it, and precisely what that revision gives up.
- The single most economical revision: about 100 words. If one change would resolve the most, say which, and what it leaves standing.
- Bottom line: a few sentences stating which of my commitments I can keep together and which I cannot.
