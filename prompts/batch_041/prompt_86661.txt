I've been rereading Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" with a reading group, and I keep hitting a wall that the commentary I've seen — mostly blog posts and a couple of handbook chapters — seems to skate over. Strawson's framework turns on a distinction between excuses, which modify our reactive attitudes toward a particular act while leaving the agent a full member of the moral community, and exemptions, which suspend the reactive attitudes toward the agent wholesale by denying that they have the relevant capacities in the first place: the very young, the severely mentally ill, those whose conduct we attribute to something that temporarily displaced their ordinary self-control. The conceptual issue I want explained is whether that two-part division is really exhaustive, because it looks to me like there's a large and familiar class of cases that fits neither. These are cases where the agent plainly has all the capacities the exemptions target, where we don't excuse the particular act as an accident or a slip, and yet where resentment still feels misplaced — not because of what the person is like now, but because of how they came to be that way.

The case that crystallizes it for me is someone raised inside a closed community that deliberately manufactured the cruelty they now display: they have ordinary self-control and ordinary moral awareness by any behavioral test, no excuse applies to the specific thing they did, and yet when I try to resent them the resentment keeps sliding off toward something else — a kind of sorrow or curiosity aimed at the causal history rather than at the person. My tentative diagnosis is that this is a third stance, which I've been calling, perhaps badly, the etiological attitude: not the participant attitude, and not Strawson's objective attitude either, since the objective attitude treats the person as something to be managed, like weather, whereas this one still treats them as an agent and a member while redirecting the response onto their history. What I want is an explanation of the underlying conceptual issue, not a summary of the essay. Is the excuse/exemption distinction as clean as it's usually presented? Does Strawson's account have the resources to absorb history-directed withholding as a special case of exemption, or does it need a genuinely third category? And if it needs one, does admitting it undercut his central claim that the reactive attitudes are natural and stand in no need of external rational justification — since a stance that responds to causal history sounds like exactly the kind of theoretical perspective the participant attitude was supposed to be independent of? I'd also like to know whether my proposed etiological attitude is a real, stable category or whether it collapses under pressure into either resentment-with-a-story-attached or plain objective management; if it collapses, tell me which way and why.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
