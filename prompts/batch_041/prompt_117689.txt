I am a high school student preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club on whether a moral saint—someone who always does the morally best thing and never lets personal projects override moral demands—has a desirable life. In our last meeting, several people claimed that such a life must be missing something important, but I want to test that claim by constructing a case rather than by trading intuitions. Specifically, I want a thought experiment that isolates a single variable and makes three theories of well-being deliver different verdicts. The first theory is maximizing impartial consequentialism, on which a life is desirable to the extent that it maximizes impartial good; by stipulation the saint's life is maximal, so this theory says it is the most desirable life. The second is an objective list theory, on which a life is desirable only if it contains certain objective goods, including non-moral projects, deep personal attachments, or intellectual pursuits that are not justified by their moral value; by stipulation the saint's life contains none of these, so this theory says it is not desirable. The third is a desire-satisfaction theory, on which a life is desirable to the extent that the person's informed desires are satisfied; I want the saint to wholeheartedly endorse her life and have no frustrated desires, so this theory also says her life is desirable. I want a case where the saint is not miserable, does not feel alienated, and does not secretly long for anything, so the disagreement between the objective list theory and the other two cannot be dismissed as the saint being deceived about her own good. The case must not rely on the saint having false beliefs about her life, and it must not rely on her being uniquely self-sacrificing in a way that makes her an outlier; the only difference from an ordinary good life should be the absence of non-moral projects. Please describe the case in no more than one paragraph, and make it realistic: no science-fictional devices, no brainwashing, and no hidden psychological suffering.

After you have described the case, do the following. State exactly what each of the three theories says about whether the saint's life is desirable, and why. Identify which single feature of the case is doing the work. Say whether removing that feature—for example, by giving the saint one non-moral project that she pursues alongside her moral commitments—collapses the disagreement. Explain how the consequentialist and the desire-satisfaction theorist would each respond to the objective list theorist's charge that they are ignoring a genuine component of well-being. Finally, tell me whether the case can be adapted so that all three theories agree, and what would have to change. I am not looking for citations or a reading list; I want the scenario and the reasoning to stand on their own.
