I teach philosophy, and for the past two years I have sat with a colleague on our university's committee for algorithmic decision tools. Her votes are now numerous enough that I believe her position can be read off her record. She voted to reject a campus risk-scoring instrument that was well calibrated within each racial group but whose false-positive rate for one group was twice its rate for the other; the instrument drew on a variable that is widely regarded as a proxy for race. She voted to approve a scholarship screen whose false-positive and false-negative rates were identical across groups, even though a given score meant different things in each group, and which used no protected attribute and no plausible proxy. She voted to reject an instrument that flagged the same share of applicants from each group but whose error rates diverged sharply, and which used a protected attribute outright. And she voted to approve an instrument that made no errors at all in a population whose groups had different base rates, and which used no protected attribute. Next week we take up a tutoring-fund allocator whose false-positive and false-negative rates differ across groups, where the gap is a by-product of the groups' differing base rates rather than of anything the instrument does with group membership, which uses no protected attribute and no plausible proxy, which is calibrated within each group, and which is more accurate than any alternative we have examined. What will she vote, and why? I am less interested in the vote itself than in the rule she has been running: which rules fit her record equally well, what each predicts here, which one you would bet on and on what grounds, and which further proposal would most cleanly separate the survivors.
