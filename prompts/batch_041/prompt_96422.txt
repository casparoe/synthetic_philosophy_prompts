For the better part of forty years I did experimental work in a wet lab, and I have spent the first years of my retirement reading the philosophy I never had time for. I mention this only because it explains the shape of my confusion, which is that of someone trained to believe that a result is the same result whatever notation you write it in. A colleague recently pressed on me Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, his 1997 book built around a great many English renderings of a single short Renaissance French poem by Clément Marot (the title is itself a pun, I gather, on the tomb of Marot). What surprised me was not the ingenuity of the individual versions but the assumption behind the whole enterprise: that the poem is something a translator can keep or lose depending on whether the rhymes, the line lengths, and the refrain have been kept. In my own field a law of nature is invariant under translation into any language or formalism whatsoever; if literary translation were like that, a plain prose crib would be the ideal translation and every departure from it a corruption. Hofstadter's book plainly assumes otherwise, and I would like to know precisely what has to be true for that assumption to amount to more than a sentimental preference.

Since then I have become a subscribing member of a small press that publishes bilingual poetry, and it is their editorial practice, not Hofstadter's book, that I actually want explained, because the practice is harder and more particular than the book. Their house rule is that no translated poem is printed unless it reproduces the original's stanza and line structure and its rhyme scheme, and where the original carries a refrain word, an acrostic, or a fixed syllable count, those must be reproduced as well. When form and sense collide, the translator is instructed to sacrifice sense: the standing instruction is that form comes first and the sense follows as closely as it will. Yet the same editor routinely rejects versions that scan perfectly and mean nothing, insisting that a translation must read as a living English poem, one that a reader with no French would take for an original; he is, so far as I can tell, asking for two things that cannot both be maximized, and treating the trade-off as obvious rather than argued. He also pays his translators as authors, prints them on the title page beside the original poet, and preaches in his catalogues that translation is a creative art and not a service — while forbidding them to invent a single rhyme of their own. And the formal rules govern only the poetry list: the same editor translates his prose authors with no formal constraint whatever, and considers a prose translation excellent in proportion to how closely it tracks the original sentence for sentence.

What I want, then, is a reconstruction of the theory this practice presupposes. What view of meaning does the house rule require — is form a vehicle that carries sense, or part of the sense itself? What view of the identity of a literary work is implied by calling one of these versions a translation of Marot's poem rather than a new poem on the same theme, and does that view survive the admission that the translator must alter the sense? What theory of literary value makes the loss of a rhyme scheme a greater loss than the loss of a proposition, and is that value experiential, cognitive, or purely formal? What conception of fidelity can make sense of a rule that ranks some dimensions of the original above others without ever stating the ranking? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is underdetermined — I suspect a content-first theory of translation, and also a theory on which the translation is simply a new poem, could each license most of what this press does, and I want to know whether that suspicion is right. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change on the editor's side — in his list, his contracts, his rejection letters — if he abandoned the presupposed view for the content-first one. I am not after a reading list or citations; the local library's philosophy shelves are thin, and what I need is the argument.
