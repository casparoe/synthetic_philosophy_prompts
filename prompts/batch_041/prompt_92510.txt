I'm a third-year student in a seminar on cooperative game theory and bargaining, and our final assignment asks us to stage an adversarial collaboration rather than a debate. The two parties are a committed defender of the Nash bargaining solution and a committed defender of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, each of whom treats their own solution as capturing what a fair or rational bargain actually comes to, and not merely as one index among many. Write the joint statement the two of them could sign at the end of a good-faith collaboration.

Open with a numbered section of claims both accept. Cover the primitives of the bargaining problem — the feasible set, the disagreement point, and what it is for something to be a solution at all; which of the standard requirements (Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, independence of irrelevant alternatives, individual monotonicity, and whatever else either side regards as a genuine axiom) each solution provably satisfies; and the meta-claims on which they agree, such as that the two solutions coincide on symmetric problems and that they must therefore diverge somewhere, so that the dispute is real rather than a confusion about the setup.

Then a disagreements table. For each remaining dispute, state the position in wording its own side would accept, and give the closest thing to a formulation both would sign. I expect at least these to survive: whether independence of irrelevant alternatives is a genuine requirement of rationality or an artifact of a particular construction; whether individual monotonicity is a requirement or a mistake; and what the disagreement point is doing — whether it fixes the baseline of fairness or merely parameterizes the problem.

Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, describe a concrete bargaining problem or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case. Please make at least one crux turn on a case I suspect neither side has fully worked through: a two-party problem whose feasible set is non-convex, so that the Kalai–Smorodinsky construction by way of the ideal point needs care and the Nash product may fail to pick out anything Pareto-efficient; and a variant in which the disagreement point is itself uncertain or endogenous, so that it cannot simply be read off the problem. Finally, say plainly whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal, and which part.
