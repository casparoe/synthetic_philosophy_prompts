I'm a science journalist who's spent alot of the last few years writing about the free will wars, and next month I'm giving a talk at a law school's criminal justice center. The audience is mostly sentencing law professors and a handful of sitting judges, plus two philosophers who work on punishment. These are people who deal with the federal sentencing guidelines and state determinate-sentencing schemes everyday; they care about what actually reduces recidivism and what a judge can defensibly say from the bench. A few are old-school just-deserts retributivists, a couple are empirical criminologists who think desert talk is just window dressing for incapacitation, and one or two are abolitionists who think the whole system should be torn down.

My thesis is this: the Dennett-Caruso fight over just deserts is really a proxy war over who gets to use the word "deserve" in public policy, and neither side is being honest about it. Caruso's quarantine model talks about "dangerousness" instead of desert, but dangerousness is just as morally arbitrary a basis for locking someone up — it's a prediction about future behavior that the person has no more control over than they do over their past behavior. Dennett wants to keep desert because he thinks it's the only thing that respects people as agents, but then he ends up sounding like a retributivist whenever he talks about punishment, which is exactly what he says he's not. And I want to test both views against a concrete case: two defendants, same crime, same culpability, but one victim lived and one died, so under most guidelines they get very different sentences. If free will is an illusion, as Caruso says, then outcome luck is just as arbitrary as the rest of it, and his quarantine model should give them the same sentence unless their future dangerousness differs. But if Dennett is right that we need desert to make sense of responsibility, then he has to explain why the dead victim makes the surviving defendant more deserving of punishment, which sounds like moral luck, not desert.

So: prep me for the Q&A. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience — not the ones I'd get from a philosophy seminar, but the ones from sentencing professors and judges who think philosophers are wasting their time. Phrase them the way they'd phrase them. Order them by how much damage they'd do to my thesis. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
