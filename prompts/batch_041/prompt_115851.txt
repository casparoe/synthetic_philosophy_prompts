I'm assembling materials for a small seminar on philosophy of psychiatry — the room will be a mix of psychiatry residents and a couple of philosophers — and I've been handed the session on Hacking's looping effects. The question I want the session organized around is not whether looping effects are real, but what they should do to our attitude toward particular diagnostic categories: depression, PTSD, anorexia, dissociative identity disorder, and so on. What I want from you is a classification instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree whose internal nodes are questions that genuinely separate positions on this, and whose leaves are the positions themselves. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a roster of famous names — first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the positions located in that space. Every node should be phrased so that a resident could answer it without already knowing which position it leads to; no node should be of the form "are you a realist or a constructionist?" I'd like roughly four to six nodes and eight to twelve leaves.

For the angle: I care specifically about distinctions that a standard overview of the debate tends to flatten. Three in particular. First, the mechanism of the loop — whether the category reshapes people through their self-conception (someone learns the label and reinterprets their life through it), through clinicians' expectations and diagnostic habits, or through institutional structures like manuals, insurance codes, and research funding; positions come apart on which of these they think does the real work, and on whether the loop can run at all when the person never encounters the label. Second, whether the instability a loop produces is an epistemic problem (we can't get a stable estimate of prevalence or treatment response), a metaphysical one (there is no fact of the matter about who has the condition, independent of the classification), or not a problem at all but rather the mark of a category that is functioning correctly. Third, what the recommended response is — revise the category, replace it with dimensions or a symptom network, keep it but change what we ask of it, or treat the loop itself as the object of study, so that a moving target is exactly what a legitimate research programme should be tracking. Please make sure the following appear as leaves, whether or not they end up with names attached: realism that treats at least one of these categories as tracking a natural kind; the analysis of disorder as an internal dysfunction plus harm; dynamic nominalism; replacement of categories by dimensional or network models; straightforward clinical pragmatism that treats validity as irrelevant so long as the category is useful; and a pluralist view on which a category is valid relative to a purpose. A leaf may be marked unnamed if nobody defends it.

Two further things. Where the tree leaves a combination of answers unoccupied, say so and diagnose it — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's coherent but unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered? And where two leaves come out the same way on every node, flag them as a pair the tree cannot separate rather than inventing a node to prise them apart. I'd rather have an honest instrument with a couple of acknowledged blind spots than a tidy one that pretends to sort everything.

Please structure the response under these headings, in this order: The branch questions (each node as a question, a sentence or two of explanation, and a note on which answers it separates, roughly 400-600 words); The tree (an indented outline from the top node down to the leaves, each leaf followed by a one-line gloss, roughly 300-500 words); Empty and crowded branches (a short paragraph for each unoccupied combination, and a note on any leaf that several distinct answer-paths lead to); Leaves this tree cannot separate (a list, with one line on why each pair resists separation); and Where this instrument is likely to misfire (a candid closing paragraph, 150-250 words). Finally, please don't be diplomatic about the framing: if the question I've posed doesn't actually carve the debate at a joint, or if the positions I've asked for don't belong on one tree, say so plainly and tell me what I should have asked instead — I'd rather find that out now than in front of the residents.
