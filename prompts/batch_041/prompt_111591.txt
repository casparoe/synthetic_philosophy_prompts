I was at a reading group on thermodynamics and information last week — we've been going through the Maxwell's demon material, Szilard's one-molecule engine, the kT ln 2 erasure cost, the usual moves — and afterwards a friend of mine who does condensed matter went on a bit of a rant about it. His claim is that the whole information-theoretic framing is a red herring. The demon, he says, is just a ratchet and pawl: a wheel that only turns one way, sitting in a bath of randomly jiggling molecules, and it ought to rectify all that jiggling into steady rotation. It doesn't, and the reason is that the pawl is jiggling too — the same thermal noise that's supposed to drive the wheel also lifts the pawl out of the way, so on average nothing ever gets rectified. He says you dont need memory, measurement, erasure, knowledge, Landauer, any of it; the demon is a passive mechanical device and it fails for boring mechanical reasons, and everyone who dresses it up in information theory is mystifying a solved problem. (He waved off the fact that if you make the pawl colder than the wheel the thing does turn — that's just a heat engine, he says — and he insists erasure can't be the real issue anyway, because the demon could simply keep its record forever and never overwrite anything.) I want to know how far the ratchet analogy actually goes, since hes using it to conclude that there's nothing special about information in physics at all.

So: give me the correspondence first, what maps onto what — the wheel, the pawl, the spring, the jiggle, the bath, the demon's decision, whatever else is in play — then the places where the mapping breaks, and I want the breaks sorted by whether they actually matter for his conclusion or are just idle differences. The thing I really want to know is whether the demon's failure and the ratchet's failure are the same kind of failure or two different ones, whether there's anything the demon does that has no counterpart in the ratchet (and the reverse), and whether the ratchet picture quietly assumes something about the demon that assumes away the interesting part. Then the smuggling report: what features of ratchets does the analogy import into demons that demons needn't have, and what does it make you stop noticing? Finally the verdict — does his conclusion survive, entirely or only in part, and which part? If a better analogy would do the job he wants, give it and say why it's better; if you think no analogy can carry that conclusion, say so and say why not.

Lay it out with headers, in this order: The mapping (a compact table, plus a sentence on anything that doesn't fit the table). Where it breaks (each break tagged "matters for his conclusion" or "doesn't"). What the ratchet picture smuggles in (short paragraph). Verdict (a few sentences). Better analogy, if any (short, or a single sentence saying there isn't one).
