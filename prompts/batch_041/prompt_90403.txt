I am a novelist, three years into a book set in Cambridge in the late 1920s and 1930s, and the spine of it is an argument between two characters I have come to like too much to let either of them be the fool. One is a young economist in Keynes's orbit; the other is an actuary's daughter who has drifted into statistical work. They have been quarrelling for a decade about Keynes's theory of probability, and I have written and cut their scene four times because I cannot tell what kind of disagreement they are actually having — which is why I am putting it to you instead of settling it in the manuscript. Put as concretely as I can: in the Treatise on Probability of 1921, probability is a logical relation between evidence and hypothesis, a matter of how far the evidence rationally supports the conclusion, but a degree that need not be numerical and need not even be comparable with other degrees — there are cases, Keynes's rain-and-umbrella case, where it is neither more likely than not, nor less, nor as likely as not, and where it is rational to let caprice decide. Alongside the degree sits a second, independent dimension, the weight of argument: how much relevant evidence there is behind the judgement, which can grow while the probability falls. Then in the General Theory of 1936 and the writing that followed it, Keynes speaks of an uncertainty for which there is no basis for any calculable probability at all, and where the point is that people nevertheless have to act. My continuist character says nothing has changed: the later talk of uncertainty is simply the far end of the Treatise's scale — non-numerical, non-comparable, low-weight probabilities — and the logical-relationist framework holds throughout. My discontinuist says the reverse: the later Keynes has quietly abandoned the Treatise, because the uncertainty that matters to him is not a degree of any relation between evidence and hypothesis but the absence of one, and the weight of argument, which remains a quantity of evidence, cannot do the work the later position needs it to do. Neither has moved in ten years, and neither is being stupid.

So take the part of someone who has both the formal theory of probability and the history of early twentieth-century economics at their fingertips, and give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Begin by steelmanning both positions in a few lines, so that I can see you have understood what each is actually claiming. Then tell me what kind of disagreement this is. Is it verbal — the two camps extending the word probability differently, one counting as probabilistic cases that the other simply refuses to call by that name — or is it substantive, a real difference over whether there is one scale of partial implication or two quite different doxastic situations? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work, a question about what Keynes actually held when, which argument cannot settle and only the archive can? Are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one asking what a formal calculus of partial entailment can represent, the other asking what a theory of rational action owes to an agent who must act now on the evidence he has? Or does the dispute rest on a disagreement about the standard of success for a theory of rationality, whether a theory must issue in an answer or may honestly return no verdict, or on starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? My own hunch, offered as a hunch, is that the verbal reading is about a third of the truth and that the real load is carried by that last question about what the theory is for. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction that dissolves the appearance of conflict, a formal result about whether the Treatise's apparatus can or cannot represent the cases the General Theory cares about, a decision to stop asking one of the two questions — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. Reason it through carefully before you commit; I want the diagnosis stated in a single sentence I can put in a character's mouth, and then defended. Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I need the structure of the disagreement, not the literature.
