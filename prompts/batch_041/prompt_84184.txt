EXAM QUESTION (30 points)

The tradition stemming from Wittgenstein's later remarks on certainty is often charged with licensing dogmatism. Put the objection as follows.

(i) Hinge commitments are, by hypothesis, exempt from evidential assessment.
(ii) Whatever is exempt from evidential assessment cannot be criticised as unreasonable.
(iii) Therefore a community that treats a false proposition as a hinge — for instance, that no human artefact can survive immersion in water, or, at the level of a whole practice, that the pronouncements of oracles are authoritative — is not open to epistemic criticism; and the view cannot explain how a community is ever rationally entitled to abandon such a commitment.

For each of the three readings of hinge certainty set out in the seminar handout, do all of the following: (1) identify which premise of the objection, if any, the reading rejects; (2) state whether, on that reading, a community's abandonment of a hinge can be assessed as reasonable, and by what standard; and (3) rank the three readings by how much of the objection each leaves standing, defending the ranking.

Handout stipulations (authoritative for this exam; not to be replaced by your own preferred reading of the literature):

(A) Non-doxastic reading. Hinge certainty is not a propositional attitude at all; it is manifested in action and in the unhesitating character of practice. Nothing is believed, so nothing is held without justification, and premise (i) misdescribes the target. Hinge change is a conversion: a global shift in what one does.

(B) Entitlement reading. Hinges are propositions. Our adherence to them is warranted by a non-evidential, defaulting entitlement: we are entitled to trust them because trusting them is a precondition of the cognitive project, and the entitlement lapses if that presupposition fails, that is, if the proposition ceases to play that role. Entitlement is not justification: it is not conferred by evidence and is not defeated by evidence against the hinge.

(C) Constitutive reading. Hinge propositions are propositions, but their acceptance is constitutive of the framework within which reasons can be given and assessed: they are not justified and not in need of justification, and the demand that they be justified is itself illegitimate. A framework is given up as a whole when it is found unworkable; the shift is not a justified move within the old framework, though it can be assessed retrospectively in terms of the workability of what replaces it.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

The objection amounts to the claim that hinge epistemology cannot have it both ways: it wants hinge commitments immune to evidential assessment, and it also wants to criticise people who get things badly wrong. Premise (ii) is true — what cannot be assessed by evidence cannot be assessed as unreasonable — so the three readings have to respond in different places.

(A) The non-doxastic reading rejects premise (i) outright. Since hinge certainty is not a belief but a pattern of acting, there is nothing there to be unjustified, and the objection misfires: it convicts the reading of a fault, holding a commitment without evidence, that the reading does not commit. This is the cleanest escape, but it has a cost. It must treat the abandonment of a hinge as a conversion, a change in what one does rather than a conclusion one draws, and a conversion is not the sort of thing that can be reasonable or unreasonable. So on (A) the change is irrational in the strict sense: it is not the conclusion of any reasoning at all.

(B) The entitlement reading keeps hinges as propositions but gives them a non-evidential warrant. Since the warrant is not grounded in evidence, it cannot be defeated by evidence, and a hinge commitment, once in place, is indefeasible; that is why this reading must also hold that the demand for a justification of hinges is illegitimate, since it is a demand we could never meet. A community that holds a false hinge is therefore not criticisable on this reading, and its eventual abandonment of the hinge cannot be assessed as reasonable. (B) leaves much of the objection standing: it concedes (i) and (ii), and the reply it offers, that the demand is illegitimate, does not help, because the objection is not asking for a justification of the hinge, only for the possibility of criticism.

(C) The constitutive reading is the most promising. On this reading the hinge propositions are propositions whose role is to constitute the framework within which reasons can be given and assessed, and the entitlement to them is defaulting, so a community is entitled to trust them unless and until the presupposition on which the entitlement rests fails. Because entitlement is not justification, a community is not required to answer for its hinges; because the entitlement can lapse, the reading can explain hinge change without pretending that the change is justified within the old framework. The shift can be assessed retrospectively, in terms of whether the new framework is workable. The residual worry is that the reading licenses any hinge at all, provided the community is internally consistent, but this is a cost worth paying, and it is lower than the cost incurred by (A).

Ranking, by how much of the objection each leaves standing: (C) least, (B) next, (A) most. On (A) the objection misfires at the level of premise (i), but the reading still cannot deliver what the objection's proponent actually wants, namely an account of when the abandonment of a hinge is reasonable, so it fares worst overall.
