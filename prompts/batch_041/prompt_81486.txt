I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term, and our final unit is on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view that there are no phenomenal properties, and that introspection misrepresents us as having them. For my term paper I want to read Blade Runner (1982) through that lens instead of the usual Turing-test angle. Two things make me think the film is worth trying. First, every piece of evidence it offers for a character's inner life is public and third-personal — the Voight-Kampff empathy test, Rachael's implanted memories, the photographs the replicants hoard as proof of a real past — so that the recurring question of whether Deckard is himself a replicant starts to look oddly empty: he has all the reports, reactions, and memories we would ordinarily treat as the whole of the matter. Second, those implanted memories strike me as a fictional model of the illusionist's central explanatory burden, namely how a being could come to sincerely believe it has a rich felt inner life that it does not have. I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the position seriously, says where the film resists the illusionist reading (Roy Batty's death, and his grief over memories that will vanish with him, seem to require that there be something it is like to lose them), and closes by saying what the film contributes to the debate that the philosophical arguments do not. Please write in American English; no citations needed, since I have the readings.
