Ok so before I read any more of this literature I want the spec written down, not another theory. What does a theory of the unity of consciousness actually have to deliver to count as any good? Give me the requirements list -- for each one, the case or consideration that forces it on us, so I can tell which requirements are real and which are just house rules that some framework happens to need.

Purpose, so you know how strict to be: I want a theory I can actually use to answer, for a given patient, whether there's one subject of experience there or two (or one and a half), and that doesn't cheat on the personal-identity question by declaring the answer obvious.

Cases I already know belong on the list, and add the ones I've missed:

- the standard comissurotomy patient: one hand doing something, the other hand undoing it, or the patient saying one thing while a hand does another
- the fact that a fair amount still gets across in those patients, and that a lot of what looks like getting across is the patient reading cues off their own body
- cases where someone acts on what they can't report seeing -- the blindsight-ish stuff, and the grasping cases where the hand gets the size right and the report gets it wrong
- the confabulation thing, where a hemisphere fluently and sincerely narrates a reason for behaviour it had no part in initiating
- coming-to on the motorway, having driven for miles with no memory of it
- dreamless sleep and anaesthesia, plus the fact that we don't say the person went out of existence and came back
- the odd ones: the hand that feels foreign, the voice that feels inserted, people who report their thoughts aren't theirs, people who report that they themselves are gone while experience carries on
- and the mundane one: normal people under load, divided attention, dual tasks -- afterwards you'd swear there was one stream, but the performance data says two

Now the case I actually care about, and I want the list built to handle it. Take a patient whose hemispheres are connected only by a deliberately thin channel -- low bandwidth, enough to pass a single yes/no signal or a coarse emotional tone, nothing like a percept or a proposition. One person or two? A person and a half? If you say the amount of unity just is the bandwidth, then ordinary unity is a matter of degree and the sharp line everyone wants between one mind and two is gone. If you say there's a threshold, above it one subject, below it two, then tell me what sets the threshold and why it isn't arbitrary. I suspect this case does more damage to the popular answers than anything else on the list, so don't leave it out.

Distinctions the list had better not collapse, at minimum: unity of experience at a time vs unity of the subject across time; unity as a feature of experience vs unity as a feature of the person or agent; having one stream vs having one self; and being unified vs being able to report that it's unified -- anyone who defines unity as reportability owes me an answer about the patient who reports unity and behaves as two.

Then mark which of these you think are non-negotiable and which are just prejudices of one camp (is 'nothing over and above the parts' a genuine requirement, or is it something a certain metaphysics needs?), and flag any pair you think can't be satisfied together. That's the part I most want. Don't score the existing theories against the list, I'll do that myself once the list is right.

If it has to be shorter than I want, cut the ones that turn out to be consequences of other entries, not the ones that are just hard.
