Longtime lurker, first time actually asking anything. Here's the wall I keep hitting. Every formal model of belief I've poked at — epistemic/doxastic logic, Bayesian agents, whatever flavor — hands you an agent who knows every logical consequence of what they know, and that's just false of me. I've believed the axioms of arithmetic since I was a kid and there are theorems of arithmetic I still dont know, and not in the hasn't-had-time-to-compute sense: I've never once thought about most of them. And I think something real is being papered over there. When I believe P and I believe P implies Q and I haven't put the two together, there's a defect in my epistemic state that has nothing to do with the speed of my mental arithmetic. But every fix I've seen or tried either (a) keeps the omniscience in a nicer costume, (b) throws out closure entirely, after which belief stops looking like a cognitive kind at all — if nothing ever pressures me to accept the consequences of what I believe, why is believing one thing rather than another anything but a mood? — or (c) explodes the second I admit people hold inconsistent beliefs, since closure under entailment plus one contradiction gets you everything.

So before I read one more theory I want the spec. What does any account of what an actually bounded agent believes have to deliver before I should take it seriously? The cases I keep coming back to, in case they're useful as test cases: me and the unthought-of theorem; the author who believes every sentence in their book and also, reasonably, that at least one of them is wrong; the person holding a thousand lottery tickets each of which they're certain will lose; someone who says it's raining and in the next breath says they don't believe it's raining; the surprise-exam setup, where the students reason their way to a conclusion that can't be right; a friend who builds proof assistants and insists the thing he actually needs is a model where what the machine knows and what the machine can search are two different objects; and the plain fact that reasoning feels like an activity, takes time, and sometimes comes with the sensation of discovery — which on the ideal picture is incoherent, since deduction never adds information.

For each requirement give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you think it's non-negotiable or just a prejudice of one camp. Then the part I most want: flag which pairs of requirements you think can't be satisfied together, because my suspicion is that the list itself is where the real result lives. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate theories, if I had to give one up to save the rest. A table is fine for the requirements themselves, prose for everything else. A short line or two on how the main families of approaches do against the list is welcome, but keep it short — the list is the point, and please skip any reading list, I'll do the reading myself. If you think the whole framing is off — that belief is being treated as a state when it's really a process, or that logical consequence is simply the wrong relation to close under — say so up front and then do the list anyway; I'd rather have the list than a lecture. American English throughout, please.
