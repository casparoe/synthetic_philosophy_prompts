I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough to have watched the James-versus-Clifford standoff get flattened into a meme — James the soft-hearted pragmatist who wants permission to believe what comforts him, Clifford the stern evidentialist who thinks unearned belief is a kind of lie. I want a dialogue that gets underneath that cartoon, for a reading group of people who already know the standard moves and will be annoyed by a recitation of them.

Write a dialogue between two proponents of these opposing views. Make both of them genuinely sharp, and set them up so that neither can be dismissed: the evidentialist should be as sophisticated as any working Bayesian, and the pragmatist should be able to handle the machinery rather than flee into sentiment. Neither may be a strawman, and the dialogue must be real back-and-forth — each speaker answering the other's actual last point, conceding where the point lands, pressing where it doesn't, rather than each delivering a monologue past the other.

Avoid the canned illustrations (no shipowner, no religious belief, no wager-style cases). Use secular, contemporary material instead. Build the exchange around the following pressure points, and make sure each one is developed rather than name-checked:

- The voluntarism problem. Can anyone believe at will? If not, what exactly is the will doing — choosing what to read, whom to spend time with, which habits of attention to cultivate, what to precommit to? Is the will to believe a category error, or a loose name for the will to cultivate? Have them fight over whether this dissolves the disagreement or relocates it.
- The belief/acceptance distinction. One might concede that belief is not directly voluntary but insist that adopting a proposition as a working premise is. Press both speakers on whether that is what is really at stake, and on whether treating something as a premise while privately assigning it low probability is stable or self-deceiving.
- Self-fulfilling credence. Choose one concrete case in which the evidence is not fixed in advance because the agent's confidence partly determines the outcome — a founder deciding whether to believe the company will survive, someone deciding whether to trust a new partner, a person deciding whether to believe a difficult project is achievable. The evidentialist must then say what sufficient evidence even means when the evidence depends on the credence. Do not let this resolve too quickly.
- The reflexive turn. Is the evidentialist's own rule justified by its consequences — does it pay rent in anticipated experience? If so, is the evidentialist a pragmatist about norms while remaining an evidentialist about beliefs? Let the pragmatist land this, then give the evidentialist a reply that is not a dodge.
- Sincerity. Have one speaker raise the objection that a belief knowingly adopted for its payoff cannot be sincerely held, so the strategy defeats itself; the other must answer without cheating.
- Substance or verbal dispute. Somewhere near the end, have them ask whether they mean the same things by belief, evidence, and sufficient — and decide, on the page, whether their disagreement is real or terminological.

Let each speaker win at least one exchange and lose at least one. Include one moment where a speaker concedes a point and immediately overreaches from it, and one where a speaker catches the other sliding from something is permitted to something is required. End with the residual disagreement precisely located rather than dissolved — no handshake, no both sides have a point.

Both speakers should argue from first principles. Neither should cite literature or invoke named authorities, and no external sources should be needed to follow the exchange. Aim for roughly 1200 to 1800 words, dialogue with at most minimal stage directions.
