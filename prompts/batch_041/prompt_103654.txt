I am a novelist at work on a book set in a settlement that governs itself by a single written charter, and I have run into a structural difficulty I cannot name. The charter contains an amendment clause; the amendment clause can itself be amended; and my characters keep discovering that this produces a particular kind of trouble. In one chapter, a two-thirds majority amends the amendment clause to require unanimity, and I cannot decide whether the amendment is valid, since it was passed under a rule it has just abolished. In another, the assembly uses its power to change the assembly's own rules to change who counts as a member of the assembly, so that the body which acted is no longer the body that must live with the result. In a third, a provision is added declaring all earlier amendments valid, including some that were arguably invalid when made. I know this is in the neighborhood of the game in which players may change the rules as they play, and of the legal theorist's claim that a system's ultimate authority cannot itself be authorized from within the system, but neither of those is quite the thing I am circling. Is there an established name for this specific structure — a rule that reaches back to authorize or dissolve the very authority by which it was made? If so, I want to know how it differs from the two neighbors I have named and where its edges fall; if not, I want you to name and define it, with the boundary cases that would tell me whether my charter contains one instance of it or three.
