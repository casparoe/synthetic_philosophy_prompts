I am the research-and-evaluation lead at a mid-sized state education agency, and roughly once a week something lands on my desk that turns on a heritability figure. A district wants to introduce between-class ability grouping and argues that reading difficulty is mostly heritable, so the classroom can only do so much; a legislator's aide forwards an op-ed claiming that educational attainment is substantially heritable and that this should temper our expectations for early-childhood programs; a vendor argues the opposite way, that because some behavioral trait is only partly heritable, its product must be the missing environmental lever; a superintendent preparing testimony wants to know whether she can safely tell a committee that genes matter but are not destiny. I have to give the director of research a recommendation inside a day, my own training is in economics and program evaluation rather than genetics, and the people I am advising have less statistical background than I do. My staff and I have been making one distinction by hand and I would like it made rigorous: we have taken to calling a heritability claim "portable" or "non-portable" according to whether the number would still describe the population we are being asked to act on once that population's environment changed in the way the proposal would change it — and we have found that we cannot say consistently when a claim is portable, or what to do when it is not.

Please write the procedure we should run instead. I want a sequence of questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than of statistics. It has to work when the claimant has no numbers at all, which will be most of the time, and it has to be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of arithmetic. The conceptual content should be whatever the science actually licenses about heritability — what the statistic is and is not a claim about, why the same trait can carry different heritabilities in different populations and at different moments, and how a trait's heritability relates to the heritability of a response to an intervention, if at all. It must not depend on any particular study, author, or reading, since we will not be able to look anything up while using it, and it should fit on a page for staff with a social-science master's degree.

Then run it on three cases: the ability-grouping proposal; the parent-training nonprofit that wants our endorsement on the strength of a moderate heritability estimate; and a case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — some claim or program we have been inclined to support, which the procedure says we are supporting for bad reasons. Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do: the questions it will leave unsettled, the claims it will wave through that a geneticist would want to stop, and the ones it will stop that are in fact harmless.
