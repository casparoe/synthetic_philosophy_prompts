I'm writing on the White Horse Dialogue — the Warring States exchange, attributed to Gongsun Long and grouped with the School of Names, in which one speaker defends the claim that a white horse is not a horse while another resists it. Two stretches of the exchange need to be kept apart. In the first, the defender holds that 'horse' is what designates shape and 'white' is what designates color, and that what designates color is not what designates shape. In the second, after the objector presses that if there are white horses there are horses — so that having a white horse is having a horse — the defender answers that if one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may be delivered, while if one seeks a white horse they may not; that were a white horse a horse, what is sought in the two cases would be the same; that it manifestly is not the same; and so that a white horse is not a horse.

I want an argument, not a survey. Defend the position that this second, seeking stretch is the actual argument of the dialogue and the first is only its staging; that the argument is a good one once its subject is correctly identified; and that its subject is a distinction usually blurred in discussion of the text — between what makes a name true of a thing and what a request made with that name calls for. Argue that the objector's insistence that there are white horses and therefore horses answers a different question from the one the defender is answering, and that the conclusion concerns the second notion rather than the first. You are arguing against the reading on which the dialogue trades on an ambiguity in 'is not' (between non-identity and non-membership); say precisely where you think that reading goes wrong, and do not simply concede that the dialogue is a piece of sophistry.

Then do three further things. First, meet what I take to be the strongest objection: that seeking is an opaque context, so the failure of 'a white horse' and 'a horse' to substitute for one another shows only that the two expressions differ in sense or in function, not that what they name differs, and that the defender therefore infers a difference in what is named from something that cannot support it. Say whether you accept the opacity point; if you do, explain how the argument survives it; if you do not, say what notion of seeking the argument requires. Second, test your reading against a parallel argument you construct yourself, of the same form but in which the modifier is not a color term and, if you can manage it, the head term is not a biological kind — and say whether the argument still goes through, and what that shows about which reading is right. Third, state what would have to be true of the text for your reading to be mistaken, and identify the features of the text itself, as opposed to features of later commentary, that bear on the question.

Keep the whole thing self-contained: no citations, no reading list, no names of scholars, no appeals to what 'many interpreters' hold — I will supply the references myself. Argue in the first person where it helps.

Finally, mark clearly which parts of what you say are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who know the text. Do not present your own reading as the consensus; where you are defending a minority or novel line, say so explicitly, and where a question is simply open, say that too.
