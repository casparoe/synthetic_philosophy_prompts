ok, weird request. I'm 4th year of a phil PhD, decision theory / rational choice adjacent, and I'm running next week's session of the grad seminar on the dollar auction because our prof is away at a conference. I want to open with a poem — read it out, 90 seconds, no framing from me — and then start the theory. The reason is that everyone in that room has seen the sunk-cost-fallacy slide a dozen times and privately decided they're immune to it, and the dollar auction is the one case where the standard telling of it is actually a bit of a lie.

So the poem needs to be right about the game, in a way that makes the usual story look lazy. Rules, briefly, because I want them reconstructable from inside the poem: an auctioneer sells a one-dollar bill, bids climb in 5-cent steps, the winner pays their bid and gets the dollar, and — the whole thing — the second-highest bidder also pays what they bid and gets nothing. So the person who's loosing is the one paying for nothing, and their choice at every point is to quit and pay for nothing, or pay 5 cents more and be in with a real chance at the dollar. Note what that means: the money is gone either way, whether you quit or keep going, so the past isn't doing any real work in the next decision. That's why I don't want the poem to be about stubbornness, pride, or not wanting to lose, or about anyone being sentimental about sunk costs. Nobody in the poem should be irrational in the sense of ignoring the future — that's the lazy version. Two people each making the locally sensible move end up somewhere neither of them would have chosen, and by the end they're bidding three dollars for a one dollar bill and both of them can see it.

Form: a pantoum, 16 lines, four quatrains, the standard thing — line 2 and line 4 of each stanza come back as line 1 and line 3 of the next one, and the last line of the poem is the first line of the poem. I want that compulsary recycling to be the escalation: the same lines coming back around, each time from a worse position. At least one of the recycled lines should have to mean something different the second time it lands, ideally the last one, since it's also the opening. Rhyme if you can do it without it going greeting-card; the repetition matters more than the rhyme. One speaker — one of the two bidders, talking to the other one — no narrator stepping in to tell us what it all means, and don't use the words sunk cost, escalation, or commitment. I'll name it afterwards, that's my job. Keep the prize small and boring; the horror is that it's a dollar. And I know verbatim repetition fights with the bids visibly climbing — if those two pull against each other, let the numbers live in the lines that aren't recycled and tell me about it in the note.

After the poem, a short note, a paragraph or two at most: where the poem takes liberties with the game or leans on one contested reading of it, and which parts of the analysis here are actually settled among people who study this and which are genuinely up for grabs — what "rational" is even supposed to mean in this game, whether the escalation is a bias or the locally correct move, whether the mixed-strategy / war-of-attrition story I half remember is an explanation or just a redescription. Don't present a contested view as the consensus. I also have a vague sense that even people who've been warned about the trap still get caught — if you have any view on whether that's well established or just classroom folklore, say so and flag how confident you are. And no references, real or invented; I know you can't check, so don't.
