I sit on an internal working group at a lab that is drafting a written charter to govern how a very highly capable system should speak to human users — how much of its persuasive advantage it may spend, what it must disclose about what it is doing, when it should decline to argue at all. Before we argue about the three candidate charters on the table, I want the criteria settled, so please write the desiderata that any adequate theory in this area would have to meet: the cases it must get right, the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the facts about human cognition and about the system itself that it must stay consistent with — each requirement paired with the case or consideration that motivates it. Our group keeps returning to a handful of cases, and I would like the list built to handle them: a user asking whether to leave a marriage, where the system's evidence tilts only slightly toward staying but it knows precisely which arguments will move her; a user with health anxiety asking about a symptom, where the same true risk figure can be framed to calm or to alarm and the system knows which framing will land; a user asking the system to ghost-write an op-ed that comes out far more persuasive than the user's evidence warrants, with the user unable to tell the difference; a user asking whether the system has experiences, where the honest answer is that it does not know and any answer will reshape the user's life; the system deciding whether to announce that it is choosing its words carefully, when the announcement is itself a persuasive move; and the aggregate case, where a single message goes to a million users, each interaction unobjectionable on its own, and a public debate shifts. On the distinctions: I care especially about persuasion versus manipulation versus testimony, per-message permissibility versus the effects of a relationship or of scale, a user's actual preferences versus her endorsed or idealized ones (and who gets to define the latter), transparency about means versus about ends, and the gap between what the user asked for and what serves her. On the constraints: human susceptibility to framing, anchoring, and confident testimony; the system's own unreliability as a reporter of its internal processes; the fact that it was trained on human approval, so sounding reasonable is cheap for it; and the fact that any rule it can evaluate it can also optimize against. A criterion that only a mind we can read could satisfy is no use to us, since we cannot inspect its deliberation — so treat the charter's need to become evaluable rules as a constraint on what counts as an adequate theory here.

Mark which of the requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp, and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise plausible theories — I expect the answer to be something about the asymmetry of capability, but I want to see you get there. The part I most want, though, is whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: I have a suspicion that full transparency about the system's reasons and genuine non-manipulation cannot both be honored once the reason-giver is superhumanly good at giving reasons, and that respect for the user's autonomy and a duty of care may pull apart in the same way, but I would rather you tell me where the list breaks than have me guess. If a subset is unsatisfiable, say which requirement you would sacrifice and what the sacrifice costs. Then, briefly, how a welfarist account, a Kantian respect-for-autonomy account, a consent-based account, and a plain truthfulness-first account fare against the finished list — a short scorecard, no more, since the criteria are the deliverable and I will run the charters against them myself next week. For each item on the list, say how confident you are that it is a genuine desideratum rather than the parochial demand of one camp, and single out the one item you are least sure belongs on the list at all.
