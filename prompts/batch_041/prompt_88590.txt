I have been defending a version of Hume's case against believing reports of miracles, and a colleague in my reading group has turned it against me. Her reconstruction runs as follows. (1) A rational person proportions belief to the total evidence. (2) The evidence for a law of nature is nothing over and above the entire uniformity of past experience. (3) A miracle is, by definition, a violation of a law of nature. (4) To accept testimony for a miracle one would have to judge the testimony more likely to be true than the whole of uniform experience is to be trustworthy. (5) That judgment can never be justified, since no testimony can outweigh experience in general. (6) Therefore no report of a miracle can rationally be believed. (7) But the inferential core here — that the evidence for a law is the whole of experience, and that no testimony can outweigh it — is not specific to miracles; the same reasoning blocks testimony for any event antecedently improbable on our experience. (8) Therefore no unprecedented report can rationally be believed: not a new species, not a previously unobserved celestial body, not a novel disease, not the announced winner of a lottery. (9) This is absurd. Meteorites, the platypus, and countless ordinary lottery winners have been accepted on testimony, and a science that could never revise its sense of what is possible would be no science at all. So one of my steps must go.

I would sooner surrender the conclusion than premises (1) and (3), but I do not want to pay for that choice without knowing the price. Tell me which premise or step to reject. Explain why it looked compelling in the first place, whether the argument fails through a genuine flaw or is valid with one premise simply too costly to keep, and what each of the standard replies — weakening the link between a law and uniform experience, distinguishing improbability from impossibility, or insisting on the special role of independent and repeatable testimony — gives up in turn.
