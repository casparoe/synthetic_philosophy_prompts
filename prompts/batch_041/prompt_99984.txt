A professor guest-lectured in my ethics course last term on Confucian role ethics, and I copied down his summary almost word for word. I'm reproducing it here from my notes:

"Confucian role ethics is the view that you are nothing but the sum of your social roles. There's no inner self sitting behind your roles as father, friend, or teacher — take the roles away and there's nobody left. This makes Confucianism a kind of communitarianism: the community comes first and the individual is just a slot in the social order. That's why Confucius treats filial piety as obedience — a child's job is to fit into the family, not to reason about it. And since morality just is playing your part well, the tradition has no real use for universal rules or principles; right and wrong are relative to whatever role you happen to occupy. The standard objection is that this can't be the whole story, because it leaves Confucianism with no way to criticize a bad role — if your role says obey, you obey — so it has nothing to say to a society built on unjust hierarchies. That objection, I think, is decisive."
