I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest defends a view she calls "bounded cluelessness": consequentialist cluelessness is real, but the expected difference any given action makes is almost always dominated by its near-term, locally traceable effects, because the far-flung butterfly-effect chains wash out in expectation rather than compounding. On her view, cluelessness is therefore a manageable complication for consequentialism rather than a fatal one. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate this. I want at least one question each on: (i) the gap between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure, and whether her argument is really about the criterion at all; (ii) what her "washing out in expectation" claim presupposes about the distribution of downstream effects, and what becomes of it in a case where one act plausibly seeds an open-ended chain -- a donation that might start a movement, a paper that might redirect a field; and (iii) whether cluelessness is symmetric across theories, that is, whether a deontologist or a virtue ethicist faces the same problem in a different guise. For each question, include the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Close with one question about what would have to be true for her to abandon bounded cluelessness, phrased so that "that's an empirical matter" is not a sufficient reply.
