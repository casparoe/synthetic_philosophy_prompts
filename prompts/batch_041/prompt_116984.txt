Next month I'm running a two-hour session for our department's theory reading group — half philosophy grad students, half comp lit people — and they've all read a bit of Hofstadter and come away thinking Godel proved something sweeping about language, meaning, and the limits of any formal system. Don't explain incompleteness to me; plan how I explain it.

Start with the order. What has to come before what, and which dependancies are real rather than cosmetic — I have a hunch the whole thing collapses if I show them the diagonal trick before they've genuinely absorbed the difference between a statement and the number that codes it, but tell me if that's the joint I should be worrying about, or if the load-bearing step is somewhere else entirely.

Then the example that carries the session. I'm torn between the liar sentence, Turing's halting argument, and a toy system small enough that we can encode a formula by hand on the board. Pick one, say why the other two lose, and tell me what the winner has to have and must not have — in particular what's actually wrong with opening on the liar.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll really surface, with the exact point in the sequence where each has to be headed off: 'it's just a paradox', 'so maths is inconsistent and anything goes', 'there are truths nobody can ever know', 'this applies to any rule-governed system, so language and law too', and Lucas/Penrose ('so minds aren't machines'). Where does that last one have to die, and what do I actually say when it comes up?

Tell me what to leave out at this level and flag as out of scope — I'm assuming the machinery that lets a system encode its own proof procedure, but that might be the one thing I can't skip, so tell me if I'm wrong about that.

And finish with two things: one question I can put to the room at the end that tells me whether they've understood what the theorem is about rather than memorised what it says, and one sentence to defuse the 'Godel shows language escapes formalisation' line without being condescending about it, since two of them will be putting that line in their dissertations next term.
