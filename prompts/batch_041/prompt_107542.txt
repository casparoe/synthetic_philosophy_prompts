I direct grantmaking at a small family foundation — three program officers, about six million dollars a year, roughly sixty letters of intent and a dozen funded projects annually. Our applicants are small community organizations, and over the past two years they have begun attaching what I can only describe as proofs of seriousness to their proposals: pledges to dissolve the organization if targets are missed, personal cash deposits forfeited on failure, executives volunteering to cut their own salaries until outcomes improve, promises to publish raw internal data, offers to pay for an outside audit of their own books, agreements to return the grant if results fall short, and one memorable pledge to hand the organization's assets to a rival if it failed to deliver.

My board has seized on these. One trustee argues we should fund only applicants who have something of their own to lose; another keeps saying we should look for people willing to burn their boats. I am not convinced, but I cannot articulate why, and I do not want to keep making these calls on instinct. The decisions are consequential and fast: each program officer assesses about twenty letters a year, we have roughly a week per proposal, we cannot conduct forensic accounting, anything requiring legal review is off the table, and grants above $250,000 need board sign-off.

Please write me a decision procedure my program officers can actually run on a proposed commitment: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers end the assessment, and which of these commitments we should stop treating as evidence at all. I want it grounded in what is actually established about when a costly gesture carries information, rather than in the maxims my board repeats. It has to survive the awkward cases: commitments that are expensive but that any organization with money can afford; commitments that change an applicant's incentives without telling us anything about the applicant; commitments whose cost falls on people other than the ones making the promise; and the fact that once we reward a particular commitment, applicants will produce more of it.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: the pledge to dissolve on failure, the forfeitable cash deposit, and the offer to fund an outside audit of the applicant's own books. At least one of these should come out differently from how I currently feel about it — either a commitment I find impressive that the procedure discounts, or one I distrust that it endorses. Tell me what the procedure cannot do, including whether any of this bears on whether a program actually works. Finally, say what would change your mind: which evidence about how commitments like these have historically predicted later performance would overturn the procedure's rankings, and where its reasoning is most likely to fail in our particular setting.
