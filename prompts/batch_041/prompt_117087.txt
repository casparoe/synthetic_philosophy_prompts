I have no training in philosophy or mathematics; I have been reading about prediction and simplicity on my own, and I have talked myself into a view that I would now like you to try to dismantle.

The view is this. Occam's razor is not a methodological preference or a rule of thumb that inquirers impose on the world. It follows from the mathematics of prediction. Treat prediction as a formal problem: an agent is fed a stream of observations and scored on how well it anticipates the next one, and it begins from a prior that spreads its confidence over all the computable ways the world might be, giving more weight to those with shorter descriptions. Such an agent is guaranteed to converge on the truth for any world that is itself computable, and no rival who starts from a different prior can beat it across the board; a rival can win only on data that happen to suit its starting assumptions, and it will lose on other data. On this picture, preferring the simpler hypothesis is not a norm we bring to inquiry but a theorem about learning. Simplicity belongs to the prior, and the prior is forced rather than chosen.

I want a map of what would count against this, and I am not asking whether it is a workable recipe. I already suspect it is not, since the prior cannot be computed and no real agent could use it. I mean the philosophical claim: what would show that simplicity is, after all, a matter of our preferences, or that the theorem does not say what I think it says. Please work through at least these, and add whatever else you think belongs:

- Descriptions have to be measured in some fixed language or machine, and the standard result is that changing the language changes the complexity of a given hypothesis by no more than an additive constant. That is offered as proof that the notion is robust. But a constant can be enormous next to the description lengths that actually decide real comparisons. Does the view still have a genuine notion of simplicity, or one that is well defined only up to a number large enough to swamp every case we care about? Is that a fatal concession or a technicality?
- The prior rewards short descriptions in bits. Scientific simplicity seems to be a family of other things: fewer free parameters, greater unification, symmetry, the absence of special cases, not needing a designer. Are these the same property, or has the view quietly identified a formal quantity with a set of aesthetic and methodological ones? If they can come apart, what follows?
- There are results to the effect that, averaged over all possible worlds, no prior is better than any other; every bias wins somewhere and loses elsewhere. If so, then the universal prior's advantage cannot come from being unbiased. It must come from an assumption that the world is simple or computable in the first place. Is that assumption doing all the work, and is the view therefore circular, or merely honest about where it starts?
- The view insists the prior is forced, yet it points to one specific distribution among many. What would it take to show that this choice is a preference dressed up as a theorem? And, conversely, what would it take to show that it really is forced?

I would be most persuaded by a case where two rival hypotheses are equally short to describe but one is plainly the better scientific choice, or the reverse, where the better choice is far longer to describe.

Then step back. Of everything you give me, which items would the view's own defenders concede count against them, and which would they dismiss as a misunderstanding? Sort what you give me from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, tell me honestly whether the view as I have stated it risks anything at all. Is there any conceivable observation or formal result that would make me abandon it, or have I stated it so that it cannot lose? If it cannot lose, say so, and say whether the fault lies in the view or in my statement of it.

Write in American English, and do not give me citations, titles, or a reading list. I cannot look anything up, and I would rather have the argument than the references.
