I'm putting together a 90-minute session for a formal epistemology seminar — 14 students, mostly grad students, some of them shaky on probability but all of them game — and I want one in-class activity that gets them to feel something that sounds like a typo the first time you hear it: that getting new evidence can leave you *less* precise about a thing, not more. I've heard the phenomenon called dilation, but I've spelled out the exact case I want them to land on below, so don't lean on the name and don't assume they'll have seen it.

Here's the toy case, which I cooked up and checked myself. Two coins, A and B. A's bias is p, B's bias is 1 − p, p unknown. On your desk are two analysts. Analyst U thinks p is uniform on [0,1]. Analyst F insists that p is exactly 1/2. Both are your bosses, so a price is only defensible to you if it's defensible under both of them.

Before any flip: U says P(A heads next) = 1/2, F says 1/2. Same for B. So for a bet on either coin alone you can quote a single price, and your highest buy price and lowest sell price are both 1/2 — no gap, no spread, total agreement. (They do disagree about P(A heads and B heads): 1/6 vs 1/4. So the disagreement is entirely about how the coins hang together, not about any single-flip probability.)

Now flip B once and it comes up heads. Ask each analyst for P(A heads next). U says 1/3 — B coming up heads is evidence that B is the heads-y coin, so p is probably small, so A is probably the tails-y one. F says 1/2, unchanged. So your highest buy price is now 1/3 while your lowest sell price is still 1/2: a gap has opened up, and you got there by learning something. More evidence, less precision.

What I want out of the activity: the students should be the ones who set the prices, in writing, before anything is flipped, and the ones who re-quote after B comes up heads, so the widening is something they do rather than something I announce. No lecture up front, and I definately don't want them hearing the word dilation before the debrief, if at all. I want them to come away seeing (a) that a credence can be a set rather than a number, (b) that the set can grow when evidence arrives, and (c) that the culprit here is disagreement about dependence, not about the marginals — the two analysts would have looked identical if you'd only ever asked them about single flips.

Please give me: the rules and roles, any materials (keep it to paper, coins, a whiteboard, nothing that needs alot of prep), a payoff scheme that makes "you have to be right under both analysts" bite without real money, a timeline for the 90 minutes, the debrief that maps what actually happened onto the machinery above, a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce — I'd rather be told where I have to step in and just say the thing myself. In particular, I worry the two-analyst framing will make them think this is about two experts disagreeing when it's really about one agent whose credence is a set, so tell me how to set it up so that lands, or tell me it can't.
