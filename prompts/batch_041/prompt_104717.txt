I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy of language, currently trying to turn a seminar paper on radical interpretation into something publishable. The part I keep getting stuck on is not Quine's official doctrine but my own verdicts: I have a notebook of cases and reactions, and I want to know whether there is a principle behind them, or whether I have simply absorbed two incompatible habits. All the cases are variants on the gavagai scenario, but I have tried to make them concrete enough that I cannot just recite the standard line. In the basic case, a field linguist observes a native who assents to gavagai whenever a rabbit is in view and dissents otherwise, and who has no other relevant verbal or non-verbal dispositions that the linguist can access. My verdict: there is no fact of the matter whether gavagai means rabbit, undetached rabbit parts, a temporal stage of a rabbit, or some scattered object made of rabbit and fly; two complete manuals that differ on this are equally correct. In a second case the linguist also observes that the native never assents to gavagai when shown a detached rabbit ear, a rabbit shadow, or a rabbit photograph, and assents only to whole, living rabbits. My verdict: here gavagai means rabbit, and not undetached rabbit parts or rabbit stages. I notice that this second verdict sits badly with the first, since the extra observations are still just more of the same kind of evidence, and if the first case really has no fact of the matter, it is unclear why the second does. In a third case the native becomes bilingual, learns English, and when asked says that gavagai means rabbit, translating it that way in novel sentences. My verdict: in the bilingual's mouth gavagai means rabbit, and the indeterminacy is gone. But again I worry that this is just more verbal behavior, so if verbal behavior underdetermines meaning in the first case, why does it settle meaning here? In a fourth case a community uses gavagai for rabbits, for rabbit meat, and for rabbit-shaped clouds, but never for rabbits as such. My verdict: gavagai means something like rabbit or rabbit-representation, not rabbit simpliciter. In a fifth case a solitary speaker uses gavagai for rabbits and for his own afterimages of rabbits, and his dispositions never distinguish the two. My verdict: there is no fact of the matter whether his word means rabbit, afterimage, or the disjunction. In a sixth case a native assents to gavagai for rabbits and for undetached rabbit parts, but dissents when asked whether gavagai and rabbit-part are the same thing. My verdict: gavagai means rabbit, and the dissent shows that he distinguishes the concepts, even though his assent pattern alone is consistent with parts.

I suspect the principle I want is something like this: a translation is correct just in case it is the unique assignment that best rationalizes the speaker's total dispositions to use the expression, including dispositions to accept or reject metalinguistic claims about sameness, paraphrase, and application. But that principle seems to make indeterminacy disappear as soon as we include enough metalinguistic dispositions, and I am not sure I want to give up the first and fifth verdicts. The alternative is to restrict the relevant dispositions to first-order, object-level uses, in which case the third verdict looks unmotivated and the sixth verdict may be unjustified. What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can test them against new cases. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts motivates it. Tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed, especially a native who uses gavagai for rabbits and for rabbit stages but also has a standing disposition to say that gavagai applies to the same thing over time, and a case in which two speakers have identical first-order dispositions but different metalinguistic ones. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up that verdict or complicate the principle to save it. I do not need references or a literature review; I need the principle and the argument for it.
