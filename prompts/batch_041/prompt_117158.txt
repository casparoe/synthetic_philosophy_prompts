I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I keep watching smart reporters — including me, in print, twice — get heritabilty wrong in the same three ways. Next month I'm running a 75-minute session at a science-writers workshop: 18 people, mostly reporters who've done the "is intelligence genetic" story and will do it again. I want them to feel the problem in their hands, not hear it in a lecture, so design me a simulation they run themselves. My rough idea: everyone gets a genotype card and an environment card, the trait is just the two added up, and they compute the heritability of the trait in the room off their own data. Then the number has to move twice for reasons that have nothing to do with genes getting more important — once where I hand everyone the same environment, and once where I split the room into two groups on different environments, so a big between-group gap appears that's 100% environmental while the within-group heritability stays high. I want them to arrive at: heritability is a variance ratio inside one population at one time, not a property of a trait or a person; a high figure is compatible with the average shifting a lot when environments shift; a high within-group figure licenses nothing about differences between groups; and you can't read causal strength off a variance decomposition. Keep the trait botanically neutral during play so nobody starts moralizing mid-game and I can cash it out in the debrief.

Give me the rules, materials, how they get a number without me doing stats on the board, a debrief that maps each round onto one of those four points, what will predictably go wrong the first time and how I handle it, and an honest note on which part of the real science (twins, SNPs, whatever) the exercise cant reproduce and I'll have to just say out loud.
