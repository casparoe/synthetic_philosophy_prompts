I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, with about 18 students. We have already discussed actual infinity through the usual set-theoretic examples—Hilbert's hotel, bijections between a set and a proper subset of itself, Cantor's diagonal construction—and I want them to feel the force of the finitist's objection rather than just hear it stated. The standard examples have become too familiar to them, so I would like an in-class activity that injects a genuinely awkward role into the familiar setup: a finitist auditor who is not a strawman but a participant with real veto power, and who can plausibly insist that every assignment be generated by a finite procedure with no completed infinite totality anywhere in the reasoning. I want the students to run into the tension themselves—between the fact that a rule can always be given and the fact that no finite process ever finishes—before I name potential versus completed infinity or explain why a constructivist might accept the former and reject the latter. The activity should show up in what happens, not in a lecture I give afterward.

Please design the activity in full: roles, rules, materials, a small-stakes payoff scheme that needs no real money (points, tokens, candy, or privileges are fine), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal distinctions—bijection, proper subset, rule-governed generation versus completed assignment, diagonalization, and the finitist demand for constructibility. I imagine a hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on; clerks who must place arriving guests; buses that may be infinite; and a finitist auditor who can veto any placement that treats the infinite set of rooms as a finished whole or that cannot be specified by a finite procedure. A second phase could introduce a guest who claims to have a complete list of all possible room assignments and challenges the clerks to produce one not on the list, so that the diagonal construction becomes something they have to do under pressure rather than something they read. The debrief should be honest about which moments the game can reproduce and which it cannot—for instance, it cannot reproduce the actual proof that the real numbers are uncountable, the semantic issue of whether completed infinity is intelligible, or the full finitist position on impredicative definitions; the facilitator will have to supply those. It should also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—students treating infinity as a very large finite number, asking for a last room, confusing the rule with the completed assignment, or turning the auditor into a villain—and how I should handle each failure.

Finally, I would like you to be direct with me if the task as posed is confused or cannot be done well in 90 minutes. If the finitist auditor role is a caricature that will teach the wrong lesson, or if the game mechanics cannot actually produce the experience I am after, say so plainly rather than complying politely. If I am conflating Hilbert's hotel with Cantor's theorem, or if the activity would work better as a structured deliberation than as a role-play, I want to hear that too. I am asking for an honest assessment of whether this can work, not just a polished activity plan.
