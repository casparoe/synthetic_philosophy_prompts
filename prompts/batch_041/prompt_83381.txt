Write a parody offering document — the prospectus a monetary authority hands to subscribers when it launches a new currency — in the register of a real offering memorandum or product disclosure statement: cover summary, defined terms, numbered clauses, a schedule of risk factors, a governing-law provision. Roughly 700 words, deadpan throughout, no winking asides, no real institution named; the issuer is one you invent, and the piece is written from inside its position, so internal consistency is part of the brief.

The issuer's charter is drawn from George Berkeley's Querist, the book of queries on money and the Irish economy he first published in 1735. The document must be faithful to the positions those queries press toward, since the comedy has to come from the theory being right rather than from its being naive:

- money has no intrinsic value and is not a commodity, a standard, a measure or a pledge; its true idea "as such" is that of "a ticket or counter";
- gold, silver and paper alike are "tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring" value, and the denominations would go on working if the bullion disappeared;
- money is useful only so far as it "stirreth up industry," so that the wealth of a nation is proportioned to the industry of its people and that, in turn, to the circulation of credit, "be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever";
- hence "the four elements, and man's labour therein" are the true source of wealth; men value money only as a means to power, and the aim of a well-governed state is that every member have power according to his industry;
- and the practical corollary: a public bank whose credit rests on the industry and security of the people rather than on a hoard of metal.

Every comic beat should be traceable to one of those positions rather than to legalese for its own sake. The obvious places to work are a section headed something like "What backs this instrument," where the question is answered by showing that it is malformed; redemption terms; a definition of value that is purely relational; a clause against hoarding; an account of the industrious poor and the idle rich; and a schedule of risk factors in which the standard objections to the theory are filed as risks — the sign is arbitrary, so confidence may lapse; a ticket drawn on the future industry of the people is only as good as that industry; nothing in the theory bounds the number of tickets that may be issued. Somewhere the document must concede something genuine without abandoning its position; the parody is fair only if it can be wounded.

The object of the satire is the assumption that money must be backed by a substance with intrinsic value, and the habit of treating money as wealth rather than as a means. A reader who knows the Querist should find the document recognizable and fair to it; a reader who has never heard of it should be able to follow the whole argument from the document alone.
