I'm a PhD student in bioethics, and I'm trying to pin down what the clinical and legal practices surrounding death and organ donation presuppose about personal identity — the kind of question Dennett's scenario makes vivid, where his brain is kept alive apart from the body it once animated and the question becomes where he is.

Consider what we actually do. A patient whose entire brain, brainstem included, has irreversibly stopped functioning cannot breathe on his own, but a ventilator keeps his heart beating and his body warm and perfused; he is dead, the family is told the person is gone, and his heart and liver may be taken while his circulation continues. A patient in a vegetative state, whose cortex is destroyed but whose brainstem still drives breathing and sleep-wake cycles, is alive; she is fed, her infections are treated, and removing her organs would be unthinkable, even though little of what made her a person remains. A patient who is fully conscious but paralysed and unable to speak is alive, plainly, though she can do nothing at all. In a few jurisdictions, a brain-dead woman who was pregnant has been ventilated for weeks so that the fetus could be delivered — the body kept functioning as life support for someone else after the person has been declared dead. And donation after circulatory death calls death after a few minutes of absent heartbeat, while an advance directive lets a person's competent earlier self dictate the care of a later, demented self that may want nothing of the kind.

What theory of the person does this package presuppose? Specifically, does it presuppose that a person is located where her brain is, the brain being the thing whose destruction ends her, or does it require only something weaker and more biological — that a person is an organism whose continued life depends on the brainstem's integrative work? Which rival accounts (animalism, psychological continuity, a higher-brain criterion, Dennett's own suggestion that the self is an abstraction rather than an occupant of any place) does the package rule out, and which does it merely fail to require? Where is it internally inconsistent — the vegetative patient who is alive but empty, the pregnant corpse on the ventilator, two different criteria of death operating in the same hospital? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practices strictly require from what they merely happen to fit, and say what would have to change in each of them if we adopted the higher-brain criterion, or animalism, or the view that the question of where the person is has no answer.
