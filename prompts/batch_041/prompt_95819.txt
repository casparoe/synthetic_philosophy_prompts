The common prior assumption, if you need the gloss, is the idea that rational agents begin with the same prior probability over the state of the world, so when two of them disagree after updating on private information, the disagreement itself is a puzzle: either someone is irrational or someone is withholding a signal. (Type spaces are just the formal bookkeeping for what each person knows and believes about everyone else's information.) The target is the assumption itself, especially the move from 'it's a useful modeling ideal' to 'anyone who disagrees must be irrational or hiding information'—not any particular living economist. The newsletter should be about 700-900 words, in the voice of a slightly self-important board secretary who has no idea anything is funny. It should never wink at the reader; it should sincerely treat the common prior as obvious good governance. Include: a note from the board president about the annual 'prior convergence' meeting; a dispute between two neighbors over something trivial—fence color, a barking dog, the recycling schedule—that the board treats as evidence of private information rather than a genuine difference in priors; a classified ad or two; a resident-of-the-month feature; and a short advice column where every answer is some version of 'update toward the common prior.' The jokes should come from the actual mechanics—posterior updating, agreement theorems, off-equilibrium beliefs, the difference between common knowledge and mere mutual knowledge, the question of whose prior gets to be the common one—not from generic HOA jokes. It should be funny to someone who knows the formal literature and still readable to someone who doesn't. Don't quote any real text or cite any papers; invent all residents and board members. Before you write, brainstorm five specific gags that come from the formal ideas, then pick the best three and build the piece around them. Don't give me a menu of options or a sketch—commit to one full draft. At the end, add a 100-150 word author's note where you state which reading of the common prior assumption you're presupposing (normative ideal, modeling convenience, or empirical claim), where the satire takes liberties with the formalism, and which part of the idea you couldn't satirize without it turning into a different joke.
