I am setting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I need one essay question on functionalism and multiple realizability. The paper is closed-book, each essay is allotted sixty minutes, and students are not expected to supply citations; the question must therefore be answerable entirely from what is printed on the paper and from material the course has already covered. My difficulty is that the obvious question — state the multiple realizability argument, then assess the familiar objections — can be answered by recitation. I want a question that obliges students to decide something and defend the decision.

Please build the question around the following stipulations and case, which I will print on the paper itself; students will meet them for the first time there, so nothing in the question may depend on prior familiarity with them.

Stipulation one. A system realizes a functional profile Shallowly when, over a stipulated range of inputs, it produces the same outputs as the target system, by whatever internal means. It realizes the profile Deeply when, in addition, its internal states can be sorted into kinds whose transition structure mirrors the target's, so that the same counterfactuals hold of it: the same interventions on internal states produce the same transitions, and each state kind is sensitive to the same features of the input.

The case. Two artificial systems are built to match a human's nociceptive and affective organization as characterized in the course. Lattice is assembled from optical rather than electrochemical components; state for state and transition for transition, its internal organization is isomorphic to the human's, and the isomorphism survives intervention, since driving or cutting any of its internal pathways has the structural analogue of the effect that the corresponding intervention has in the human. Register matches the human's entire input–output profile across the tested range — it discriminates the same stimuli, issues the same reports, avoids what the human avoids, and is altered in the same ways by the same externally applied interventions — but it achieves this by exhaustive case lookup: for each input it retrieves a stored entry and emits the stored output. No partition of its internal states corresponds to anything in the human. Outside the tested range it emits a fixed default.

The deliverables are three. First, the examination question itself, in three subparts with marks totalling twenty-five, at least one of which carries enough weight to be a genuine discriminator between strong and weak answers. Subpart (a) must require a verdict on whether the multiple realizability argument needs Deep realization or only Shallow realization, and whether Register satisfies whichever notion it needs. Subpart (b) must require an account of what restricting realization to the Deep notion costs, including at least one kind of system that functionalists would ordinarily want to count as a subject of experience and that the restriction appears to exclude — a partial realizer, a range-limited realizer, or a realizer that needs external scaffolding. Subpart (c) must require a position on whether functional properties can be causally relevant at all, given that realizers do the causal work, and on whether Register makes that position easier or harder to sustain. Second, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words, written as an exemplar for the marking scheme rather than as a survey. It should reach a definite verdict wherever the question admits one, and where it does not, it should say precisely what would settle the matter rather than retreating into the claim that the question is ill-posed. Third, a marking scheme that names the specific moves a first-class answer must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks, with one line per error on how students typically go wrong. Attributions to named authors and references to texts should neither be required nor rewarded. If any subpart strikes you as ambiguous or as admitting a cheap answer, say so, and tell me how to tighten it.
