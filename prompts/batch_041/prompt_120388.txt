For about two years now I have been sitting in on the acquisitions meetings of a small university art museum, mostly because a friend of mine runs them; she is the curator with final say over what the museum buys and how each object is catalogued, and I have gradually become convinced that her decisions are principled even though I cannot state the principle. Here is the run of verdicts that has me stuck. On a canvas long venerated as the work of a seventeenth-century master and then shown by technical analysis to be a twentieth-century forgery, she ruled that the museum should not buy it as a work of art at all and should keep it only as a document of the deception. On a competent painting made last year in the manner of that same old master, though copying no particular picture, she ruled the same way. On a genuine painting by the master whose surface is now largely nineteenth-century restoration overpaint, with the original composition and much of the original paint surviving beneath, she ruled that it should be bought at full value as the work itself. On a bronze cast made decades after a sculptor's death by a foundry working from the sculptor's own surviving plaster mold, she ruled that it should be bought as the work, at something close to full value. On a photographic print made long after the fact by a master printer from the photographer's own original negative, she ruled full value. And on a painting genuinely by the artist but accompanied by provenance papers that a dealer had forged, she ruled full value, on the ground that the object itself was untouched by the fraud.

Next month the committee must decide on a life-size marble figure that a sculptor designed, and for which she supplied the drawings and a small clay model, but which was carved at full scale by two assistants in her workshop, who worked out the details themselves; her own hand never touched the marble, though the division of labor was entirely ordinary for her time and place. I would like you to predict how my friend will rule, and then to do the harder work: set out the competing rules that all of her earlier verdicts are equally consistent with, say what each of those rules predicts about the workshop figure, tell me which rule you believe she is actually running and why you prefer it to the others, and name the one further object that would most cleanly separate whichever rules are still standing after the workshop case is settled. Please answer in American English, and keep the aesthetic vocabulary plain; I want the reasoning, not a survey of the philosophy of art.
