After thirty-five years as a molecular biologist — cloning, sequencing, and more grant-panel arguments than I care to recall about whether a stretch of DNA "meant" anything — I have retired and, rather to my own surprise, fallen in with a philosophy of biology reading group. We keep circling the question of genetic information, and twice now someone has tried to settle a dispute by intoning a slogan instead of making an argument. In one session a colleague insisted that 'DNA is a code,' and from that inferred that genes carry information in the very same sense a sentence does: content that can be true or false, that can be read correctly or misread, and that therefore vindicates a robustly semantic notion of genetic information. In another, someone leaning on 'the genome is a blueprint' quietly assumed a one-to-one correspondence between a gene and a trait. I have used all of these phrases my entire career, and it has dawned on me that I could not say precisely what any of them asserts — which I find genuinely embarrassing.

I would like you to take the part of a philosopher of biology, equally at home in molecular biology and in the philosophy of language and mind, and do for these slogans what a careful analytic philosopher does for any loose piece of language: extract the distinct, precise theses they might be expressing. Please think this through thoroughly before committing to anything. I want the readings laid out as a menu, running from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical — I expect the interesting action to lie in the distinctions between syntactic and semantic senses of "information," in how much work arbitrariness and convention are doing, and in whether a code is being assimilated to a blueprint, a recipe, or a program. For each reading I want your verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, or simply false — and, crucially, whether that reading is the one that would actually license the move my colleagues wanted to make with it. Do not trouble yourself with citations, reading lists, or who said what; I want the conceptual map, argued from first principles rather than from authority.

Give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
