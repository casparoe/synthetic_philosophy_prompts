Q2. Short answer (10 points). (a) (3 points) What is Thomson's violinist example meant to show? Be explicit about what the example does and does not assume about the moral status of the fetus. (b) (3 points) State the central claim of Marquis's future-like-ours account of why killing is wrong, and say what that account assumes about the fetus's moral status. (c) (4 points) Consider the following case. Dana is the only person in the country whose blood can be filtered to keep a stranger, Alex, alive; without the treatment Alex will die within six months. The treatment requires Dana to be hooked up to a machine for twenty minutes a week during those six months. It is boring and mildly uncomfortable, but harmless, and it does not interfere with her work, travel, or plans. A proposed law would require Dana to undergo the treatment, with a small fine for refusing. Using Thomson's argument and then Marquis's argument, say whether the law would be morally defensible — that is, whether Dana would be morally required to comply — and say whether the two arguments give the same answer about Dana.

And here's what the student wrote:

(a) Thomson wants us to imagine waking up and finding ourselves plugged into a famous violinist who will die unless he can use our kidneys for nine months. She thinks we are not required to stay plugged in: even if the violinist has a right to life, that right does not give him a right to the use of your body. She then applies this to pregnancy, granting for the sake of argument that the fetus is a person with a right to life, and argues that the right to life is not a right to be kept alive by somebody else's body. So abortion is permissible even if the fetus is a person. The example is neutral about whether the fetus really is a person; it is built to work even for someone who thinks it is.

(b) The future-like-ours account says that what makes killing wrong is that it deprives the victim of the future they would have had, a future of experiences, activities and enjoyments. The fetus has a future like ours, so killing it deprives it of that future, which is why abortion is wrong. This is really a version of the potentiality argument: the fetus is wrong to kill because of what it has the potential to become. That is also its advantage over Thomson, since it doesn't need the fetus to count as a person already — it explains why killing infants and people with severe cognitive disabilities is wrong too, even though they aren't persons in the strict sense.

(c) In Dana's case, Thomson's argument says Dana has no obligation. The machine uses her body without her consent, and her right to control her own body outweighs Alex's need. The case is structurally identical to the violinist — a person who will die unless another person's body is used to keep them alive — so the answer comes out the same way. Marquis's argument doesn't have much to say about Dana's case, because it is about killing and not about failing to help someone. So the two arguments give different answers about Dana: Thomson's lets her refuse, and the future-like-ours argument doesn't address it.
