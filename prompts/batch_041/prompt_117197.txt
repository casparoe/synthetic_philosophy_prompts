I'm in a slightly odd reading group — half graphic designers, half cognitive-science grad students — and this month we paired the Helvetica documentary (the Hustwit one, 2007, made for the typeface's 50th birthday) with Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project from Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies. I'm supposed to kick off the discussion and I keep going in circles, so I'd like you to write me the reading instead.

Here's the frame I want to use. Letter Spirit is the project whose goal is a program that designs all 26 lowercase letters in a single consistent style — the letters are built on a grid, so you get a "gridfont," and the program is handed a few seed letters in a style it has never seen and has to invent the other twenty-odd, checking as it goes that each new form is (a) still recognizably that letter and (b) clearly a sibling of the letters already made. What makes the project interesting is that two constraints pull at once: the letter-category, which is what makes every 'a' an 'a' even though no two 'a's you have ever seen are identical, and the style, which is what makes all 26 of these particular letters hang together as one family. The category has to be fluid — how far a form can slip and still be an 'a' is a matter of degree, not a yes/no — and the style has to be fluid too, since the program can't just store one canonical 'a' and squash it. So: read the film through that lens. My hunch, which I don't trust, is that Helvetica is secretly Platonist about letters — it treats the letterforms as if they were the letters themselves and Helvetica merely the cleanest window onto them, the cure for the messy hand-lettering and cramped scripts of the fifties — while the postmodern critics in the film are the nominalists. Does the film actually hold a theory of what makes an 'a' an 'a'? Is the claim that Helvetica is neutral and transparent a perceptual fact about letter-identity or a mid-century institutional achievement (subways, corporations, government forms) that installed one style as the default center of gravity for the category? And does the film's own habit of cutting from one Helvetica 'a' to the next, everywhere in the city, dramatize the slipperiness of the category or just the reach of a brand?

Lean on the specific scenes and voices: the designers who say Helvetica is boring and limiting, the one who insists that legibility and communication aren't the same thing and that a deliberately hard-to-read letterform can communicate more, the ones who hate it on ideological grounds and reach for hand-drawn type as a rejection of conformity, the third camp who like the challenge of making it speak differently, and the New York subway signage guy with his fierce one-typeface conviction — plus the film's closing turn toward everyone styling their own pages. Where does the Letter Spirit framing illuminate these disagreements, and where does the film push back on it — for instance, if a reader can be trained or bullied into accepting any 'a' as the 'a', is the letter-category really a perceptual constraint at all, or is Letter Spirit smuggling in an essentialism the designers are right to reject? A good answer will be honest about the places the reading strains rather than forcing the film to illustrate the theory, and should end with what the film knows about letter-identity, style and ideology that a program designing gridfonts wouldn't discover. Please don't just summarize the film or give me a list of themes — I want the argument, and I want it to survive a room with actual type designers in it.
