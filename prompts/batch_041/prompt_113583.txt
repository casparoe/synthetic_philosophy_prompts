For the past term I have been sitting in on a colleague's advanced metaphysics seminar, and I have kept a private list of the modal verdicts he lets drop, because I have become convinced that a single principle generates them and that he has never stated it and may not even see it. On artifacts he is an origin essentialist of the strictest sort: the hand-carved rocking chair in his office, he says, could not have been made from a different plank of oak, though it could have been finished with a darker stain and could have been carved a week later; the bronze casting on his windowsill could not have been poured from a different batch of metal, though it could have been exhibited in a different city. On people he is equally firm at the coarse grain: our mutual friend's daughter could not have had different biological parents and could not have been a bonobo, though she could have been raised abroad and could have become a surgeon rather than a poet. He has also insisted, in a throwaway remark I admit I may be misremembering, that if the daughter had had a monozygotic twin, the twin would not have been her, even though the two would have come from one and the same fertilized egg and would have carried the same genome.

What I cannot get him to say is where, exactly, the essential origin stops, and whether it stops at the same place for persons as for artifacts. At the department party last week I asked him whether the daughter could have come from a different sperm and a different egg, that is, a different sperm from the same father and a different egg from the same mother, in the same act; he changed the subject, which I take to mean he has not decided. Please predict what he will say when he finally does decide, and tell me which of his earlier verdicts constrain the answer and which leave it open. Lay out the two or three candidate rules that his record is consistent with, state precisely what each one predicts about the different-sperm-and-egg case, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and name the further case, the one I should put to him next, that would actually force the rules apart; if you think his aside about the twin already rules something out, say what and why.

Throughout, I would like you to separate the parts of this that are settled among people who work on it from the parts that are genuinely contested, and not let a contested position pass as the consensus. I would rather have an honest map of the disagreement, including the places where the biologically real cases make the philosophical options harder than the textbooks suggest, than a confident verdict. Please answer in American English.
