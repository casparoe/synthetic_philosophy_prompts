I'm a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a philosopher of language — a woman who has spent a career on the rule-following problem, and who, in the course of the novel, first decides the problem is not merely unsolved but unserious, and then recants. The book only works if the position she finally lands on is one that a well-informed reader can't easily knock over, so I need an honest assessment rather than a survey.

Please act as an expert philosopher of language and mind — someone at home in the later Wittgenstein and in the contemporary literature on the normativity of meaning, dispositions, and interpretation — and rank a menu of proposed resolutions to the skeptical challenge about rule-following (the challenge usually traced to a certain influential reading of Wittgenstein's later remarks, on which no fact about a speaker determines whether she meant addition rather than some deviant function that agrees with addition on every case she has ever considered and diverges beyond). The menu below is mine, the labels are mine, and I'd rather you not worry about who has held what; assess the positions as stated. No citations, no reading lists — I want your judgment, not a bibliography. If you think two entries collapse into one, say so and merge them.

Before ranking anything, spend a paragraph or two making explicit what a resolution would have to do to count as adequate: what the challenge actually demands, and what would count as cheating (for instance, simply stipulating that meaning facts exist, or simply accepting the skeptical conclusion and calling that an answer).

The menu:

*The dispositional answer.* What I meant is fixed by the dispositions I had; an utterance is correct just in case it matches what I would have said.

*The communitarian answer.* Nothing about the individual fixes meaning; correctness is fixed by communal agreement, so an utterance is correct when it conforms to what the community accepts.

*The quietist answer.* The demand for a fact that constitutes my meaning is itself confused; the challenge rests on a picture of meaning as an inner state awaiting inspection, and once that picture is dropped there is nothing left to answer.

*The primitivist answer.* Facts about what I meant are real and irreducible — not facts about dispositions, agreement, or anything more basic — and asking for a further analysis is like asking what a color is really made of.

*The best-interpretation answer.* A speaker's meaning is whatever the most coherent and most charitable overall interpretation of her behavior would assign; meaning is not a hidden fact to be discovered but a construct of the best theory of her.

*The prospective-commitment answer* (a hybrid I invented for the book). What fixes it that I meant addition is nothing in my past — neither my dispositions nor my community — but the pattern of corrections I would accept and the commitments I have already undertaken; meaning is real but forward-looking, constituted by the normative stance I will take toward future uses.

*The genre answer* (also mine). A practice has meaning because it belongs to a genre of activity with internal standards — arithmetic, chess, navigation — and correctness is fixed by what the genre requires. Addition and its deviant rival diverge because only one of them belongs to the genre, and genre membership is fixed by the point of the activity rather than by facts about anyone's head.

Test each entry against two cases, which I suspect discriminate:

(i) The solitary practitioner: someone who works out arithmetic alone, on an island, and is never corrected by anyone.
(ii) The uniformly deviant community: everyone accepts a continuation that, by our lights, is wrong, and no one ever notices or dissents.

Then, for each entry, give (a) a score from 0 to 1 for how well it discharges the challenge, (b) one sentence of justification, (c) the strongest objection it faces, and (d) its verdict on the two test cases. Group the entries into tiers. Then tell me which one you would bet on, which one my character should land on if she is meant to be difficult to refute, and finally what my menu leaves out — a seventh position I ought to have included.

Think carefully and reason at length before you score anything; I would much rather have a considered ranking than a fast one, and please don't hedge by assuring me that each position captures something. I want an ordering.
