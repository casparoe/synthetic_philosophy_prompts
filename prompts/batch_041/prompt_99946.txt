A friend in my reading group keeps ambushing us with arguments and this one I can't shake. He swears it shows Bayesian confirmation theory is broken, and honestly every step looks airtight to me.

(1) Evidence E confirms hypothesis H for a person only if learning E would raise her credence in H.
(2) Learning E would raise her credence in H only if her credence in H given E beats her unconditional credence in H, i.e. P(H|E) > P(H).
(3) By Bayes's theorem, P(H|E) > P(H) exactly when P(E|H) > P(E).
(4) Now take any E the person already knows. Her credence in E is 1. And P(E|H) is also 1, because you can't conditionalize your way below certainty — if P(E) = 1 then P(E|H) = 1 for any H. So P(E|H) = P(E).
(5) So for any already-known E, P(H|E) = P(H). No boost. E doesn't confirm H.
(6) But the second you've conditioned on a piece of evidence it becomes already-known, i.e. old. So after your very first update, every bit of evidence you will ever have is old evidence.
(7) So a rational agent's credences can only ever move once, on the first thing she learns, and never again on the basis of evidence.
(8) Which is nuts. General relativity is supposed to have been confirmed by the excess precession of Mercury's perihelion, and astronomers had been staring at that precession since the 1800s — it was old evidence by the time Einstein showed up. So old evidence clearly does work, which means something above has to be wrong.

Where does it go wrong? I really don't want to give up (3), Bayes is the one piece I trust completely, and I'm fond of (2) as well. But (8) seems undeniable. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked so innocent, and what it actually costs to give it up — and if there are two ways out, price them both.
