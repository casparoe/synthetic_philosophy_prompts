I am preparing a two-page research proposal for a small grant in animal ethics, and I would like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a proper plan. The idea concerns the metric that has become standard in discussions of the ethics of eating: the number of animals killed per calorie, or per gram of protein, used to argue that beef is preferable to chicken and that a plant-based diet is preferable to both because it avoids slaughter. Two things bother me about it. First, the metric treats every death as interchangeable, so that one field mouse killed by a combine counts the same as one cow, and it is silent about the suffering of the life that precedes the death. That means it may be answering a different question from the one we care about, namely which diet produces less total disvalue. Second, it collides with the old argument that animal agriculture benefits animals by bringing into existence animals who would otherwise never live: if that argument has any force, then a diet that kills more animals may also create more, and a metric built only on deaths cannot even represent the trade-off, let alone resolve it. My suspicion is that the debate is running together two distinct questions, how many deaths a diet causes and how much disvalue it causes, and that separating them changes which diets come out ahead. I want the proposal to test whether that suspicion is right.

Please write the proposal so that it does the following. It should state the research question precisely enough to have a determinate answer; for example, whether any single scalar can rank diets by harm, and if not, what the minimal structure of an adequate comparison would be. It should explain why the main existing positions, the utilitarian one, the rights-based one, and the fewest-deaths argument, each leave this open rather than settling it. It should describe a method a philosopher can carry out with no new empirical data, distinguishing the candidate metrics by their formal properties and by thought experiments in which they come apart. It should say what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure, including the risk that the whole question dissolves into a verbal dispute about what we mean by harm. And it should give a realistic scope for the time and length available.

Assume the audience is philosophically trained but not specialists in this literature, so positions should be identified by their arguments rather than by the names of authors. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review by reference; I will add those myself. Keep the whole thing to roughly the length of a two-page proposal.
