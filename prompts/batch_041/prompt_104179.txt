I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry, and I have found that the notion of a looping effect is one of those ideas that everyone can recite and almost no one can use. I should therefore like you to write a conceptual explanation of it, pitched at readers who already know the rough shape of the idea — that psychiatric classifications are what Hacking calls interactive kinds, that the people classified can come to understand how they are classified, and that this understanding can in turn alter the people — but who cannot say what the idea is supposed to establish. The specific tangle I want untied is this. A looping effect can be read as an epistemological claim (our categories are unstable, so our generalisations about them are fragile, and the evidence for a category can be partly generated by the act of applying it) or as a metaphysical claim (the category does not pick out a natural kind, because the kind is partly constituted by the classificatory practice itself). I want you to say precisely what the phenomenon does and does not support, and where it leaves the question of whether a diagnostic category latches onto something real.

To keep the explanation from collapsing into a recital, please make use of the following distinction, which I have found useful in teaching but have not seen handled well. Call the first-order loop the case in which the extension of a category shifts because its members respond to being classified — the textbook case of a person who comes to exhibit the symptoms they are told to expect. Call the second-order loop the case in which the criteria of application shift because the classifiers respond to the fact that the extension has shifted — the diagnostician revises the manual, or the researcher redesigns the instrument, in the light of what the first-order loop has done. I should like an account of whether these two can in principle be told apart in practice, whether one is more damaging than the other to the realist's position, and whether the familiar worry about self-fulfilling prophecy is simply the first-order loop under another name or a genuinely different phenomenon.

Finally, please address the two objections I find hardest. The first is that interactive is too cheap a diagnosis, since money, marriage and gender are equally interactive and no one concludes that they are not real. The second is that a realist who treats a category as held together by a cluster of mechanisms rather than by a single essence has an obvious reply to the looping objection, and I want to know whether that reply succeeds or merely relocates the problem. Please write in British English, and do not trouble with citations or a reading list; I want the argument, not the bibliography.
