Question:

Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg describe a phenomenon they call the unilateralist's curse and propose a principle of conformity as a remedy. (a) Explain the mechanism that generates the curse, and state the assumptions the argument relies on. (b) Explain how the mechanism relates to the winner's curse, and identify the most important disanalogy between the two. (c) State the principle of conformity and explain how it is supposed to relieve the curse. (d) Consider the objection that the principle would paralyze beneficial innovation, and say how the authors would respond.

Student's answer:

The curse arises in situations where several agents are each independently able to undertake some initiative that would significantly affect the others, and each is motivated not by self-interest but by a concern for the common good. Because the agents have imperfect information, they disagree about the expected value of the initiative. If each agent is free to act on his own judgment — that is, if the decision procedure is unilateralist — then the initiative will be undertaken whenever at least one agent judges that its expected value is positive. Since the agents' estimates are noisy, the agent who acts is likely to be the one with the most optimistic estimate, that is, the one in the upper tail of the distribution. So the initiative is disproportionately likely to be undertaken in precisely those cases where its true value has been overestimated and it is in fact harmful. The curse is thus a selection effect rather than a failure of any individual agent's reasoning: it is the decision procedure, not the agents, that is at fault.

This is structurally similar to the winner's curse in a common-value auction, where the winning bidder is the one who has most overestimated the value of the object and therefore overpays. The main disanalogy is that in an auction the bidders are rivals with opposing interests, whereas the agents in a unilateralist situation share a common goal, so the curse can arise even among perfect altruists who wish each other well.

The obvious remedy is what the authors call a principle of conformity: an agent should refrain from undertaking such an initiative unless there is something close to unanimity among the similarly situated agents that it is worth doing. In practice this would have to be a legally binding rule enforced by some international authority, since voluntary restraint would be defeated by the fact that any single defector can trigger the outcome.

One might object that this would paralyze beneficial innovation, since almost any new technology will be opposed by someone. The authors' reply is that the principle is not meant to apply to all decisions, but only to unilateral actions with significant effects on others, and that the costs of inaction must be weighed in the same scales as the costs of action.

The curse would disappear if the agents' estimates were unbiased, since then the most optimistic estimate would be as likely to be too low as too high. The real problem is the overconfidence that leads each agent to think he knows best.
