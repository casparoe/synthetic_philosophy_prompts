I cover philosophy and the human sciences for a general readership, and for most of this year I have been reporting on a dispute within speech-act theory that its participants insist is still live: whether the force of an illocutionary act — promising, naming, marrying, resigning, refusing, warning — is essentially a matter of social convention, or essentially a matter of a speaker communicating an intention that a hearer recognizes. The two camps I have been interviewing line up roughly as follows. The convention-first camp, in the tradition associated with Austin and Searle, holds that performative and illocutionary acts are constituted by rules: the act of marrying, betting, or naming a ship exists only because there is a practice whose constitutive rules assign it that status, and this is why uptake by an audience can matter — the practice, not the private intention, confers the force. The intention-first camp, in the lineage of Strawson and Grice, holds that what makes an utterance an act of a given kind is the speaker's intention to produce an effect by means of the hearer's recognition of that intention; conventions are a common and useful instrument for securing that recognition, but they are not what the act consists in, and accounts that make convention constitutive tend to smuggle the intention back in through the essential rule.

My reporting keeps snagging on a class of cases I have started calling unreceived performatives — utterances that appear to do their work with no hearer, or no competent hearer, on the other end. A vow made silently and never disclosed. A resignation letter posted to a personal page that no one with authority to accept it ever reads. A forgiveness pronounced aloud after the person forgiven has left the room. An agreement recorded by a checkbox that no human being will ever look at, accepted by a machine. A refusal that is never communicated because the offer never arrives. I would like the two camps to be made to give verdicts on these rather than retreat to their textbook examples.

What I need is the document I would have commissioned if I were a foundation rather than a writer: the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two camps could actually produce, and that both camps could put their names to. I want the claims they agree on — about what a speech act is, about what has to be true for an utterance to count as one, about which of Austin's and Searle's distinctions survive and which were artifacts of the examples chosen, and about what unreceived performatives show. Then the precise formulations of where they still differ, each phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording as its own. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, with each crux accompanied by a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it and by each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance.

I should say that I suspect the two sides have been talking past each other on part of this, and that the word convention is doing at least two different jobs — a regularity that solves a coordination problem, and a rule that is constitutive of an act — so that some of the apparent disagreement may be verbal. If that is right, I want the equivocation identified exactly, the residue of genuine disagreement isolated, and a formulation proposed that removes the verbal part. Do not cite literature, do not append a reading list, and do not tell me what some unnamed body of scholarship holds; the parties to this document are the two camps themselves, and it has to be legible to a reader who has never opened any of the founding texts. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table whose columns give the sub-question, the formulation each side would sign for its own position, and why the difference matters in practice, followed by the crux section, followed by your note on the verbal residue.

Two further things. First, this is a document, not a survey: where you have to choose between a formulation that both sides would find vacuously agreeable and one that is substantive enough to be worth signing, choose the substantive one and show the work. Second, after the joint statement, in your own voice, come down on the crux that survives the drafting. Tell me which camp you think has the better of it and why, and name the single consideration that would make you change your mind. I do not want the options laid out and the adjudication left to me; I want your verdict, defended.
