In David Lewis’s discussion of penal substitution, an offender has been justly sentenced, and an innocent friend volunteers to undergo the punishment in his place. We tend to think that even if the friend submits to it, the authorities may not simply let the offender go: the punishment seems to belong to the offender in a way that cannot be transferred. Yet the Christian doctrine of penal substitution says that Christ bore the punishment for sinners. Lewis presses the question whether we really believe in penal substitution or whether we believe something else—perhaps that the sinner is somehow included in Christ, so that the punishment is not transferred but shared, or that Christ acts as a representative rather than a substitute. Write a pantoum in which this tension is enacted formally. Use two voices, the offender and the volunteer. The pantoum’s interlocking repetitions should make the same words migrate from one speaker to the other, so that a line that first sounds like a transfer of punishment later sounds like a participation in it, or vice versa. Let the poem explore what it would mean for punishment to be transferable, whether guilt can be assumed, and what is left of justice if it can. Do not resolve the tension; let the final quatrain leave the two readings standing side by side. Aim for four to six quatrains. Begin directly with the poem itself, without preface or commentary.
