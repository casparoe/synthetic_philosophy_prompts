For the past two years I have been writing a novel about a man — a municipal clerk, of all people — who cannot stop re-checking his front door, and in the course of researching him I stumbled on the story of the digger wasp that the cognitive sciences have turned into a parable, and I have not been able to put it down since. The story runs roughly as follows: a female wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it by the antennae to her burrow, and leaves it at the threshold while she goes inside to inspect the nest; if an observer nudges the cricket a few inches away while she is inside, she emerges, finds the prey, drags it back to the threshold — and then, as though the entire sequence has been reset, goes in to inspect the nest again, dropping the cricket once more; the loop can be run many times, and by some tellings it runs indefinitely. Douglas Hofstadter coined the word sphexishness for this sort of mechanical rigidity, and Daniel Dennett pressed the example into service in his case for compatibilism, holding it up as the image of a life that is all routine and no freedom. I now teach a small upper-level seminar — part literature, part philosophy of mind — in which I use the wasp as a recurring figure, and I am writing the final examination.

What I want is a single essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the free will and determinism debate, and that forces students to notice how slippery the parable actually is. Several things need to come into view at once. First, sphexishness as Hofstadter and Dennett use it is a description of behaviour — rigidity under a certain class of perturbations — yet it is almost always heard as a claim about mechanism, about what is going on inside the wasp; we watch the behaviour but never see the program, and it is not obvious that rigid behaviour entails a rigid mechanism, or that a rigid mechanism must show up as rigid behaviour. Second, the tempting escape route — that we are not sphexish because we can step back and notice our own routines — seems either to fail or merely to relocate the problem, since whatever does the noticing may itself be running a routine, and the wasp is presumably not troubled by its own sphexishness either. Third, and this is the part I most want tested, some ethologists have since reported that the wasp's behaviour does adapt when the disturbance is repeated often enough, and that repeated inspection of a disturbed nest might be a sensible response rather than a glitch; if that is right, then what looked like a proof of mindlessness may have been an artefact of how the experiment was arranged, which raises the question of what evidence could ever establish that a creature is sphexish rather than merely observed under the wrong conditions.

The students are strong third- and fourth-year undergraduates who have read a little philosophy of mind and a chapter or two of Dennett, but they are not specialists, so the question must be self-contained; I will reproduce the wasp anecdote on the exam paper, and please do not build the question around named papers or a reading list, since they will be answering from the anecdote and their wits. They have ninety minutes for the essay. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words, and a marking scheme worth twenty marks, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth and, for each point, one line on how candidates characteristically miss it. The question should end by asking what an adequate criterion of non-sphexishness would have to look like, and whether such a criterion can be stated without quietly presupposing a substantive account of freedom; I want the marking scheme to separate an A from a B precisely by whether the answer does anything with that last demand beyond gesturing at reflection or consciousness. I have no interest in a question that rewards the student who says the wasp is a machine and we are not.
