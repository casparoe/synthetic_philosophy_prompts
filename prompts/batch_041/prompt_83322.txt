I'm TAing our department's first-year PhD proseminar in formal epistemology this term and I've somehow ended up writing the take-home final. I want the one long essay question to be on imprecise probabilities, because it's the unit I know best and the one the students seem most confident about. Which is exactly the problem: maybe a third of the class can already do the standard dance — "a credal set is just a set of probability functions, Ellsberg shows people are ambiguity averse, imprecision lets you stay silent when the evidence is thin" — and if I ask anything in that register they'll hand me back the same four paragraphs, and I'll learn nothing about whether they actually get it. So I want the question built around a tension I only noticed while I was prepping the unit, and I want to watch them try to see it.

Here's the tension. In section we've been running with two things that I'm now pretty sure can't both be right. First, our decision rule: an act is permissible iff it maximizes expected utility relative to at least one of the probability functions in the agent's credal set (I've been calling it the "somewhere-best" rule, mostly so they stop saying "maximin"). Second, we've been assuming, without much fuss, that a credal set is convex — the usual grounds being that if you can't decide between going with P1 and going with P2, you should be fine with the fifty-fifty mixture, so mixtures get pulled in too. But those two commitments seem to want different answers to a question we never asked out loud: what is a credal set supposed to *be*? Is it the agent's actual opinion, which is simply unsharp — one determinate state of mind that happens not to single out a number? Or is it a bunch of candidates, one of which is her real (sharp) opinion, with her not knowing which one? Those are definately not the same picture, and I think the "somewhere-best" rule and the convexity assumption each presuppose a different one of them. I want students to have to say that out loud, with a case, instead of hiding behind the formalism.

So what I need from you: (1) the essay question itself, phrased so that someone who's read the standard material still can't answer it by defining terms and listing motivations. It should force them to (a) lay out the two readings of a credal set clearly enough that the difference is actually visible, (b) construct a concrete case — a bet, an urn, whatever — in which the two readings license different acts, and (c) say which reading the "somewhere-best" rule needs, which reading the convexity/sure-thing argument needs, and whether we get to keep both or have to give something up. (2) A model answer, maybe 700-800 words, written at the level of a strong first-year grad student. And (3) a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific errors that should cost marks — the big one being the student who writes "imprecise credence just means I'm not sure what my credence is" and then sails on for three pages without noticing that that's a different state of mind from the one they were supposed to be describing. No citations or reading list needed; they won't have anything with them.
