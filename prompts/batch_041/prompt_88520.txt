I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a small interdisciplinary center that funds one-day workshops for mixed audiences of philosophers and cognitive scientists, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into the actual document. The seed is an old thought experiment of Douglas Hofstadter's that he calls the careenium, which I keep coming back to in arguments with a friend in our cognitive science reading group: imagine a giant billiard table covered with an enormous number of tiny magnetic balls (he calls them simms) that careen around and stick together into stable clumps (simmballs). The clumps have a life of their own — they form, drift, and knock each other apart in regular, recognizable ways — and the whole point of the example is that the same goings-on can be described at either level: as simms jostling simms, or as simmballs shoving simmballs. Hofstadter's paper asks, in effect, who is doing the shoving, and my suspicion is that the question as usually posed is really three questions wearing one coat. I want the proposal to argue that whether the simmball pushes the substrate around has different answers depending on what you take a cause to be: if "push" means difference-making (the interventionist sense, on which to be a cause is to be something whose manipulation would change the effect), the simmball-level description looks as legitimate as the simm-level one; if "push" means production — the transfer of oomph, the thing that actually moves the ball — then only the simms push, and the simmball is at best a useful fiction; and if "push" means being a real pattern that earns its keep in prediction and explanation, the question becomes whether the clump-level regularities are robust in the right way. My half-formed thought is that the familiar exclusion worry about higher-level causation only gets its grip because people slide between these senses, and that once they are separated the residual dispute is not metaphysical but normative — it is about which notion of causation an explanation is entitled to invoke, and for what purpose.

Please write the proposal itself (two pages, continuous prose, not a bullet-point skeleton): state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the standard positions leave it open, and describe the method. The method I would like proposed is constructive rather than exegetical — a family of toy careenia, deliberate variations on the setup, designed to pull the two levels apart: cases where the clump-level description supports counterfactuals or interventions that the sim-level description does not, cases where the reverse holds, and one or two cases where the levels come apart the other way, with a causally rich sim-level story to which the clump-level description adds nothing. The proposal should say why this approach beats the obvious alternatives (surveying the mental-causation literature, or simulating a real neural system), what a result would look like — ideally a small taxonomy of senses of "push" plus a verdict about which disputes dissolve and which survive — and what would count as the project failing, which for me would be if the three senses of causation turn out to coincide on every toy case I can build, so that the wedge never appears.

Two constraints. First, do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will add references myself, and I would rather the proposal stand on arguments than on who said what. Second, do not lean on claims about what Hofstadter really meant or on the details of his text — treat the careenium as a toy I am free to modify, and write for a reader who knows the standard literature on mental causation but has never encountered the example, so it should be self-explanatory within a sentence or two. The workshop is one day with a short written output afterward, so keep the scope honest: say what can actually be finished in that time and what would be a stretch.
