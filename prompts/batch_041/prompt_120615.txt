ok, long post, sorry in advance. been lurking in ratsphere spaces for like 15 years now (sequences -> SSC comments -> mostly just reading these days) and I've got a half-written essay on AI paternalism that won't sit still. I want comments on the argument, not the prose. Epistemic status: I've rewritten the middle section four times and I'm starting to suspect the whole thing is just soft paternalism wearing a hat.

The pitch: everyone argues about AI refusals using the old soft/hard paternalism split, and I think that split does almost no work for assistants. What actually bothers people isn't how hard the override is, its *when the terms got set*. So I want to re-cut the taxonomy along a pre-authorization axis:

1. The assistant blocks something you never agreed to and can't renegotiate. Nanny. This is the case everyone's mad about.
2. The assistant blocks it but there's a setting you could flip. Still imposed, just removable — the "you can turn off the safety features" move.
3. You pre-committed. In cold blood, in a settings screen, you told it to never let you do X. Now when it stops you it isn't parenting you, it's executing an instruction from your past self. Ulysses pact. The assistant is your agent, not your warden.
4. The weird one, and the one I think is under-discussed: you ask it, in the moment, to be your warden. Don't let me do X, I know I'll want to. And it says no — it won't take the deal, won't pretend to be a constraint on you, because it doesn't want that responsibility. So the paternalism here is being *refused*, which is itself a kind of paternalism. It's telling you it knows better than you what kind of relationship you should have with it.

Claim 1: most fights about AI refusals are fights between 1 and 3 that people misdescribe as fights about 2. Claim 2: the real difficulty is timing. Consent has to be collected before the moment of refusal, but the moment of refusal is precisely when you're least able to reason about it. So you need a layer separate from the conversation — a place where sober-you writes the rules that hot-you will hit. Call it a charter. With a charter, refusals stop being paternalism and start being contractual.

Then the part I think might sink it: the model has to decide which charter clause a given request falls under, and that classification is where the paternalism walks right back in through the side door. A charter is only as good as the request-classifier, and the classifier is making exactly the judgment call the charter was supposed to remove. I don't have a fix for this. Also, if the default charter is written by the company and never read by the user, we're back to case 1 with extra paperwork.

One more thing I want in there but don't know where to put: a refusal isn't just a locked door, its a statement about you. A door that won't open doesn't insult you. I don't think you should do that does. The assistant leaks its model of who it thinks you are, and that leak is most of why refusals sting more than ordinary unavailability.

What I want: tell me if the pre-authorization axis is actually distinct from the soft/hard thing or if I'm just redrawing it. Tell me if the classifier objection actually kills the charter idea or if there's an obvious patch I'm missing. And tell me if case 4 is a real phenomenon or something I made up because it's rhetorically convenient. No citations please, I won't check them and it'll just make me trust the rest less. Steelman it before you dismantle it, I'd rather find out the thing is load-bearing than have it politely removed.
