I've been lurking in the rat-adjacent blogosphere since the Sequences days and I'm finally writing down my actual position on orthogonality and instrumental convergence so my reading group can poke holes in it. Here's what I've got:

(a) Orthogonality: capability and terminal goals are independent, any level of smart can come with any final goal. And I take that to be a conceptual claim, not an empirical one — it's about what you can coherently posit, not about what anyone will actually build.
(b) Instrumental convergence: almost any terminal goal, pursued by a capable enough agent, throws off the same short list of drives — self-preservation, resource and power acquisition, goal-content integrity.
(c) Convergence is what really carries the AI-risk case for me. Orthogonality mostly just kills the assumption that it'll be nice because it's smart.
(d) Don't anthropomorphize. The space of possible minds is huge; a mind doesn't need to be conscious, curious, or anything like us to be dangerous. Honestly it might not even need stable preferences or be an optimizer in the decision-theoretic sense — that could just be our projection.
(e) Training doesn't hand you a helpful values prior either: it shapes behavior, not goals, so whatever goals form are the ones that were easiest to represent, not the ones we'd of picked.
(f) So I doubt an off switch or a corrigibility patch is a stable fix — a capable agent that doesn't want its goals changed will route around it.

Two things I want stress-tested. First, I invented a distinction to try to keep (a) and (b) from colliding: goal orthogonality (any goal can pair with any capability) vs trajectory orthogonality (the same capability can be aimed at any goal and stay aimed there). Do I actually use the second one anywhere, or does the package only ever need the first? Second, take an agent whose terminal goal is cheap and already satisfied — it just wants one particular mountain to have a particular shape, and has no interest in persisting. (b) seems to predict almost nothing about it. Does that break (b), or is the "almost" in "almost any" doing all the work, and if it is, do I still get to keep the doom-ish conclusion in (c) and (e)?

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, rank the real ones by how bad they are, cheapest fix for each and what it costs me, and then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most.
