I'm in the third year of a philosophy degree and am currently taking a graduate-level course on decision theory and formal epistemology; the assessment is a 6,000-word term paper due at the end of the semester, and I'd like to use it to convert a half-formed worry of mine into a proper proposal. The worry concerns the result — usually credited to Good — that the expected value of information is never negative: an agent who will go on to choose optimally cannot do worse in expectation by learning something than by not learning it. The standard defense is that the informed agent could always simply disregard what she has learned, since she retains every act that was open to her uninformed counterpart, and so one of her options is to behave exactly as she would have without the information. I have come to suspect that this defense establishes less than it is taken to establish, and that the theorem is more accurately read as a bookkeeping identity between two decision problems sharing a single prior and a single preference relation than as a normative claim about the rationality of seeking or shunning information. What I want the paper to do is prise apart those two readings and then ask which of them survives once we relax the assumptions the identity actually leans on: first, that learning alters the agent's beliefs without altering the set of acts available to her, and second, that it alters her beliefs without altering her preferences over outcomes. Cases of information avoidance — a patient who declines a predictive test, a diner who asks not to be told the calorie count — are ordinarily dispatched by saying that the theorem concerns only instrumental value and that such agents have belief-dependent utilities; I want to press on whether that reply genuinely dissolves the difficulty or merely redescribes it, and whether any version of the non-negativity result holds when preferences themselves are sensitive to what one has learned.

Could you turn this into a short research proposal? I need the central question stated in such a way that it has a determinate answer and it is clear when the answer would come out one way rather than the other; an account of why the existing discussions leave the question open rather than settling it; the method — what I would actually do across the semester, which should be conceptual and formal rather than empirical, since I expect to work with small two-act, two-state decision problems and to attempt either counterexamples to unrestricted non-negativity or proofs of suitably restricted versions; a description of what a positive result and a negative result would each look like, together with which considerations would decide between them; and an honest assessment of what would render this too large or too diffuse for a single term paper. Please leave out any bibliography or reading list — I will supply the citations myself — and I would prefer that the proposal refer to positions and arguments rather than to particular publications.
