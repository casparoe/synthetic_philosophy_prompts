Heres the setup. The discursive dilemma: a group decides something where two premises feed a conclusion. Say three reviewers on a committee deciding whether to accept a paper. Premise 1: is the method sound. Premise 2: is the result novel. Rule for acceptance: only if both. Reviewer A says sound yes, novel yes, accept. B says sound yes, novel no, reject. C says sound no, novel yes, reject. Majority on method: sound (A and B). Majority on novelty: novel (A and C). So the majority's own premises imply accept — but majority on the conclusion is reject (only A accepts). Every individual is perfectly consistent; the group isn't. So you can't keep all of: majority rule on each issue, collective consistency, and responsiveness to how people vote.

The standard escapes are premise-based voting (vote the two premises, let the conclusion fall out), conclusion-based voting (vote accept/reject, ignore the premises), or restricting which profiles you'll let in the door. I want to argue those are all dodges.

My actual claim: the mistake is demanding that the group output a single consistent judgment set at all. The real output of an aggregation is a distribution over judgment sets, and any talk of "what the group thinks" is just a lossy summary of that distribution. On this view the dilemma is an artifact of a bookkeeping choice, not a deep result.

A friend already told me that's cheating, because the committee still has to DO something — accept or reject the paper — and at that point you've collapsed back to one verdict and the dilemma is right back. My planned reply is that the collapse is a further decision made by a different procedure than the aggregation, so it isn't the same dilemma; the inconsistency lives in the summary, not in any act the group is forced to perform.
