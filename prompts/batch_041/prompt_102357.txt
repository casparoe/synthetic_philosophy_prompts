I'm a 4th year phil PhD (metaethics, drifting into population ethics) and I've somehow volenteered to run a 90-minute session on the procreation asymmetry for a mixed reading group -- mostly econ and bio grad students, a couple of lawyers, one very opinionated undergrad. They can all handle abstract argument and they've read a bit of utilitarian stuff, but none of them do population ethics. So I don't need the asymmetry explained to me, I need a teaching plan for it: the order the pieces have to go in so it actually lands, the one case I should build the whole session around and what that case has to have going for it (and if the obvious one is a trap, why), the misconceptions they'll walk in with or form halfway through and exactly where in the sequence I head each one off, and a single question I can throw at them at the end that tells me whether they've got it or just nodded along.

The thing I'm actually worried about: they'll all cheerfully agree with the asymmetry in the abstract -- obviously don't bring a miserable kid into the world, and no particular reason to bring a happy one -- and then the second they see it means that adding happy people isn't a good thing in itself, they'll quietly downgrade it to some conditional reading where it only counts once the person exists, and feel like they kept the intuition when they've actually gutted it. I want the arc to make that move feel like a loss rather than a save. And the bit I really want them to walk out with is the difference between the axiological version (adding a happy person doesn't make the world better) and the deontic one (there's no reason to add one) -- they conflat these constantly and I think it's half of why the whole thing feels slippery to them.

Two detours I don't have time for and keep having to shut down: the life-isn't-worth-living antinatalist stuff, and the repugnant-conclusion stuff. Tell me what I can leave out entirely and announce that I'm leaving out, and what I can't afford to skip even if it costs me time. I'd way rather they leave with one sharp thing than a fog of population-ethics trivia.
