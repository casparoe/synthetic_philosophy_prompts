I write a monthly ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and for the past year most of my reading has been about what a person should do when someone who seems as well placed as they are to get a question right ends up disagreeing with them. I have interviewed people on both sides of this, and the arguments have a strange quality: each camp's favourite case turns out to be the other camp's counterexample, and after twenty minutes the two of them are usually arguing about whether the other one even counts as a peer. I have room for a long piece and I do not want to write another survey that hands each camp a paragraph. What I want instead is the document the two sides should have produced years ago: draft the joint statement that a committed conciliationist — someone who holds that a disagreement with an equally well-placed interlocutor should move you toward their view — and a committed steadfast thinker, who holds that you may hold your ground, could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Organize it as follows. First, a numbered list of claims both would sign: what makes someone a peer for the purposes of the argument; what the disagreement is actually about, whether the first-order question, or the epistemology of disagreement, or both; which standard cases both agree are genuine disagreements and which both agree are only apparent; what each side takes the other's strongest argument to be; and whether either position is self-undermining when turned on the disagreement about disagreement itself — that is, whether a conciliationist must lower their confidence in conciliationism once they notice that steadfast thinkers disagree with them, and whether a steadfast thinker can consistently refuse to budge while conceding that the other side may refuse too. Second, the residual disagreement, with each side's position written in wording its own adherents would accept rather than in the form its opponents prefer to attack. Third, the cruxes — my guess is that one is whether a peer's reliability can ever be assessed without leaning on the very reasoning under dispute, and another is whether repeatedly revising toward a peer's view has any stable stopping point or merely dissolves your opinion into theirs by increments. For each crux, name a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Three cases keep coming up in my interviews and nobody has a ready answer for any of them: two agents who share all their evidence, know each other's credences, and know they are equally reliable, one of whom has credence 0.7 in the claim and the other 0.3; a disagreement in which the very thing at issue is which of the two is the more reliable judge, so that neither can tell whether the other is a peer without first settling the question they are disagreeing about; and a disagreement that survives one round of mutual concession, where the question is what the second and third rounds are supposed to look like. I would also like the statement to say, if it is true, which part of the quarrel is verbal rather than substantive — for instance, whether one side's ought concerns what you may believe and the other's concerns what you may do, or whether the two sides are working with different standards for when a disagreement counts as one between peers. Make it accurate enough that both camps recognize themselves in it, and neither can complain that I let the other side write its half.
