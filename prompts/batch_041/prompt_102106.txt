I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on formal systems and the limits of mechanical reasoning; we use Gödel, Escher, Bach as a spine but spend most of our time working problems, and I am now writing the take-home portion of the final. I want its centerpiece to be a question on the MU-puzzle and on what Hofstadter calls jumping out of the system. My students have all met the puzzle before, in the weak sense that they have been told MU cannot be derived and can recite the slogan that you have to step outside the system to see why; what they cannot do is reproduce the reasoning, or say precisely what kind of move the stepping out is, or explain why it is a move a purely rule-following agent might not be able to make. So that the question is answerable with no reference material at all, here is the system exactly as I hand it to them: the only axiom is MI; the symbols are M, I, and U; and the four rules are (1) a string ending in I may have a U appended, (2) a string beginning with M may have everything after the M doubled, (3) any occurrence of III may be replaced by a single U, and (4) any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The question should force three things that a summary cannot supply: an actual proof that MU is not derivable, obtained by finding a property that every derivable string possesses and MU lacks, with a demonstration that the property is preserved by each of the four rules and holds of the axiom; an account of why the mere failure of however many attempted derivations is no evidence for the claim and of what sort of argument does establish it; and a characterization of the jumping-out move itself, namely what is being jumped, why the proof of non-derivability must live outside the system, and whether the capacity to jump is one that a machine which only applies rules could ever have. Pitch it at students who are capable but have never seen the invariance argument.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 to 900 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 5 marks each, for 25 in total, and for each point add a one-line note on how students characteristically get it wrong; I have found that the invariance proof and the jumping-out discussion each have their own distinctive failure modes and I want them kept separate. In the model answer I especially want the treatment of the trivial-looking case handled honestly rather than waved through, and I want it made explicit which step of the preservation argument is doing the real work. Please end your reply with a final line beginning with the word Crux: followed by a single sentence naming the one move that separates an A answer from a B answer.
