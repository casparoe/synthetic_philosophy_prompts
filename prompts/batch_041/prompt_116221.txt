Hey — hoping you can do me a favour. I'm in this small reading group (half philosophers, half entomologists, long story) and we're doing a session on pest control, and I've got a draft I want torn apart before I inflict it on them. I'd like your comments on the draft below. It's about insecticides/rodenticides/slug pellets and the ethics of all that, but I've tried to keep the factual stuff vague on purpose so the philosophy has to carry it.

Draft, working title "The Invisible Million":

1. Most arguments about pest control start with the pest's moral status — can an aphid be wronged, does a wasp have a welfare, that sort of thing. I want to bracket all that. Grant, arguendo, that the pest matters, or at least don't settle it here. My claim is that even if we grant the pest full standing, the practice of pest control has a structural feature that the individual-status debate can't reach.

2. The feature: pest control is set up so that individual harms can't be registered. A spray is licensed by a field trial with a mortality rate, not by any decision about a particular beetle. The unit of justification is the population, and the individual isn't just unconsidered, it's unrepresentable within the practice. Call this designed invisibility. Contrast a lab animal: a protocol, a number, a named harm. A pest gets a kill rate.

3. That gives me a distinction I find useful. There's the harm question (how bad is it for the insect) and the authorization question (what sort of thing may be done to an individual without any procedure addressed to that individual). Animal ethics spends nearly everything on the first and almost nothing on the second, and the second is where pest control is distinctive. Predation is the obvious comparison; the disanalogy is that nobody authorized the predator, whereas we authorize the spray.

4. Two consequences. First, the usual move that it's necessary to feed people answers the harm question and leaves the authorization question exactly where it was — it's a justification at the level of populations that never asks whether the individual beetle was owed anything. Second, a technology that removes bycatch doesn't make the practice less troubling, it makes it more legible: if we had a spray that killed exactly one target species and nothing else, the authorization problem would stand out in high relief, because we could no longer say the harm was an unavoidable side effect of something else.

5. A test I've been calling the single-insect test. Take something you do routinely to a million unindividuated insects — spray a rose bush, put down bait, vacuum out a wasp nest — and ask whether you'd do the same thing, under the same justification, to one insect you can see and identify and whose life history you know. If the answer is no, what does the no show? Not that the million are more important, and not that the one is. It shows the practice's justification isn't doing the work it appears to do, because it can't survive individuation. I think that's a real argument and not just squeamishness, but I'd like to be told if it's just squeamishness.

6. Where I think I'm vulnerable: (a) I say designed invisibility, but a lot of it is ordinary cognitive economy, and maybe nothing follows from the fact that a practice is structured a certain way; (b) someone will say authorization questions are about the legitimacy of institutions, and pest control mostly isn't institutional, it's a homeowner with a spray can; (c) push the authorization point hard and it seems to condemn any practice that acts on populations, including vaccination programmes and traffic law, which is absurd.

What I want from you:
