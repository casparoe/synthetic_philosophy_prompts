I need a dialogue written for a reading group next month, and I want to keep it off the standard establishment-versus-separation script, so I'm making the setup up instead of using a real country.

The made-up country: it currently has an established church — the head of state is nominally its head, its bishops sit in the upper house, its clergy are paid out of general revenue — and, alongside that, a payroll levy that the tax office collects on behalf of any registered religious body and hands over to it. A commission is now drafting a reform with two halves. First half: abolish the establishment, bishops out of the upper house, no more general-revenue salaries, head of state cut loose. Second half: keep the levy and widen it, so that any registered worldview association, religious or secular, can collect it — humanist and atheist groups would be in. Enrolment is automatic: you start out enrolled in whatever your parents were enrolled in, and you can switch or leave at any point. The wrinkle I want them to really dig into is this: leaving the levy doesn't get you your money back. Your share just goes to a neutral public culture fund instead. So opting out is a redirection, not a saving. There's also an opt-out friction problem — as drafted you have to file the form in person, once a year, at a district office.

The two characters are both on the commission and both good at this. One drafted the reform and thinks it's the first arrangement that's actually neutral: it treats belief and unbelief identically, it strips the old church of its privileges, and it gives people a say over where a slice of their taxes goes. The other voted against it and thinks it's a trick: the state is still collecting by coercion, auto-enrolment inflates the old church's numbers and therefore its political weight, and the moment the state has to decide which bodies qualify as worldview associations it's back to adjudicating religion, only now through a tax code. They're having a drink after the hearing.

Write the dialogue. Real back and forth — they should pick up each others actual points and press on the weak spots, no strawmen, and nobody winning by being smug. Things I'd like them to hit: the who-counts-as-a-worldview problem, wether auto-enrolment at birth is a fair basis for a levy, the redirect-to-a-fund rule, and what happens to the old church's buildings and endowments once the establishment goes. Don't let it turn into a debate about whether religion does good in the world, that's not the interesting question here. 1500–2000 words, something you could read aloud in one sitting.
