For the better part of forty years I measured rates of chemical reaction, and I never once doubted that the dispositions I attributed to my samples — that this alloy would crack under that load, that this solution would conduct once that salt was added — were as real as the samples themselves. Since retiring I have been reading metaphysics, and I have discovered that philosophers have made a great deal of trouble for precisely that assumption. A thing can be arranged so that the arrival of the triggering circumstance destroys the basis of the disposition at the very moment it would have manifested; or the basis can survive intact while an interfering condition blocks the route from basis to outcome; or the outcome can be produced by something else altogether, in a thing that never had the disposition to begin with. I take these to be three genuinely different ways for appearance and reality to come apart, and I gather that a good deal of effort has gone into keeping them apart.

What I want from you is a short story, of roughly two thousand words, that makes that difference matter to a reader who has never heard of it, without ever explaining it.

Requirements, which I would like you to follow closely:

1. The protagonist is trying to find out whether he has a certain trait of character — courage, honesty, loyalty, whichever you judge most tractable — and he has devised a procedure for finding out. The procedure is the heart of the story, and it must be mundane: a regimen, a wager, a standing arrangement, a test he administers to himself. No invented technology, and no thought experiment translated into literal machinery.

2. The procedure must be self-defeating in a specific way. It guarantees that the circumstances in which the trait would show itself will arise, and in guaranteeing this it also guarantees that the trait cannot show itself. He knows this and proceeds anyway.

3. The story must leave it genuinely undecidable, and not merely unstated, whether what happened to him was the loss of the basis of the trait, the obstruction of an intact basis, or the production of the appearances by something else. Arrange matters so that all three accounts predict exactly the same observations. Do not settle it at the end; finish on an action or a scene.

4. Include a second character whose conduct is indistinguishable from the manifestation of the same trait, and about whom the protagonist has reason to suspect that nothing corresponding to the trait is there at all. This character is not a villain and is not explained.

5. The condition that blocks the protagonist's trait must itself be a disposition of his — his caution, his pride, his tenderness — so that the reader cannot tell whether the trait was replaced or merely obstructed.

6. The words disposition, fink, mask, and counterfactual must not appear anywhere in the story, and no character may deliver the interpretation: no therapist, no confessor, no wise friend. I want the reader to arrive at the problem from the events alone.

Give the story a title, and stop when it is over. I do not want a commentary or an explanation appended.
