I'm a PhD student who spends half the week in a machine-learning reading group and half in a philosophy of science seminar, and lately one sentence has been doing an enormous amount of work in both rooms: there is no such thing as a free lunch. In the reading group it surfaced when a visiting student presented a new architecture as a step toward a genuinely general-purpose learner; someone cut her off with the no-free-lunch theorem, saying it settles the matter, since any method that gains on one class of problems must give the gains back on the rest. In the seminar, a classmate invoked the same sentence to close a discussion of Hume, claiming the theorem is Hume's point made rigorous — that expecting the future to resemble the past is a free lunch, and there is no such thing, so inductive inference is unjustified. Then a labmate, overhearing, said the phrase just means nothing comes for nothing, which made the other two uses look like mystification. My suspicion is that these people are not asserting the same proposition at all, and that at least one of them has the slogan doing work it cannot do; what I can't do is lay the options out clearly enough to say which.

Could you give me a menu of the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely strong? For each reading I'd like to know exactly what is being asserted — what is being averaged over what, who is supposed to be paying for the lunch, and whether the claim ranges over all methods and all problems or only over ours — and then a verdict: whether it is true, false, or empty; whether it is a mathematical result, a philosophical thesis, or merely an aphorism dressed as one; and whether it is the kind of thing that could be proved, or only assumed. Please separate the readings that people habitually run together: that no method is a priori better than any other, that any advantage in one place is repaid elsewhere, that a method must carry some inductive bias to learn anything at all, and that nothing can be learned in the end. I would also like to know what extra premise each of my three interlocutors would need in order to get from the slogan to the conclusion they drew with it, and which reading each of them most likely had in mind.

Finally, tell me which of the readings would actually make the slogan interesting rather than a truism about trade-offs, what would have to be true about the distribution of real-world problems for the strong reading to hold, and whether any of the readings are really restatements of one another rather than rivals. If the honest answer is that the sentence is true on every reading that anyone could defend and that the disagreements were about something else entirely, say so plainly, and tell me what the something else was.
