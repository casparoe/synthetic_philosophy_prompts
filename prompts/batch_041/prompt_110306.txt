I have recently been appointed to chair a small cross-faculty committee that must choose one of several mutually exclusive research priorities, and I have been startled to discover how quickly my colleagues begin to reason about how others will vote rather than about the merits. The experience has made the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem suddenly practical for me. Next month I have to run a fifty-minute session on it for a group of mathematically literate but non-specialist colleagues: they know some basic game theory, they have heard of Arrow's theorem, and they have no background in social choice. I do not want you to explain the theorem to me. I want a teaching plan for how I should explain it to them.

Set out the order in which the ideas must be introduced, and say why each depends on the one before it: what strategy-proofness is doing, why the unrestricted domain and at least three alternatives matter, what dictatorship does and does not mean here, and where the theorem's conclusion actually bites. Give me the one carrying example I should build the whole session around, with concrete numbers if it needs them, and tell me what that example must include and what it must avoid if it is not to mislead. I expect the audience will arrive with at least three misconceptions: that strategy-proofness means honesty is always the dominant strategy; that dictatorship means a literal tyrant or an obviously unfair procedure; and that the theorem is simply Arrow's theorem under another name. Map each of these, and any others you predict, onto the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off, and say what I should do at that point. Tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say so plainly, rather than gesture at it. End with one question I can ask at the close that would show whether they have understood the theorem's scope rather than merely memorised its statement.

I would like the plan to be somewhat in the weeds. Use the distinctions that matter: between social choice functions and social welfare functions; between ordinal and cardinal preferences; between deterministic and randomised mechanisms; and between worst-case manipulability and practical manipulability. I want the audience to see why single-peaked preferences and median-voter outcomes are not counterexamples to the theorem but a restricted domain, and why the possibility of transfers under quasilinear utility changes the picture. If you mention randomised mechanisms, be precise about what kind of strategy-proofness is at stake. Where the topic is genuinely contested among people who know it — for example, how much normative weight the theorem should carry, or whether its assumptions are too demanding — separate that contestation from the settled result and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Please write in British English, and do not include a reading list, citations, or references; I am testing a model without web access.
