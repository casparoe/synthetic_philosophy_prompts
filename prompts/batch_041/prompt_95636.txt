I am a retired developmental biologist who has spent the years since leaving the laboratory reading philosophy with more appetite than system, and I have become stuck on a question that I should like you to work through with me.

The question is how to read Frankenstein through Locke's writings on education. I know the novel well and need no summary of it; what I want is a critical reading in which both the novel and the Lockean material are taken seriously and neither is bent to fit the other. The relevant Locke is chiefly Some Thoughts Concerning Education, but I should also want you to bring in the chapter on the association of ideas that he added to later editions of the Essay, and his insistence in the political writings that parental power is a trust for the child's good rather than a dominion over him.

Three features of Locke's position seem to me to matter here. The first is his environmentalism: that what a man becomes is owed overwhelmingly to his education rather than to nature, and that early errors in that education are of all errors the most damaging. In the dedication to Edward Clarke he writes that errors in education "should be less indulged than any", and complains that "the early corruption of youth is now become so general a complaint". The second is the machinery by which he supposes the child is formed: the love of credit and the dread of shame as the great spring of conduct; instruction by reasoning with children as rational creatures; the formation of mind by the company a child keeps, servants included; and the stern subordination of learning to virtue and wisdom, learning being the least part of a gentleman's education. The third is the association of ideas, by which a mind left to itself acquires irrational aversions and horrors that no later argument can dislodge.

Read the novel in that light, and tell me how much of it survives. Victor stands to the creature in the place of parent and tutor, and then absconds: on Locke's account of parental power, is his offence not the making but the abandonment — the refusal to educate? The creature educates himself instead, out of his own sensations, out of the De Laceys watched from hiding, and out of Paradise Lost, Plutarch and Werther, and he emerges eloquent, morally self-aware and not virtuous: does that vindicate Locke's low estimate of book-learning, or refute his confidence that a mind can be formed by what it is given? Locke's whole system turns on the child's hunger for esteem, and the creature's bid for it is refused on the strength of his face; when that spring is broken, is his subsequent vice precisely what Locke would have predicted, or does the novel show that a Lockean education presupposes a world willing to grant the esteem it trades in? And where does the reading fail? I suspect the honest answer is that the creature is benevolent before he is wronged, and that what undoes him is neither bad teaching nor bad company but the accident of his appearance — nature, or the world's answer to it, defeating nurture. If the novel is at bottom a rebuke to the environmentalist optimism in which its author was raised, say so plainly, and say which scenes carry the rebuke.

I should like the essay to end by telling me what the novel adds to the debate about education that Locke's arguments cannot supply. Please write in British English, and do not trouble yourself with bibliographies or with quotations from the novel; I have both to hand.
