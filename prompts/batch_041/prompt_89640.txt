A widely read popular book on nationalism argues roughly as follows. Here is its central argument as well as I can reconstruct it, and I would like it audited rather than admired:

"Nations are not ancient communities that grew organically out of a shared language and a shared past; they are modern constructions. Hobsbawm demonstrated that the ceremonial trappings of nationhood — flags, anthems, mass oaths, the elaborate pageantry of late nineteenth-century monarchies — were deliberately manufactured to bind the loyalty of populations uprooted by industrialization, and that the age-old customs nationalists invoke are in nearly every case recent fabrications. Anderson then explained why so many people believed in these fabrications: print capitalism allowed a reading public to imagine itself as a community of strangers. An imagined community, in his sense, is one that exists only in the minds of those who believe in it, with no reality outside them. Since both authors show that national identity is a fiction, appeals to national tradition have no authority, and we would be better off without nations."

I am not a specialist, and I cannot tell where the seam falls between the defensible and the indefensible. My own guess is that the middle of the passage is broadly right and that the last sentence overreaches, but I would rather be told than left guessing.

Please go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a historian working on nationalism would accept as stated, what they would regard as a forgivable simplification, and what is simply wrong. As part of that:

- Separate the distinct theses the passage runs together — that nations are modern, that particular national traditions are recent, that national identity is "imagined," and that national identity is therefore illegitimate — and say which of them each author actually holds.
- Say which claims in the passage belong to Hobsbawm, which to Anderson, which to neither (and to whom they do belong), and where the two disagree with each other.
- Flag any term the passage uses in its everyday sense that the scholars used in a technical one.
- Say whether the modernist premise the book assumes is itself uncontested, or whether serious historians place nations, or at least their ethnic raw material, considerably earlier.
- Say whether the concluding inference survives once the rest is corrected, and if it does not, exactly which step fails.

Then consider a case the passage never discusses. In my grandmother's village there is a harvest festival that everyone treats as immemorial — the songs, the costumes, the route of the procession. A local archivist showed me records suggesting that the festival in its present form was assembled in 1936 by a district teachers' association as a patriotic pageant, drawing on a few half-remembered harvest customs. My cousins conclude that the festival is fake and that nobody should care about it. Does the corrected version of the book's argument support them? What would Hobsbawm and Anderson each say about a case like this, and does it matter to the festival's standing that the villagers now experience it as something inherited?

Finally, tell me whether inventedness is better treated as all-or-nothing or as a matter of degree, and what evidence would settle the question for a particular tradition.

Please write in American English. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need to know which sentences I can repeat and which I should drop.
