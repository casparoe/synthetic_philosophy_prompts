I'm producing an episode of a political-thought podcast, and my guest next month is a comparative political theorist whose work traces how the word "liberal" and its cognates drifted apart across the Atlantic; her thesis is that the divergence is not a labeling accident but tracks two genuinely different political projects that now happen to share a name. I want eight to ten interview questions that get past the stock observation that "liberal" means left-of-center in America and something else in Europe, and that actually press the substance of her claim.

Please make sure the set includes, at minimum: a question on the economic axis — why parties that call themselves liberal in Europe (the German Free Democrats, the Dutch VVD, the Danish Venstre) sit on the market-friendly center-right, while the American "liberal" is bound up with the welfare state and civil rights; a question on the translation problem, namely what a nineteenth-century continental liberal actually meant and whether any of that older sense survives; a question on the "neoliberal" epithet, which in European usage functions mostly as an accusation rather than a self-description; and a question on whether "social liberalism" names a coherent position or merely straddles the two traditions. I'd also like one question that forces the conceptual issue rather than the historical one: whether the split is best understood as a disagreement over which of two faces of liberalism to foreground — liberty as restraint on state power versus liberty as something the state is used to secure — or whether that framing is itself an American import.

For the two or three questions where I expect my guest to deflect with a stock move — I anticipate she will retreat to "it's all about local party-system incentives" — write the follow-up that makes that answer do real work, so she can't use it to change the subject. End with a question about what her thesis would look like if it were wrong: what evidence from party programs, ordinary usage, or intellectual history would count against the claim that these are two distinct projects rather than one tradition wearing two labels.

Separately from the questions, I'd like a short self-critical note on where the reasoning behind them is most likely to fail — which historical or conceptual claims about the divergence I should not stake the episode on — and what would change your mind about it. Please write everything in American English, and keep the questions tight enough to be asked aloud.
