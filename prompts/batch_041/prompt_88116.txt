I teach a third-year undergrad seminar on Wittgenstein and the philosophy of language, 15 students, one 90-min session, and I want to run something that makes them feel the family-resemblance point from the inside instead of me just telling them there's no one feature common to all games. The textbook version is easy and they've half-heard it already. The part I actually want them to hit is the harder one: once you say the category has no common core, just overlapping similarities, you've got a problem, because resemblance by itself over-generates — you can chain from chess to a boxing match to a street fight to a rock, naming a shared respect at every step — so resemblance can't be what's doing the excluding. Something else has to be doing it, and for Wittgenstein that's use, the practice, the language game. I want them to run into that wall themselves and have to reach for use/practice, and I don't want to say the words family resemblance out loud until the debrief.

Can you design the whole thing for me? Rules, materials, the timeline inside the 90 minutes, some kind of score or payoff so it's actually a game and not a worksheet, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the philosophy. A few constraints:

- No front-loaded lecture. They should be building and defending a definition before they know where it's going.
- Use a concrete concept with cards or tokens. I'd rather it not be the usual chess/solitaire/Olympics list — give me fresh everyday and contemporary cases (speedruns, LARP, a rigged casino machine, kids playing house, whatever) so they can't pattern-match the example.
- It has to include a deliberate spoiler move where a chain of resemblance walks from the in pile straight into an out item, so they see that the resemblance story needs something else bolted onto it.
- End on a genuinely contested borderline case they have to rule on, where reasonable people split, and make them notice that no further feature settles it.
- 15 people, so it needs to work with the whole room, not in pairs only.

Then be definately honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and tell me what this activity can't reproduce — which part of the actual view I'll still have to supply myself, and where the same data could just as well be explained by some rival picture (a prototype account, or just a big disjunctive definition) so they don't walk out thinking they've proved more than they have.
