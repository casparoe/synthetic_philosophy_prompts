I do reinforcement learning for a living — MDPs, value functions, Q-values, policies, softmax action selection, epsilon-greedy, argmax tie-breaking, regret bounds — and I have basicaly no philosophy. I keep running into the old puzzle people call Buridan's ass, and I want you to translate the whole thing into my vocabulary and then tell me what got lost.

Here's what I want translated, as plainly as I can put it. I don't remember who said what, so please don't hang anything on names or texts — work from the claims themselves.

1. An agent facing two options it regards as exactly equal in every respect has no reason to prefer either.
2. If acting requires a reason, and there's no reason to go left rather than right, the agent can't go either way, so it stands there and starves. The standard reading seems to be that this is a reductio of something in the setup, not a claim that asses starve.
3. One escape: the will is not a mechanism that has to follow the balance of reasons. It can simply choose, with no preference to go on, and that capacity — choosing where nothing favors either side — is arguably where freedom actually lives, rather than in choosing what you already want most.
4. Another escape: the will can suspend the choice and shift its attention, so that it comes to see one option as more salient or attractive and then chooses with a preference. The preference isn't manufactured out of nothing; it's grown by an act of attention.
5. A test case I like: two agents identical in every respect — same history, same information, same tie. One goes left, the other goes right. If claim 3 is right, the difference came from each agent's will, and there is nothing in the situation to explain it.

Now do the translation. Give me each of 1 through 5 restated as precisely as you can in my terms — states, actions, Q-values, policies, deterministic versus stochastic action selection, exploration, tie-breaking, the cost of deliberation, whatever fits — one at a time, not blended into a mush.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each claim: what survives the translation cleanly, what survives only if you fudge it, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. And for the residue, give me a verdict in each case: is this something my framework is missing, i.e. a real phenomenon RL can't represent, or is it a sign the original claim was doing something confused that the translation just exposes?

Some specific things I want an opinion on:

- Is "the will can choose without a preference" expressible as a policy at all, or does any policy in my sense already presuppose something the medievals would call a reason?
- In my world, argmax over tied Q-values is broken by an arbitrary rule — lowest index, first in the list, whatever — and nobody thinks that's freedom. Is that a translation of claim 3 or a change of subject? If it changes the subject, say exactly where.
- Does claim 2 come out as anything more than "the agent's policy is a partial function, undefined on ties," the ass as a program that hangs? If that's the honest translation, does anything of the original worry survive it?
- Claim 5: identical policies in identical states give identical actions unless you inject randomness from outside the agent — a seed, a noise source. So the divergence comes from outside. Does that make claim 3 incoherent, or does it mean the medieval notion of a will as the source of the tie-break has no counterpart in a framework where the source of randomness is by definition not the agent?
- And is there anything in the medieval picture — that a choice made for no reason is still yours, that responsibility attaches to it anyway, maybe even that a gratuitous choice is more yours rather than less — that my vocabulary simply cannot say?
