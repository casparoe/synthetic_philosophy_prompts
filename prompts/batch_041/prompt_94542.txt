You are a philosopher of practical rationality who also writes satire, and you are careful about the difference between a genuine fallacy and a heuristic that merely misfires. Write a piece of sustained deadpan parody: a mock self-help column — the kind that appears in an in-flight magazine or a corporate wellness newsletter — together with a short author bio.

The columnist is a "decision-hygiene coach" who has built a modest brand on a single rule: the past is a sunk cost, and a clear-headed agent never lets it weigh on a present decision. The joke is that he is right more often than the genre usually permits, and wrong in precisely the ways the decision-theory literature would predict. Let the humor come from getting the theory right, not from inventing it.

Requirements:

- The column must contain three worked examples drawn from the coach's own practice.
- At least one example must be a case where ignoring the past is genuinely correct — the ordinary bad-money-after-good scenario, presented as a triumph.
- At least one must be a case where the coach's rule, applied honestly, licenses an outcome he would rather not own: treating his past commitments as weightless makes his future behavior predictable, and therefore exploitable, by anyone who has learned his rule.
- At least one must be a case where honoring the past is defensible — not because the past costs themselves matter, but because the act of honoring them functions as a commitment device that reshapes his future incentives, so that a rigid rule-follower outperforms a case-by-case optimizer.
- Stay deadpan throughout: no winking, no authorial nudges, no "of course, this is a simplification."
- Name no theories, theorists, or studies, and include no citations, footnotes, or reading suggestions. A reader who knows the literature should be able to label each example unprompted; a reader who does not should find the piece funny and then faintly uneasy.
- Open with the coach's rule as a standalone pull-quote, and close with a one-line brand tagline that becomes funnier the more of the theory you bring to it.

Maximum 500 words, including the pull-quote, the bio, and the tagline. Think through your three examples before you write, so that each one lands its theoretical point cleanly.
