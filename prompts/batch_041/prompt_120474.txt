"Everyone thinks the debate about whether socialism can work was settled by the Soviet collapse, but the real debate happened in the 1930s, Mises and Hayek on one side and Lange on the other. Mises started it by saying that without private ownership of capital you can't have real market prices, and without prices you can't know what anything costs, so rational planning is flat out impossible. Hayek then made the stronger version of the point: no central planner could ever gather all the information about what people want and what's scarce, because that knowledge is scattered across millions of heads, so the planner would have to collect it all in one place, which can't be done. The price system is basically a giant machine for collecting all that scattered knowledge and funneling it to the center, which is why markets work at all. Lange replied that this is a technical problem, not a fundamental one. A Central Planning Board can just set prices by trial and error, nudging them up and down until supply and demand match, and tell factory managers to keep producing until marginal cost equals price. Do that and you get the same outcome as a competitive market, and if a price comes out wrong you just adjust it again next period. Hayek's answer to that was basically that bureaucrats would be lazy and wouldn't have the right incentives, which is an argument about motivation, not about whether the thing works in theory. So Lange won the theoretical round and Hayek retreated to saying it wouldn't work in practice. And now even the practical objection is dead, because Amazon knows what you want before you do and computers solve millions of equations a second, so the information problem Hayek was worried about just isn't there anymore. The USSR failed because it was a dictatorship with terrible incentives, not because planning is impossible. Honestly the same logic shows every big company is already a little socialist country inside."
