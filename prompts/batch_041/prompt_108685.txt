I serve on the editorial board of a university press that is commissioning a two-volume world history survey for undergraduates, and in three weeks we have to settle a question we have already deferred twice: what the architecture of the volumes should be. The series editor wants the past told from everywhere at once — eight synchronic slices, each taking the whole inhabited world at a single date, on the argument that only a simultaneous cross-section lets every society appear as the contemporary of every other, rather than as a latecomer on somebody else's timeline. Our chair thinks this is unteachable: students need causal narrative, exam questions have to be comparable across sections, and the regional specialists we have under contract can only write the chapters they were trained to write. A third group rejects both proposals, insisting that any chronological spine, however global its contents, imports a European periodization at the joints — that medieval and early modern do not travel cleanly outside Europe, that civilization as a unit of narration reifies societies that were always porous and internally divided, and that the real units are processes (a religion's spread, a commodity chain, a fiscal apparatus, a disease regime) that begin and end on no schedule and so cannot be dropped into a slice or a chapter without distortion.

I teach the second half of the survey myself, so I am not a neutral party, and I find both complaints compelling in ways that seem to pull against each other. The synchronic plan buys universality at the price of explanation, and I suspect it does not actually escape a standpoint: someone chooses the dates, and the dates do the arguing. A colleague points out that if the decisive divergence in living standards between Western Europe and China arrives around 1800 and turns on coal and New World resources rather than on culture or institutions, then a slice placed at 1750 and another at 1850 will tell a story nobody intended. The civilization-by-civilization plan buys narrative at the price of exactly the ranking the series exists to avoid, and the process-based plan buys neither, since someone must still decide which processes get chapters and in what order. Two disputes we keep circling: whether the slave trade belongs to an Atlantic unit or to a global one, and whether a chapter that sets the Qing, the Ottomans, Bourbon France, and Dahomey side by side treats them as commensurable or insults them by pretending that they are. One of us says that refusing to compare them is the old trick of denying that they were coeval; I think that is right and also that the comparison is usually a category mistake.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one architecture — or a hybrid, if you can specify the hybrid precisely enough to be built — give the reasoning in terms the series editor, the chair, and the dissenters can each respond to, and set out criteria that volume editors and section authors can apply when a topic's placement is contested, criteria that would at least discipline the slave-trade and Qing-versus-Ottoman questions above. It should state plainly what the recommended structure costs us: which questions it will make unaskable and which readers it will lose. Give the strongest version of the case against your own recommendation, not a straw man, and say what would have to be true for that case to win. End with a motion we can vote on and a date by which the series editor must deliver a revised outline and a sample chapter.
