I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've talked myself into a position about AI oversight that I want attacked. The position: a judge that is weaker than the systems it is judging can still reliably extract the truth from them if they are forced to argue against each other, so debate is a real path to overseeing models smarter than we are -- we don't have to be able to check the answers ourselves. The concrete version I care about is this: two copies of the same model, prompted to take opposite sides, argue in front of a smaller judge from an earlier checkpoint, and the judge picks a winner. What would count against that, ranked by how much it should actually cost me? I want the formal results about what a judge of that kind can and cannot extract, the experiments that would settle it, and the conceptual objections -- for instance, that the honest debater must already know the answer, that the debaters share the judge's blind spots because they share its training data and architecture, or that the setup optimizes for persuading the judge rather than for being right. Tell me what defenders of debate would themselves concede, and whether the position has been hedged with idealized judges and unlimited rounds until nothing could count against it. If a single experiment would most embarrass me, say which one.
