Two friends of mine, both amateur translators, spent most of a dinner party arguing about a single translation problem, and I have not been able to settle it in my own mind. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange point by point.

The problem is this. A short poem is written in a language other than English. The first letters of its lines spell the name of the person the poem addresses, and that name is also an ordinary noun in that language, so the hidden name doubles as a word belonging to the poem's imagery. The poem also rhymes in a strict scheme and holds to a fixed syllable count per line, and its closing rhyme is a near-homophone of the name, so the hidden name and the spoken rhyme echo each other. It is to be translated into English for a bilingual edition.

Ana began. A translation of this poem, she said, must keep the acrostic. The name hidden down the left margin is not decoration; the poem's whole gesture is that the addressee is at once named and not named, present in the first letters and absent from the surface sense. If the English lines do not spell her name, the reader is not reading this poem but a summary of what it says. And because the name is also a common noun, the acrostic is a second semantic thread running beneath the first; cut it and you cut the poem in half.

Bruno replied that this confuses form with content. An acrostic works because the words of that language happen to begin with those letters; in English the words carrying the same sense begin with different letters. To spell her name, then, the translator must choose English words for their initials rather than their meanings, and the lines will end up saying something other than what the original says, and saying it worse. The honest solution is to render the sense as well as English allows and inform the reader in a note that the original is an acrostic. A version that keeps the shape and loses the sense, he said, is not a translation but a forgery.

Ana answered that a note is an admission of failure. The sense Bruno wants to preserve is not detachable from the form: the point of the poem is that the name is simultaneously hidden and disclosed, that reading down the margin delivers what the poem is about. A note tells the reader that a trick exists; it does not let the reader undergo it. Besides, she said, not all constraints are equally hard. Had the constraint been that no word may exceed five letters, an English translator could often keep it at little cost. The acrostic is a hard case, not evidence that form is inessential in general.

Bruno said that she then owes a principle. When is a constraint part of the meaning and when is it incidental? Rhyme, syllable count, acrostic, lipogram, a text that reads the same backward—by her reasoning every formal feature is part of the meaning, and in the limit nothing is translatable. What she is really doing, he said, is deciding in advance what the poem truly is and then calling her decision the poem.

Ana replied that the limit is not a reductio but a fact about some texts. When a text cannot be carried across, the honest course is to say so, or to offer a version that openly presents itself as a version—an arrangement rather than a performance of the original score—instead of pretending to fidelity. And Bruno's side smuggles in a theory of its own: that there is a language-independent sense which can be lifted out and re-dressed. If sense were separable in that way, one could render the poem as a paragraph of prose and call it the same poem.

Please work through the exchange and say who is right at each step, not who wins overall: which claims are correct, which are overstated, and where the two of them are talking past each other. Then say what you take to be the most important thing neither has noticed. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your adjudication, and single out the one point about which you are least sure.
