I've been keeping a list of verdicts about when a mandatory payment counts as theft, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Income tax is theft: the money is taken without my agreement, and my having voted and lost doesn't change that. A toll on a road I chose to take is not theft, even though I'd have grumbled paying it. A speeding fine is not theft, since I broke a rule I knew about. A penalty for not buying health insurance is theft, since I did nothing wrong -- I just declined to buy something. A passport renewal fee is not theft, even though I doubt it costs them that much to make the thing, because I want the passport and can walk away. Property tax is theft: I bought the land outright, and if I stop paying I lose something already mine. Two things bother me about this list. First, my reason for the income-tax verdict and my reason for the toll verdict look like the same reason pointing in opposite directions -- in both cases I benefit from what the money funds, and in both cases I could in principle rearrange my life to avoid paying -- so at least one verdict has to give. Second, it may be that "theft" in my mouth just means "a tax I happen to think is unjust," in which case the list is doing no work at all. Is there a principle that gets these verdicts, or close to it? Tell me what it implies for a tax on unrealized gains, for a tax whose revenue is earmarked for something I would have funded voluntarily anyway, for revenue the government raises simply by printing money, and for a fee charged by a private body that the state has empowered to compel payment. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and whether it matters that the whole thing seems to rest on a view about what the state is allowed to do that I haven't given you.
