I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works on epistemic logic, and I've come round to a view I now want you to try to break. The view is this: the so-called problem of logical omniscience is not a problem at all, and the whole industry devoted to patching it up — adding an awareness predicate to the models, admitting impossible worlds, giving agents a proof budget or a resource bound — is treating a feature of the formalism as a bug. My claim is that the K operator in a standard epistemic model was never meant to describe what is going on inside a thinker's head. It describes the informational situation an agent is in: what is settled, given what she has accepted and the logic she is committed to. Read that way, knowing every logical consequence of what you know is not an embarrassing idealisation but the correct description of a certain kind of state, and the air of paradox comes entirely from a decision to use the word 'knowledge' for it. I've been trying to make this precise with a distinction of my own: every agent has an acceptance set — the things she has actually taken on board — and a closure of that set under her logic, and I want to say that knowledge-talk is about the closure, while all our ordinary epistemic criticism, blame and praise is about the acceptance set. The mathematician who accepts the axioms of arithmetic but has not yet spotted a trivial consequence does not fail to know anything; she simply has not accepted the consequence yet, and that is a fact about her acceptance set, not about what she knows.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed this is. What would count against it — which cases, which formal results, which intuitions, and which ordinary ways of talking about knowledge? Sort them by how much they should move me, from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones I could concede without giving up the core of the view. I am especially interested in whether the position is drifting towards unfalsifiability: if every apparent counterexample is absorbed by the reply 'that is about acceptance, not knowledge', is there anything left that could refute it, and is that a cost or a virtue for a view of this kind? I would also like you to say which of the repairs I dismissed at the start are actually doing necessary work that my distinction cannot do, and where the closure/acceptance line predicts an intuition that turns out simply to be wrong.

Tell me what the strongest version of my view should concede as a genuine defeater, and what would have to be true for me to be wrong. I will say in advance what would move me most: a case where the distinction I am drawing is not merely coarse but actively misdescribes something we care about — where treating a failure of acceptance as irrelevant to knowledge makes a real epistemic difference disappear. Finish by saying what would change your own verdict, and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail.
