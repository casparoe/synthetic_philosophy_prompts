I run a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mind in which the final assignment is not a position paper but an adversarial collaboration: two students who hold opposing views must jointly produce a single document that both of them can sign. This term the pairing has fallen out along the oldest fault line in the field, the dispute over original versus derived intentionality, and I have promised to draft a model version of the genre myself so they can see what it is supposed to look like. One student takes the line associated with Dennett: intentionality is not a special possession that some systems have and others merely mimic, but rather what we ascribe when we adopt a stance that lets us predict and explain a system, so that when the stance works — as it does for the two-bitser, whose behavior we predict by saying it believes a coin is a quarter — the system really does have the beliefs in question and differs from us only in degree and complexity, the boundary between the original and the derived being drawn by the interpreter rather than discovered in the system. The other student takes the line associated with Searle, and having grown tired of the Chinese room, argues it afresh: derived aboutness is observer-relative, in that a sentence or a machine has it only because we assign it, whereas original aboutness is intrinsic to a mind and is conferred by no interpreter, and this difference is one of kind, so that no assembly of non-intentional parts, however reliably we can predict it by treating it as believing things, thereby comes to understand anything.

I want the document these two should have produced. Have them, as I have asked, test their formulations against a case that is neither of the textbook examples: a warehouse robot that, when one of its sensors is disabled, prints the sentence "I believe the part is missing" and reroutes to fetch a replacement — behavior the intentional stance predicts perfectly, and a self-report that appears to say something about the robot's own states. My students keep talking past each other about whether this case changes anything or merely restates the old quarrel, about whether the design stance (treating the robot as built to detect parts) can do all the explanatory work without any appeal to what it believes, and about whether part of their disagreement is really a question of what the word intentionality is meant to name. Give me the joint statement in this form: a numbered list of exactly five claims that both of them could sign, each one or two sentences, followed immediately by a table of exactly five residual disagreements, one to a row, whose columns give (a) the disagreement in wording that neither side could object to, (b) each side's own preferred formulation of its position, (c) the crux beneath it, and (d) a test, case, or argument that both sides agree would bear on that crux, together with what each side predicts it would show; for each row, add whether you judge the disagreement to be substantive or merely verbal, and, if merely verbal, what turns on the words. There should be no introduction and no conclusion — the document must stand on its own as something both students could put their names to. Write in American English.
