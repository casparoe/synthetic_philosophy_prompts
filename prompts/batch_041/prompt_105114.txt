I want an analysis of the Late Bronze Age collapse of the eastern Mediterranean palace societies—the wave of destructions and abandonments around 1200 BCE that took down the Hittite empire, the Mycenaean palace centres, and Ugarit, and that left Egypt diminished but intact—conducted through a framework I will state here, and which I want you to treat as a theory under test rather than as received wisdom.

The framework proposes that collapse is best defined not by population loss, de-urbanization, or the toppling of monuments, but by legibility. It distinguishes three outcomes. Collapse proper: the successor order cannot read the predecessor's record—its script, its accounts, its calendar, its titles—and therefore cannot inherit or contest its claims. Descent: the political centre weakens and scale falls, but the record remains readable and the successor continues it. Reorganization: a new centre takes over the same territory and population and reads the old record well enough to claim continuity with it. On this account, what we call the collapse of a civilization is really a failure of transmission, and the diagnostic question in every case is who came next and whether they could read.

Analyze the Late Bronze Age through this framework. Identify the successor orders region by region—the Aegean, Anatolia, the Levant, Egypt—and say which of the three outcomes each represents, answering the legibility question concretely: which scripts and archives survived, which went dark, and who could still read what. Then test the framework against a case it was not built for: the abandonment of the Classic Maya lowland cities in the ninth and tenth centuries, which we routinely call a collapse, and where the script in fact survived and was still being written centuries afterward. Does the framework force us to deny that the Maya collapsed, and is that a genuine insight or a reductio? Finally, say where the framework is silent—for instance in a society with no script at all—and what it would take to extend it without trivializing it.

I want the verdict to run in both directions: what the framework explains about 1200 BCE that a story of invasion or drought does not, and what the episode shows about the framework itself—whether the legibility criterion is a real discovery about what collapse is, or a redescription of what we already knew with a bias toward literate societies smuggled in.
