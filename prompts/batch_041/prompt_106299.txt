I write about math and ideas for a general-audience science outlet, and I'm doing a piece on the continuum hypothesis and why it refuses to sit still. A philosopher I interviewed handed me the argument below and I honestly can't tell if it's sharp or just sophistry, so I'm outsourcing the verdict.

(1) CH makes a perfectly definite claim: that every uncountable set of real numbers can be put in one-to-one correspondence with the reals themselves. (2) Our best theory of sets, ZFC, settles neither CH nor its negation -- from any model you can build another model where the answer flips. (3) A claim whose truth can be flipped just by moving to a different model isn't describing the mathematical universe; it's describing the model. (4) So CH has no truth value. (5) And this is the general rule: any statement independant of ZFC is neither true nor false, and calling a mathematical statement true just means it's provable in whatever system you settled on. (6) Which would mean there's no fact of the matter about whether ZFC is consistent, or whether ZFC plus some large cardinal is consistent, or about the sentences Godel taught us to build that say of themselves that they're unprovable. (7) But that's absurd -- a theory either is or isn't consistent, and the Godel sentence is true even though the theory can't prove it. (8) So (5) is wrong, and independence by itself can't rob CH of a truth value.

He thinks this exposes the multiverse crowd as confused. I'm not so sure, but I also can't say where his reasoning comes off the rails, or whether it even does. Which numbered step do I throw out, and what does throwing it out commit me to about the rest?
