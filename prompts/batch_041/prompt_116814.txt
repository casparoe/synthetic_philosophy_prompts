Two friends of mine went at each other for most of a six-hour drive and I was stuck in the back seat with no dog in the fight, so I could'nt really referee. I'd like you to do it instead. Take the role of a philosopher who works on well-being and the philosophy of desire, and think the whole thing through carefully before you commit to a verdict — be willing to say that one of them is just flatly wrong at a given step, and equally willing to say a step is fine.

Sam has been sitting a lot of long retreats lately and has a view he's taken to calling tranquilism: the only thing that ultimately matters is the absence of craving and suffering, full stop — not pleasure, not accomplishment, not any positive good. If there were a pill that erased all craving and left you flat but at peace, he says, that would be the best life available, and anything you'd miss about the highs is just the craving talking. Nadia is a grad student (not in ethics, she insists) and pushed back the entire drive. Roughly how it went:

(1) Sam: craving is itself a form of suffering — it's the itch — so getting rid of craving isn't a rival to getting rid of suffering, it's the very same project.

(2) Nadia: fine, but then your view says nothing at all about pleasure. If craving is a suffering, removing it is one entry on the list, and you still owe me an answer on whether pleasure is a good. And a life with no craving but also no pleasure doesn't obviously beat a life with some craving and a lot of joy in it.

(3) Sam: the joy you feel while craving is contaminated — it's the relief of scratching, not the pleasure of the thing itself. And if you could have the joy without the craving, you'd take it, which shows the joy was never the point; the quiet was.

(4) Nadia: 'contaminated' is doing all the work there. People who love climbing love the wanting-to-climb; that's not an impurity, that's the thing. And 'you'd take the joy without the craving' doesn't show the joy is worthless — it shows you'd rather have the good thing with less bad attached, which is exactly what a hedonist says. She also claims he's sliding between two senses of craving: the motivational state of wanting something, and the painful feeling of wanting it. If it's the painful feeling, then it's true but trivial that craving is a kind of suffering, and his view is just hedonism with a gloomy face; if it's the motivational state, then it's false that craving is always suffering, since absorbed, engaged wanting isn't painful at all.

(5) Sam: even granting all that, the difference is that every high is a loan taken out against a craving. The fact that everyone reaches for them doesn't show they're good, it shows everyone's thirsty.

Start by laying out each of their positions as strongly as you can, then for each of the five moves tell me whether it's right, wrong, or right but for a bad reason, and say briefly why. After that, say what the two of them are both missing — I have a suspicion there's a distinction neither of them has in hand but I can't put my finger on it. Please don't cop out with 'it depends on your theory of welfare'; if it does depend on that, say which theory makes which move come out true, and whether any of those theories is independently plausible.

Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just explain it to me the way you'd write it out in an email.
