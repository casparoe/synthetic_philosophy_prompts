I keep coming back to Quine's field-linguist example — the native who utters a single word whenever a rabbit scurries past, and the linguist who cannot tell whether the word names the rabbit, an undetached rabbit-part, or a momentary rabbit-stage — because the textbook tellings always let the linguist off too easily. The familiar escape is holistic: ask whether this is the same one as before, then adjust the translation of the identity vocabulary until the whole manual runs smoothly. I want a case where that escape is closed by the evidence rather than by stipulation, and I want it built to order. The case is for a post arguing that indeterminacy is not an artifact of a lazy or under-resourced fieldworker: it should survive even when the linguist is maximally diligent and the evidence is as rich as real fieldwork ever gets.

So please construct one, to these specifications. Pick a community and a recurring situation of your own — not the rabbit — and keep it mundane and realistic: foraging, weaving, a market stall, anything without exotic technology. Describe what the speakers do and say, including any particle or construction that looks like it might carry identity. Then give two rival translation manuals, M1 and M2, that fit every observation you have described; if there is any conceivable observation that would separate them, the case fails, and you should say so. The manuals must differ in the beliefs they attribute, not merely in the referent of one term — one should credit the speakers with enduring objects, the other with short-lived stages or with parts and a replacement relation — and they must assign different logical forms to whole sentences, not just different glosses for a noun. Crucially, the identity-looking particle must not break the tie: translating it as same should force compensating adjustments elsewhere in the manual, and translating it as more or again should force the opposite adjustments, so that the evidence never gains independent purchase on the question. This is the part I care most about, so exhibit the compensation explicitly in both directions.

Then do the checks. First, what does Quine's indeterminacy thesis say about your case, and does the indeterminacy survive or collapse? Second, what does a principle-of-charity interpreter say — does maximizing agreement or true belief select a manual, and if so which, and on what ground? Third, what does a causal or informational account of reference say — does the causal chain from world to utterance select a manual? I would like these last two to come apart: ideally charity is indifferent, or favors the manual that causal grounding rejects, while causal grounding selects the other, so that the two most popular determinacy-makers pull in different directions. If you find that no such case exists, tell me why, and show me the obstruction rather than fudging it. For each verdict, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens if I remove it — in particular, whether adding an independent way the speakers re-identify a token (a naming practice, a correction routine, a tally) collapses the indeterminacy or merely relocates it. Finish with the strongest attack a defender of determinate translation could mount against your case, and Quine's reply to that attack. Keep the scenario to a paragraph and spend the rest of the answer on the analysis.
