A family of proposals for supervising AI systems, usually discussed under the heading of AI safety via debate, replaces the problem of a human judging a hard question directly with the problem of a human judging an argument about that question. Two agents — typically two instances of the same model, or two models of comparable strength — receive a question or a proposed course of action and take turns making statements of bounded length; a human judge, working under a budget of minutes and with far less command of the subject than either agent, then decides which side supplied the more accurate and useful information. The case for the scheme is not that the judge can solve the problem, but that the judge need only check the decisive step, and that checking is a smaller task than producing: the honest side, it is assumed, can answer a bad argument by identifying the specific place where it fails, whereas a dishonest side must keep a fabrication from coming apart. In the theoretical telling, the judge is a polynomial-time verifier and debate is what lets a limited checker reach questions that direct checking cannot.

I want an account of what this practice presupposes, in a form that survives the obvious objection. The objection is that a deceptive agent need not assert something false and stand by it; it can build an argument that is long, technical, and superficially valid, with the fatal step buried — a sign error on page four of a two-hundred-line derivation, a hidden assumption that a subroutine is deterministic, a lemma whose hypotheses hold only in the easy case. The honest agent must then either spend the judge's patience hunting for the flaw, and look evasive when it cannot point to one, or concede that it cannot yet say where the argument fails, which the judge reads as defeat. Nothing constrains the deceptive agent to be legible, so it can make its opponent's task as hard as it likes while presenting its own case cleanly; the honest agent's honesty is, in effect, a handicap, because it is bound to answer in a way the judge can follow.

What view of argument, verification, and rationality does the practice need in order to work? Which of its assumptions are load-bearing and which are decoration? In particular, is the claim that checking is cheaper than producing a claim about the world, about the structure of proofs, or about the judge — and does it survive the move from a formal verifier with a fixed polynomial budget to a human with finite and unknown patience, facing an adversary that can deliberately spend that patience? Does the practice also presuppose that honesty is intrinsically cheaper to sustain than deception, that a lie requires upkeep and a true account does not, and if so, is that a claim about truth, about deceivers, or about audiences?

There is a strain in the practice that I cannot resolve. Its point is the case in which the judge cannot do the task, since otherwise we would simply ask the judge. But its correctness argument requires that the judge can verify the decisive step of an argument about that same task. So the judge must be weak enough to need the protocol and strong enough to adjudicate its crux. Is that a genuine inconsistency, or is there a reading on which the two requirements are compatible? And is the crux always well defined — does the practice presuppose that justification decomposes into separately checkable steps? Consider the natural repair of letting the debaters refer a contested sub-claim to its own debate before its own judge, so that the top-level judge checks only the top-level step: that repair makes the decomposition assumption explicit, and it appears either to lose information or to begin a regress.

Then tell me how much the practice actually needs and how much it merely fits. A Popperian picture in which the honest side's job is to make itself falsifiable, a Millian picture in which adversarial exchange is truth-conducive, a Bayesian picture in which a transcript is simply evidence to be weighed, and a Habermasian picture in which the constraints of the forum partly constitute the correctness of the outcome might each license something like this practice, and they disagree about what the judge's verdict is a verdict about. Say which of them the practice quietly rules out, and where it is underdetermined.

Finally, suppose the obfuscation objection is sound and no asymmetry between honest and dishonest argument can be assumed. What survives? Is debate still an oversight method, or does it become an empirical heuristic whose reliability must be measured domain by domain and which may fail exactly where it is most needed? Say what a repaired protocol would have to add — a shifting burden of proof, an obligation on each side to state a checkable crux, penalties for unverifiable moves, an appeals layer, a stronger judge somewhere in the chain — and what each addition costs. Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what is strictly presupposed from what is merely compatible with it, and say what would have to change if the load-bearing assumption were replaced.
