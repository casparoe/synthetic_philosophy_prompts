Question:

Setup: a seminar has twelve students. Everyone in the room is 5'6" or shorter, and exactly two students are tied for tallest (both 5'6"). Nobody in the room is asleep.

S: The tallest student in the seminar is asleep.
S*: The tallest student in the seminar is not asleep.

(a) Give Russell's analysis of S, and say whether S is true, false, or neither true nor false in this setup. Explain your reasoning.
(b) How many readings does Russell's theory assign to S*? For each reading, give the truth value in this setup, and say what the difference between the readings comes to.
(c) Russell says a definite description has no meaning on its own -- it only does its work inside a complete sentence. Explain what that amounts to, and say what Russell thought was misleading about the surface grammar of sentences like S.
(d) Does Russell's analysis treat "the tallest student in the seminar" as a referring expression? Justify your answer using what happens in this setup.

And here's the anwser I'm looking at right now:

(a) Russell says "The F is G" is really three claims rolled into one: that there is an F, that there's at most one F, and that whatever is F is G. So S unpacks as: there's a student in the seminar who is the tallest, nothing else in the seminar is the tallest, and that student is asleep. In symbols, ∃x(Sx ∧ Tx ∧ ∀y((Sy ∧ Ty) → y = x) ∧ Ax). Since nobody in the room is asleep, the last conjunct fails, so S is false. It's false rather than neither true nor false, because the existence and uniqueness claims are asserted by the sentence rather than assumed. The deeper way to put it is that the description has a sense but no reference here: "the tallest student in the seminar" gives us a way of picking out a student, but because of the tie it never manages to pick anything out, and that failure of reference is what makes S false. Russell's contribution was to show that a description can have a sense without a reference, so we don't have to treat the sentence as meaningless.

(b) S* has two readings, depending on whether the description takes narrow or wide scope relative to the negation. Narrow scope: ¬∃x(Sx ∧ Tx ∧ ∀y((Sy ∧ Ty) → y = x) ∧ Ax), where the negation applies to the whole claim that there is a unique tallest student who is asleep; that reading is true here, since there's no unique tallest student. Wide scope: ∃x(Sx ∧ Tx ∧ ∀y((Sy ∧ Ty) → y = x) ∧ ¬Ax), where the description takes scope over the negation; that reading is false here, again because of the tie. So S and S* can both be false -- S is false, and S* is false on the wide-scope reading. That's the anomaly Russell's theory was designed to explain: it shows that the law of excluded middle doesn't hold for sentences whose descriptions fail to pick out exactly one thing, which is why he thought a special theory of descriptions was needed in the first place.

(c) The point is that "the tallest student" doesn't mean anything by itself; it's an incomplete bit of language that only does its work as part of a whole sentence. The grammar of S makes it look like we've named a particular person and then said something about him, but on Russell's analysis there's no such person being named at all -- the sentence is really a general claim about the students in the room. The grammar is misleading because it hides the existence and uniqueness claims, and because it makes the description look like a name, which is what leads people to think there has to be some object for it to stand for.

(d) No. Since the description isn't a constituent of the proposition and gets broken up into quantifiers and predicates, "the tallest student in the seminar" isn't doing any referring -- nothing in the analysis stands for an object. In this setup that's exactly why S comes out false rather than meaningless or about some mysterious nonexistent student.
