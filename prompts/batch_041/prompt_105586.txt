I sit on my university's animal welfare oversight committee -- the one that has to sign off before anybody does anything to a living creature -- and the chair has asked me to circulate a written recommendation before next month's meeting, because we're split and the last two meetings dissolved into the same argument with no vote. Two protocols are on the agenda. The first is a crab study: the lab wants to give shore crabs repeated electric shocks in an avoidance-learning paradigm, and under our current policy crabs fall under "invertebrates, minimal oversight," which means a one-page form, no anaesthesia, no analgesia. The second is a Drosophila protocol, flies heated in a chamber to see whether they'll trade off escaping the heat against a preferred odour -- routine, except one of our grad-student members pointed out we've never actually justified why a fly sits outside full review while a mouse sits inside it, and nobody had an answer. We're also under pressure from the provost's office to adopt a "graded precaution" framework this year: one member has drafted a scoring sheet that rates a species on markers (nociceptors, central integration of noxious signals, motivational trade-offs, flexible avoidance learning) and lets the level of required protection scale with the total score. Another member thinks that's incoherent -- in his words you're either a subject of welfare or you're not, and a checklist just launders anthropomorphism into paperwork. He's not wrong that it could slide, and honestly the provost keeps joking that if we go down this road we'll be grading the building's smart thermostats next.

So please draft the memo I'll read out. It needs a bottom line I can actually move: what we do with the crab protocol (approve, approve with conditions, or reject, and what conditions if any), what we do with the fly protocol, and whether we adopt the graded framework now, later, or never. Give the reasoning in a form the crab lab and the scoring-sheet author could both push back on productively, and give me a time frame for whatever you recommend so the chair can calendar it. The part I most need is the honest treatment of the losing side -- the "you either have it or you don't" objection is the strongest thing against grading, and I don't want it hand-waved with a shrug about uncertainty. Related: if you do end up defending a gradient, tell me where the floor sits and why, because "somewhere between a crab and a thermostat" is where I keep getting stuck. Keep it to something I can read in ten minutes.
