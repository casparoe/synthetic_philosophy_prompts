Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student and I have to turn in a short research proposal for our works-in-progress seminar -- like 1500 words, one term of actual work -- and I want to use it to think through somehting that's been nagging me about Bradley's regress.

Here's the half-formed idea. Everyone knows the regress: if a relation is what unites the terms of a proposition (or a fact, or whatever), then you need a further relation to unite the first relation with its terms, and so on. The standard responses are either to deny that unity needs a relational explanation -- it's brute, or the relation is "unsaturated", or the proposition is just primitive -- or to accept the regress and call it benign because it's a regress of analysis rather than of being. What bugs me is that both sides seem to grant the same thing: that "why is this a proposition rather than a mere collection of its parts?" is a legitimate explanatory demand that any theory of propositions owes us an answer to. I'm starting to suspect the demand itself is the problem -- that it's really a demand for a *ground* dressed up as a demand for an *explanation*, and that once you separate "what makes it the case that this is unified" from "what explains why it is unified", the regress loses its grip. Or maybe it doesn't and I'm wrong; that's what the term is for.

So turn this into a real proposal. I need (1) the question stated sharply enough that it has a definate answer -- right now "is the regress vicious?" is mush; (2) why the existing positions leave the question open, argued in terms of the moves rather than names, since I'll add citations myself; (3) what I'd actually do over the term -- I think the method has to be comparative, e.g. run the same regress for ordinary material composition (why is a heap not unified the way a proposition is?) and for relations versus monadic predication, to see whether this "unity demand" is special to propositions or shows up everywhere, and whether it's the kind of thing that can even be satisfied in principle; (4) what a successful result would look like and, honestly, what would count as the project failing -- I want a clear "if X turns out to be the case, the idea is dead"; and (5) a realistic scope, i.e. the most I can honestly bite off in one term and what I should explicitly park for later.
