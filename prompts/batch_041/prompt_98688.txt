I teach the third-year normative ethics seminar at a small college — the first half of the course is consequentialism and deontology, the second half is the doctrine of double effect, and by December my students can recite the four conditions the way they recite the periodic table. That is precisely the problem. For the final I want a single essay question, forty-five minutes, twenty-five marks, built so that the recitation is worthless: the crux has to be a case in which the harmful outcome is neither produced by the agent's own hand nor merely overlooked, but is brought about by another agent whose behaviour the first agent is knowingly relying on. The familiar trolley cases and the familiar medical cases never force this issue, because in all of them the bad effect sits inside the agent's own causal control, where calling it a side effect is at least defensible; I want a case in which that label is itself what is in dispute. The question should also be one a student cannot answer by reaching for the standard contrast between intending and merely foreseeing, since I want them to see that the contrast has more than one reading.

Here is the case I want the question to turn on, and please treat these facts as fixed. A besieged city's emergency council learns that a militia is about to overrun a district where several thousand civilians are sheltering, and that the militia's advance can be turned aside if the council broadcasts a false report that a rival column has massed at a small settlement on the far side of the valley. The militia will believe the report and redeploy, and it will do so because it takes the report for genuine military intelligence; nothing about the settlement figures in the deception, and the settlement's inhabitants are not mentioned in it. But the council also knows, with something close to certainty, that the militia will sack that settlement on its way through — it has sacked every village it has passed, and the council has no doubt that it will do so again. The council does not want the settlers dead, would gladly forgo the killing if it could keep the diversion, and orders nothing; it simply broadcasts, foreseeing that a few hundred people who would otherwise have lived will be killed by men it has, in effect, sent. The district is saved.

What I want from you is the finished exam item. First, the essay question itself, phrased so that no student can pass by listing the four conditions, and pitched so that a good student is forced to do two things the course has not rehearsed: to give two rival readings of what the doctrine must mean by intention, or by treating a harm as a means, when the harm is executed by someone else's free choice, and then to adjudicate between them. Second, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that actually adjudicates — that reaches a verdict on whether the council acted permissibly under the doctrine of double effect, and defends one reading of the doctrine against the other rather than surveying the options. Third, a marking scheme for twenty-five marks that names, one by one, the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific moves that should cap an answer at a second-class mark, with a line on how students typically go wrong at each. Please write this as a committed answer: choose the reading of the doctrine you think is right and build both the model answer and the scheme around it, and where your own verdict is genuinely contestable, say in a sentence or two where the contest lies rather than hedging inside the answer itself. No citations, no reading lists, no names of commentators — the students write from the course material and their own heads, and the model answer should be writable the same way.
