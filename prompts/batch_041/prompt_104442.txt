I work as a software engineer and I run a monthly workshop on decision-making under uncertainty for a mixed group of engineers, analysts, and product managers. I have a 75-minute slot and roughly 16 participants. I would like you to design a runnable in-session activity — a game or structured exercise with rules, materials, and a debrief — that makes participants feel the force of a result I came across recently and still find hard to accept. I believe the result belongs to something called imprecise probabilities, though I have no formal background in the area.

The result is this. Two boxes, A and B, will each show either a red or a green light. The organiser prepared them in one of two ways, and you do not know which. In twin mode, the boxes always show the same colour, and Box A is equally likely to show red or green. In rival mode, the boxes always show opposite colours, and Box A is again equally likely to show red or green. You have no information about how the organiser chose between the two modes, and no way to find out. Before seeing either box, what should you think about the probability that Box B shows red? Under every hypothesis consistent with your information, Box B is fair: it shows red with probability exactly one half. Your range is the single point one half. Now suppose Box A is revealed and it shows red. Box A's colour is equally likely under both modes, so seeing it tells you nothing about which mode was used. Yet if you ask what range of probabilities for Box B is now consistent with your information, you get the whole interval from zero to one: in twin mode Box B is certainly red, in rival mode it is certainly green, and you have no basis for ruling out either. Learning an apparently irrelevant fact has made you completely uncertain about something you were previously certain of.

I want the participants to run into this themselves rather than hear it explained, and I do not want them to hear the technical name for it until the debrief. The rules, the physical setup, or the incentives should arrange for them to commit to a probability or a price for Box B, then see Box A, then revise, and to feel the widening in their own commitments. A betting or market-making framing would land well with this audience: for instance, participants could quote a buy price and a sell price for a contract that pays out if Box B shows red, under a rule that the quote must be defensible without any assumption about the mode — they must not be able to lose money no matter which mode was used. Before Box A is revealed, the safe buy and sell prices coincide; afterwards, they should pull apart to the extremes. The group will be mixed in mathematical confidence, so the activity should not presuppose comfort with formal notation.

Please give me the materials and how to prepare them (I am happy to prepare physical props in advance, and I can write a short script to generate the box pairs if that helps); a minute-by-minute run of the session; the rules, roles, and payoffs or scoring; a debrief that maps what happened onto the underlying idea, including the point at which participants will want to insist that their best guess is still one half and how to handle that; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure; and an honest note on which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which part I will have to supply in words. In the debrief, please be frank that a precise Bayesian with a fixed prior over the two modes would not experience this widening at all, and explain plainly why the widening is a feature of the imprecise-probability approach rather than of probability theory as such.

Please write the whole thing in British English.
