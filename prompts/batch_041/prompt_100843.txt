Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between purely self-interested parties, defection is the uniquely rational move. (2) In the iterated prisoner's dilemma, cooperation is often rational, and reciprocity is the mechanism; I take this to hold even in finite games, since a sufficiently long finite game is for practical purposes indefinite. (3) The same expected-utility reasoning that makes defection rational in the one-shot case makes cooperation rational in the iterated case — repetition changes the payoffs, not the underlying rationality — so self-interest plus repetition is the whole story. (4) Human cooperative and punitive dispositions are evolved adaptations for repeated social life: people will incur real personal costs to punish free-riders, and that costly punishment is essential to explaining large-scale cooperation. (5) In repeated games among patient players, essentially any mutually beneficial arrangement can be sustained as an equilibrium, so what repetition buys you is a menu of possible cooperative outcomes rather than a prediction about which one we actually get.
