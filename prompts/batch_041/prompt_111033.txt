Okay, I've talked myself into a deflationary view of "collapse" and I want you to try hard to break it. Here's the view. There's no such thing as societal collapse as a single phenomenon — no natural kind, no shared mechanism, no useful category. Every case we file under "collapse" is, once you press on it, a change in one or another kind of complexity that some later observer happens to care about: headcount, cities, writing, roads, tax rolls, monumental building, long-distance trade, centralized authority. Specify which of those you mean and the drama evaporates — you get a continuous curve, a reallocation, a shift to a different scale or a different place. The Bronze Age thing, the Maya thing, Easter Island: on this view their not three instances of one event at different sizes, they're unrelated trajectories that got filed together because the filing flatters a moral about hubris and limits. "Collapse" is a story we tell, not a thing that happens.

So what should move me off this? Give me the strongest cases, sorted by how decisive they'd actually be. Specifically: is there a case where the loss is sharp rather than gradual, where it's not a loss relative to some variable I got to pick in advance, and where "they just reorganized at a different scale" stops being true without being a pun? What about the ones where something is gone for good — a script nobody reads again, a technique that doesn't come back for a thousand years? Is a long recovery time a fact about the world, or just my impatience? And is there a difference between a society that shrinks and one that loses the *capacity* to shrink gracefully — and if so does that give the collapse concept a foothold I'm not seeing?

Then be honest about the meta-question. Have I defined the view so loosely that nothing could count against it? If every apparent counterexample gets absorbed by "well, they reorganized," say so, and say whether that's fatal or just the price of a deflationary account. And what would a defender of the view — me — actually have to concede as counting against them, as opposed to what critics would shout from the cheap seats? I'd rather find out now than mid-argument. The case I'd personally find hardest to explain away is a collapse nobody was around to observe, one that isn't relative to any describer at all — so tell me whether that's even coherent, and what it would take to make it real.
