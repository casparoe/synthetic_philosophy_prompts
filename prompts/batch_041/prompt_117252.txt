I chair the selection panel for our faculty's internal seed-funding scheme and I've hit something I can't figure out on my own. It's me plus five colleagues, we get through maybe thirty-five applications a year, each one between £15k and £40k, and everything is minuted because unsuccessful applicants are entitled to written feedback and about one in ten appeals. Our stated rule is that a project gets funded only if all three of the following hold: it's doable in the twelve months with the money asked for, there's a credible track record behind it, and it fits the aims the scheme was set up for. In practice we vote yes/no on each of the three criteria, then vote yes/no on the overall question of whether to fund, and up to now I'd always assumed those two votes would agree. Last month they didn't. Every one of the three criteria passed with four votes out of six, and the funding question failed three to two. I went back and forth on which number to write into the minutes and ended up writing the funding vote, but I genuinely don't know if that was right, and a member has since complained that the minutes now misrepresent the comittee's reasons. (I think the other direction — funding passing while a criterion fails — can't actually happen if everyone is internally consistent, but tell me if that's wrong.) Two other things have shaken my confidence. In March I reworded one criterion, splitting what had been a single 'fit with the scheme's aims' question into two separate ones, and a decision flipped as a result — same panel, same applications, same views, different answer. And one member has started saying they don't really engage with the criteria at all and just want to vote on the bottom line, while another insists the criteria are the whole point and the bottom-line vote is a formality. I'm not a philosopher; I did one term of political philosophy as an undergrad and that's the extent of it.

What I want from you, as someone who actually knows the formal work on group decision-making and can also write for non-specialists, is a decision procedure I can run in a ninety-minute meeting and put in the chair's briefing pack on one page. It should tell me: the order of operations for taking votes, whether we take premise votes, a conclusion vote, or both, what to do when they come apart (and I want a rule, not 'use your discretion'), how to record the outcome so the minutes can't be accused of misrepresenting anyone, and how to word criteria before the meeting starts so that the wording can't be quietly doing the work. It needs to survive the things that actually happen to us: a member who abstains on a criterion they feel unqualified to judge, a member who votes strategically (I've watched someone vote yes on feasibility for a project they'd told me privately was unfeasible, and I'm fairly sure it was to help the overall outcome), a member who refuses to vote on the premises at all, a two-way tie, and me — the chair — being the one who decides what the criteria are and how they're worded, which I now realise is a lot of power and which I'd like the procedure to constrain rather than flatter. Say plainly which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls in disguise, and tell me the failure modes: where will this procedure misfire, and what can it not do no matter how I set it up? If there's an impossibility result lurking here — some list of good properties no rule can have all of at once — I'd rather know which property we're giving up and why that one.

Then run it on three cases. First, our March-style profile: six members, the three criteria each passing four to two, and the funding question failing three to two — walk me through exactly what the procedure outputs and what I say to the member who complained. Second, the same six members' views but with the third criterion split in two, showing what changes and why. Third, invent a case where your procedure tells me to do something I won't like — I don't know what that'll be, but I want to see one, because if the procedure never produces an unwelcome answer it isn't doing anything. Don't give me a reading list or citations, I won't follow them up; if you want to name a result or an idea, name it and explain it in a sentence. Think it through properly before you commit — sketch the candidate rules and what each does to case one, then pick one and defend the pick — and don't smooth over the parts where the formal literature and a real committee with tired members in a Tuesday afternoon meeting come apart. Finish with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...} containing only the short name I should give the default rule in the briefing pack, a few words at most.
