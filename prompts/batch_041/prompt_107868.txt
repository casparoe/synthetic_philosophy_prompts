I've been retired about six years now — thirty-five years in volcanology, mostly tephra and ice cores, and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, which is humbling in exactly the way I hoped it would be. Anyway, I got into an argument at dinner with a friend who does early modern history and it's still bugging me a week later. Her line is that people who come out of the physical sciences are definately the worst offenders when it comes to treating natural events as the real drivers of history: we find the volcano and the ice core satisfying because they're clean and quantifiable, and then we quietly let them carry the whole story, while the messy human stuff — taxes, factions, who controlled the grain supply — gets demoted to mere detail. I half agree with her and I half think she's throwing away real causal weight to win a point. So what I want from you is not a pep talk for either side.

You're a philosopher of the historical sciences — someone who has thought hard about causation, evidence, and the seduction of single-cause stories — so please take this seriously, and start by writing out in a few lines the criteria you're going to use before you rate anything. Then sort the following into tiers (S down to D, or whatever labels you prefer) by how much causal weight you'd actually assign them in a serious historical account of the episodes they're attached to: (1) the Tambora eruption of 1815 and the year without a summer that followed — harvest failure, food riots in Europe, and I've read it helped push settlers westward in America; (2) the dust veil of the mid-500s, several years of dim sun and cold summers, with plague arriving into a weakened Mediterranean; (3) the ENSO cycle — El Niño and La Niña — as a driver of famine and dynastic trouble in monsoon Asia; (4) Old World pathogens after 1492, smallpox and the rest, the ones people call the real conquerors of the Americas; (5) the progressive salinization of irrigated fields in southern Mesopotamia, offered as the reason the political centre of gravity drifted north; (6) the Little Ice Age and the seventeenth-century crisis, with famine, revolt and witch trials all bundled in; (7) the Maunder Minimum, the sun's quiet spell in the later 1600s, and the claim that it drove the cold; (8) the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 and its effect on Enlightenment arguments about a benevolent cosmos; (9) Milankovitch orbital cycles — climate as the slow geological backdrop that made farming possible in the first place; (10) the claim that early farmers had already pushed CO2 and methane up enough a few thousand years ago to hold off the next glaciation, i.e. humans as a geophysical force long before industry. For each one, in a sentence or two: say whether it's doing the work of a background condition, a trigger, an amplifier, or mostly a redescription of something we already knew, and then name the specific way you think historians and popular writers tend to overreach with it. Give me a confidence rating for each too. No citations or reading lists please — I'm not asking for those and I wouldn't check them — and if I've mangled a date or a gloss, just silently fix it and rate the underlying phenomenon rather than my sloppy version of it.

Two things I'd like woven in rather than tacked on at the end. First, the asymmetry I keep noticing: for the natural side we have annually resolved proxies — tree rings, ice cores, varves — and for the human side we have patchy documents written mostly by winners, so I suspect the evidentiary record itself is shoving natural causes up the tiers regardless of their real weight. Tell me whether you think that's a genuine problem and what it does to your rankings. Second, the vocabulary: the literature now likes to call volcanoes and pathogens actants, or even actors, and there are people who want to insist that matter has its own liveliness — and I honestly can't tell whether that's a useful reframing that keeps us honest about how much of history isn't about us, or a metaphor that quietly licenses exactly the causal sloppiness my friend is complaining about. Give me your view and let it feed back into the tiers. Then finish with two lines: which single item on my list you'd actually put money on, and which one you'd drop entirely.
