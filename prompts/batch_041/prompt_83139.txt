I am a retired population ecologist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy on my own, without any formal training in it, and I have written a short essay that I would like you to comment on. I would rather be told where I have gone wrong than where I have gone right; corrections of what may be elementary mistakes will not offend me.

The essay concerns Malthus. Its argument is as follows.

My starting point is a habit of thought carried over from ecology. In population biology we routinely build models whose long-run predictions are not expected to hold on any particular schedule; a logistic model may predict a plateau that arrives early or late, and we do not on that account call the model false. When I first met the standard verdict on Malthus — that history refuted him, that the crisis he forecast never arrived — I thought the verdict too quick, and I set out to say why. I found that saying why is harder than I expected.

My central distinction is between a theory's mechanism and its clock. A mechanism claim says what tends to happen, and why: that populations grow until they press against the means of subsistence, and that the pressure is relieved by mortality or by restraint. A clock claim says when: that the pressure will manifest within a generation or so, that the crisis is imminent. Malthus, I argue, was making both kinds of claim, and they have different fates. The mechanism can be vindicated by events that arrive centuries late; the clock, as a claim about timing, cannot be vindicated by a late arrival at all, since a clock that is wrong about when is simply wrong.

From this I propose a rule: a long-run prediction counts as vindicated only if the mechanism it named is the mechanism that actually produced the observed outcome, and not merely a mechanism that could have produced some outcome eventually. On this rule, Malthus's mechanism survives only if the pressures we now observe are consequences of the same ratio of growth to subsistence that he identified, and not of quite different causes — war, institutions, distribution — that happen to resemble his prediction when viewed from a distance. I suspect this rule is the most contestable thing in the essay.

The harder problem is the rescue. When the crisis did not come on schedule, the theory was saved by auxiliary assumptions: emigration, later marriage, the demographic transition, agricultural improvement. Some of these, I think, are legitimate refinements and some are not, but I cannot find a principled way to draw the line. I have a vague sense that the difference between a refinement and a mere rescue turns on whether the auxiliary assumption was independently motivated, but that criterion seems to me to prove either too much or too little, and I cannot tell which.

My verdict, then, is double. Malthus's mechanism was in some sense right, and his predictions were in some sense never at risk, because the clock could always be reset. If both of those are true, I am not sure the theory has been either vindicated or refuted; it may be no more than a framework for describing what happens when it happens.

I would like your comments on three things, in the order of how much they matter to me.

First, the mechanism and clock distinction. Is it a real distinction, or have I merely relabelled the familiar point that a theory can be right about one thing and wrong about another? If it is real, does my proposed rule of vindication actually follow from it, or have I smuggled in an extra premise?

Second, and more embarrassing to me: I suspect I am using the vocabulary of confirmation and falsification loosely. Point out where I have conflated a prediction's being false with its being unfalsifiable, where I have treated a rescue as illegitimate when a philosopher would say I have merely described ordinary scientific practice, and where a term of art is being used in a way no one trained in the field would use it.

Third, the verdict itself. Is it too generous or too harsh to Malthus, and does the phrase right but early do any real work, or does it paper over the difference between a claim that was confirmed late and a claim that was never genuinely at risk?

Please write in continuous prose rather than as a list, and where you object to something, quote or closely paraphrase the specific sentence of mine you are objecting to.
