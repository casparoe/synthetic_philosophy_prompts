I'm presenting a paper at a small workshop on moral psychology and political disagreement next month, and the room is going to be rough in a specific way, so what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than more slides. My thesis has two halves and I care about both. First, descriptive: I argue that the famous finding -- conservatives draw on all five of Haidt and Graham's moral foundations while liberals basically draw on harm and fairness -- is partly an artifact of how the questionnaire was built. The fairness items are written as equal-treatment items and the loyalty, authority and sanctity items as group-boundary items, so the contrast is as much about what the questions ask as about what people value. And even where the difference is real, I think its mostly a difference in which considerations get named and made salient on a survey, not in the weights people put on them when they actually have to decide something. Second, normative: I argue that none of this touches who's right. Grant every descriptive claim and the most it does is shift the burden in reflective equilibrium, by making the liberal intuition set look less like the default. That's an epistemic point about whose intuitions get counted as data, not a moral point about whose values are correct. I should say up front that I'm not running a new factor analysis of my own, I'm re-reading the existing one and arguing about what it can bear.

The room: mostly social psychologists who use the MFQ in their own work and have a lot invested in it. Also (a) a psychometrician who only cares about factor structure and measurement invariance across groups, (b) someone just back from fieldwork who will say the foundations don't carve anything at the joints outside WEIRD samples, (c) a dyadic-morality person who thinks all of it funnels through a single harm-and-victim template and the five foundations are just costumes, (d) a developmentalist from the moral/conventional-distinction tradition who thinks the framework ignores a distinction that has been doing real work for decades, (e) one expressivist metaethicist, and (f) one intuitionist who likes reflective equilibrium exactly as it is and won't see what my second half is supposed to change.

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them, each with what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft. Order them by how much damage they do rather than by who speaks first, tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- you'll know the one.
