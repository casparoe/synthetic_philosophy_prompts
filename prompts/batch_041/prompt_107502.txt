I keep noticing the same shape in stories about practices that die — dueling, slavery, bear-baiting, foot-binding, pick your favourite. The arguments against them were lying around for ages and nobody moved; what actually kills them seems to be something else, a war, a shift in who has to look who in the eye. But the bit I can't find a word for is what the defenders do at the very end. They stop saying the practice is good and start saying it isn't anyone else's business — it's a private matter, a matter of honour, of conscience, of local custom, of self-government. And that move can't be refuted, because it isn't a claim about the practice at all, it's a claim about who's allowed to have an opinion. So the practice never really loses the argument, it just runs out of people willing to be seen defending it. Is there a name for this terminal move, the defence migrating from 'this is good' to 'this is ours to decide'? I've been calling it a jurisdictional retreat, but I don't know if that's taken, and more to the point I don't know if it's a distinct thing or just the old idea that a bad cause's last defence is some lofty-sounding principle. How does it relate to the other thing — that the winning argument usually predates its victory by a century or more? And does the retreat mean the norm is dying, or is it how the norm survives, giving up the public case and keeping the practice as a private right? That last part is what I actually want carved up, not just labelled.
