Two friends of mine, Nadia and Rob, got into it at a barbecue last weekend about our mutual friend Dave, who has decided his estranged brother is eventually going to come back around. I know this is the James-and-Clifford thing and I already know the textbook versions of both, so please don't just tell me what each of them would say in general -- I want the actual argument they had sorted out.

Here's how it went.

(1) Nadia said Dave's belief isn't wishful thinking, it's load-bearing. If Dave assumes his brother will come around, he keeps inviting him, doesn't get defensive at Thanksgiving, treats a two-line text as an opening. If he assumes it's hopeless he goes stiff and cold, and the hopelessness comes true. So the belief isn't a mistake about the evidence, it's part of what makes the evidence turn out that way -- and he can't wait for proof, because the proof only shows up if he believes first.

(2) Rob said you can get all of that without the belief. Dave can tell himself 'odds are maybe one in ten, but I'll keep the door open anyway' and then do every single thing he'd do if he believed. The behavior and the belief come apart. If he can't keep it up without the belief, that's a fact about his discipline, not a license to believe.

(3) Nadia said that's a fantasy about how people work. Nobody does years of one-sided warmth while privately expecting failure -- the private verdict leaks out in tone and timing. And 'one in ten but I'll act like it's likely' isn't a stable state; it decays into either hope or resignation within a month. So the real choice isn't believe versus act-as-if, it's believe versus quietly give up.

(4) Rob said then Dave is believing something because of what the believing does to him, which is the exact thing that's corrupting about it. The belief is supposed to track how things actually are with his brother -- the history, whether he's drinking again, whether he's back in therapy. You don't get to promote a proposition because promoting it is convenient. And the self-fulfilling bit doesn't rescue you: if believing nudges it from 10% to 30%, fine, believe 30% -- but what you'd be doing is using the belief to manufacture its own evidence, which is like buying a lottery ticket so you can honestly say you're holding a winner.

(5) Nadia said but if we agree the belief really does raise the odds, then the fact that Dave is going to hold the belief is itself a reason to think reconciliation is likelier. There's nothing circular about updating on facts about yourself, including facts about what you're going to do. And he can't decline this bet: he's either going to expect his brother back or not, there's no waiting room where he suspends judgment for five years. If he can't not-choose, then 'wait for the evidence' is also a choice, and one that quietly settles the thing in the pessimistic direction.

(6) Rob said 'he has to choose' is true of a lot of things and doesn't make belief voluntary. And the pessimistic default being a choice doesn't make it unsupported -- the evidence about his brother is probally what it is, and if it's bad, settling toward resignation is the honest response, not a rigged one. If you think the hopeful belief is 30% justified, show me the evidence for it that isn't just 'the belief will make me feel better about the odds.'

They've both asked me to settle it and I cant. Go move by move and rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Then say what specific thing each of them is missing. Don't tell me it depends or split the difference by default -- if one of them is closer to right overall, say so and say why. Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
