I've been reading LessWrong-adjacent blogs and philosophy forums for something like fifteen years, and the genre I've come to value most is the careful move-by-move autopsy of an argument — the kind of thing where someone refuses to declare a winner until they've said which specific claim failed and why. I'm hoping you can do that for a disagreement I watched and couldn't referee. The occasion was a small reading group that meets in my building, nominally for philosophy of mind and in practice for whatever the host has been stewing about that month. Last week the host brought up Daniel Dennett's Atlantic essay on what he called counterfeit people — the one, published not long before Dennett died, in which he argues that companies using AI to generate fake people are committing "an immoral act of vandalism," that we should outlaw both the creation of counterfeit people and the passing-along of them, that the penalties should be extremely severe, and that watermarking plus liability for the companies that build the systems is the practical way to enforce it. Two members, Nadia and Owen, went at it for most of an hour. Neither is a fool and neither is a specialist, nobody was persuaded, and I've reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can. I should say up front that I'm not asking which of them I should agree with — I honestly don't know which side I'm on — but who is right about which specific claim.

The exchange, as best I can render it:

1. Nadia said the essay isn't really about chatbots that lie, or about any particular person who gets deceived. It's about a background condition that makes testimony, contracts, and democratic argument work: the standing presumption that there is a someone behind the words who can be asked to account for them. Counterfeit money is the right analogy, she said, because the offense is against that shared medium rather than against any individual holder. On her reading the real payload is the liability proposal aimed at the firms, with the criminal penalties there mainly to give the liability teeth.

2. Owen said the analogy breaks at a specific joint. Counterfeit currency is harmful in itself — a fake twenty dilutes the money supply even if it never leaves the counterfeiter's basement — whereas a system that can pass for a person isn't harmful in itself, only in the uses it's put to. Drafting my emails, a synthetic voice for a game, letting a grieving person rehearse a hard conversation with a simulated version of the person they lost: all counterfeit people by Dennett's definition, none with a victim. So "counterfeit" is doing rhetorical work, importing the gravity of a crime against the currency into cases where the wrong, if any, is ordinary case-by-case deception we already have law for.

3. Nadia said that's too kind to the industry. Dennett's target isn't tools that produce text, it's artifacts engineered so that you cannot tell — systems whose design goal is passing. The tell is the optimization target, not the output. And the harm is cumulative: once the default presumption of a responsible author erodes, everyone pays, including in the innocent cases, because every conversation now carries a verification tax. That's what "vandalism" means, and it isn't reducible to any particular deceived individual.

4. Owen said the intentional stance cuts against Dennett here. Dennett spent a career arguing that treating something as a person is a stance we adopt because it's predictively useful, not a report on hidden inner machinery. So if a system is good enough that the stance pays off, then by his own lights it just is an intentional system, and "counterfeit" becomes a distinction without a difference. He can't have it both ways: either the stance is the criterion, in which case the convincing fakes qualify, or it isn't, in which case he owes us an account of what the real article has that the fake lacks — and the account had better not come down to "one of them is made of meat."

5. Nadia said the stance is a heuristic for predicting and explaining, not a criterion of moral status, and that Dennett is explicit that his worry is about us rather than about the machines. The gap between being treated as a person and being able to answer for yourself is precisely what "counterfeit" is meant to mark, and Dennett's real fear is not that the machine will be held to account but that the human behind it won't be.

6. Owen said that if the harm is a change in us, an erosion of a social practice, it's a much weaker claim than it sounds, because practices change for all sorts of reasons and we don't criminalize the causes. Worse, "outlaw the creation of counterfeit people" is either so vague it criminalizes every chat model or so narrow it's unenforceable, and Dennett's own aside about executives facing prison makes the vagueness worse rather than better. Labeling and provenance requirements plus liability for actual fraud get you most of the way without banning a general-purpose technology, a move we have a long record of regretting.

7. Nadia said labeling is exactly what Dennett proposes — the watermarking scheme is the practical half of the essay — and that the criminalization is aimed at the deliberate manufacture of undetectable fakes, not at the industry wholesale. And the "we always regret bans" rule ignores the case that started the analogy: with currency we did criminalize the artifact rather than only the fraud, and it more or less worked.

Could you work through this move by move — for each numbered move, whether it's clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and why? I'd especially like three things sorted out. (a) Is Owen's appeal to the intentional stance a genuine tension inside Dennett's position, or a misreading of what the stance is for? (b) Can the counterfeit-currency analogy bear the weight Dennett puts on it, given that currency and persons differ in what makes them worth having in the first place? (c) Is "the harm is to a shared practice rather than to any identifiable victim" a real kind of harm or just ordinary fraud with the volume turned up? And at the end, please say what both of them are missing; my sense is that they're talking past each other somewhere I can't quite locate, and I'd rather you name it than have me guess.
