A small foundation has three researcher-years to spend on the conceptual limits of AI safety via debate, and I have been asked to advise on the agenda. Assume the basic protocol is settled -- two agents argue, a weaker judge decides -- and set aside empirical scaling work; I want conceptual and theoretical questions only. Give me a defended ordering of the open problems most worth those three years: for each, why it is upstream of the others, how a philosopher or theorist rather than an ML empiricist could make real progress on it within a year, and what result would move it up or down the list.

To keep this from collapsing into a summary of the existing literature, build the agenda around the following crux, which I take to be the live one: the danger is not that the dishonest debater has a better argument, but that they have an argument the judge cannot evaluate -- one whose soundness check costs more than the judge's budget while producing a plausible-looking version costs almost nothing. Call this a verification asymmetry. I want to know which sub-questions that asymmetry generates, which of them are answerable with existing tools (say, treating debate as an interactive-proof game against a resource-bounded verifier), which dissolve once stated precisely, and which are the cases where a negative result would genuinely bear on whether debate is a viable oversight method at all. End by naming the two questions you would fund first and defending the choice against the runner-up.
