I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I have been asked to referee an argument that has been running for three weeks in my department's decision theory reading group. The disputants are Mira and Julian; I have no stake in the outcome, and I genuinely cannot tell who is right. What I want is an assessment of the moves they have actually made, not a summary of how this problem is usually handled.

The dispute concerns a variant of the absent-minded driver problem. A driver must pass two intersections. He is absent-minded: on arriving at an intersection he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, and he has no memory of any decision made at an earlier intersection. He knows this about himself. At each intersection he may exit or continue. Exiting at the first has value 0, exiting at the second has value 4, and continuing past the second has value 0. He maximizes expected value. Mira and Julian agree on all of this.

Mira. The expected value of a rule that continues with probability p at each intersection is 4p(1 − p), maximized at p = 1/2, giving expected value 1. The driver should therefore continue with probability 1/2. Since he cannot rely on his memory, he should carry a coin, flip it on arriving at each intersection, and continue only when it comes up heads. The coin is not an expository convenience: the answer to "what should he do?" is "follow the coin."

Julian. The coin cannot be the answer, because at the intersection the driver faces a choice between two acts and can compare them. Given the rule p = 1/2, conditional on his being at an intersection, the chance that he is at the first is 2/3 and at the second 1/3. Exiting has expected value 0. Continuing has expected value (2/3)(2) + (1/3)(0) = 4/3: if he is at the first he reaches the second and then follows the rule, collecting 4 half the time and 0 half the time; if he is at the second he collects 0. A driver who does the best he can with what he knows at the moment will continue, and since he cannot tell the intersections apart he will continue at both. So the "optimal rule" can be carried out only by acting against his own best judgment at the moment of acting. Julian adds that the problem as posed gives the driver no coin, and that importing a commitment device changes the subject.

Mira. (1) The coin does not depend on the driver's willpower; it is a physical device that settles the matter at the intersection, so the claim that the rule cannot be executed is false. (2) Julian's reasoning is self-undermining. The driver's rule is the same rule at both intersections, since he cannot tell them apart; so if the argument for deviating here is sound, it is sound at both, and the rule it supports is "continue with probability 1." Julian borrowed p = 1/2 to compute his 2/3 and 1/3 while recommending a departure from p = 1/2, but those probabilities are themselves fixed by the rule the driver follows; using them to argue that he should not follow it is circular. (3) Applied consistently, the act-by-act test has only one resting point, "always continue," where the driver's expected value is 0 — no better than exiting at once — against 1 for the rule Mira recommends. A test that lands him on a strictly worse rule is the wrong test.

Julian. (1) When the coin tells the driver to exit, he knowingly takes an act that by his own lights at that moment has expected value 0 rather than 4/3. That is obedience, not rationality; the coin can do its work only because the driver has already decided not to deliberate at the intersection, and that prior decision is doing all the work. (2) Julian grants that the driver should adopt the coin rule at the outset, as a choice made before the journey — but that answers a different question. "Which rule should I adopt before starting?" and "what should I do when I find myself at an intersection?" are distinct, and Mira has answered the first while presenting it as an answer to the second. (3) That act-by-act reasoning lands the driver on a worse rule shows only that act-optimality and plan-optimality come apart here; that is a feature of the problem, not a defect in act-optimality. (4) The circularity charge is misdirected: which intersection he is at is not something he chooses, and if he is at the second, then his continuing at the first has already happened.

Mira. (1) If he is at the first, the continuation has not already happened — it is what he is deciding now — so Julian's last point works only if the driver already knows he is not at the first, which is precisely what he does not know. (2) Either the driver follows the rule, in which case the coin decides and the act-by-act comparison is idle; or he does not, in which case his rule is not p = 1/2 and Julian's 2/3 and 1/3 are the wrong numbers. There is no way to use those numbers to show that he should abandon the rule. (3) Once he has adopted the coin rule he is not deliberating at the intersection but executing a plan chosen for its value taken as a whole; that the coin sometimes overrides what looks locally better is what a commitment device is for. And he can have a coin: he knows he is absent-minded, and putting a coin in his pocket is something he can do.

Please assess each of the five moves in turn, saying whether it is right, wrong, or right only in part, and why. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular, whether their disagreement turns on a distinction neither has named, and whether the question "what should the driver do?" has a single answer here at all. Finally, tell me who has the better of the disagreement overall.
