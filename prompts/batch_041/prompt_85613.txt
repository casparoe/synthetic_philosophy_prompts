I run an upper-level seminar on suffering-focused ethics — it's cross-listed so the room is a mix of advanced undergrads and first-year grad students. I'm writing the final now and I want one essay question that the taxonomists do worse on than the people who actually think, because I think the standard taxonomy is whats generating the trouble here rather than resolving it. Closed book, no internet, 75 minutes.

The view I want them to take apart is one I've been assembling in lectures all term. I'm sure versions of it are floating around, but this is my formulation, and I want the question to hand the students exactly these five clauses:

1. Well-being is a single quantity. Pleasure is positive, pain is negative, and a life at zero is neither worth living nor worth not living.
2. There's a threshold T below zero. Suffering worse than T — call it severe suffering — is lexically worse than any pleasure whatsoever: no finite amount of pleasure makes up for it.
3. Suffering between T and zero — mild suffering — is commensurable with pleasure and trades off against it at some exchange rate.
4. Right action maximizes the resulting ordering: first minimize severe suffering, then, holding that fixed, maximize total hedonic value.
5. And here's the part I want them to catch. The people who hold 1-4 also describe the view as an incommensurabilist one. Their line is that severe suffering and pleasure aren't on the same scale at all, which is why no quantity of pleasure can offset the suffering.

What I need from you:

(a) The essay question itself, aimed at whether this is a coherent position, and if it isn't, which clause has to go and what the rest of the view costs once you cut it.
(b) A model answer, 650-700 words.
(c) A marking scheme out of 20, naming the specific moves a good answer has to make, the marks for each, and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong.
(d) A short note on what separates an A from a B, where the line is a specific intellectual move and not "more depth."

Two things I dont want in the answer. No bibliography — it's closed book and half of them would invent sources anyway. And none of the lazy "so the view is inconsistent, next question" move; the interesting answer says what you give up and what you're buying with it.
