I'm putting together an episode of a small podcast on science and religion, and next week's guest is a philosopher of religion who works on early modern hermeneutics. Her thesis, which I want the whole interview to hang on: the principle usually credited to Galileo — that when natural philosophy delivers a genuine demonstration, scripture must be reinterpreted to fit it — is not actually the science-friendly, liberal principle everyone treats it as. She thinks it's the opposite: it quietly makes theology purely reactive, a discipline that never gets to constrain inquiry and only ever catches up, and it hands interpretive authority to whoever currently controls the demonstrations. Her alternative is a strict division-of-labor rule: scripture is about God's saving action and never about the furniture of the world, so apparent conflicts are always a category mistake rather than something to adjudicate. (This is a made-up scholar for the episode, so don't worry about matching anything she's actually published.)

Draft me eight to ten questions, in the order I should ask them, that actually get at the content of that position instead of letting her restate it. I want at least one question on each of these, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:

- The vacuity worry: if scripture never says anything about the world, what do we do with passages that look like they're doing cosmological work and matter to the story — the sun standing still as a sign, the waters above the firmament, the earth not being moved? If she says those are phenomenological or accommodated language, press on whether that leaves the text with any falsifiable content at all.
- The circularity problem: how does she know a demonstration is a demonstration without already knowing which way the interpretive question should come out? She'll probably say demonstration is independent of the exegetical dispute, so ask what that looks like when the "demonstration" is contested by competent people in real time.
- The accommodation regress: if God accommodated a mistaken cosmology, why stop there — why not a mistaken morality, or a mistaken view of God's own nature? And if she says morality is different in kind, make her say what the principled difference is.
- The jurisdictional point: is her reading of Galileo really a hermeneutic thesis, or a claim about who gets to decide? Ask whether the position would look any different coming from someone who wanted the theologians to win.
- The counterfactual: if Copernicus had never happened, would the division-of-labor rule even have been available, or is it a rule we only formulate after we've already won?

End with one question about what evidence or argument could actually count against her reading of Galileo as hostile rather than friendly — something that would make her drop it, not just complicate it. No generic openers about how she got into philosophy, and please keep the follow-ups tied to the specific reply, not just "can you say more."
