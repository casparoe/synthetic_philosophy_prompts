Here's the fight as I understand it. Camp A says the standard solution concepts — Nash equilibrium, backward induction in sequential games, iterated elimination of dominated strategies — aren't extra assumptions we bolt onto the model. They just *are* what you get when you assume the players are rational and that this rationality is common knowledge. So "common knowledge of rationality" (CKR) is doing the real explanatory work, and the solution concepts are its logical shadow.

Camp B says that's wishful thinking. On their view, CKR is either too weak to pin down what happens, or it's the wrong kind of assumption entirely — it tells you about what players believe at nodes that actually get reached, and says nothing about the counterfactual, never-reached parts of the tree, which is exactly where backward induction lives. So backward induction needs something more than CKR (some stronger or "thicker" notion of rationality, or extra certainty assumptions), and the epistemic program is at best bookkeeping and at worst smuggling the conclusion into the premises. I've also heard someone claim that in some games CKR is flat-out inconsistent with the players knowing which outcome they'll reach, which would be wild if true, but I can't tell whether that's the same point as Camp B's or a different one.

To make this concrete and keep us honest, use this little game as the running example: a two-player sequential game with three decision nodes. Work out the backward-induction path, and then treat that game as the test case for what CKR alone does and doesn't deliver.
