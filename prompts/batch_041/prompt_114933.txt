I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since the old days, and next month I am opening a recurring evening at our group's house: a short paper, two hours of argument, wine available throughout, hard stop at eleven. I have been asked to give the opening remarks at the first one, about five minutes, explaining why we are serving alcohol at a gathering that is nominally for thinking, and what the rules are. The room is mixed: maybe a third of them know the Laws and will notice if I mishandle the Athenian stranger; the rest know the Republic at most, and a few are there mostly for the wine. So I cannot just deliver a mini-lecture on Plato's drinking-party proposal. The passage has to do the structural work of justifying the evening's design, which means the shape of the remarks has to come out of the passage rather than being decorated with it.

What I want to build around is the uncomfortable structure of the Athenian's argument about drinking in the Laws. He does not defend drinking as pleasure or as social lubrication. He defends the supervised drinking party as an institution with a function: it is a cheap, reversible field for practicing self-command, the way war is an expensive, irreversible field for practicing courage. Wine intensifies pleasure and passion, dissolves the fear of disgrace, and returns the drinker to something like a child's condition, which is precisely what makes it usable. It is a test that does not ruin you when you fail, provided the failure happens under supervision. And the whole thing depends on a sober ruler of the feast and on there being a correct use of the practice, as opposed to mere drinking.

The same passage has features that do not transfer cleanly, and I want the remarks to be honest about them rather than smoothing them over. First, the therapeutic strand: wine is also recommended as a remedy for the harshness and sullenness of age, making the old young enough to sing and to feel shame again. That is a different job from testing character, and arguably a different institution. Second, the disciplinary strand: the practice is supposed to work because the drinker is being watched by someone entitled to stop him, and because the law, not the group, sets the terms. Our house has no legislator and does not want one. Third, the proposal sits inside a project that also wants to fix the songs, censor the poetry, and freeze the music; the drinking party is not a free-expression zone in the original.

I want the remarks to distinguish what we are borrowing from what we are declining, and to say plainly what stands in for the sober symposiarch: a role rather than a person, a fixed end time, an explicit agreement about what is and is not decided at the symposium, and a shared norm about who may say that someone has had enough. I also want the speech to land the point that the passage is not about alcohol revealing truth. The drinker becomes childish, not clairvoyant. It is about character becoming visible, to the drinker and to the others, in a setting where that visibility is survivable. That is what makes the evening worth holding, and it is also the thing that can go wrong.

Please write the opening remarks. Five minutes, roughly eight hundred words. Address the room directly, as adults who are there voluntarily. Do not lecture, and do not quote the Laws; paraphrase throughout, and avoid quotation marks entirely unless you are certain of the exact wording. Include one concrete failure mode, a night of this kind that went badly, and say what the rules are meant to do about it. End with the house rules stated plainly, in the voice of someone who has thought about why they exist. And take seriously the objection that a group of people who distrust authority is the worst possible group to run a supervised drinking party. Do not dodge it; give the honest answer, including what we lose by not having a legislator.
