My dad and I have had the same argument like four times now. He says that since nobody can predict the weather more than a couple weeks out, projections for the climate in 2100 are worthless — it's the same chaotic system, so how could anyone possiby know anything about it? I've tried the "weather isn't climate" line and he just rolls his eyes, and honstly I can't defend it any better than the slogan.

So can you actually explain the conceptual stuff underneath this? I want the version that's honest about how much the usual slogans oversell things. In particular:

- Why determinism and predictability come apart, and where the standard way of saying that is too glib. Chaotic doesn't automatically mean unpredictable, right — the solar system is chaotic and still predictable for huge stretches of time? So what actually fixes the horizon: how fast uncertainty grows, how precisely we can measure things, how far out we're looking, something else?
- What stays predictable about a chaotic system even when you can't say where it will be, and why. Is there a principled line between the questions that survive and the ones that don't, or is it just "averages are fine, details aren't"?
- How much of the butterfly effect, as people usually state it, is actually right? Does any small nudge really blow up, or do only some perturbations grow? And if the dynamics are deterministic, where does the "new information" that chaos supposedly generates actually come from?
- Is there any case where even the statistics go bad? I have a vague sense that something like having more than one stable state, or the system wandering around for ages before it settles into its typical behavior, breaks the "the statistics are still predictable" story — is that right, and is it related to the climate case at all?
- And how is it legitimate to trust computer simulations of chaotic systems when we never have the exact initial conditions? I've heard shadowing invoked as the answer — that even if our simulated trajectory isn't the real one, it stays close to some real trajectory — but I've also heard that's a much narrower result than people make it out to be.

American English please, and don't dumb it down — I'd rather have the real thing than a metaphor.
