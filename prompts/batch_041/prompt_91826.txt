I'm writing a piece on how we decide which animals are conscious and how much, and I want to build it around Kafka's "A Report to an Academy" — Red Peter, the ape who learns to drink and shake hands and speak in order to get out of his cage, and who tells the academy that he can no longer describe what his life as an ape was like. Most readings of the story stop at "animals are people too" or at assimilation as self-betrayal. I want something more useful to a reader following the actual debate about the distribution of consciousness across species: whether sentience is one graded scale or a bundle of partly independent capacities, and whether an animal's ability to tell us about its experience is a legitimate criterion or a distorting one.

Here is the difficulty I'd like you to take seriously rather than dissolve. If reportability is the test, then the only animals that pass are the ones we have already enculturated, which makes our evidence about animal minds a record of animals we have remade rather than of animals as they are — and Red Peter is the limit case, a witness whose testimony is void precisely because the transformation that made him able to give it destroyed his access to the thing he is testifying about. Does the story dramatize that problem better than the philosophical arguments do, or does it trade on it — does Kafka's ape actually confirm the reportability criterion by failing it? Where does the reading illuminate a scene, and where does it flatten the story into an illustration? I'd like a critic's essay, faithful to Kafka in both directions, and I don't need a survey of the literature.
