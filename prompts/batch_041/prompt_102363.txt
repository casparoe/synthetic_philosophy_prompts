Had a long, dumb argument with my friend Maya last night about this game her old econ professor used to run — an auction for a single dollar bill where the runner-up still has to pay whatever they bid, but gets nothing. She thinks its just a party trick that shows people are bad at math. I think the interesting part isn't the people, it's the shape of the thing: the game basically manufactures a situation where the only move that isn't an outright loss is to keep bidding, so a perfectly clear-eyed person can see the whole trap laid out and still not be able to get out of it. We went around in circles for two hours and I'm still annoyed about it.

So I want to turn it into a short story, and I don't want the obvious version — two guys at a table bidding past a dollar. Here's the setting I want instead: a small town where, once a year, the right to run the towns only ferry is auctioned off, with the same cruel rule — the second-highest bidder forfeits whatever they bid and gets nothing. Two families have been bidding against each other for three generations. This year the ferry isn't even profitable; everyone can see the license is worth less than the bids are going to climb to, both families know the other knows, and the town council that runs the auction quietly leans on the forfeited runner-up money to fund the school — which both families also know.

Write me the story. Third person, follow one of the two families, maybe 1,500 words. I want the beat where a character can see the entire trap laid out in front of them and still writes the next number on the card — and I don't want it explained to me like a lecture, I want it to just happen and feel inevitable. Don't name the game and don't cite any economists; I don't want it reading like a case study. And one more thing — give me a moment where somebody tries to bind themselves out of it, some promise or rule they swear to, and it doesn't quite hold, because thats the part I actually can't stop thinking about.
