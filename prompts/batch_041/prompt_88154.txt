I'm writing a novel about a professional forecaster and I roped two friends into arguing about his inner life for an evening so I could get the arguments right. They went at it for about an hour, I took notes, and now I definately can't tell who won, which is a problem because the book sort of needs an answer.

Setup: my character keeps a running set of probabilities in a notebook. They don't add up. He'll write down 40% that the bill passes, 40% that it fails, and 30% that it does neither, and he's untroubled that those don't sum to 100. What he does do is track the world well — over ten years, the things he called 70% happened about 70% of the time. And he never bets, on anything, ever. That's a character trait.

Nadia's position: the whole "your credences have to be coherent" business is a rule about money, not about truth. The only real argument for it is that an incoherent person can be walked into a set of bets that loses whatever happens — but my guy never bets, so he's immune, and even if he did, the worst you could say is he'd lose money, not that he believes something false. Meanwhile he's actually accurate, his numbers match the world, and isn't that the thing that matters? She says the fancier arguments that try to rebuild coherence out of accuracy instead of money don't work either, because calling a credence "inaccurate" requires some way of scoring it, and there's no non-arbitrary way to pick the scoring rule — some rules make incoherent people look fine, others make them look terrible, and you can't justify the choice without already assuming coherence. She also thinks an argument that only ever convicts incoherent people is a bit of a cheat: a perfectly consistent guy who's 99% sure of something false gets off completely, which shows the whole framework was never about being right, just about being tidy.

Pete's position: no, the money argument was always the weak one, and it's dodgeable in exactly the way Nadia says. But the accuracy argument doesn't need bets at all. The claim is just this: for any set of numbers that breaks the axioms, there's a coherent set that does better in *every possible world* — not on average, not usually, every world. So the incoherent person isn't losing a bet he never made; his numbers are simply a worse guess than numbers he could have had instead, and he could have known that in advance. My character is accurate by luck — the world that happened to be actual was kind to him, but the notebook was dominated all along. And the scoring-rule worry is backwards: the result holds for any rule that rewards accuracy in the minimal sense, so the arbitrariness objection never bites. As for the overconfident guy, sure, he's coherent, but he isn't making a mistake he can be blamed for — given his evidence those were his best numbers and the world just went against him. You can't demand accuracy of someone; you can only demand that they not be beaten by their own lights.

Nadia came back at that. "Beaten by their own lights" — that's the whole problem. Pete's argument only shows the incoherent numbers are worse *from the standpoint of some coherent set of numbers*. Why should my guy care? His own numbers say his numbers are fine, and they track the world, so the charge is that he's failing a standard he doesn't have and doesn't need. She said the "every possible world" move is doing something sneaky too: there's no world in which you get to watch how the incoherent numbers would have done under other worlds, so the comparison is against an alternative version of him that doesn't exist. And she said if accuracy is the only good, then a person who's perfectly coherent with credence 1 in something false is doing nothing epistemically wrong, which she finds absurd.

Pete said fine, that's a real cost, but the alternative is worse: if you deny accuracy is the fundamental good, you owe an account of what is, and every candidate he can think of either collapses back into accuracy or smuggles in something like "matching the evidence," which is just another rule about numbers. He said he'd rather bite the bullet on the overconfident guy than say coherence is arbitrary. There was a stretch at the end about whether the standard updating rule is also justified by accuracy, Pete claiming it's the rule that keeps you maximally accurate from your own point of view, Nadia saying that argument obviously assumes your current numbers are trustworthy, which is exactly what's in dispute for her guy, and then some back and forth about whether the 40/40/30 notebook even counts as representing beliefs rather than, like, moods.

So: I need a verdict, point by point. (1) Is the betting/money argument the real basis for coherence, or a red herring? (2) Does the "dominated in every possible world" move rescue the accuracy story, or is Nadia right that it's comparing him to someone who doesn't exist? (3) Does the scoring-rule arbitrariness point land? (4) Is the coherent-but-overconfident case fatal, or a bullet Pete can bite? (5) Can "accuracy from your own point of view" justify the standard updating rule? (6) Does the notebook count as belief at all? Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, tell me who's closer overall, and say what both of them are missing. And if there's a name for the thing they're actually fighting about, tell me that too — I'd like to know whether this is a settled fight or a live one before I put it in a character's mouth.
