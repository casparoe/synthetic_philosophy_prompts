A friend in my local-history group keeps settling arguments with "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" — most recently to wave off the fact that a village named in a later tax roll simply doesn't appear in an earlier survey of the same district, which she reads as the village existing all along and the survey just missing it. I know the sentence is true in some boring way, but I want the full menu of precise things it could be claiming when people actually deploy it: the tautological reading (no evidence entails no absence), the probabilistic reading (a missing record doesn't shift my credence at all), the strong reading (silence is itself positive evidence something wasn't there), and whatever else lives in between. For each, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false, and which one actually backs what she did with it. In passing, say how this relates to the "argument from silence" as historians are supposed to discipline it — something about whether a source would have been expected to record the thing if it happened, and whether the source is the kind we can trust on that front — and whether any of this changes for texts versus archaeology. No reading list, just the readings and your verdicts, as a list I can take back to the group.
