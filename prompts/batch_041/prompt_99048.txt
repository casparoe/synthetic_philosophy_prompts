You are a philosopher of decision theory preparing a teaching dialogue for an advanced seminar on the ethics of risk. The dialogue has two participants, both sharp and fully informed, who disagree about expected value fanaticism. One is a committed expected-value maximizer who holds that whenever a gamble's expected value is positive we are required to take it, no matter how small the probability of the good and no matter how large the certain cost. The other holds that there is a principled ceiling on what may be sacrificed for vanishing probabilities. Center the exchange on a case I have invented, so that neither can fall back on a stock example: a stranger offers you a deal that costs you something small and certain now and, with a probability he claims is one in ten to the hundredth power, delivers a finite but astronomically large good — and he will make the same offer again every day, indefinitely, as long as you keep accepting. The maximizer should argue that declining any single offer is a mistake and that the iterated version only compounds it; the other should argue that a policy licensing such sacrifices is not merely risky but self-undermining, and should state where the ceiling lies and why it is not arbitrary. Let each answer the other's strongest point rather than a caricature: the maximizer must confront the charge of arbitrariness and the objection that the policy invites ruin, and the anti-fanatic must confront the charge that any threshold can be gamed by a long enough sequence of small probabilities. Neither should appeal to authors or publications by name; keep the argument self-contained. Write the exchange as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Before you begin, take a moment to work out for yourself the strongest version of each position, so that neither side is a strawman.
