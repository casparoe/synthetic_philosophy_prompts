I am a novelist, and I have written a line of dialogue that I need to understand rather than merely admire. A woman says something warm to an old friend, and the warmth is carried by a word that is a slur; both of them belong to the group the word targets, and they are alone when she says it. Some chapters later I want the same woman to say the same thing to the same friend in a room with other people in it. I need to know whether the two occurrences do the same work, and I have stopped trusting my ear. What I want from you is not a scene but a controlled construction: a case engineered so that the contending accounts of what slurs do are compelled to give their verdicts in the open. The verdicts must be generated by the accounts and not stipulated. If the case can only be made to work by assuming the conclusion, tell me so, and tell me which of the specifications is the one that cannot be met.

Please build that construction, and then demonstrate, step by step, that it satisfies each specification below.

The construction. One sentence, no more than thirty words, in the mouth of a person who is not thinking about language, written out in full with W standing in for the word. It is said twice: once in private, among members of the group, and once in public, with people outside the group present. Between the two occurrences exactly one thing may change — the social setting, meaning who else is present and what kind of occasion it is. Everything else is held fixed: the same speaker, the same addressee, the same words, the same warmth, the same position of the word within the sentence, and use rather than mention in both. The sentence must be a kindness to the addressee; the speaker must be an uncontested member of the group the word targets, and the addressee a member as well. The word must be a real one in current use, whose standing as a slur nobody would dispute. The scene must be ordinary — a kitchen, a car, a shop doorway — and must contain no classroom, no courtroom, no dictionary, no discussion of the word, and no quotation or mention of it.

The verdicts to be produced.

First, the account on which the word's derogatory force is part of its truth-conditional contribution, carried by the word wherever it appears and whoever speaks it. This account must return the same verdict in both settings, and that verdict must be that the sentence is derogatory — in the private setting quite as much as in the public one. It may not soften the private case by appeal to the speaker's warmth or to the friendship. If its only escape is to say that the two occurrences involve different words, say so, and say what the escape costs.

Second, the account on which the derogation is a separate, non-at-issue layer of meaning, expressed rather than stated. This account too must return the same verdict in both settings, namely derogatory, and must earn it with the standard diagnostics run on the private occurrence: negation, questioning, embedding in a conditional, and the prefixing of perhaps. The derogation must survive all four, and must not be cancellable by a later disclaimer of intent without a repair.

Third, the account on which the offence lies in the social act of using the word — a matter of norms and licence rather than of meaning. This account must return different verdicts in the two settings, exonerating the private occurrence and condemning the public one. It must reach its exoneration without appeal to the speaker's attitude, her warmth, or her intention, since all of these are identical in the two occurrences, and it must say precisely which facts about the setting do the work: whether licence attaches to the speaker, to the audience, or to the occasion. If its exoneration amounts only to saying that the word remains derogatory though permitted, rather than that no derogation occurs at all, it must say that plainly.

One further constraint. The word must be one for which no accepted neutral counterpart exists, so that no account can be stated or tested by substituting a neutral word for it. If the word you choose does have such a counterpart, say what difference it makes.

Then the audit.

- For each of the three accounts, give the verdict for each setting and the reasoning that generates it.
- Identify the single feature of the construction that does the work, and say why the case fails without it.
- Say what becomes of each verdict when that feature is removed — when the private occurrence is made public, when the addressee is replaced by someone outside the group, and when the speaker is replaced by an outsider — and whether the case then collapses into the uncontroversial one.
- Say whether anything separates the first account from the second here, or whether the construction leaves them notational variants of each other.
- Say how a defender of the account that fares worst — the one obliged to call an affectionate exchange between friends derogatory — would attack the construction, and whether the attack costs that account anything or merely reduces it to one of the others.
