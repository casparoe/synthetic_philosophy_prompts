I'm maybe 200 pages into a novel set in Edinburgh in the 1750s -- the protagonist is a merchant's widow who's been reading Hume's essays and is convinced she's caught him contradicting himself, and honestly that subplot is turning into the whole book -- and last week two friends came over who both know far more economics than I do, and they ended up in a full-blown thing over exactly the passage I've been trying to dramatize. Let me reconstruct it as faithfully as I can, because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. Ines went first: Hume says money isn't one of the wheels of trade, it's the oil that makes them turn more smoothly, and that within any single kingdom it doesn't matter whether money is plentiful or scarce, since prices simply rise to match the quantity. So when he then says the good policy of the magistrate consists only in keeping money still increasing -- because that keeps alive a spirit of industry in the nation and increases the stock of labour, which he says is where all real power and riches actually live -- she says that's just flatly inconsistent, a mercantile leftover he never cleaned out. She also noted, a bit smugly, that the essay is from the 1742 volume.

Marcus said that's too quick. On his reading, the neutrality claim is about the long run and about comparing two kingdoms that each already have a settled price level, whereas the magistrate line is about the path: the new money doesn't raise prices all at once, it passes from hand to hand first, and while it's still passing it goads people into working harder and trading more. So there's no contradiction, just two different time horizons, and he thinks this is why people read Hume as an early theorist of money mattering in the interval before it gets priced in. Ines came back: fine, but then the "good policy" is a treadmill -- the stimulus only exists while the increase is still being absorbed, so the magistrate has to keep inflating forever, and the moment anyone anticipates it the lag disappears and the whole effect with it. Marcus said Hume isn't recommending anything, he's describing why rich trading nations are industrious, and anyway Hume can't believe money is a lever you can just pull, because he's suspicious of banks and paper credit and at one point even toys with the idea that the ideal bank would be one that locked up every coin it recieved and never put any of it back into circulation -- which, Marcus said, only makes sense if Hume thinks manufactured money is close to worthless. Ines pounced on that: if money is of no consequence, why does it matter whether it's coin or paper? Why does he care about the dearness of provisions and labour pricing English manufactures out of foreign markets, and about cheap-labour countries underselling the rich ones? Marcus said that's the international case, where relative price levels do the work, and Hume keeps that seperate from the domestic neutrality claim, and that the bit about manufactures migrating to poorer nations is about real factors like cheap provisions and cheap labour, not about the quantity of money at all. He also floated something I couldn't follow about Hume's essay on interest -- that Hume denies low interest is caused by plenty of money and traces it to the growth of commerce instead -- though he admitted he might be mixing that up with the essay on the balance of trade, and Ines said she thought he had it backwards but wasn't sure either.

Can you adjudicate this move by move, the way a referee would? I don't want a general summary of Hume on money, I want each of their claims rated from clearly mistaken through clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each, including the small factual squabbles they couldn't settle (which collection the money essay is actually from, and whether Marcus has the interest essay right). And then tell me what both of them are missing -- because it feels like they're each defending a Hume who's tidier than the one actually on the page, and I need to know which tidying is less of a distortion before I put any of it in my character's mouth.
