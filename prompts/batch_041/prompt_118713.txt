I teach a module on reporting mass violence to a room of journalism masters students, and the first session is always a disaster — I stand there explaining what the treaty definition actually requires and you can watch the room decide the whole thing is a legal technicality that has nothing to do with them. So I want to open with a laugh instead. Could you write me a parody of an internal newsroom style guide, the kind of memo a standards editor circulates to the international desk about how to describe an atrocity? Deadpan the whole way through, no winking, formatted as numbered clauses with little section headers, maybe 650-750 words. Make up the organisation (a wire service, a regional agency, whatever feels plausible) and don't use a real outlet's name or a real editor's name. The humour has to come out of the actual material rather than being bolted on top of it, which means the guide should be quietly obsessed with the real problems: that the definition turns on intent to destroy a group, in whole or in part, and not on the size of the body count, so the desk is forever trying to infer a state of mind from a paper trail; that the list of protected groups is short — national, ethnic, racial, religious — while Lemkin's own version of the crime was much wider, covering a group's language, culture, political and social institutions and economic life, which the treaty then narrowed; the dodge where an official will concede that acts of genocide have occurred without ever conceding that the situation is one (I'm fairly sure there was a briefing in the 90s where a spokesperson did exactly that and a reporter asked how many acts it takes to make the thing itself — whether or not I'm remembering it right, I want that move formalised as a house rule); and the awkward fact that using the word is itself a move with consequences, which is why waiting for a court is not the neutral option it looks like. I've had a go at this myself twice and it came out either to on-the-nose or so dry that definately nobody would laugh.

I'd also like an approved-terms ladder — a ranked list running from the safest euphemism up to the word itself, with notes on when a desk is permitted to climb a rung — plus a short section on attribution and scare quotes, and something about the gap between what a court has found and what the desk can see with its own eyes years earlier. Two things I care about. First, the target of the joke is the machinery — the hedging, the tiering, the deference, the fear of being seen to take a side — and never the victims or the dead; if a line would land as a joke about mass killing rather than about the people who write memos about mass killing, cut it. Second, it has to be legible to a student who has never read the convention, but someone who works in this area should read it and think yeah, that's basically what happens, so please include at least one clause that isn't an exaggeration at all — something a specialist will recognise as the plain state of affairs, just stated flatly and filed under house style. A closing section on who has to sign off before a word goes out, and how long that takes, would be a nice place to end. No citations or real cases needed.
