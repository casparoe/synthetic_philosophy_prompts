I teach an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of human extinction, and I want to use Alfonso Cuarón's Children of Men (2006) as a test case for Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture in Death and the Afterlife: the thought experiment in which we learn that human life will end a few decades after our own deaths, and his claim that much of what we value rests on an unexamined confidence that human life will continue without us. Cuarón's film is that experiment dramatized — two decades of infertility, an eighteen-year-old as the youngest person alive, a state that treats refugees as a security problem, and Theo's cousin the Minister of Arts enjoying the pleasures of the past in a walled enclave — so does it confirm the conjecture or refute it? Keep the reading faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of Scheffler, and don't soften Scheffler to fit the film. I especially want to know where the film resists him — whether what decays in it is the capacity to value, or instead hope, solidarity, and the social conditions under which valuing is sustained; whether Theo's partial re-engagement through Kee and her child is a counterexample (love reviving valuation with no confidence in any future) or an instance of the conjecture (what revives him is a future, in the shape of a child); and whether the film suggests the conjecture needs restating, for instance that what we need is not a real future but the form of one, or that our confidence is carried by particular attachments rather than by humanity in the abstract. Let the final point say what the film adds to the debate that Scheffler's argument alone does not. You cannot consult the film, so flag what you are unsure of rather than asserting it; and if the film is not really a test case for the conjecture, or the reading I am asking for cannot be done honestly, say so plainly instead of writing a smooth essay around the difficulty. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
