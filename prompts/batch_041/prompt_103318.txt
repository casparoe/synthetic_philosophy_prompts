I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long while, and the Judy Benjamin problem is one of the few puzzles where the standard treatments still leave me unsatisfied. I want to settle, for my own thinking, which responses to it actually deserve respect. I'll lay out the setup from scratch, because I'd rather you reason from the description than from however the literature happens to be filed in your memory.

The setup: an agent's uncertainty is divided into four cells — Red-and-HQ, Red-and-elsewhere, Blue-and-HQ, Blue-and-elsewhere. Her prior splits evenly between Red and Blue overall, and within each color she has a fixed prior over HQ versus elsewhere; take it to be three-quarters HQ if Red and one-half HQ if Blue, so the four cells carry weights 3/8, 1/8, 1/4, and 1/4. She then receives a message that does not tell her where she is. It tells her something about the relation between being in Red and being in HQ — in the clean version, that if she is in Red then she is in HQ. The question is what a rational agent should do with an input of that shape.

Candidate responses, described by what they do rather than by who proposed them:

(a) read the message as the material conditional (not-Red, or HQ) and condition on it;
(b) read it as a constraint that the conditional probability of HQ given Red is 1, and otherwise leave the prior as untouched as possible;
(c) read it as that same constraint but choose the new distribution that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence from the prior subject to it;
(d) read it as soft evidence bearing on the truth of the conditional, handled by Jeffrey-style probability kinematics rather than by conditioning;
(e) hold that the input is not the sort of thing that should move a Bayesian at all, or that the puzzle exposes a defect in the framework rather than a rule to be chosen.

What I want is a tier ranking of these by how much you would trust the underlying rule as a general-purpose account of how a rational agent should respond to a report about a conditional, not merely by which is popular. For each, give a sentence or two on what earns it its tier, and flag any that you think are popular for bad reasons.

Then stress-test the ordering against a variant: the message is graded rather than categorical — she is told that if she is in Red she is probably (say 80%) in HQ — and the source is itself somewhat unreliable, so that even the graded report is only a noisy signal. Say which of the rules above extend cleanly to that case and which quietly come apart, and whether that shifts the ranking.

I would rather you push back than perform agreement. If the puzzle as I have framed it is confused — if the candidate responses are not really alternatives to one another, say, or if ranking them is the wrong frame because they are answering different questions — tell me plainly instead of producing a ranking anyway. Be explicit about how confident you are in the ordering, which parts you are least sure of, and what would move a rule up or down a tier.
