For the past year or so I have been sitting in on a philosophy of mind seminar run by a colleague in the philosophy department. I am a software engineer by trade, working mostly on distributed storage systems, and I have no formal training in philosophy, so I have been attending largely as a curious outsider. My colleague has now asked me to contribute a single question to the end-of-term assessment for the module, which is taken by third-year undergraduates and taught master's students, and the topic is the extended mind thesis. Her brief to me was that the question should not be answerable by reciting the Otto and Inga case and the parity principle, and that it should ideally be written by someone who does not already know which objections happen to be fashionable; I am therefore writing to you for the question, a model answer and a marking scheme.

I have devised a case of my own to hang the question on, borrowed from the sort of problem I deal with at work, and I would like the question built around it rather than around the standard examples. Two colleagues, Priya and Tom, share a single note-taking system: a server-hosted store that both of them read and write, that is maintained and updated by a third-party vendor neither of them controls, and that runs a routine which silently rewrites and compresses older entries overnight, so that the text they consult in the morning is not always the text they typed. Priya consults the store dozens of times a day for facts she would otherwise have to remember — names, commitments, half-finished arguments — treats what she finds there without question, and is functionally lost when the service goes down. Tom does the same, and they frequently read and edit the same entries. Neither of them can tell, at the moment of recall, which sentences originated with her, which with him, and which with the summarising routine.

What I want from you is three things. First, the exam question itself, pitched so that a good student can answer it in a sixty-minute essay under exam conditions with no access to notes. Second, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five, which should name the specific moves a first-class answer must make, the moves that separate it from a solid but unremarkable answer, and the specific errors that ought to cost marks — I would much rather the errors be ones students genuinely commit than ones invented for the sake of having something to deduct for. The students will have read the 1998 Clark and Chalmers paper, Clark's later book-length defence, and the standard objections, so the question may presuppose those; it should not require any citation, reading list or reference to material they have not seen. Finally, please tell me honestly whether the case I have invented is doing real philosophical work or is merely decorative. If the shared ownership and the silent rewriting turn out not to put any genuine pressure on the thesis, I would rather know, and I would rather the question be rewritten around whichever feature does bite.
