I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I'm writing a seminar paper on epistemic norms. I've been reading about van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I think I understand the basic idea: a rational agent's current credence in a proposition should be constrained by her credences about her own future credences—roughly, if she is certain that her future credence in A will be x, then her current credence in A should also be x. But this seems to land me in an absurdity. Suppose I know that tomorrow I will be given a drug that will make me irrationally convinced that I can fly. I am certain that my future credence in the proposition that I can fly will be very high. Does the Reflection principle then require me to believe right now that I can fly? That seems clearly irrational, and I don't think any reasonable epistemic norm could demand it. So I'm wondering whether the Reflection principle is simply false, or whether I'm misunderstanding its scope. Is it meant as a universal requirement of rationality, or is it a more local coherence constraint that only applies when one's future self is not epistemically compromised? And if it is only local, what exactly is the condition that rules out the drug case—is it that the future credence is based on information I now regard as inadmissible, or is it something about the reliability of the future self? I've also heard people describe Reflection as a diachronic norm about how to change one's beliefs over time, but I'm not sure that's right either, since the principle seems to be about the coherence of one's present credences rather than a rule for updating. Any clarification would be very helpful, because I suspect my confusion is making me misread the secondary literature.
