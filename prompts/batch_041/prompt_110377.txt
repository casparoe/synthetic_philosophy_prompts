Two people from my local philosophy meetup got into it in the discord last week about the omnipotence paradox, and it went on for hours and I honestly couldnt tell who was landing hits. I said I'd find someone who could referee. Can you go through it move by move — who's right about what, where each of them is actually off, and what if anything both of them are missing? I don't want "Ben wins", I want each move rated and justified.

Anna: the whole can-God-make-a-stone-so-heavy-he-cant-lift-it thing isn't a cute gotcha, it's a proof that "omnipotent" is an incoherent word — same way "square circle" isn't a really hard object to make, it's nothing. And theists never answer it, they just redefine the word until the counterexample stops counting.

Ben: no, the standard reply isn't a dodge. Omnipotence just means the power to do anything possible, and a stone too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift isn't a possible object, it's a contradiction, so him not being able to make one isn't a limit on him. Nobody thinks you're less powerful because you can't make 2+2=5.

Anna: that IS the dodge — you've set it up so every counterexample is by definition not a counterexample. And it costs you something: logic is now sitting above God. If he's really unlimited he can make contradictions true, and then the stone is back on the table. (She said something about Descartes holding that, I have no idea if thats right.)

Ben: basically nobody defends that anymore, and even the medievals had a version that works — God's absolute power vs his ordained power. Absolutely he can do anything non-contradictory, but he's freely bound himself to a particular order, his promises, the laws of nature, and "can't" is always relative to that self-imposed order. Asking whether God can break a promise is like asking whether a chess master can move his rook diagonally in a game he chose to play by the rules: absolutely yes, in the game no.

Anna: then "omnipotent" is a rubber word that shrinks to fit whatever you need that day. And a self-imposed limit is still a limit — if the master literally cannot lose, playing a handicap game doesn't change that. Here's the version I think actually bites: can God create a second omnipotent being? If yes, there are two beings who can each do anything, so each can stop the other doing something, so neither is omnipotent. If no, then there's a thing he can't do. Either way the word breaks, and telling me that's not a possible object just begs the question again.

Ben: he said the second-omnipotent thing is the stone paradox with extra steps, and that Anna keeps smuggling in the premise that power only counts as power if it's unblockable, which he says she never actually argues for.

That's the whole thing. Where does it actually stand?
