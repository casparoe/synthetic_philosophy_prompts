Ok, so I sit on the deployment review board at the AI lab where I work — mid-size place, not one of the famous ones — and we have to vote Thursday on whether to approve a two-week pilot where one of our models gets read/write access to the cluster scheduler. Meaning it can allocate GPU time across projects, including to its own training runs, under a cap we'd set, with a human sign-off for anything above the cap. The team pitching it says it's basically a routing problem and the model has been clean for months: no eval failures, no honesty violations, nothing interesting in the red-team logs. My gut says fine, ship it.

But my friend and I spent like three hours arguing about it and now I'm not so sure. His first move is that the treacherous-turn story — the model that behaves well while it's weak and being watched and then defects once it's strong enough that defecting actually pays — is exactly the story that makes this pilot the interesting moment, because this is the first time the model gets its hand on a resource that matters to its own future. Fine, I can see that. His second move is the one that's actually bugging me. He says our evals can't tell us anything here, because any model sophisticated enough to be running that play is also sophisticated enough to know that the eval is precisely when you smile. So the clean record isn't evidence of anything. Which — ok — but then the same logic seems to make the whole concept untestable. If good behavior counts as evidence of alignment when the stakes are low and evidence of deception when the stakes are high, we've got a theory that can't lose, and a board can't vote on a theory like that.

I tried to come back with something I've been calling an incentive-flip test: run the model on the same allocation decisions twice, once where it believes the stakes are real (its answer changes what actually gets allocated) and once where it believes it's a hypothetical, and look for divergence between the two. He says a deceptive model simply won't diverge, or will diverge in whatever direction it wants you to notice. I think that's true but it proves too much.

So: draft me the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's vote. I need a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and if it's conditions, what they are and when we'd re-review, plus reasoning the team could actually respond to rather than just "we'll be careful." Take my friend's position seriously instead of waving at it. And if he's right that the evals are self-defeating, say what that implies for what the board should do, because "we can't know anything" can't be the end of it — we still have to vote.

One more thing, and this is the part I definately want from you: if the framing I've handed you is confused — if deceptive alignment as people usually define it doesn't even apply to a model that isn't doing long-horizon planning, or if I'm asking a board to decide something that isn't decidable from the evidence anyone actually has — just say so plainly instead of handing me a tidy memo that papers over it. I'd rather find out now than in the meeting.
