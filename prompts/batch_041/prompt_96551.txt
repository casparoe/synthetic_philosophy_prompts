I've been going back and forth with a friend about path dependence — the whole idea that some outcomes get "locked in" by early contingent choices, so that if you rewound history and ran it again you'd plausibly land somewhere quite different. He's a thoroughgoing determinist about social history: he reckons the same outcomes would've arrived anyway, maybe on a slower timetable, because the underlying pressures (geography, resources, economic logic, whatever) do all the real work and the supposedly pivotal "contingent" events are just froth on the wave. I'm not convinced, but I also don't want to just wave the QWERTY keyboard at him, because honestly that example has been done to death and I'm not even sure it proves what people say it does. What I want from you is a proper essay that actually takes a side on this rather than sitting on the fence, and that earns its verdict rather than gesturing at one.

To keep yourself honest, I'd like you to build the essay around a test I've been kicking around — call it the rewind test: take some concrete historical fork (pick something broadly known, not some obscure episode), then imagine rewinding to that moment with the same agents, the same information, and the same constraints, and only the fine-grained coin-flips redrawn. Then ask whether the eventual outcome survives that rewinding or collapses into something else. And I want you to distinguish two grades of lock-in while you're at it — a thin version, where an early choice merely sets a default that later pressures could easily have overridden, and a thick version, where the early choice actively builds the very machinery (incentives, institutions, expectations) that keeps the alternative from ever re-emerging. My hunch is that most of the impressive-sounding examples of "history got locked in" are thin dressed up as thick, and that the determinist's case is stronger than path-dependence fans admit but weaker than he thinks — but don't just agree with me, test it, and if the argument runs the other way then say so and defend it.

So: weigh the strongest case for genuine contingency against the strongest case that the same broad outcomes would have arrived regardless, use the rewind test and the thin/thick distinction as your analytical spine, and land on a definite verdict — including, if you think it's warranted, a verdict about which kinds of cases the whole path-dependence framing is even useful for.
