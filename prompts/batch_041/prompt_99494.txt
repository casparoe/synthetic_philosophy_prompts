Ok so I'm a retired biologist — spent about 35 years doing field work on insect populations before I hung up my boots — and since then I've been reading philosophy, which turns out to be a whole different sport. Here's my situation. A friend of mine, smart guy, did a PPE degree years ago and still keeps up with the stuff, gave me his take on "luck egalitarianism" and it sounded at first like a genuinely good idea and then, by the end of his little speech, like something no sane person could possibly hold. I want to know how much of what he said I should actually beleive.

His pitch, as best I remember it: luck egalitarianism is the view that the only thing that makes an inequality unjust is whether it traces back to luck. If you're worse off than me because of brute luck — genes, parents, the country you were born in, getting hit by a bus — then that's unfair and the state should even it out. If you're worse off because of choices you made, that's on you, and the rest of us owe you nothing. So a luck egalitarian wants to fully neutralize the effects of luck and leave the effects of choice completely untouched. The catch, he said, is that this makes the state the enemy of the imprudent: the guy who won't wear a seatbelt, ends up paralyzed, gets left to die in the street rather than have his care paid for. And that's so repugnant that it shows the whole thing is just strict equality wearing a clever disguise — in the end it wants to equalize talent and effort alike, which is absurd. And on top of that, he said, you can't even state the doctrine without assuming we can tell luck from choice, which nobody has ever managed to do, so it isn't just wrong, it's incoherent.

Here's my trouble. As a scientist the basic move appeals to me — it's like separating real signal from noise, you only want to correct for the stuff the person had no hand in. So I'm inclined to think he's being unfair to it, but I don't trust my instincts yet and I don't want to defend a position just because it sounds tidy.

So: walk me through his speech sentence by sentence. What would a serious luck egalitarian nod along to, what's a fair simplification that any competent popularizer would make, and what is straight-up a misrepresentation of the position? What would the defender say about the seatbelt guy, and about the "disguised strict equality" charge — does his conclusion actually follow from anything true he said? And last thing, is the "can't tell luck from choice" point a fatal objection, or just a hard problem that everyone in that neighborhood has to deal with? Thanks.
