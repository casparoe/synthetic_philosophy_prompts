I chair the standards committee of an international consortium of public research laboratories, and in three weeks the consortium's governing board votes on whether to adopt a binding safeguards floor for frontier models deployed in critical infrastructure. The board has asked my committee for a written opinion recommending an instrument, and I need it in circulation before the meeting.

The board has framed the decision as a commitment race, and I think that framing is correct. Each major bloc now has reason to lock in its preferred floor before a rival does, because the first standard to become entrenched tends to become the de facto global baseline: reversing an entrenched technical standard is costly, and coordinating the world onto a second one afterwards is harder still. The race is not really about who is stricter. It is about who moves first, and the danger is that moving first locks in a floor that no party would have chosen with more time to deliberate.

Two instruments are on the table. The first is an unconditional floor: our consortium adopts its (relatively strict) safeguards standard now, unilaterally, for a fixed term of five years, with no dependence on what anyone else does. The second is a conditional instrument: our floor binds only if at least two other major jurisdictions adopt an equivalent floor within eighteen months, and if they do not, it lapses. A majority of the committee favours the second, on the ground that the unconditional version hands the rival bloc the benefit of our restraint without securing any reciprocal restraint. A minority holds that a commitment which can lapse on someone else's inaction is not a commitment at all but a policy with an exit, that rivals will price it accordingly, and that if we want to set the baseline we must simply set it.

There is a further wrinkle the board has not yet appreciated. Both drafts contain a ratchet clause: once the floor takes effect, it may be raised but never lowered for the term of the instrument. That clause is what makes either instrument a genuine commitment rather than a promise, and it is also what makes the race dangerous, since an irreversibility that would stabilize a good standard would equally stabilize a bad one.

Please draft the committee's opinion, under 1,200 words, addressed to the governing board and written so that a member could vote from it. It should recommend one course of action — either instrument, a modified version, or a reasoned refusal to adopt either — state what the board should actually do and by when, and give the reasoning rather than merely the conclusion. It must treat the strongest case for the position it rejects honestly, not as a strawman, and it must say what would count as the recommendation having been wrong: what development over the next year or two should lead the board to conclude that this decision was a mistake, and what it should then do about it. Please also address, directly, whether a conditional instrument can do the work of a commitment at all, and whether the ratchet clause changes that answer. No citations are needed; I will add references myself.
