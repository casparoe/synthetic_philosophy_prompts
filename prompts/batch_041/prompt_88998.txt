My friend and I got into it last night about honor cultures vs. victimhood cultures — he says honor at least makes people own their stuff, I said dignity culture is the obvious answer, neither of us convinced anybody. But a thing came up in the argument that I can't name and it's bugging me.

When someone has actually been wronged, they often refuse the exact thing that would settle it, and not because they're holding out for more. The guy in my office who got a mediated apology from the coworker who'd been needling him for months — HR sat them down, the coworker read the thing off, and he walked out more furious than he went in. Or my sister, who won't take "I'm sorry, I didn't mean it like that" from our dad, because, she says, "I didn't mean it" is just a way of not admitting he aimed it. What she wants isn't an apology, it's for him to see it the way she sees it without her having to explain it. An apology that arrives on schedule, or because someone made him give it, or that just repeats her own description back at her, is worse than nothing — it proves he still doesn't get it. And then everyone else reads her refusal as her being difficult.

Is there an established name for this? I know it's somewhere near the honor/dignity/face stuff and the victimhood-culture thing, and I know lawyers have written about court-ordered apologies, but none of those quite fit — honor seems to be about your standing with everyone else, not about what's going on inside the person who did it. Also, is this specific to being wronged, or is it just the general fact that some goods (love, a real gift, sincerity) can't be delivered to order? Don't just hand me a label. I want to know where the edges are, what the nearest things are that aren't it, and whether there's a real distinction here or I'm just describing stubbornness with extra steps.
