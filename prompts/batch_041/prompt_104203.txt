I'm a second-year philosophy student and I'm taking a seminar on nineteenth-century critiques of modernity, where we have just spent three weeks on Kierkegaard. Most of that time went on the writings of the mid-1840s in which he attacks the daily press as an engine of levelling, and on the passages in his long review of a contemporary Danish novel where he treats the public as a phantom or abstraction rather than any actual collection of people, and chatter as the mode of speech proper to it. Our tutor has set an unusual assignment: take a phenomenon that did not exist in Kierkegaard's lifetime, project him forward onto it, and argue for the projection in a way that could survive the objection that we are simply dressing up our own opinions as his. I have deliberately chosen a small, concrete case rather than social media in general, because the general version is too easy and lets me get away with vague gestures towards loneliness and distraction. The case is a discussion platform of the sort a few hundred thousand people might use, with three design features. First, every account is pseudonymous, and nothing about a user's history, reputation or earlier contributions is visible to anyone else. Second, the only number displayed beside a contribution is a count of endorsements. Third, a language model will, on request, draft a reply to any post in the voice of the person requesting it, so that a contribution can be fluent, well-formed and entirely detached from anyone's considered view.

What I want from you is the substance of the argument, not an outline. Begin with the diagnosis: what would Kierkegaard make of this arrangement, and in particular is it continuous with the press he attacked, or does the difference in kind matter, so that his categories need to be stretched or replaced? I am suspicious of the easy answer that it is simply more of the same, and equally suspicious of the easy answer that it is a wholly new form of unreality. Then move to what he would prescribe, bearing in mind that he had no institutional power, no interest in reforming institutions, and no expectation that the crowd would be improved by better information. I would like you to keep at least three distinctions alive rather than collapsing them into a general complaint about modern life: between the crowd and the public, between levelling as a process and any particular agent who levels, and between what a person is and what a person appears to be in a medium that shows only counts.

Two further things I would like handled explicitly rather than passed over. The first is the self-consistency problem: he published under pseudonyms himself and conducted a public feud through a satirical Copenhagen weekly, so say plainly where his own practice sits awkwardly with the critique instead of smoothing it over — I would much rather have the friction than a tidy answer. The second is a question about the reach of the whole exercise: does the diagnosis depend on a religious telos, the single individual standing before God, in such a way that a secular reader can take over the diagnosis but not the remedy? If so, say what is left of the critique once that telos is removed. Throughout, mark which of the claims you attribute to him are conceptual and which are empirical, since the empirical ones are the ones a sceptical reader could actually falsify. Close with a short, honest statement of what this kind of anachronistic projection cannot establish — what it is doing if it is not prediction, and where it stops being interpretation and starts being invention. Please write the whole thing in British English, and there is no need for citations or reading lists; I want the argument, not the apparatus.
