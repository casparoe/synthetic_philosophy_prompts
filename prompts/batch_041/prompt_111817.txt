I'm in a seminar on theories of well-being and last week was the experience machine -- the Nozick setup, float in a tank, machine feeds you any experience you like, you can't tell the difference, would you plug in. Honestly I've read that thought experiment a hundred times and I could recite the standard replies in my sleep, so the thing that actually got us arguing was a variant my roommate threw out at the end of the session. His version: the machine can give you the experience of having refused the machine. It can hand you the relief of saying no, the satisfaction of picking the messy real world, the memory of standing there with your hand on the switch and walking away. So the experience of choosing is simulable; it's the choosing that isn't. Which means if you're a hedonist the two lives are experientially identical and nothing's missing, and if you're not a hedonist you owe an account of what's lost that doesn't just say "the experience of reality," because that's in the tank too. That's the spot I want the poem to sit in.

So: for the last session I want a poem, 16 to 24 lines, no fixed form, in the voice of someone talking to a person who isn't in the room -- a partner, a friend, whoever, just someone absent. The speaker is explaining that they chose the real world and turned the machine down. The catch is that every single claim the speaker makes about their own choice has to be a claim the machine could have made true by simulation. So by the end a careful reader genuinely cannot tell whether this is a poem about refusing or a poem the machine is writing for someone who's inside it, and the poem must not resolve that either way. I want a refrain, word for word the same, at least three times, and it has to stay true on both readings. And don't announce the ambiguity in the last line -- the last line should be the same kind of statement as everything else. No Matrix imagery, no blue pills, no cords going into the back of the skull.

It should be accurate enough that the hedonist in our seminar reads it and thinks "yeah, and that's fine," and the person who isn't reads it and thinks "no, something's gone missing," and both of them agree the poem got the setup right. Some context that might matter for the voice: the seminar room is always to hot, we're all a bit fried by the end of term, and I'll be reading this out loud, so it has to work in the ear.
