I host a monthly reading group for people who came to philosophy through LessWrong and adjacent corners of the internet; most of us are comfortable with basic probability and expected utility, though only a couple have formal training in decision theory. Last month we did Newcomb's problem and it turned into a two-hour argument about causal versus evidential decision theory, so I know this group will not accept a hand-wave. I want to run a session on Good's theorem -- the result that the expected value of information is nonnegative for an expected utility maximizer, so a rational agent should never pay to avoid a free signal. The trouble is that everyone nods along and then goes back to thinking that information can obviously hurt you, because they have all internalized the winner's curse and the dangers of strategic disclosure. I want an activity that makes them feel the theorem's force before they see its limits.

Design a 90-minute activity for 12-15 people that does this. The first half should put them in a single-agent decision problem where a signal is offered, and they should feel that having the signal is better -- ideally they should be able to compute the expected value of the signal and see that it is nonnegative, and then see that they can always ignore it. The second half should introduce a variant where the same signal hurts them, and they should discover why: perhaps the signal is public, or it changes their beliefs about what other people will do, or it is costly, or it interacts with a non-expected-utility decision rule, or it changes what they care about. I don't want them to just be told the assumptions; I want the rules of the game to make the assumptions visible. I also want them to see that the ability to ignore the signal is what makes the theorem work, and that in a strategic setting the ability to ignore the signal is not enough, because the signal changes other people's actions even if it does not change yours.

Materials can be minimal: index cards, a projector, maybe a small amount of play money or tokens, and a whiteboard. I'd like the activity to be runnable by someone who knows the theorem but has never facilitated a game like this, so include a timeline, roles if needed, and the two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle them. The group is friendly but argumentative, and they will push back if the game feels rigged. I'd rather they feel the pull of the theorem and then the pull of the counterexample than have either side handed to them. Include a debrief that maps what happened onto the theorem's conditions -- fixed action set, fixed utility function, conditionalization, costless information, single agent -- and a frank note on which condition the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply.
