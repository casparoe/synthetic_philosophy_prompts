For the past two years I have been trying to reconstruct the life of a great-grandmother who left almost no trace: a single line in a parish register, a name on a deed, and nothing else. The work has pushed me into a philosophical position I did not set out to hold. I now think that a statement about the deep past is true only if some possible investigation could, in principle, establish it. Where the evidence is gone for good, I am inclined to say that there is no fact of the matter, rather than a fact that happens to be permanently inaccessible. I have been calling this anti-realism about the past, though I am not certain the label earns its keep. What matters to me is the constraint itself, and I have always meant it as a constraint on statements about the past specifically, not as a general theory of truth.

A colleague in the philosophy department has now produced an argument that he says makes the position untenable, and I cannot find the faulty step. He asked me to grant three things: that knowledge is factive, so that if someone knows that q, then q; that knowledge distributes over conjunction, so that if someone knows that q and r, then that person knows that q and knows that r; and that every truth is knowable, so that for any proposition q, if q is true then it is possible for someone to know that q. The third he presented as the only honest way to state the constraint I have been defending, and I did not have an answer ready.

His argument then runs as follows.

(1) Take any true proposition p about the deep past.

(2) Form the conjunctive proposition: p, and no one ever knows that p.

(3) That conjunction cannot be known. If someone knew it, then by distribution they would know that p and would know that no one ever knows that p; by factivity, the second of these entails that no one ever knows that p, which contradicts the first.

(4) So it is impossible for anyone to know the conjunction.

(5) By the knowability of every truth, if the conjunction is true, then it is possible for someone to know it.

(6) From (4) and (5), the conjunction is not true.

(7) Hence either p is not true, or someone knows that p.

(8) Since p was arbitrary, every truth about the deep past is known by someone.

I cannot accept (8). There are things about the past that no one will ever know, and I do not want to be forced to conclude that they are therefore not truths, which would make the past hostage to who happens to go looking. I also do not want to give up factivity or the distribution of knowledge over conjunction; those seem to me about as secure as anything in epistemology. And I do not want to keep my position alive by quietly weakening the knowability claim until it does no work.

Please tell me which step I should reject, why it looked acceptable, and what rejecting it costs. If the honest answer is that I must surrender more than one of the three assumptions, or that the position I have been defending is not the one I thought it was, I would rather hear that than a repair that works only because I have stopped paying attention. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want the diagnosis and the price.
