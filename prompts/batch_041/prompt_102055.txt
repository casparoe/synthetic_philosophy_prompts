I am a PhD student in philosophy of literature, and I need to pin down the interpretive rule my supervisor is actually running before I commit a chapter to it. Her rulings on a run of cases: on a poem whose author's letters say it concerns a stillborn child, where the published text never mentions a child and works entirely through winter fields and a locked house, she held that the letters settle nothing and the poem means what the text sustains; on a set of posthumous fragments with no recoverable intention, she held that they nonetheless have a determinate meaning, namely the one a suitably informed reader would assign; on a lyric whose author left a stanza-by-stanza account of his aims, which the text fully bears out, she held that the poem means what the author said; and on a play whose author called its last scene sincere, where the staging and diction make sincerity almost unsustainably, she held the scene ironic.

The case I must write on is this: a novelist appends an author's note disclaiming any autobiographical reading, and the drafts and letters in her archive show the disclaimer was sincere — the parallels were invented, not lived. Yet the published text's chapter arrangement and its central recurring image strongly invite the autobiographical reading, and I would argue the novel is a more coherent and more powerful work under that reading. Predict my supervisor's verdict on whether the novel means what the author intended; identify the rule she is running, and the rival rules that fit her earlier rulings just as well; say what each predicts here; give me your best guess with reasons; and name the further case that would best discriminate among them. Please answer in British English.
