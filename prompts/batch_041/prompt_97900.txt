I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works in classical analysis, and who holds, with real conviction, that intuitionism is just ordinary mathematics with a self-imposed handicap — that we constructivists give up perfectly good theorems for the sake of a scruple. I would like to answer him with a poem instead of another round of the same argument, and I want the poem's form to carry the argument, so that the very constraint he finds so irritating is what makes the thing work. So please write the poem, and obey the following rules in it.

The poem should run about twenty-four to thirty lines, in loose quatrains, with a refrain that returns at least three times and that the poem is never quite able to upgrade into the stronger claim it wants. The governing constraint is this: the poem may never assert a disjunction unless the page has already settled which disjunct holds, and it may never assert that something exists without exhibiting the witness for it on the page — no "there is a such-and-such" that leaves the reader to locate the such-and-such later, no "either this or that" that keeps both branches alive. The poem may refute by contradiction — it may assume a thing and derive absurdity from it — but it may not prove by contradiction, i.e., it may not establish that something is the case merely by showing that its denial collapses. Where the poem cannot meet the strict standard, it must retreat to the honest fallback: not that the thing exists, but that it is not the case that it fails to exist. The refrain should be that fallback, or the longing to get past it; and the poem should feel the distance between the two claims rather than treating the second as a dodge or a formality.

The turn I am after: somewhere in the last third the poem should want to insist that something must be the case — that it cannot simply be that nothing answers — and should then have to notice that this insistence is not yet having anything in hand. That gap is the whole point of the poem. If the poem closes it, it has lied about what it is doing. Keep a classical ghost in the room as well: let there be one or two lines that would pass without remark in a classical setting — a disjunction left open, an existence claim with no witness attached — and have the poem refuse or rewrite them in front of the reader, so that the refusal is visible as an act and not just as an omission. The whole thing should read like a construction being assembled rather than a fact being reported.

After the poem, give me a brief craft note as a table with exactly these four columns: Lines, Formal device, Intuitionistic feature it encodes, What a classical version would have done instead. Use one row per device, and include at least one row for the refrain, one for the turn, and one for each place the poem retreats from a bare existence claim or leaves a disjunction unresolved. The table should describe only devices actually present in the poem as written; do not add rows for things the poem might have done.

The poem itself should not name any of these properties. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the disjunction and existence properties can watch them doing the structural work, and good enough that someone who has never heard of them wants to read it twice.
