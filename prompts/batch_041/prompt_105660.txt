OK so I spent about 40 years in a molecular biology lab, retired a few years back, and lately I've been reading philosphy of biology for fun, and it's driving me a little nuts. Everyone in my old field says DNA is a code, or that genes carry information, and the philosophers either tell me that's a dead metaphor that should be thrown out or that it's fine as long as I mean something very specific by it. I'm rewriting a teaching chapter and I'd like to actually settle this for myself instead of nodding along.

So before I read any more of these papers, give me the desiderata. What does any theory of genetic information — or of the claim that DNA is a code — actually have to deliver to count as adequate? I don't want a definition of "information," I want the spec: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse, the facts it can't contradict. And for each item on the list, the case or consideration that puts it there, so I can see why it's a genuine requirement and not just somebody's taste.

Some things I'd want it to survive, off the top of my head — but please don't let me set the whole agenda:

- the codon-to-amino-acid mapping looks chemically arbitrary; nothing about a given triplet's chemistry makes it "want" its amino acid, and yet the same table is used almost everywhere, with the odd exception (mitochondria, a few protists). Is arbitrariness-plus-near-universality a requirement, or a red herring?
- the code is redundant, several triplets for the same amino acid, but the synonymous ones are not interchangeable in practice (codon usage shifts translation speed and folding). So what has to be true of "same meaning"?
- one stretch of DNA is often doing several jobs at once — coding for protein, binding a factor, bending, marking a splice site — and the reading frame matters. A theory of the code has to have something to say about that, surely.
- if I take Shannon information seriously, a random sequence has maximum information, which is exactly the wrong answer, so the notion of information at work can't be that one.
- the interpreter problem: something has to read the code, and the reader is the cell's own machinery, so the "meaning" seems to live in the machinery as much as in the DNA.
- and what is a genetic "mistake" or a miscoding, if the mapping is just chemistry? A code seems to need room for getting it wrong.

Then tell me which of the requirements — mine and the ones I've missed — are actually load-bearing and which are just prejudices of one camp, and whether any two of them can't both be met at once, because that's the bit I most want to know. Mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are preferences. If it's cheap to do, a short line on how the main camps fare against the list — the "it's just chemistry, drop the metaphor" crowd, the people who want information to be about something and grounded in selection, and the ones who think a code is a code in the engineering sense. Don't score every nuance, I mostly want the spec. And I'm not asking for a reading list or citations, I don't have journal access anymore and half of what I'd look up would be paywalled — just the ideas, and which single requirement you think knocks out the most candidates.
