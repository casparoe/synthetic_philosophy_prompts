And here's what the student wrote:

"(a) Early on Austin separates constatives, which state things and are true or false, from performatives, which are things like 'I promise' or 'I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth' -- they don't state anything, they do something, so they can't be true or false, only happy or unhappy depending on whether the circumstances are right. Later he gives this up for a three-part picture. Every utterance involves a locutionary act (saying something with sense and reference), an illocutionary act (what one does in saying it, like promising, warning, or ordering), and a perlocutionary act (what one brings about by saying it, like persuading or alarming). The old distinction doesn't survive as a division between two kinds of utterance, because constatives are just one species of illocutionary act, the act of stating; and constatives can be unhappy too, not only true or false.

(b) Locutionary: the spokesperson utters the sentence with its meaning. Illocutionary: the spokesperson apologizes. Perlocutionary: the audience being appeased, or the story dying down. Really, though, the apology isn't performed until it lands, so the illocutionary act here is the audience being appeased.

(c) One way it can go wrong is that the spokesperson may not be sorry at all and is just reading words someone else wrote. Austin's procedure requires the speaker to actually have the feeling, so a case like this is a misfire: the procedure is defective, and the apology never comes off."
