Hey — I host a small interview podcast on history and philosophy of science, and next month I've got a historian on who's spent her career arguing that volcanoes, pathogens and climate belong in the cast of historical actors, not just in the background as conditions. Her book makes one move I want to build the whole episode around: she seperates three things — a condition, a cause, and an actor — and says historians happily grant nature the first two and refuse it the third, and that the refusal is a metaphysical choice rather than a finding. She takes the word actant from actor-network theory but breaks with it on purpose: she won't hand non-humans intentions, so she calls her own view asymmetrical actancy — nature acts, but it doesn't mean.

I want 8-10 questions that make her work for it, and a follow-up for each, keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Please make sure I get at least one on each of the following, and keep them concrete:

- What does actor buy over cause? What does the relabeling actually explain that cause didn't? And put the causal-selection problem to her: everything is a cause of everything downstream, so what principle picks out the volcano instead of the shipping schedule or the price of wheat? I want her to name the principle, not wave at assemblages.
- The enviornmental-determinism charge, which she'll definately get from the audience. If the 1815 Tambora eruption and the following year's crop failures in New England and Europe end up in the causal story of westward migration or bread riots, where did human agency go? She'll say vulnerability and contingency are social, so the volcano only does work through a social structure — I want the follow-up that makes that reply cost her something (if the social structure is doing the explaining, what's left of the volcano as an actor?).
- Method. What would count as evidence for or against an actant explanation, and what would she accept as a case where the volcano should be demoted back to a condition?
- The political and rhetorical stakes. The volcano did it sounds like it launders responsibility — does a bad harvest excuse a landlord? She'll say causation isn't blame. Follow-up: then why call it an actor at all, when actor is a word carrying intentions, and her own view keeps intentions out?
- One on the threshold: is there a cutoff — does a frost that kills a vineyard count, or only a civilisation-ending eruption — and who gets to decide?

Also, near the end I want one question that makes it hard for her to keep saying the objections are just anthropocentrism, since she leans on that a lot. And don't ask her where the field is headed or what got her into history.
