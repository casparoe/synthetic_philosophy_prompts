I am a features journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am at work on a piece about why a dispute that looks like the purest form of armchair metaphysics turns out to bear on decisions people actually make. The dispute concerns how ordinary things persist through time. On one side stands endurantism: a thing — a person, a river, a company — is wholly present at every moment of its existence, numerically one and the same thing on Monday and on Friday, with no portion of it left behind in the past. On the other side stands perdurantism: such a thing is extended through time much as it is extended through space, so that what exists at any single moment is only a temporal part of it, and the whole is a four-dimensional entity whose earlier and later parts are equally real. A third variant treats the momentary stages themselves as the only things that exist and construes persistence as a matter of similarity or counterpart relations among them.

What I need from you is not a tour of the familiar puzzle about change — how one thing can be straight on Monday and bent on Friday — nor a survey of the standard endurantist manoeuvres, which I already understand to include treating the having of a property at a time as itself time-relative, reconstruing properties as relations to times, and letting tense do the work. I need the downstream consequences.

Assume the role of a philosopher of metaphysics who also works on applied ethics, the philosophy of law, and social ontology, and who is used to explaining to non-specialists why abstract commitments have practical costs. Take your time with this, and begin by brainstorming: before you commit to any implications, generate a long list of candidates, then discard the ones that turn out to be rhetorical rather than substantive. Only the survivors should appear in your answer.

Please describe the most important implications of this dispute for a single setting: whether and how an institution — a corporation, a university, a church, a state — can be held to account for wrongs it committed decades ago, and whether it can owe anything to people it wronged before most of its current members were born. For each implication, be careful to separate what follows from the metaphysical thesis itself from what follows only once further assumptions are added, and note where the two rival views converge rather than diverge. I have a fact-checker and no need of a reading list, so please give me the arguments themselves rather than references to the literature.

Return a single JSON object and nothing outside it, with the following fields and contents:

- `headline`: a string of one sentence, free of jargon, stating the single most important implication.
- `implications`: an array of objects ordered from most to least important, each containing:
  - `rank`: integer.
  - `claim`: the implication in one sentence.
  - `favors`: one of `endurantism`, `perdurantism`, `stage theory`, or `neither`, indicating which position makes this consequence most pressing (use `neither` where the consequence holds on all of them).
  - `reasoning`: two to five sentences tracing the inference from the metaphysical premise to the practical consequence.
  - `auxiliary_assumptions`: an array of strings naming what else must be true for the inference to go through.
  - `strength`: one of `strong`, `moderate`, or `speculative`.
  - `misstatement`: the tempting overstatement a journalist would be inclined to print, and why it is wrong.
- `not_implications`: an array of objects, each with `claim` and `why_not`, covering conclusions that look as though they follow but do not.
- `dropped_candidates`: an array of objects, each with `candidate` and `reason_dropped`, recording the weaker implications you considered and set aside.
- `bottom_line`: two or three sentences for a general reader, flagging the one element most likely to be misreported.

Where an implication is genuinely speculative, say so in the `strength` field rather than softening the claim itself.
