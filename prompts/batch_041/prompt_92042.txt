I want to work through a real episode with the tools of interest-group theory, and I want the analysis to cut in both directions — the theory illuminating the case, and the case testing the theory.

The episode is the failure of the United States to enact significant federal gun-control legislation in the aftermath of the Sandy Hook Elementary School shooting in Newtown, Connecticut, in December 2012. The facts I want you to treat as fixed, and not to second-guess: the shooting produced an unusually broad and intense public demand for action, including for universal background checks; in April 2013 a bipartisan Senate compromise on expanded background checks came to a vote and failed, commanding a majority but falling short of the sixty votes needed to end debate; and no federal measure of comparable scope passed in the decade that followed. Don't worry about finer legislative detail — I'm not asking for a history of the bill.

Bring two theoretical resources to bear. The first is Mancur Olson's logic of collective action and its central asymmetry: concentrated benefits that a small, well-organized group can be mobilized to defend, set against diffuse costs spread across a very large and poorly organized majority. In this episode the organized side is a comparatively small set of gun owners, manufacturers, and advocacy organizations; the unorganized side is the broad public that favored new restrictions. The second is the idea of a "second face of power" — that power operates not only by winning votes but by keeping issues off the agenda altogether and by shaping which proposals count as live options in the first place.

What I want, in order:

1. A disciplined mapping. Identify the players, their preferences, and their organizational capacities, and state precisely what Olson's account predicts here — not "the concentrated side usually wins," but the mechanism, applied to this case.

2. What the second face of power adds. Does agenda control and the shaping of "reasonable" options explain anything the roll-call vote does not? Or, in this case, is it doing no work, since the issue plainly reached the floor?

3. The harder half — where the theory breaks. Olson's model seems to predict that the diffuse majority simply loses. Here it nearly won, and the defeat came through an institutional threshold rather than through any collapse of demand. Does that show the theory is under-specified — that it needs veto points, preference intensity, or malapportionment to do the explanatory work — or is there a defensible reading on which Olson predicted this outcome after all?

4. The inversion. Can the pro-restriction majority itself be seen as a diffuse interest subject to Olson's logic — which would explain why its mobilization was so intense and yet so short-lived? And if so, what does that do to the standard story in which the concentrated side simply out-organizes the many?

Be explicit about where each theory falls silent rather than forcing it to speak. Close by saying how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in the analysis you are least sure of, with a brief account of why.
