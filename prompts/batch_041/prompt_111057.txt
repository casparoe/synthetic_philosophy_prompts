I'm a phil prof (mostly metaethics, but I run the free will reading group) and I keep running into the same thing. One of my collegues in the department, a committed Strawsonian, has a line he deploys whenever a grad student starts worrying about whether determinism is compatible with moral responsibility: "we can't help holding people responsible," and then he sits back like the case is closed. He said it again last week, when a student in the seminar asked whether, if her actions were determined, it would still be fair to resent her for them, and the whole discussion died on the spot. I think there's something in it, but I also think it's doing about four jobs at once and I can't tell which one he's entitled to. I definately want a menu, not a verdict: lay out the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making, from the trivial to the radical, and say for each whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or flat out false. The obvious axis is what kind of "can't" it is -- a claim about human psychology (resentment and indignation fire whether we like it or not), a claim about what's conceptually available (seeing someone as a person just is being exposed to the reactive attitudes, so the "can't" is like not being able to see the duck-rabbit as a rabbit once you've seen the duck), a normative claim (we could drop them but have no reason to, because the demand for an external justification of the whole practice is the confused thing), or not really a claim about ability at all. But the "we" needs unpacking too -- all of us, or those of us who are already participants, or just the person doing the blaming -- and so does the scope, since I'm fairly sure the original thought was about the framework of attitudes taken as a whole rather than about any particular attitude being unrenounceable.

Two things to keep in mind while you do it. First, he can't just mean that suspension is impossible, because we do suspend it: we take the objective attitude toward the very young, toward people we think are mentally ill or brainwashed, and -- this is the interesting case -- toward people whose histories we judge to be bad enough that what they did stops being the point. Second, keep the excuses/exemptions distinction and the quality-of-will idea in play, because I suspect the readings that actually get him his conclusion are the ones where blame answers to ill will rather than to causal history, and the ones that don't are the ones where he's quietly sliding from "we can't help it" to "there's no fact of the matter," which is a much bigger and much worse claim. Also tell me which reading you think he actually means when he says it, and which one I should press him on at the next meeting. I'd rather not win the argument; I'd rather find out what he's actually committed to.
