I'm a science and ideas journalist putting together a feature on the psychology of information avoidance — people who decline to open the envelope, take the test, or read the diagnosis. A colleague of mine, who knows his decision theory, tells me the whole literature is misconceived, because I. J. Good long ago proved that the expected utility of an informed decision can never be lower than that of an uninformed one, so anyone who would rather not know is simply failing to reason correctly. I'd like to get the philosophy straight before I file. Is my colleague's reading of Good right, and if it is, is there really nothing more to be said about the wish not to know than that it is a mistake? I'd be grateful for a careful, unhurried account, in British English.
