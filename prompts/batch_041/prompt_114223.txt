I'm a 4th-year PhD student in political science and in three weeks I have to present my third chapter at our department's political economy workshop. The room is: mostly people who run survey and field experiments on political behavior, two formal theorists who do mechanism design, a public-choice economist who sits in from the business school, and one democratic theorist who I'm fairly sure thinks the entire enterprise is a category error. Its not a friendly crowd.

The chapter defends a narrow version of futarchy — the idea being that we vote on what we want and let markets figure out how to get it. My version: for a restricted class of decisions, the elected body sets the metric and the success threshold, and a prediction market picks between two or three concrete policy packages. The running example is a county committing to cut unsheltered homelessness by some percentage in five years, with the market choosing between a housing-first package and an enforcement-plus-treatment package. My own twist is that instead of one conditional market you run a matched pair of markets on the counterfactuals — what the metric looks like in five years if we do A versus if we do B — and the payout gets settled by an independent audit against a pre-registered measurement protocol. I also claim the mechanism is only appropriate for what I call instrumental decisions and not constitutive ones, and I already suspect that distinction is the softest thing in the paper.

Build me a Q&A prep sheet for the question period. I want the objections this crowd will actually raise, not the ones I'd recieve from the prediction-market people online — ordered by how likely each is times how much damage it does, each one written in the vocabulary of whoever will actually ask it (the experimentalist will say identification and mean something specific by it; the mechanism designer will want to know what exactly the market is a proper scoring rule for; the public-choice guy goes straight to rent-seeking and who captures the mechanism; the democratic theorist asks who the principal is and how you hold a price accountable). For each objection give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should just concede up front, which objection I should refuse to concede on, the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
