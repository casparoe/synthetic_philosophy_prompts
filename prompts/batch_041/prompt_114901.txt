I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice and social cooperation — sixteen students, mostly philosophy and economics majors, fluent with Nash equilibrium and dominance but innocent of the recent work on delegation in cooperative AI. I have ninety minutes with them and one idea I keep failing to convey from the lectern, so I want to stage it instead. The idea is what that literature calls a safe Pareto improvement: a modification of a game — a change to the rules, or to what the players are able to do — such that everyone is better off under the modified game with certainty, whichever of its equilibria ends up being played, and however the players would otherwise have settled things. That quantifier is the whole point. The notion is meant as a guarantee against miscoordination rather than a bet on the likely outcome: you cannot count on the other side to play the equilibrium you would prefer, so a change that improves matters only in expectation, or only on the assumption that the other party does its part, is not safe. In the setting that motivates the term you are instructing a delegate to play on your behalf while the other side's delegate does the same, you hold no probabilistic beliefs about how either delegate will actually play, and you want a modification whose payoff does not depend on resolving that uncertainty.

Design me a runnable activity for the session: rules, materials, a payoff scheme in points or play money rather than real money, a timeline that fits the ninety minutes, and roles or structure that force the players to confront the question of which way things will settle. The students should first feel the ordinary problem — a situation with more than one way to settle in which the settlement they reach is not the one they would have chosen — and then be given the chance to propose and adopt modifications to the rules of their own game. What I want them to run into is the discovery that several of the modifications they find attractive are not safe: they pay off only if the other player does his part, or they merely looked good on the trial run they happened to get, or they leave open a way of playing that is no better than where they started. At least one modification available to them should be genuinely safe, and it should feel like a smaller and more sober thing than the one they wanted. By the end they should be able to say what separates an improvement that is safe from one that merely seems likely to work, and what the safe ones cost.

No lecture up front, and no use of the term safe Pareto improvement — or of safe in this sense — until the debrief, which should map the moments of the activity onto the parts of the definition (the modification, the reference outcome, the quantifier over equilibria) and be candid about what the staging cannot show. Tell me too what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and name the feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in my own voice — I would rather know in advance where the game is going to have to lie to them.
