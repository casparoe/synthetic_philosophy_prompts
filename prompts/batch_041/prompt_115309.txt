I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and for the last year a small reading group I belong to — four of us in the back room of a pub, one of whom teaches Kant and one of whom has read nothing but decision theory since 2011 — has been circling Williams's objection to impartialist morality without ever quite landing. The strand we keep snagging on is the homely one: the case in which a man saves his wife rather than a stranger, where Williams's point is that the man needs no thought beyond the fact that she is his wife, so that any additional justification to the effect that impartiality permits him to prefer her enters as one thought more than the occasion calls for. The pattern of the exchange is by now almost liturgical. I say the objection shows that impartialist theories cannot be the theories agents actually deliberate with. Someone else replies that this conflates the criterion of rightness with the decision procedure — that a theory may assess actions from outside while the agent acts from attachment, and that no sensible theory needs to be present in the agent's head at the moment of action. I then say that this reply either proves too little (every theory whatsoever can be insulated from deliberation, so the manoeuvre earns consequentialism nothing in particular) or proves too much (it concedes that the theory is unfit to be thought in, which was precisely the complaint). Then we order another round, and nothing has moved in twelve months.

My own suspicion, offered as a suspicion and not as a settled view, is that we are running two arguments together under one name. The first is the integrity objection proper: that a morality of impersonal demands alienates agents from the projects and attachments that give their lives shape, which is a claim about how much a theory may ask of a person and what it may ask them to become. The second is the superfluous-thought complaint, which is a claim about what an agent's reason may be — that the justifying thought, if it must be a moral one, misdescribes the man's situation rather than merely over-taxing him. These two complaints have different structures, pull in opposite directions (the first says the theory demands too much, the second that it demands the wrong thing at the wrong level), and it is not obvious that any single reply — least of all the criterion/decision-procedure distinction — could answer both. A third possibility nags at me as well: that the real dispute is whether the fact that she is his wife is itself a reason or merely an exemption from a reason that would otherwise apply, in which case this is a substantive disagreement about the structure of reasons wearing the costume of a disagreement about moral psychology.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I should say at the outset that a bibliography or reading list would be useless to me. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is: whether it is substantive or verbal; whether the two sides differ over facts, over standards of what a moral theory is for (guiding action versus evaluating it), over the unit of assessment (acts, characters, deliberative frameworks), or over starting intuitions that neither side can argue for; whether the impasse marks the question as ill-posed or merely hard; and whether the sides are answering different questions under a single heading. I would like the diagnosis to engage with what each side actually says rather than with caricatures of them, and I would like a frank answer about what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or an argument that none should be expected, and why. Please write in British English.
