I'm in the second term of a course on the ethics of armed conflict, and my tutor has set an exercise in constructing cases rather than in reporting positions. I should say at the outset that the two views set out below are stipulated for the exercise: treat them as definitions I am working with, not as anyone's actual position, and don't spend any of your answer on their provenance, on the surrounding literature, or on who is supposed to hold them. I am assessed on whether the case does the work I ask of it, so a short case that genuinely generates the verdicts will serve me better than a vivid one that merely announces them.

The two stipulated views.

The Liability View. A combatant is permitted to kill an enemy combatant if and only if that enemy is liable to be killed. Liability attaches to anyone who is responsible for an objectively unjust threat. Responsibility here requires agency and control — the person must be the author of the threat rather than its instrument — but it does not require culpability: a soldier who is entirely blameless, and even one who is non-culpably ignorant that his side's cause is unjust, is liable if the threat he poses is unjust. Conversely, someone whose body is being used as a weapon, being physically compelled or so completely dominated that his movements are not his own, is not liable, however much harm he does. Permission and liability are exact mirrors: one may kill precisely those who are liable, no more and no fewer. The view also denies that a combatant can justify killing an enemy by appeal to his own self-preservation where the threat to him arises only from his participation in an unjust enterprise.

The Licence View. The permission to kill in war does not derive from liability at all; it derives from a reciprocal convention. Combatants whose political authorities are parties to the convention grant one another licence to kill, and between two parties the licence is symmetrical and wholly indifferent to the justice of either cause. The licence is confined to parties: a force whose authority is not party to the convention lies outside it, so that its members may be killed without wrong by any party, while they themselves hold no licence of their own.

The case. One contemporary scenario, invented rather than drawn from any real conflict, in at most two paragraphs of plain prose, satisfying all of the following.

(i) Neither side has a just cause against the other. Both are attempting to seize a territory that belongs to neither of them, from a population that wants neither, and the encounter is arranged so that neither side's fire is a response to an attack already under way — both are firing in furtherance of their own wrongful objective. You may simply stipulate this; I don't want it argued for.

(ii) On the Liability View the verdict is symmetrical: each side's ordinary combatants are permitted to kill the other's. The symmetry must not rest on any excuse, mistake or ignorance on either side.

(iii) On the Licence View the verdict is asymmetrical: one side's combatants are permitted to kill the other's, and the other side's are not permitted to kill theirs.

(iv) The asymmetry in (iii) must come from standing alone — from what each side's authority has or has not signed up to — and not from anything the two sides do. So make the difference a matter of declaration or membership, and make the two forces otherwise as alike as you can: comparable size, comparable weapons, comparable discipline, and no difference in how restrained they actually are in the fighting.

(v) Both sides are ordinary state forces of the kind one meets in any textbook: no child soldiers, no mercenaries, no drones or autonomous weapons, no genocide, no exotic technology.

Then, after the case:

(a) Give the derivation for each view separately, showing that the verdicts in (ii) and (iii) follow from that view's own criterion rather than from anything I have stipulated. If a verdict can only be reached by stipulating it, say so and revise the case.

(b) Identify precisely which feature is doing the work in each direction, and say what becomes of each verdict once that feature is removed — in particular, tell me what happens if both authorities are parties to the convention, and what happens if one side is given a just cause.

(c) Say what the case shows about whether the moral equality of combatants is one thesis or two, since that is what the exercise is for: I want to see whether the permission to kill and the liability to be killed can come apart, and in which direction. Note where the case reverses the usual direction — the view normally used to argue for inequality between combatants yielding equality here, and the view normally used to argue for equality yielding inequality — and say plainly what produces the reversal.

(d) Finish by giving the strongest objection available to each view against your own case — for the Licence View, whether the non-party's members might retain permissions of self-defence that the convention cannot take away; for the Liability View, whether the side in possession might have a defensible claim after all — and say honestly which of the two objections is the harder to answer.
