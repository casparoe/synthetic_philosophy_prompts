I'm in a third-year formal epistemology module and next week our reading group has a visiting speaker who's spent her whole career defending Jeffrey conditionalization — the rule where instead of getting evidence E for certain and conditionalizing on it, you get something weaker: the probabilities of a partition E1...En get shifted to new values q1...qn and you keep your old conditional probabilities P(A|Ei) fixed (I think the jargon is 'probability kinematics'). Our lecturer wants everyone to hand in 8-10 questions for the Q&A, and I want them to actually press her alot rather than letting her say 'conditionalization is just the special case where one cell goes to 1' and move on. Could you draft them, with a follow-up for each one, keyed to the answer she's most likely to give?

Things I want hit, roughly one question each:

- the fixed conditionals. What's supposed to license holding P(A|Ei) fixed? And what happens when the uncertain news is itself about how reliable the source is — say a lab instrument reports that a sample is contaminated, but the same reading also makes you doubt whether the instrument is calibrated, so it seems like it should also move P(contaminated | that reading). Does her rule have anything to say about that kind of second-order update, or is it just silent on it?

I get a report that leaves me 0.7 confident the drawn ball is red, and another that leaves me 0.6 confident it's heavy. Updating on each by Jeffrey's rule, keeping the conditionals fixed at each step, gives me different credences in 'red and heavy' depending on which report I hear first (0.32 vs 0.30, if I did it right). Feature or bug? And if the reply is 'you shouldn't be holding those conditionals in that order', whose fault is that — mine or the rule's?
- what stops the rule from being empty. The q's are just whatever I happen to feel; is there anything in the view that separates updating on uncertain evidence from simply changing my mind? If not, is it a rule of inference or only a coherence constraint?
- I've heard there's a sure-loss / Dutch book worry about people who update this way, something about being exploitable over time — I dont trust my memory of it, so have her explain what the worry actually is, then press on whether it does real damage.
- whether 'uncertain evidence' is even the right picture. Why not treat the report itself ('the instrument said contaminated') as the certain evidence and just conditionalize on that? What's supposed to be lost by that move and what's gained by hers?

And finish with something about what could count against her view — what would make her drop it. No 'what got you into philosophy' filler please, and I'd rather she not be able to answer any of them with a paragraph from her own book; the urn case and the calibration case should be the centre of it.
