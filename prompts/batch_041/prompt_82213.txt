I am a secondary-school student writing an extended essay on whether it makes sense to say that science gets closer to the truth, and I have reached the point where I need a number rather than a further discussion. The proposition I want priced is this: there exists a formal definition of comparative verisimilitude, that is, a definition that ranks theories by how close they come to the truth, which satisfies all four of the following. (a) A true theory can be ranked closer to the truth than a false one. (b) Some false theories can be ranked closer to the truth than other false theories, so that a sequence of theories which are all strictly false can still count as progress. (c) The ranking is not hostage to the language in which the theories are stated, so that a harmless change of notation, or the splitting of one predicate into two, cannot reorder a pair of theories. (d) The ranking agrees with the obvious cases, for instance that a claim which is slightly off about some magnitude is closer to the truth than a claim which is wildly off. For context on the difficulty: Popper's own proposal was, roughly, that B is closer to the truth than A when B implies everything true that A implies, and implies nothing false that A does not already imply; in 1974 Tichý and Miller showed that on this definition no false theory can be closer to the truth than another false theory, which is precisely the collapse that condition (b) forbids. I have also read that there is an impossibility theorem in the literature showing that no measure of closeness can satisfy a whole list of natural requirements at once, which is why I want the four conditions above treated as the list rather than being allowed to expand or contract once you start answering.

Two candidate repairs that I have been mulling over should probably appear as separate terms in your decomposition, because I suspect they fail for different reasons and I want to know which failure you take to be the serious one. The first restricts the comparison to a theory's observational consequences: count only those consequences that can be stated in a restricted observation vocabulary, and call a theory closer to the truth when it adds true observational consequences without adding false ones. My own suspicion is that this helps only if the observation language is not closed under the truth-functional operations, since the proof that ruined Popper's definition appears to manufacture a sentence out of a false consequence of one theory and a true consequence of the other, and that manufacture needs negation, conjunction and disjunction to be available; if the observation language is closed under those operations, the same collapse should simply recur one level down, among the observational claims. The second repair replaces the containment test with a ratio: rank a theory by the proportion of its consequences that are true, so that a theory with many true consequences and a few false ones can beat a theory with fewer of each. This worries me because the proportion depends on how finely the vocabulary is carved up, which is exactly the dependence that condition (c) forbids, and because a sufficiently weak theory can secure a high proportion by saying almost nothing.

Please give me your credence in the proposition, then the decomposition: a number and one line of justification for each of (i) the probability that the 1974 result is fatal to the whole family of content-based definitions rather than a defect of Popper's particular notion of content; (ii) the probability that the observational-consequences restriction escapes it; (iii) the probability that the ratio version escapes it; (iv) the probability that condition (c) is satisfiable at all, since I gather some philosophers hold that every such ranking is language-relative, in which case the four conditions may not even be jointly consistent and my proposition is false for a reason that has nothing to do with Popper; and (v) the probability that some quite different approach, such as measuring how close a theory's models are to the actual world, or counting only those consequences that are relevant in a technical sense, satisfies all four conditions; then the combination.
