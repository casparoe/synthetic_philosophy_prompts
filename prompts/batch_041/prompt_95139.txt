i'm the new rabbi at a mid-size shul and my first proper sermon lands on shabbat vayikra, the week we start leviticus and spend what feels like an hour reading about animal sacrifice. i need help writing it, because the room is split three ways and i can't finesse it. a big chunk of the older members take the korbanot straight -- for them the offerings are something god actually wanted, and they will hear the maimonidean line (that the whole sacrificial apparatus was an accomodation, that you can't ask a person to jump from one extreme to its opposite in one step, so god left the mode of worship more or less exactly as everyone in the ancient near east already practiced it -- temple, altar, smoke, incense, priests, even down to which animals were acceptable -- and turned the whole thing toward himself, blotting out idolatry without abolishing the service people were used to) as an insult, as if the torah were a concession to pagan bad habits. then there's the cohen family, whose sixteen year old went vegan last year and asked me at kiddush, very politely, why god needed to smell burning meat. and in the third row is a retired professor who taught the guide of the perplexed for thirty years and will hear every slip. ramban's objection is going to come up whether i raise it or not -- he says maimonides' explanation is a band aid on a gaping wound, since noah and abel were bringing offerings long before anyone worshipped idols -- and i'd rather raise it myself and admit that maimonides doesn't obviously win. i also don't want to fudge the awkward bit that the commandments are supposed to stand forever, so a reading that turns the sacrifices into a stage we've outgrown can't be the whole story.

so: seven or eight minutes, spoken, no chapter and verse, no hebrew i don't translate on the spot, no lecture hall voice. and the thing i care about most -- build the sermon the way the argument itself works. gradual, no sudden leaps, one step at a time, so that by the end the traditionalists and the vegan kid are somehow standing in the same place, without either of them being told they were wrong to walk in with what they walked in with. and give her a real answer, not a pat one.
