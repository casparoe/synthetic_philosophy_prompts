Two camps in the philosophy of biology have spent decades arguing past each other, and I want the document that would actually conclude their exchange. On one side stands the species monist: someone who holds that there is a single privileged species concept, one that — once the metaphysics is set right — captures a real division in the living world, so that competition among rival concepts is competition over which one is correct. On the other side stands the species pluralist: someone who holds that several mutually incompatible species concepts each track a genuine pattern in nature, that none is privileged over the rest, and that much of the apparent conflict dissolves once we ask which pattern a given inquiry needs.

Write the joint statement that a well-informed monist and a well-informed pluralist could both sign at the close of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the following, in order.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These should cover what species-talk is for; which phenomena are uncontroversially in play (reproductive isolation, gene flow, ecological differentiation, phylogenetic history, cryptic and asexual diversity, and so on); what a species concept is supposed to do; and what would count as success or failure for one. For each agreed claim, add a parenthetical saying whether the two of you take it to be a claim about the world or about our classificatory practices — this is where I expect the real progress to lie. No signed claim may be merely definitional or vacuous; if a formulation is true by construction, mark it as such and drop it.

Second, the surviving disagreements, each stated twice: once in wording the monist would accept as a fair rendering of the monist's own view, and once in wording the pluralist would accept for the pluralist's. Where a disagreement turns out to be verbal, say so explicitly and explain what the parties were really doing when they thought they were disagreeing.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. For each crux, identify a case or argument that both parties agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

To keep this from floating free of the biology, run both frameworks through the same four cases and report where their predictions diverge: a ring species encircling a barrier; a complex of obligate asexual lineages with no gene flow; two populations exchanging genes freely while occupying sharply distinct niches; and a microbial group whose cohesion is disrupted by horizontal transfer. Then add a section on the practical stakes — conservation law, regulatory categories, the Endangered Species Act and its analogues — and say plainly whether either party thinks those stakes bear on the metaphysical question, and why.

Do not supply citations, reading lists, or references to particular authors; argue from the ideas themselves. The value of the exercise is in finding formulations that are neither slanted nor toothless, so spend your effort there rather than in reciting the standard positions.
