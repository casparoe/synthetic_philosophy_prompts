Right, so I've got to present my "position" at the reading group next week — herding and information cascades — and I'd rather someone found the holes in it now than in the room. Can you audit it for me? Here's what I actually think.

1. A cascade is a state where every individual move is optimal given what that person can see, and yet the group ends up throwing away most of the private information that was in the room. So "irrational" is the wrong word — the agents are fine, the action space is the problem. My view is that if people were choosing from a continuum rather than from a couple of boxes, there'd be essentially nothing to explain, because each choice would leak its owner's signal and learning would just carry on.

2. The thing I find most striking about real cascades isn't that they form, it's that they're metastable — they can sit there for ages and then invert completely off the back of one small public announcement. Like a supersaturated solution.

3. But in actual markets and firms I don't think the mechanism is mostly informational at all, it's reputational. Managers copy each other because being wrong in a crowd is cheap and being wrong on your own is expensive — they haven't inferred anything from the crowd, they're covering themselves.

4. The fix, in organisations, is procedural: get people's private signals written down before they can see anyone else's. Blind polling before the discussion starts, forecasts logged independently and only then revealed, that sort of thing. Think of a hiring commitee where each member has to write their verdict before reading the previous member's.

5. I also think prices do a decent job of aggregating what participants collectively know — nothing fancy, just that they're usually a reasonable summary.

6. And I dont think you can blame anyone for a cascade, because every step in one is exactly what a Bayesian would do.
