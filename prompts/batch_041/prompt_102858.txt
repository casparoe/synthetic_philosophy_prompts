Ok so I've fallen down a rabbit hole on panpsychism — the view that experience, or at least some faint flicker of it, goes all the way down, so that even the basic bits of matter have a kind of inner life — and honestly the part I can't stop chewing on isn't the cosmic stuff, it's the combination problem: fine, grant that every fundamental particle has some minimal micro-experience, but then how do a few billion of them sitting in a skull fuse into one single subject that says "I" and means it? Why isn't a brain just a crowd of micro-experiencers shouting past each other instead of one unified someone? I want you to write me a short story that gets at this, but please don't turn it into a lecture where characters stand around explaining the problem to each other — I want the idea doing work in the plot, the way a good robot story makes you *feel* a logic puzzle instead of just stating it.

Here's some seed material you can use or throw away. I imagine a near-future clinic that treats "unbinding" — a condition where a patient's experiences stop merging, so they report seeing and hearing and thinking in parallel streams that never quite become theirs, like a committee that lost its chair. The clinic's procedure is a kind of enforced combination: they don't manufacture experience, they just get the little bits to cohere into a single point of view again. Our protagonist could be the technician who administers it, or a patient about to go under, or maybe someone who's lived their whole life with an unusually thin binding and has learned to eavesdrop on their own parts before they merge — and who's about to find out what "they" lose when the parts finally do combine into one. There's something in there about whether a unified self is a gain or a kind of lossy compression, a forgetting of the many into the one, and I'd like the story to leave me uneasy about which side it lands on rather than tidying it up.

Keep it around 1500 words, third person or first, whatever serves it. The one thing I realy want is that a reader who already knows the combination problem should feel it being taken somewhere new rather than just illustrated — and a reader who's never heard of panpsychism should still be able to follow the story and catch the chill of it without a glossary.
