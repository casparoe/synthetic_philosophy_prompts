Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about whether 'better than' is transitive, and we went in circles; I would rather you settle the question by construction than by reciting the literature. Acting as a philosopher who works on the logic of comparative value, and beginning with a brief brainstorm of the candidate structures before you commit to one, build me a thought experiment with three options, A, B and C, meeting all of the following at once. Each option is strictly better or worse than each of the others, with no indifference, no incommensurability, and no vagueness about where any option falls. The betterness of each option follows from a rule a reasonable person could actually hold. And yet A is better than B, B is better than C, and C is better than A. The cycle must not come from a chain of imperceptible increments, as in a sorites series or the self-torturer, and must not come from aggregating different people's preferences, as in a voting paradox: the intransitivity has to sit in one person's all-things-considered betterness. Keep the options ordinary and realistic — careers, apartments, medical treatments — and the scenario to a paragraph.

Then do the check, thinking carefully at each step. State the three pairwise verdicts and derive each from your rule rather than asserting it. Name the one structural feature of the case that makes the cycle possible, and say why removing it collapses the case into an ordinary transitive comparison. Give the three reactions, each argued in a couple of sentences: that transitivity is constitutive of betterness and the case must therefore be misdescribed; that betterness itself can cycle; and that only the choice procedure cycles while the underlying values do not. Finish by saying how a defender of transitivity would attack the case, whether that move can be made without begging the question, and whether the case can be turned into a money pump. If the constraints cannot all be satisfied together, say so, prove it as tightly as you can, and identify the single constraint that must give way and what happens once it does. I need this because I want to show my friend that intransitivity does not require vagueness or a spectrum, so a case that secretly relies on either will not do.
