I recently read a short introduction to the debate between moral particularism and moral generalism, and I would like to know how much of it I can trust before I build any further thoughts on it. The explanation ran roughly as follows. Morality, on the particularist view, is a matter of responding to each situation on its own terms. There are no moral principles: because every situation is unique, no general rule can tell you what to do, and anyone who believes they can work out what to do by consulting a rule has mistaken a rough rule of thumb for a genuine guide. The particularist's central insight is that reasons are entirely context-dependent — a feature that counts in favour of acting one way here may count in favour of the opposite way there, or count for nothing at all, so nothing whatsoever can be said in advance about what supports what. This is essentially the older idea of prima facie duties restated in new vocabulary. The generalist, by contrast, holds that moral thinking consists in subsuming a case under the correct principle and reading off the answer, which is precisely why generalists are helpless before genuine moral difficulty. And because the particularist denies that any two situations are truly alike, the view drifts toward the claim that morality is whatever the situation seems to demand to the person caught up in it, which leaves it unable to explain how moral disagreement is even possible.

I have no academic training in philosophy, and I have been turning this over on my own. Some of it rings true to me — I do think the details of a situation matter in a way that a rule seems to flatten out — but the last part bothers me, and I suspect the description of the generalist is a bit of a cartoon. What I want is a careful audit rather than a verdict on the whole debate. Please take the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a thoughtful particularist would happily endorse, which are reasonable simplifications that a specialist would let pass, which are subtle misrepresentations, and which are simply wrong. I would particularly like to know whether the closing claims — that the view cannot account for moral disagreement and that it slides into "whatever seems right to the person in the situation" — actually follow from the parts of the view that survive scrutiny, or whether they depend on something the passage has quietly gotten wrong. I would also like to know what a generalist would rightly object to in the passage's description of their own position, since I gather that being able to apply a principle is not the same as applying it mechanically.

Finally, please be explicit about your own confidence. Where the passage's summary is contested among people who study this, say so rather than presenting one reading as settled, and tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would lead you to a different assessment of the summary, and at which point your own reasoning is most likely to be going wrong.
