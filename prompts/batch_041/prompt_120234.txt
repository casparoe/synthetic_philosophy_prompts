I have to present to the funding committee of a mid-sized UK animal-welfare foundation a proposal that we commit a fixed share of next year's grants budget — I am proposing something between ten and twenty per cent — to the problem of insect suffering, on the strength of what is usually called the scale argument: that the number of insects alive at any moment, and the number of insect lives consumed by ordinary human activity in a year, is so vast that even a very low probability that any single insect has morally relevant experience yields an expected quantity of suffering that swamps the entire vertebrate case. My figures are deliberately crude — something on the order of 10^14 to 10^15 insect lives touched annually by agriculture and pest control in a country our size, set against a per-insect credence for sentience that I am willing to put as low as one in a hundred — and you should treat those as my working assumptions rather than as things to be sourced.

The position I am actually defending in the room, though, is not straight expected-value maximisation. I concede that taken at face value the scale argument proves too much: the same arithmetic that tells us to walk away from chicken welfare tells us to walk away from global poverty relief, and for that matter to spend the entire budget on whatever the least probable and most enormous stake happens to be. So what I defend is a two-stage rule. Until the credence interval is narrower than I can currently make it, the insect share should go on closing that interval — invertebrate nociception, the behavioural and pharmacological evidence for pain-like states, the question of what functional architecture is genuinely required — and only once it has closed past a threshold I state in the talk should we shift to direct intervention. I argue that this is not evasion but the only defensible allocation under this much uncertainty, and I have a stopping rule I can set out precisely.

The audience is not sympathetic. It is five or six economists and grant-makers trained in expected-utility theory, who know the small-probability, vast-stakes literature well enough to reach for the mugging objection before I have finished my second slide, and who will hear the stopping rule as risk aversion dressed up as epistemology; two invertebrate neurobiologists who work on nociception and are quietly convinced that anything without a centralised brain is being discussed under a category error, and who resent being asked to put a price on a credence; and one colleague from the farm-animal side who thinks the whole exercise is an unforced reputational risk and a diversion from pigs and poultry.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from these people, phrased the way they would phrase them and in roughly the order they will arrive, ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumble it; for each, a reply I can deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive, and — separately, and without softening it — the question I am hoping nobody in the room asks.
