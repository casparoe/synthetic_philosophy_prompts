I have been bothered for some years by a puzzle that I can state plainly but cannot place in any literature I know, and I would like to learn whether it already has a name, or, failing that, whether you can name and define it precisely.

The puzzle concerns what it is for two people to mean the same thing by a word or an instruction. Suppose I teach a student a rule for continuing a sequence — continue by adding two — and we work through a hundred cases together without a single disagreement. It seems natural to say that we now follow the same rule, that the student has grasped what I meant. But consider what my teaching actually fixed. Every case we covered is compatible with the student having attached to my words a rule that agrees with mine on all cases so far and diverges on the hundred-and-first, and nothing in the cases we covered, or in my tone, or in the state of either of our brains so far as I can inspect it, rules that alternative out. The disagreement, when it arrives, will not look like a mistake about the evidence; it will look like two people discovering that they had been following different rules all along, without any fact having changed. The same worry arises with two annotators labelling a corpus: they may never disagree, and yet it is unclear whether that is because they are applying one criterion or because they happen to coincide on everything they have seen.

Two things about this make me think I am after something more specific than the familiar problems. First, it is not the problem of induction. I understand that problem, or at least its shape — the future may not resemble the past, the evidence so far underdetermines the next case, and one wants to know whether the continuation is reasonable or merely habitual. That problem is about what licenses a prediction. Mine is about what makes my present word mean what it does, prior to any prediction; the question is not whether the student should expect the next case to go one way, but what it was for my earlier utterance to have had a determinate content at all. Second, it is not the indeterminacy of translation. I know the rabbit case, where the linguist cannot tell whether a word means rabbit or undetached rabbit part, but that puzzle is about passing from one language into another, and it is usually granted that within a single language, between two people who share it, words mean what they mean. What I am describing happens inside a single language, between two competent speakers, and it threatens the idea that there is anything for them to share. Nor is it vagueness: a vague word has borderline cases, whereas here every case in the range is perfectly decidable, and the trouble is that one totality of decisions is compatible with more than one rule. I have tried the obvious repair, writing the rule out explicitly, but any written rule is a further string of words that must itself be applied, so the repair moves the difficulty up a level rather than closing it.

There is a further wrinkle that makes me doubt the standard dismissals. Suppose the follower of the rule is not a person but a machine whose entire input-output dispositions I have specified and can enumerate in advance. I know exactly what it will do on every input, including those it has never seen, and I know that on some of them it will be wrong. If meaning were just the disposition to respond, the machine's meaning would already be settled by facts I possess, and its errors would form part of that meaning — which cannot be right, since an error is precisely a case in which the response fails to match the meaning. So the dispositional answer, whatever its merits for people, cannot be the whole story here, and the machine case seems to sharpen the puzzle rather than dissolve it.

So: does this pattern — the failure of any finite history of use, or of any disposition to respond, to fix what a rule or a word means, together with the way that stating the rule merely relocates the difficulty — have a name? If the nearest established concept is one I have not mentioned, I want the differences spelled out point by point; and among the three neighbours I have described, I want to be told which is closest and why the other two are not. If nothing fits exactly, coin a term, define it, and give me its boundary cases: what would count as an instance and what would merely resemble one. A bare label will not help me; what I need is the carving. No reading list or citations, please — I could not check them in any case.
