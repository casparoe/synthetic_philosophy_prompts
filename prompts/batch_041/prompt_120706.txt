I have been reading decision theory threads for long enough to have watched the wager dismissed and revived several times over, and my impression is that the dismissals and the revivals are usually answering different questions. What I want before I read another one is the specification: the list of things any adequate treatment of Pascal's wager has to deliver.

Fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to a purpose. I am not asking for a reconstruction that files the wager correctly in the history of probability. I want a treatment that could guide an agent who has to decide something on Monday morning. With that standard in view, give me the requirements list: for each requirement, the case or consideration that puts it there, and a note on whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

Here are the candidates I would expect to see. Tell me which of them are genuine desiderata, which are prejudices, and what I have left off.

- The many-religions case, in its hedging form as well as its disjunctive form. If several incompatible creeds each promise an infinite reward, does the calculus select one, leave the agent indifferent, or license a mixed strategy? Whatever it says has to follow from the structure of the problem rather than from the order in which the alternatives happen to be enumerated.
- The arithmetic of infinities. Once a payoff is infinite, expected value stops behaving: sums of infinities, differences of infinities, and a positive probability of an infinite loss all have to be handled rather than waved at. So the treatment has to declare what its values live in (extended reals, hyperreals with infinitesimal credences, a lexicographic ordering) and what a zero credence is supposed to mean for a possibility that has not been ruled out.
- The action/belief distinction, and its diachronic version. The move usually attributed to Pascal is that one cannot believe on command but can act as though, and let habituation do the rest, so the object of choice is a policy of self-modification rather than a belief held at a time. A treatment that ignores this is answering a different question from the one he posed.
- The mugging. Any rule on which a tiny probability of an enormous payoff dominates will let a stranger with a plausible story extract your wallet, and then extract it again tomorrow. Does the treatment accept that as the price of the rule, block it, and if it blocks it, by giving up what? I take it as non-negotiable that the resulting theory not be sure-loss exploitable in a way the agent can see coming.
- The gap between prudential and epistemic grounds. Being paid to believe is not evidence, and a successful wager is at best a reason to arrange one's life, not a reason the proposition is true. The list should keep these separate even if a proponent is happy to run them together.
- What credence is even available. Credence needs a determinate proposition to be a credence in; if the hypothesis on offer is characterized only as whatever is infinitely good and pays infinitely, say whether the wager has a target at all.
- Consistency with how belief actually forms and revises. Prudential argument moves choices, not credences, and the religious credences people report do not behave like expected-value-sensitive degrees of belief. A theory whose central prescription is that agents adopt a credence on evidential grounds they lack is not obviously about the same thing as the agents it addresses.
- Continuity with the finite case. Whatever the treatment says about infinite payoffs should either carry over to a small probability of a very large finite good or explain, non-arbitrarily, where the discontinuity comes in.

Then the part I most want. Mark any pair or triple on that list that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude the standing positions: the reading on which the wager is compulsory, the indifference or hedging reading, and the reading on which the whole thing is a mugging and can be ignored. If the list itself turns out to be inconsistent, that is the result I am after, so state it plainly rather than softening it into a menu of trade-offs.
