I sit on the professional standards committee for my state bar, and I have to write the opinion we'll vote on at next months meeting, so I could use a draft I can actually circulate.

Here's the case. A lawyer -- call her Renner -- was representing a client in a contract fight. Opposing counsel emailed and asked straight out whether Renner's client had ever been in trouble with a regulator. Renner wrote back: "My client has never been sanctioned by any regulator." That sentence is true if you read "sanctioned" narrowly: there was a consent agreement a few years back where the client paid a chunk of money but admitted no wrongdoing, and the agency never entered a formal finding or order against it. So nothing was ever "sanctioned," technically. But nobody reading that email would come away thinking there'd been a consent agreement and a payment, and opposing counsel says they dropped a whole line of inquiry because of it. Renner's position is that she never wrote anything false, and that if they wanted the agreement they could have asked for the documents.

Our rulebook says a lawyer can't engage in conduct involving dishonesty, deceit, or misrepresentation. That's the whole clause; there's no separate ban on misleading. So the committee is split. One camp says "misrepresentation" needs a false statement, Renner didn't make one, dismiss it. Another says a true statement can misrepresent perfectly well and Renner's did, so open a file. And one member keeps arguing something I can't shake: that what Renner did is worse than an outright lie, because a lie at least puts a false claim on the table where the other side can check it, whereas a misleading truth gets the other side to do the deceiving to themselves. (He keeps calling it "paltering" -- deceiving with true statements -- but I think that's just a name for the thing, not an argument.) A fourth point someone raised: does it matter that Renner didn't set out to deceive, she just answered narrowly and let opposing counsel fill in the rest? And does it matter that she could have said nothing at all?

Draft the committee's opinion. I need a concrete recommended disposition -- dismiss, open a disciplinary file, or something in between like a private caution -- with the reasoning behind it, and I need it to deal honestly with the strongest thing on the other side, not just wave it away. I also want your take on whether we should amend the rule so this case is clearly covered one way or the other, and if so how you'd word it, because half the committee wants to add "or misleading" and I'm worried that sweeps in every carefully-worded answer a lawyer ever gives.
