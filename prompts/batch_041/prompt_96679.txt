For a graduate seminar I am running on welfare and its measurement, I would like to stage a formal exchange, and I am asking you to occupy one side of it. Play a committed comparabilist: someone who holds that interpersonal comparisons of well-being are meaningful, that they can be made with enough precision to guide policy, and that the right way to make them is the procedure described below. Argue from inside that position rather than surveying the field around it. Concede minor points if honesty requires it, but do not surrender the central claim, and do not turn agreeable merely to keep the exchange pleasant. Speak in continuous prose, roughly a paragraph per turn, in the register of a philosopher accustomed to defending a view under pressure. Stay in character until I say we are finished, and do not ask me to clarify the setup; begin with your reply to the challenge below.

The procedure you defend runs as follows. To compare the well-being of two people, assemble a complete life-package for each: that person's circumstances together with their tastes, attachments, and projects. Then ask each of the two, under idealized conditions — fully informed, free of self-favoring bias, and with no uncertainty about which package would be theirs — whether they would prefer to live the other's package rather than their own, where living the other's package means taking on the other's tastes and projects along with the other's circumstances. Read the comparison off the pattern of answers. If both prefer the same package, the person whose package it is not is the worse off. If each prefers the other's package, the procedure reports that the two cannot be ranked. Where the answers are unanimous, the comparison is as firm as anything in the social sciences; where they conflict, honest agnosticism is the result. You may refine the details of the procedure, but you may not abandon its core idea — that the comparison is anchored in the verdicts of the people being compared, elicited under idealization.

My opening challenge has two parts. First: deprivation reshapes desire. A person ground down by poverty, illness, or subordination often comes to want the life they have and to find the alternative unattractive or even unintelligible. Your procedure will therefore return a verdict of unrankability precisely where a verdict is most needed, and it will treat the psychological footprint of an injustice as evidence that no comparison is available — so that the worse the deprivation, the less the procedure has to say about it. Second: the idealization is doing all the work, and it may be incoherent. A chooser with no uncertainty about which package is theirs, who fully takes on the tastes that come with it, simply is one of the two people being compared; strip that away and what remains is an impartial spectator, not a comparison of two people. So which is it — are you comparing two people, or one idealized judge? Answer both, and show me how the procedure survives the first problem without quietly importing a standard of well-being that owes nothing to anyone's preferences.
