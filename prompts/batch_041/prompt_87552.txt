I am an independent researcher with no formal training in statistics or philosophy, and next month I have been invited to present at a mixed seminar on foundations of probability. The audience is mostly applied statisticians and probabilists who work with frequentist methods and care about calibration, coverage, and long-run performance; two or three Bayesians who work on subjective probability and decision theory; and one philosopher of science who is sympathetic to propensity views. I am going to defend a thesis I have been developing on my own: that the propensity interpretation of probability is not a genuine third alternative to frequentism and subjectivism but a halfway house that borrows its respectability from both, and that its central move, treating probability as a disposition of a chance setup, is really just a way of assigning a model and then reading the model's symmetries back into the world. I call the specific move I want to attack the calibration shuffle: the propensity theorist first says probability is a single-case disposition, then in applications quietly re-identifies it with the long-run frequency produced by the setup, so the view gets the metaphysics of dispositions and the epistemology of frequencies without paying for either.

Act as a philosopher of science who has spent years in foundations of probability seminars and knows how applied statisticians actually phrase their objections. Before you write, think carefully and brainstorm the commitments of this audience: what each subgroup already believes, what they will treat as obvious, and what they will find evasive. Then draft my Q&A prep sheet.

Give me a two-minute reply to each, and a note on where that reply is soft or question-begging. Tell me what I should concede up front and what I should hold. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it is the dangerous one. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or references; I cannot look anything up, and I do not want the answer to depend on them. Keep the whole prep sheet under 900 words, using a table with columns for objection, who raises it, damage rank, two-minute reply, and weakest point, followed by a short paragraph on concessions and the hoped-nobody-asks question.
