I'm trying to get my commitments straight on the levels of selection debate before a reading group, and I want someone to audit them rather than lecture me. I've got a nagging feeling that a couple of these don't fit together but I genuinely can't tell which ones. Here's where I've landed:

1. The gene's eye view is basically right. Selection just is differential replication of genes, and "for the good of the species" is never a real explanation of anything.
2. Group selection is real and got a bad rap. The consensus that killed it off in the 60's and 70's overcorrected, and plenty of cases are best described as competition between groups.
3. In humans specifically there are genuine group-level adaptations: traits that are a real fitness hit to every individual who has them, and that persist only because groups containing them outcompete groups that don't. I take that to be an emergent property of groups, not a restatement of individual advantage.
4. Kin selection already handles altruism. Once you see that helping relatives is just helping copies of your own genes, there's nothing left for group selection to explain.
5. Most of the genome is junk, most differences between species are drift, and inferring an adaptation from the fact that a trait is currently useful is the cardinal sin of the field.
6. The whole dispute about levels is at bottom a dispute about bookkeeping — which description you happen to find convenient — not a substantive disagreement about how the world works.

Which of these can't be held together, how much damage does each clash do, and what's the cheapest thing I'd have to give up in each case? I suspect some of these only look like they conflict, so tell me which ones those are and why they're fine, and rank the ones that are actually broken by severity. If you had to pick one revision that cleans up the most, what would it be?
