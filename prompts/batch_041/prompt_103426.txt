I am drafting a paper on the extended mind and want the specification in hand before I assess any further proposals. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of cognitive extension must meet: the requirements a view has to satisfy to count as an account of cognition's extending beyond the brain, rather than a restatement of the familiar fact that we use tools.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Cover, at a minimum:

- The cases a theory must classify correctly — a person who keeps information in a notebook and consults it as the rest of us consult memory; someone rotating a shape on a screen rather than in the head; two people who remember a shared episode together; a person with a worn or implanted device; and ordinary reliance on a search engine — and the verdict the theory must return for each.
- The distinctions it must not collapse: causal influence versus constitutive part; the personal versus the subpersonal level; a resource one is coupled with versus one merely used; and a resource that is reliable and automatically endorsed versus one that must be deliberately consulted and checked.
- The results it must remain consistent with: that ordinary cognition is realized in neural processes; that not everything a person causally depends on can count as part of the mind, on pain of a cognitive bloat in which the world is annexed wholesale; and the developmental and clinical facts about how people actually offload memory and calculation onto external props.

Then evaluate three constraints I have been toying with, telling me for each whether it is a genuine desideratum or merely a prejudice of one camp, and fold the genuine ones into the list: first, that the relation must be diachronic — it has to make sense of a resource being present, absent, and swapped, not merely of a single episode; second, that the theory must say what the extension is for, that is, name the cognitive function the external resource performs and say whether that function could in principle be performed internally; third, that extension should be a matter of degree, with each case falling somewhere on a scale, rather than all-or-nothing.

Two further cases I keep getting stuck on, which the finished list should adjudicate: a person who offloads arithmetic to a calculator for sums they could do in their head, and a person whose phone autocompletes their messages with suggestions they accept without checking.

The part I most want is where two or more of the requirements cannot be satisfied together; if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, that is itself the result. Tell me also which single requirement excludes the most otherwise plausible theories. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases; a brief closing scorecard of how the parity-principle view, the complementarity view, and the integrationist view fare against the list is welcome, but it should not take over. Write in American English.
