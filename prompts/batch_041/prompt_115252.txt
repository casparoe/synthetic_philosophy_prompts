I run a small mediation service that mostly handles campus and municipal disputes, and I keep getting cases where the two sides aren't just disagreeing about facts or interests—they're coming at it from different moral frameworks, and a split-the-difference deal feels like both sides betraying something. I want to build a reusable proceedure out of the idea that moral compromise can be a kind of gains from trade: each side gives up something it ranks lower for something it ranks higher, and if the rankings differ enough there's a package both prefer to no deal. The key distinction I care about is between a compromise that leaves both sides with a package they rank higher than no deal and a compromise that just divides the disputed thing in half. But I don't know how to turn that into steps a facillitator with no philosophy training can actually run in a 90-minute session.

We usually have two parties, sometimes with 3-5 reps each, a neutral facilitator, no authority to impose anything, and we need to end with either a written agreement or a clear no-deal. We can't ask people to rank their values on a single scale, and we can't ask them to abandon a core commitment. I want a checklist or a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values question rather than a facts question. It should say how to tell a genuine moral trade from a sellout, and how to handle a party that says a value is non-negotiable.

Then run it on two cases. First, a dispute over whether a student group can host a speaker who has argued for restricting immigration; one side values open inquiry, the other values not normalizing xenophobia. Second, a city council fight over removing a statue of a local founder who was also a slaveholder; one side values historical memory, the other values not honoring someone who did that. For each, show the steps, the proposed trade, and where the procedure either produces a deal or says no deal. Include one case where the procedure tells me to stop even though I want to keep going. And tell me what this procedure can't do—where it will misfire or where a facilitator just has to make a judgment call.

Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning—no preamble, no restating the question.
