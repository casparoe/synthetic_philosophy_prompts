So a popular cosmology book I read has a chapter on Gödel's rotating universe and I want to know how much of it to trust. Here's the arguement as best I can reconstruct it. Gödel found a solution to Einstein's field equations describing a spinning universe, and in that universe there are loops in spacetime — closed timelike curves — so in principle you could travel back and meet your younger self. The book says Gödel's point was that since relativity permits a universe where you can go into the past, and since time travel is obviously impossible (grandfather paradox and all), this shows that time isn't really a feature of the world at all, just something we project onto it. It says Gödel therefore held that time is ideal — a form of intuition rather than a property of things in themselves — and that he suspected the actual universe might be one of these rotating ones. It also says the result embarrassed Einstein and that cosmologists eventually set rotating-universe models aside because of the paradoxes they'd allow. The chapter's punchline is: "Gödel showed that if general relativity is correct, time doesn't exist."

I don't know enough to sort this out. Which sentences here would a philosopher of physics accept, which are oversimplifications, and which are just wrong? Is that a good objection to Gödel, or does it miss what he was actually claiming?
