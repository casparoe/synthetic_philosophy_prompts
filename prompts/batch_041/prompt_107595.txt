Ok, I'm building a problem set for an upper-level game theory course. The students know Nash equilibrium, theyve seen the definition of a mixed strategy, and they've seen iterated elimination of dominated strategies stated in a way that allows the dominating strategy to be a mixture. I need one exercise that makes them see that saying no strategy is dominated and saying nothing gets eliminated are not the same claim, and that the mixed part of the dominance definition is actually doing work. So build me the game.

Specs, and I want all of them met at once:

- Two players, simultaneous, complete information, three or four pure strategies each, no chance moves, and write out the full payoff matrix with real numbers, not letters.
- At the start, no pure strategy of either player is strictly dominated by another pure strategy. If you only allow pure-versus-pure dominance, round one eliminates nothing and the whole thing is stuck right there.
- Even so, at least one strategy is strictly dominated by a genuine mixture of that player's other strategies, with positive weight on more than one of them. Give me the weights and verify the strict inequality column by column, not as one aggregate comparison. A mixture that puts all the weight on one strategy doesn't count, that's just pure dominance again.
- The elimination has to keep going after that. At least one further round is needed, and that later round should work by ordinary pure dominance, so the mixed step is what unlocks the cascade. Tell me what survives after each round.
- It terminates in a single surviving profile, which should also be the unique Nash equilibrium of the game, and tell me whether that equilibrium is strict.
- Don't make it symmetric or pretty just for looks.

Then write it up the way you'd write the model answer. Say which strategy gets eliminated when and why, show the payoff comparisons for the mixed step column by column, describe the set of mixtures that do the dominating (is there only one weighting, or a whole range of them?), and say whether that same elimination could ever be reached without appealing to mixtures at all.

And be honest about whats carrying the weight. Which feature of the payoffs makes the mixed step necessary instead of decorative, and what happens to the cascade if I change it? What if I relax strictly dominated to weakly dominated — does the example survive, and does the order in which you eliminate things start to matter? What if I drop the requirement that the later round be pure dominance?

Last thing. Somebody who thinks dominance by mixtures is a swindle will attack this exercise, probably by saying that a rational player would never actually randomize, or that the argument is smuggling in common knowledge of rationality. Give me the strongest version of that objection and say whether it lands — and in answering, be clear about whether the mixture is being used as a plan or only as a benchmark for comparison.
