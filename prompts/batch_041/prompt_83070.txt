A colleague who teaches a general-education course on language and technology has circulated the following summary of Douglas Hofstadter's essay on Google Translate, and I want a careful audit of it before I pass it along to students. The summary reads: "Hofstadter's essay is the classic statement of the thesis that machine translation is impossible. He shows that Google Translate works by crunching word-co-occurrence statistics over enormous corpora, with no semantic representation of any kind standing behind the fluent output. Since the meaning of a sentence depends on the speaker's intentions, and a program that sees only text has no access to intentions, no such program can ever translate; the evidence he offers is a series of examples in which the system produces grammatical but tone-deaf renderings that miss irony, idiom, and register. His prescription, then, is that we must build a machine with a genuine understanding of the world -- a real mind -- which makes the essay a version of Searle's Chinese Room argument: symbols get shuffled around, but nobody is home. The practical upshot is that machine translation should not be trusted for anything serious."

My own sense is that the examples are the strongest part of this and that the word "impossible" is doing too much work, but I don't know the essay well enough to say where the summary goes wrong. Please go through it claim by claim and sort each into one of three bins -- faithful to what Hofstadter actually argues, a defensible compression by a competent popularizer, or a misstatement -- saying in each case what a careful defender of the essay would concede and what they would resist. I am particularly interested in four things: whether the argument from speaker meaning is really his argument or the summarizer's reconstruction; whether the essay's diagnosis is best put as a claim about the shallowness of the process -- about what the system fails to do -- rather than as a barrier in principle; whether the Chinese Room comparison is a fair way to file the essay, or whether it imports a conclusion the essay neither needs nor argues for; and whether the closing "practical upshot" follows from the parts of the summary that survive the audit. One further question: if the premise that the system has no semantics is granted, does that by itself establish that its translations are bad, or does that step need an additional premise? If so, state it.
