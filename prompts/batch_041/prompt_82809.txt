Our society's marking scheme is a mark out of 12: up to four marks for accurate exposition of the positions discussed, up to four for the strength and coherence of the argument, and up to four for the treatment of the follow-up question. A mark of 10 or above requires that the answer contain no errors of substance.

**Question.** William Molyneux asked Locke whether a man born blind, who had been taught by touch to tell a cube from a sphere of nearly the same size, would be able to tell which was which by sight alone if his sight were restored. (a) Explain what turns on answering no rather than yes. (b) Give the strongest reason for each answer and say which you find more convincing. (c) A classmate has asked two further things: whether your answer changes if the man is not asked to say which object is the cube, but to say which of the two would roll more easily if it were pushed, and whether it matters that he is an adult who has spent his whole life interpreting the world through his hands. Answer both.

**Answer.** Molyneux's question, which he put to Locke in a letter that Locke later printed in the Essay, asks whether a man born blind who has learned by touch to distinguish a cube from a sphere would be able, on having his sight restored, to tell which was which by looking, before touching them again. Molyneux and Locke both answered no. Leibniz, in the New Essays, is usually said to have answered yes, but I read him as closer to Locke, since he holds that the man would have to learn to connect what he sees with what he has felt.

(a) If the answer is no, then the ideas of sight and of touch are not joined by nature but only by experience, which supports the empiricist view that our spatial ideas are built up from the senses rather than being innate. If the answer is yes, then something must already be common to the two senses, and the mind must bring its own structure to what it receives.

(b) The strongest reason for no is Locke's: there is no necessary connection between a visual idea and a tactile one. The man has never had occasion to pair this appearance with that feel, so nothing tells him which is which; his new sense would deliver a chaos of colours and lights, out of which no shape would stand out until experience had taught him which patterns go with which bodies. The strongest reason for yes is that the man is not a blank slate: he already understands a cube as a body bounded by six equal squares, and once he sees the two objects he can work out which appearance could belong to a body of that shape. I find the no answer more convincing, because the yes answer seems to require him to know already which visual features go with squareness, and that is exactly what is in dispute.

(c) I think he would answer the rolling question straight away. He knows by touch that a sphere rolls and a cube does not, and the round look of the sphere is the look of the very thing that rolls, so as soon as he can see he can say which one would move. This shows that the no answer is really about naming and identifying rather than about perception itself, and it shows that it makes no difference that he is an adult, since the question is about the ideas themselves rather than about how long he has been using his hands.

It is often said that people who gain their sight in later life cannot at first match what they see to what they have felt, which bears out the no answer. The case therefore supports the empiricist, since if the man cannot tell the two apart at first sight, that proves that spatial ideas come from experience and are not innate.
