I run a little philosophy night out of my kitchen — five or six friends, a couple of their teenagers, nobody with a degree in anything, me least of all. Lately we've been doing hard choices: what to do when two things you care about pull against each other and neither one seems better than the other. I've been reading about it alot and I think I finally get the shape of it, but I'm not sure I could defend it, so last week I set homework and told everyone to write out their answer instead of just arguing at me from the couch.

Here's the question I gave them.

Suppose someone — we called her Dara — has to choose between staying in her hometown and keeping her late father's hardware store going, and taking a research job across the country that she's wanted for ten years. If she stays, the store survives; her brother works there and the neighborhood counts on it; and she promised her father before he died that she'd keep it open. If she goes, the store closes, her brother is out of work, and she finally gets to do the work she thinks she was made for. Take it as given that neither option is better than the other. Then answer three things. One: is this just a hard choice, or is it a case of incommensurable values, and what's the difference between those two things? Two: run the small-improvement argument on it — if we make the job a little bit better, does it become better than the store, and what does the answer to that show? Three: does it follow that there's no right answer here and nothing for Dara to actually reason about?

One of the guys wrote this. I've typed it up exactly as he wrote it.

1. I don't think this is just a hard choice. A hard choice in the ordinary sense is when you aren't sure which is better — you don't know the facts, or you can't see the consequences. Dara knows the facts. The problem is deeper than that. The two things she's choosing between aren't the same kind of good. One is about loyalty, keeping faith with her father and her brother and a place; the other is about her own life and the work she was made to do. There's no common ruler you could lay across both of them, so the values are incommensurable. That's different from being unsure, and I think that's the whole point of the distinction.

2. The small-improvement argument (I want to say it's Raz's, or else it's the Chang woman, I always mix those two up) is what's supposed to show this. If the store and the job were equally good, then a slightly better version of the job — a bit more money, a nicer lab — would have to be better than the store, because you'd have equality plus an improvement on top. But it isn't better. The slightly improved job is still not better than the store. So they were never equal. And since neither one is better than the other, and they aren't equal either, there's no way to compare them at all — they're incomparable. I believe this is what Chang calls "parity," which as far as I can tell is just her fancy word for incomparability, her way of saying there's a fourth option alongside better, worse and equal where nothing at all can be said about which is better.

3. So no, there's no right answer, and in a way there's nothing to reason about. If there's no fact of the matter about which is better, then Dara can't get it wrong, so she's free to just pick one and go, and she shouldn't feel bad afterward about the store closing, because regret would only make sense if she'd done something worse than the alternative. The one thing that complicates all this is the promise. A promise gives you a common measure — you can ask whether keeping her word outweighs the research — so once the promise is in play the values aren't really incommensurable anymore; the promise puts them back on the same scale and makes it an ordinary hard choice again.

If something he says is defensible but he's stated it way too strongly, say so and say what the careful version would be. If he's just made something up — like attributing an idea to the wrong person — flag it, but honestly I care way more about the reasoning than about who said what. Don't be nice about it, I'd rather find out now than keep teaching the group something wrong. And at the end give me, in a paragraph, what the best answer to my three questions actually is, so I have something to hold his up against.
