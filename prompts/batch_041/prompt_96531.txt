I volunteer on the grants panel for a small regional arts council — we hand out maybe forty small grants a year and I'm first reader on most of them, which means about fifteen minutes per application and no real expertise in whatever medium the applicant works in (we get everything: ceramics, choral music, video, a woman doing puppet theatre about her grandmother). A friend in my book club keeps pressing Tolstoy's What Is Art? on me — the claim that art isn't about beauty or technique at all, it's about one person having felt something, calling that feeling back up in himself and putting it into external signs so other people catch it, and that this catching (his word for it is infection, I think) is what makes a thing art at all, with the strength of it depending on how individual the feeling is, how clearly it comes across, and above all how sincere the artist is — and I've started to suspect his theory would serve our panel better than the aesthetic-quality language we currently use, which honestly just means we fund our friends. But I can't bring a whole book into a fifteen-minute review, and I dont want to hand my colleagues a purity test either. So can you turn it into a procedure I could actually run on an application?

I want it as questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision up to the board because the question is one of values rather than anything I can check. Two worries to build in. First, the whole point of the view is that the feeling has to actually arrive in a receiver, so I can't test it alone in my chair — tell me what the procedure does when I feel nothing but a colleague does, or the reverse, and how much weight two readers can honestly carry. Second, he thinks you can spot real art by the fingerprints of the fake: recycled material, photographic imitation of reality, reliance on one big striking effect, work that just distracts you. I want those as a checklist, but I also want you to be honest that I'd be using them as a guess about the artist's sincerity, which I can't actually inspect. And make the procedure say out loud that the step from is-this-art to is-this-good-art is a moral step — that's where a funding decision stops being about craft and starts being about which feelings we want circulating in our town, and a first reader shouldn't get to make that call alone.

Then run it on three applications. (1) A video installation by someone with a strong CV — technically amazing, but it borrows most of its imagery from a much better-known artist, builds to a single big reveal at the end, and left me cold even though I admired it. (2) A documentary shot on phones by a group of ex-factory workers about the plant closing, which made me cry, made two colleagues cry, and which several people from that town tell me is exactly how it was; no score, no staging, nothing fancy. (3) The one I actually care about: a difficult theatre piece by a young company, almost no plot, long silences. I've seen it twice and think about it constantly — but it takes a lot of prior theatre-going to sit through, most of our panel would be lost, and I genuinely couldn't tell you what feeling it's supposed to transmit. I suspect your procedure is going to tell me to refuse #3 and fund #2, and if that's where it lands I want you to say so plainly, and then tell me what the procedure can't do: where it will misfire, and which of its steps I should stop pretending is a test and admit is just me.
