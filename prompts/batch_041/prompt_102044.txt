There is a tension I keep meeting in every case where an agent deliberately arranges things so that he — or his successors — cannot later do what they would otherwise do, and I have been unable to find whether it has a name. I would like either the established term, if there is one, or a proposed one properly defined; but in either case what I am after is the carving, not the label.

The cases are familiar. A man who knows he will eat the ice cream at midnight throws it out, or asks his host not to serve dessert. A legislature locks in a fiscal rule so that a later majority cannot undo it cheaply. A constitution protects a right in language deliberately hard to amend. A founder leaves instructions meant to govern an institution long after he is gone. In each, an agent at one time arranges matters so that an agent at a later time cannot, or cannot without cost, do what he would otherwise do.

What puzzles me is how such arrangements are defended. The defense is always that the later agent is the same agent as the earlier one, so that the arrangement is an exercise of self-government rather than a constraint imposed from outside. The man who throws out the ice cream is the man who would otherwise eat it; the majority that locks in the rule is the majority that would otherwise undo it; the people who protect the right are the people who would otherwise infringe it. On this account nothing has been taken from the later agent, since he is only being held to his own will.

But the arrangement does work only if the later agent's will diverges from the earlier one's. If the midnight self wanted what the morning self wants, there would be nothing to throw out. So the justification appeals to a sameness that the mechanism presupposes to be absent, and the mechanism presupposes a divergence that the justification denies. Those who design these devices seem to know this: they build them because they expect the later self to want otherwise, and they are seldom wrong to expect that the later self will chafe against the device. Each resolution I have met takes one horn and abandons the other. Either the later agent is a different agent, in which case this is one party ruling another and the talk of self-government is a euphemism; or he is the same agent, in which case one wonders why persuasion would not have served and why a constraint was ever needed.

Several things I have read seem adjacent without being quite this. The ordinary problem of paternalism concerns one agent overriding another, which is not the shape of the case, since here overrider and overridden are supposed to be one. The suggestion that we are divided or successive selves makes the two agents genuinely distinct, which dissolves the puzzle rather than locating it. And in constitutional writing there is a related but different contrast between the authority that makes a constitution and the authority that the constitution then governs. None of these, as far as I can tell, names the structure I am pointing at: a justification that must invoke sameness together with a mechanism that must presuppose difference.

Does this structure have a name? If it does, tell me how it is distinguished from each of the neighbours above and which fits best. If it does not, give me a name and a definition that says what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. I would particularly like to know whether the tension vanishes in the case where the later agent in fact endorses the arrangement, or only becomes invisible there; and whether the same structure appears when two agents bind each other by mutual agreement rather than one binding himself.
