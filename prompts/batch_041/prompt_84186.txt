Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — the second of the Untimely Meditations, published in 1874 — distinguishes three ways in which a person or a culture can stand towards its past: the monumental, which looks to past greatness for models to imitate and rivals to surpass; the antiquarian, which reveres, tends and preserves what has been handed down; and the critical, which drags the past before a tribunal, condemns what it finds unjust or stifling, and tries to break with it. Nietzsche treats all three as capable of serving life and as capable of turning against it, and holds that which mode is healthy depends on the strength, the needs and the situation of whoever is doing the remembering.

Below are nine ways of engaging with the past that are common in the present. For each one: (a) say which of the three modes it most nearly instantiates, or, if it is genuinely a hybrid or fits none of them, say so and explain briefly why; and (b) give it a single score from −5 to +5 for how far it serves life in Nietzsche's sense, where +5 means it maximally strengthens the capacity of those who engage in it to live, act and grow, and −5 means it maximally weakens, deadens or displaces that capacity. Keep each justification to two or three sentences. Then give a final ranking of all nine from most to least life-serving, and name the item you are least confident about, saying what would have to be true of it for your score to move by three points or more.

1. A retired teacher who spends a decade reconstructing a great-grandmother's migration route from parish registers and ships' manifests, and privately prints the result for relatives who never asked for it.

2. A city that removes a nineteenth-century colonial governor's statue from its central square and re-erects it in the local museum, on a plinth whose new label explains who paid for the statue and what the man did.

3. A multinational firm's corporate-history page, which traces the business back to a single artisan's workshop and presents every later merger, rebranding and plant closure as the unfolding of the founder's original vision.

4. An edit war on a collaborative encyclopedia over the casualty figures of a twentieth-century battle, in which contributors repeatedly revert one another and each side cites sources that are themselves partisan.

5. A national school textbook that presents the wars of a medieval dynasty as the first stirrings of the modern nation and its defeats as national humiliations.

6. A repertory theatre that revives a canonical play with the blocking, pacing and costuming recorded from a celebrated production of a century ago, treating that production itself as the thing to be preserved.

7. A music recommender that resurfaces for a listener both the songs of her adolescence and songs from decades before her birth, tuned to whatever keeps her listening longest.

8. Descendants of people enslaved on a university's founding endowment petition it to return the human remains and objects in its collections and to endow a centre for research into the institution's own history.

9. A therapist who helps a patient retell a family history of failure and shame as a history of endurance, so that the patient can act differently towards his own children.

Finally, in no more than 200 words: if Nietzsche is right that each mode can be healthy in one situation and poisonous in another, what does your ranking suggest about which of the three modes is most at risk of being over-served in the present, and which most under-served? Argue from the pattern your own scores produced, and do not assume that the nine items are a representative sample of anything.
