Three chapters into the new novel and I've hit a wall I can't think my way through, so I'm outsourcing the philosophy to you.

Here's the setup. My protagonist joins a small secular community — no god, no guru, just a charter and a rota for the cooking — whose entire doctrine is that only the absence of craving and suffering matters. Everything else people call good (love, work, beauty, being right, the satisfaction of finishing a thing) is either a route to one of those two absences or a distraction from them. I've been calling it tranquilism in my notes. I'm not asking whether it's true. I'm asking for the spec.

What I need is the list of things any theory in this area has to get right before I can put it in characters' mouths without the book reading like a first-year seminar. The cases it has to handle, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse (a life being good vs. a stretch of time being good, craving vs. merely wanting, the absence of a bad thing vs. the presence of a good one), the facts about how people actually work that it can't contradict. With the case or consideration behind each item, please, so I can see which ones are load-bearing and which are just how one camp likes its furniture arranged. Mark those too — non-negotiable vs. a matter of taste.

And keep it in the weeds. I don't need the survey of hedonism, desire-satisfaction and objective lists; I've read enough of that to fake a paragraph of dialogue. I want the awkward joints. How does the theory handle time — does dreamless sleep count toward a life's score, is tranquility a state you're in or an achievement you have? What does it say about the woman who gets her cravings amputated rather than satisfied? Can the doctrine even be stated without prescribing a craving for itself? Is "mattering" a separate requirement from "being desired"? Whatever you've got along those lines.

Two things I care about more than you probably will:

1. The purpose clause. This theory doesn't have to be true, it has to be livable — good enough for a real community to run on and for one woman to hold onto while she's doing the dishes. So if a requirement follows only from wanting a tidy academic object (full comparability between lives, a single number at the end of it), flag it as that, because my characters don't need it and it makes them sound like actuaries.

2. Tell me if some pair of the requirements can't both be met. That's the scene. That's where the fight in chapter nine goes. If the list itself turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly — that's the most useful sentence you could write me. Also flag the one requirement that does the most excluding, the one a theory can't survive losing, because that's the one my antagonist will attack.

Then, short — a page at most — where tranquilism actually strains against the list. Not a verdict on the view, just the joints that creak. I need to know which scenes are genuine trouble and which are my protagonist being dim.

A table is fine for the list. No reading list please, I'm not going to read any of it, and I can't have a character cite a book I've never opened.

Last line, keep the shape exactly: Verdict: [jointly satisfiable | not jointly satisfiable] :: [the one requirement that does the most excluding, six words or less]
