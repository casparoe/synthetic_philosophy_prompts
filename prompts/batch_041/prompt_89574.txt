I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I need a poem for an occasion I would rather not be writing for. A colleague who taught me most of what I know about building software died in the spring, and his memorial is in three weeks. In the meantime I have been reading Douglas Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop, badly, I am sure, and the claim I cannot get out of my head is that a person's "I" is not confined to one skull: the thing that is you is partly re-created in the heads of the people who know you, and it goes on running there after you stop. I would like a poem that holds that idea, but holds it as a fact about two particular people rather than as a thesis.

I would like the poem to be a pantoum of exactly five quatrains, because the form does the argument for me: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, and the poem closes by handing the opening stanza's first line back as its own final line and its third line back into the last stanza's second. The repeated lines should be identical word for word, as the form requires, and their meaning should change as they change mouths. Concretely, I want the poem to begin in his voice and end in mine, and I want a reader who knows the idea to be able to point to the line where the "I" changes hands.

One thing I have added myself, and want the poem to carry: what survives in me is not a copy of him. It is what I was able to see of him and what I needed from him, so it is partial and edited, and part of it is my own invention. I do not want that framed as loss or as a betrayal of the original; it is simply what being carried inside another person amounts to. At least one repeated line should cross that boundary, a sentence that in his mouth was about him and in mine is about me.

Keep the diction concrete, please. I would rather the poem not contain the words consciousness, identity, soul, or pattern, and I would rather it never mention Hofstadter, brains, or loops; the vocabulary should come from the ordinary furniture of a shared working life, the way he took his coffee, a chair nobody moved, a log file. One of the repeated lines should be the kind of thing he actually used to say at work, the habitual line about going to look at the logs when something broke, and it should survive verbatim into someone else's mouth in the last stanza, where it no longer means quite what it meant.

Please give me exactly five numbered quatrains, numbered 1 through 5, with no title, epigraph, dedication, or note before or after. Unrhymed is fine; I care about the repetition, not the rhymes.
