I'm in a social choice / voting theory course right now and we just did the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, and I've got this argument that seems to prove way too much. I can't find the bad step and its driving me nuts. Here it is:

(1) A voting rule is strategy-proof just in case no voter can ever do better by reporting a ranking other than their true one.
(2) Gibbard–Satterthwaite: if there are at least three candidates, voters can rank them in any order, the rule can make any candidate win, and the rule is strategy-proof, then the rule is dictatorial.
(3) So any rule that isn't dictatorial and has at least three candidates must be manipulable.
(4) A rule is manipulable only if some voter can get a better outcome by misreporting.
(5) In a big election, one vote never changes the winner, so no voter can get a better outcome by misreporting.
(6) So in a big election, every rule is strategy-proof.
(7) Then by (2), every big-election rule is dictatorial.
(8) But plurality rule in a big election obviously isn't dictatorial.
(9) Contradiction. So one of these has to go.

I'd really like to keep (2) and (8). (2) is the theorem and (8) seems like common sense. So I suspect the problem is somewhere in (4)-(6), but I can't articulate what exactly is wrong. Which step do you reject, and why did it look so plausible? And what does rejecting it cost — e.g., if I give up (5), what do I have to say about strategic voting in large elections, and does that undermine the way the theorem is usually applied? I don't need citations or anything, just the diagnosis.
