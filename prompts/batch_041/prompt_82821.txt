Before a workshop on information in biology, I have to circulate a short position statement, and I'd like an audit of it first. Here is where I've landed.

(i) The genetic code is a code and not a metaphor. A stretch of DNA carries semantic information about the protein it specifies, and the relation is arbitrary in the way a code's mapping is arbitrary: nothing in the chemistry of a codon makes it mean its amino acid.

(ii) Nothing is about anything except by virtue of selection. A state or structure has content only if it was shaped, over a history of selection, to be used as a proxy by some consumer system; there is no content without a consumer, and no consumer without selection.

(iii) Codes do not require readers. Transcription and translation are mechanisms rather than interpretations — the ribosome is no more a homunculus than a lock is.

(iv) The parity thesis is false. Genes are not merely one developmental resource among many; epigenetic marks, maternal cytoplasmic factors and the rest do not carry information in the way genes do.

(v) The information in a genome does not depend on that genome's own history. A gene assembled from scratch by a machine, with no evolutionary past whatsoever, and inserted into a cell where it is translated into a working protein, still codes for that protein — and my confidence that my own genome carries information about my traits does not rest on any particular selection story.

Tell me which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest way out is in each case. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and what the repair costs me elsewhere in the list, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Lead with the most serious problem in your first sentence, then give the audit: no preamble and no restating of my list.
