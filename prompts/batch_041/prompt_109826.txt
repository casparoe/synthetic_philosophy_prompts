Next week's episode of my little philosophy-of-tech podcast is with a researcher who reckons the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the ones showing you can't have calibration within groups plus equal false-positive rates plus equal false-negative rates all at once, unless the base rates are equal or the predictor is basically perfect — have been read way too dramatically. His line is that they're not really about fairness at all, just about what happens when a single threshold has to do two jobs, and that once you see that, the only interesting question left is which errors you're willing to trade off against which.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions that make him work for it. I want at least one that pins down the exact conditions and a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give (if he says "ah but only when the base rates differ", I want the follow-up ready). Also: one on whether the result is a fact about fairness or a symptom of a badly chosen target variable; one on whether letting the system abstain on borderline cases, or randomise its outputs, dissolves the trade-off or just shunts it somewhere less visible; and one on the subgroup version, where the guarantee has to hold for every intersection of groups rather than one at a time. Finish with a question about what would actually change his mind. Keep it conversational, no softballs, and write it in British English.
