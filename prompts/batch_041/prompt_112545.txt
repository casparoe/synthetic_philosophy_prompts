I am the editor of a widely implemented technical standard — the kind of document that gets written into commercial contracts and, in several jurisdictions, incorporated into regulation by reference. My committee meets monthly, and the first item on every agenda is the same question, which I have to answer before any other business can proceed: is the change to the text before us a clarification or a change? Our rules turn on the difference. A clarification may be issued in a minor revision without notice, on the ground that the standard always said this and we are only saying it better. Anything that alters what the text requires needs a full revision, an impact assessment, formal notice to every registered implementer and a migration period measured in months. Call it wrongly in one direction and we silently move the goalposts for thousands of implementers who have built to the old reading; call it wrongly in the other and we spend nine months and a great deal of goodwill on a wording fix.

The only principled way I know to draw the line is the one Quine dismantled. A clarification leaves the meaning of the text untouched; a change alters it. That presupposes that there is a fact of the matter about what the text means, independent of what we go on to do with it — and, more specifically, that some sentences are true or false in virtue of meaning alone while others are true or false in virtue of how the world is. Quine's argument in 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism' is that the distinction will not bear that weight: no belief is beyond revision, the difference between revising a belief and revising a meaning is one of degree and convenience rather than kind, and much of what we call a change of meaning is a redistribution of commitments across a body of sentences. I find the argument convincing and I still have to answer the question on Tuesday morning.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my committee can actually run, written so that a member with no philosophical training could apply it. Specifically:

- the questions to be asked, in order, and what answer to each would be sufficient to move on;
- the point at which the procedure returns a plain no;
- the point at which it must stop and hand the matter to the committee, because what remains is a question about what we value rather than about what the text means — how much disruption is tolerable, whether the purpose of the clause justifies the cost, who should bear the burden of a wrong call;
- a short form for the routine cases (typographical errors, cross-references, dead clauses) and the full form for everything else;
- a form of words for the minute, so that a decision can be recorded and later defended or overturned.

Three constraints on the procedure. First, the drafting committee was dissolved in the 1990s and its minutes were never published, so there is no authorial intention to appeal to and nobody left to ask. Second, several clauses are inconsistent with others in the same document, so 'what the text always meant' is not always a single thing, even before we begin. Third, implementers' practice is itself a fact that changes: what would have been a clarification in 2004 may be a change now, and the reverse.

The procedure must not pretend to have recovered the distinction it is replacing. Tell me openly what you are putting in its place — settled usage among implementers, the purpose the clause serves, the cost of error in each direction, convention, something else — and be candid about what is lost when the substitute takes over. Two moves are available to you, and I would rather you use them knowingly than rediscover them: the legal-philosophical picture of a settled core of a rule surrounded by a disputed penumbra, and the idea of an explication, in which a loose notion is replaced by a precise one that need only resemble it and is judged by its exactness and usefulness rather than by fidelity to what we always meant. Neither settles the operational question on its own, so do not stop at either. Say also where the procedure will misfire, and in which direction it will misfire if we follow it strictly.

Then run it on three cases from our current backlog.

1. A clause requiring that a message be acknowledged before a transaction is committed. Read strictly, the acknowledgement must arrive before the commit; read loosely, it need only be sent before it. The strict reading deadlocks the protocol in a configuration we ourselves recommend elsewhere in the document, so no implementer has ever taken it. The committee proposes to insert the word 'sent' and to call this a clarification.

2. A clause defining a business day by reference to the days on which banks are open, without saying whose banks. Roughly a third of implementers have read it as referring to the receiving institution and two-thirds to the sending institution; interoperability has held because most traffic crosses jurisdictions with overlapping calendars. The committee now proposes to specify the receiving institution, describing this as the removal of an ambiguity.

3. A numeric limit in a clause that has stood unaltered and unquestioned for fifteen years. It is now apparent that the original drafter derived the figure from two other parameters in the document and made an arithmetic error. The arithmetically correct value is materially different. The committee's position — and, I should say, mine — is that this is a clarification, since the text was plainly meant to state the derived figure and merely failed to; the alternative is a full revision, notice to thousands of implementers and a migration period, all for a number that everybody now knows is wrong.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and what a committee member should do when it runs out.
