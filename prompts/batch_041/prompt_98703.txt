I teach in a university philosophy department, and for the past two years I have also sat on the grants committee of a small charitable trust, which is how I have come to spend my autumns arguing about a dead woman's reading of G. E. Moore. The trust was established by a local benefactor who read Principia Ethica as an undergraduate and never quite recovered from it: the fund exists, as she put it in the letter that governs us, to support the enjoyment of beautiful things and the pleasures of human affection, which she took to be the greatest goods Moore says we can be certain of, and she left two instructions to her trustees that we are supposed to apply to every proposal. The first is that we should judge a candidate by asking what the world would be like if it contained nothing but that thing. The second is that the worth of a whole is not the sum of the worth of its parts, so we should not assume that a proposal is worth whatever its components are worth added together. She also gave us a rule I have come to dread: one grant per cycle, with whatever conditions we choose to attach, and no splitting the sum.

This year we have two finalists, and the sum is enough for only one of them as submitted. The first comes from a regional museum that wants to clear, replant, and open to the public a derelict nineteenth-century walled garden on the edge of town; the drawings are genuinely lovely, the site is currently a bramble pit, and the museum's projection is that perhaps a few hundred people a year would wander through it, most of them on their way to something else. The second comes from a neighborhood organization that wants to run a multi-year program pairing isolated elderly residents with teenagers for weekly visits; the organizers are careful to say that what they are offering is companionship and reliable presence, not friendship in any thick sense, and they have decent evidence that participants on both sides would be measurably less lonely. My fellow trustees have staked out three positions. One holds that persons matter more than plants and that the mentoring program is therefore the better buy. Another holds that the garden is the only proposal that answers the trust's distinctive purpose, since the mentoring program is exactly the sort of thing any foundation would fund. A third, a retired banker who has never once been moved by the donor's letter, says the bequest is simply incoherent, because beauty and friendship cannot be weighed on a common scale, and that we should fund whichever project shows the better outcomes per pound and stop pretending otherwise.

I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting, and I want it to be the kind of document the committee can actually vote on. Please draft it: recommend a concrete allocation, with a date by which the funds must be committed and one or two conditions the recipient could realistically meet, and engage the donor's Moorean framework seriously rather than treating it as decorative — including whether these two goods are commensurable at all, whether a garden that nobody much notices is worth anything, and whether reliable companionship counts as the good she had in mind. Give the strongest version of whichever position I end up rejecting, and tell me plainly where the donor's instructions run out and ordinary judgment has to take over. I don't need citations or a reading list; the committee would not read them. What I need is a recommendation, and reasons a trustee who disagrees could respond to.
