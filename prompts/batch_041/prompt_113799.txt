I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past few years teaching himself philosophy, and I have been asked to run a ninety-minute session for a university reading group that has been working through decision theory — about fourteen people, a mix of graduate students and staff, all comfortable with expected values and payoff matrices but not with the philosophical literature.

What I want them to feel is the pull of superrationality: the thought that when two agents confront the same problem by the same reasoning, each may treat the other's choice as a function of her own, so that cooperating in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma can be rational with no communication, no contract, and no repetition. They should get there by playing, not by being told; the name should come only in the debrief.

My sketch, which you should feel free to improve or replace outright: each participant privately writes a short decision rule — a few lines of plain conditional reasoning, no programming skill assumed — and hands it in. I then play every rule against every other rule and against itself in a round robin, using the usual payoffs, where mutual cooperation beats mutual defection and unilateral defection beats mutual cooperation, and I post the running scores. I expect the self-play entries to be the hinge. Into the pool I also slip a few facilitator-written bots, including at least one that defects unconditionally and one that mirrors whatever rule it is paired with.

Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme (no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the idea: the fixed-point character of a rule that fares well against its own copy, the tension between topping the scoreboard and being the sort of agent that can be mirrored, and what the presence of the defecting bots does to the lesson.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular the first-person inference that my choice just is his choice, and the case where the two agents are only partly alike, where I gather the notion is far less clean. Finally, say what would change your mind about the design: which pattern of results in the room would show that I am in fact teaching ordinary reciprocity or risk-aversion rather than the idea I am after.
