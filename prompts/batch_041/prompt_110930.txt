Ok so this is a slightly weird ask and I'm not sure I'm the right person for it, but here we are. I'm a fourth-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, my thesis chapter on Neurath and the Vienna Method is basically the only thing of mine anyone has read, and my friend who curated the Isotype show that's opening at the design museum next Thursday has asked me to do the opening remarks. Seven minutes, standing up, no slides, maybe eighty people in the room. Roughly half of them are data-viz and data-journalism people plus a few design historians who know the Vienna story better than I do and will notice if I fudge any of it. The other half are the museum's trustees, a donor, and two people from the city culture office who have never heard the word Isotype and are mainly there for the wine. It has to land for both halves. It can't be a lecture.

Here's the thing that's actually making this hard, and it's why I'd rather you draft it than me. The show's poster has, in enormous type, the slogan Neurath had on his office wall — "Words divide, pictures unite" — and the whole framing of the exhibition is that Isotype is the ancestor of the modern infographic, the universal language of data, pictures that explain themselves. I think that's close to the opposite of what he thought. He called what he was making a language-like technique rather than a language, he said over and over that it was a helping language that always came with words and that he knew it could never stand on its own, and he insisted his pictograms be countable — same-sized figure repeated, never a blown-up one, almost never any perspective — partly because a picture can lie. Blow up the symbol and a thousand looks like more than twelve hundred and the reader has no way to check. So the images aren't transparent, they're a built system of conventions somebody has to learn. Which means the sentimental thing I'd normally say at an opening — these pictures need no translation, they speak straight to anyone — is precisely the thing I shouldn't say, and the honest version is more interesting but much less rousing. I want the speech to do that rather than apologise for it: hold the room while I take away the thing most of them walked in believing.

Other constraints, all real:

- The occasion has ritual obligations I can't dodge. I have to thank the curator, the lender, the Arntz estate, and the donor whose name is on the wall. But the method's grammar treats people as interchangeable units of a type — the figures in the charts are literally the same person repeated — and Neurath's museum was a team operation where credit was collective (Marie Reidemeister did most of the actual transforming and got almost none of it for decades). I want the thanks to be sincere and also to sit a bit awkwardly against the view I'm describing. Let the awkwardness show, don't smooth it over with a joke.
- Don't make it about how prescient he was about infographics. The designers in the room don't need it and the trustees will glaze over.
- One live moment: I want to hold up one of the charts and ask the room to read it with the caption covered, and I want them to genuinely not be able to. Then use whatever happens. Keep it to thirty seconds though — this is an opening, not a workshop.
- It has to end honestly. My instinct is the boat: he thought knowledge is like a ship we have to rebuild at sea, no dry dock, no neutral starting point — and by that standard, freezing the charts in a vitrine and calling them masterpieces is a strange thing to do. I'd like the last thing I say to leave the show slightly in question without being a downer, and without me pretending I have a better answer.
- No quotes from him except maybe the one on the poster, no citations, no German unless you gloss it in the same breath, no "as Wittgenstein said". I'll be speaking this aloud, so write it to be spoken: short sentences, a couple of spots where I can pause.

Give me the whole speech. And at the end, in a couple of lines, tell me the one move in it I'll be tempted to soften when I rehearse and why I shouldn't.
