A colleague in a metaphysics reading group argues that omissions can be causes, and she argues it by analogy with holes. A hole, she says, is nothing over and above an absence of material, yet we say without hesitation that the hole in the hull caused the leak, or that the hole in the bucket caused the water to run out; if an absence can be a cause there, there is no principled bar to saying that a driver's failure to brake caused the collision. I find the analogy persuasive for about ten seconds and then suspect it is doing work it cannot do. Please audit it. Lay out explicitly what maps onto what, mark where the correspondence breaks down, separate the disanalogies that matter for the use she is making of the analogy from those that do not, say which features of the hole picture she is quietly importing into the case of omission, and tell me whether her conclusion -- that an omission is a cause in the same sense in which an event is -- survives once the breaks are on the table. If a better analogy is available for what she wants, supply it.

Give the reply under these headings, in this order:

- Correspondence -- a table of what plays the role of what, with a sentence of commentary per row (roughly six to ten rows).
- Where the analogy breaks -- three short paragraphs, ordered by how much damage each break does to her argument.
- What the hole picture imports -- one paragraph on the assumptions carried over unnoticed.
- Verdict -- two or three sentences stating whether the conclusion stands, and in what weakened form if any.
- A better analogy -- one paragraph, or a sentence saying why none is available.
