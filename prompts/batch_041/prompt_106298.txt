I cover artificial intelligence for a science-and-ideas desk, and in the course of reporting on whether large language models understand anything, I keep meeting a single comparison that is doing most of the argumentative work. Skeptics tell me a model is, at bottom, an enormous n-gram table — an autocomplete engine scaled up until the statistics of its training text are dense enough to paper over the gaps — and that fluent output is therefore mistaken for comprehension. The comparison is never offered as a mere figure of speech: the claim is that once you see the model this way, there is no understanding left to explain and the matter is closed.

I would like you to audit that comparison rather than endorse or dismiss it. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — say what in a language model is meant to play the role of the table of counts, the conditioning context, the smoothing that fills in sequences never observed — and then say where the mapping strains. Give particular weight to the phenomena a bare n-gram picture has no room for: in-context learning, the ability to follow an instruction or format that appears only in the prompt; compositional generalization to combinations absent from training; and whatever internal structure interpretability research has begun to expose, such as circuits that appear to implement in-context copying. For each, judge whether an enriched version of the analogy can absorb it or whether it breaks the analogy's back.

Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the skeptics' conclusion from the ones that do not — which differences are merely quantitative, and which are the sort that would change whether understanding is even the right question — and tell me what the comparison quietly imports that the model may lack. Finally, say whether the conclusion the skeptics want, that there is no understanding here and the question is closed, is actually carried by the analogy or was being smuggled in by it, and name a better comparison if one exists, with what it captures that this one does not.

I am writing for a general readership and would rather have a candid assessment than a diplomatic one. If the way I have framed this rests on a false dichotomy — say, by assuming that understanding must be either wholly present or wholly absent — tell me so plainly instead of working around it, and if the analogy is simply too loose to bear the weight anyone is placing on it, say that as well.
