I am planning a ninety-minute session on the two-envelope paradox for our departmental paradox seminar: a mixed room of philosophy and economics graduate students, whiteboard only, comfortable with expected value and conditional probability but with no measure theory. Do not explain the paradox to me. Plan how I should explain it.

Assume the audience arrives knowing the bare setup and the naive argument that the other envelope has expected value 5a/4. I need the order in which the pieces must come for that argument to be diagnosed rather than dismissed as a howler, and I need the audience to be able to say at the end exactly which step fails.

My intended carrying example is a variant I have not seen used. The smaller amount is the result of a fair die, so the pair is one of (1,2), (2,4), (3,6), (4,8), (5,10), (6,12), and you open one envelope. On this variant "always switch" is false and "it cannot matter" is also false: I make it out that you switch on 1, 3 and 5 and also on 2, 4 and 6, and stand pat only on 8, 10 and 12. Check that, since the example is the hinge of the session, and tell me whether it will carry the weight I want or do the opposite. My worry is that it resolves the paradox too cheaply, by making it look like a slip about knowing the prior, and leaves the audience thinking there is nothing left to see.

If my worry is right, the plan needs a second movement. There is a version in which the amounts come from a perfectly proper probability distribution and yet the expected value of the other envelope exceeds the amount you see for every amount you could see. I want to end the session by showing why that version is not a counterexample to the diagnosis, without presenting the general theory. Tell me what that second movement has to contain and where the extra work actually lies.

Then give me: the order of the ideas and the dependency that forces it; the misconceptions this audience will form, in the order they will form them, each with the point in the sequence where I should head it off — I expect at least "there is no uniform distribution over the amounts, so the problem is ill-posed", "symmetry settles it", "this shows expected value is the wrong criterion", and the conflation of the probability before opening that you hold the smaller envelope with the corresponding probability after opening; one closing question that will show whether they can locate the fallacy rather than recite a resolution; and what I should explicitly refuse to do at this level.

Throughout, keep what is settled apart from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material. That the switching argument as stated is invalid, and that any acceptable treatment must say where, I take to be settled; whether the fixed-amount version has a fully satisfying resolution I take to be disputed, and I do not want one party's resolution presented to me as the consensus.
