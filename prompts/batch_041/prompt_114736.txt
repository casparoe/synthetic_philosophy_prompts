I teach a second-year undergraduate module in metaphysics, and next month I have to give the session on whether a person could have come from a different sperm and egg — origin essentialism, and the arguments on either side of it. The topic has recently become awkwardly personal for me: my partner and I had a daughter last year using an egg from a donor, and since then I have been unable to stop turning over the counterfactual our own treatment made vivid, namely whether a different egg from the same cohort would have meant a different child, and what my strong sense that it would have is actually tracking.

What I want from you is not an explanation. I can state the position myself, and I do not need sources or readings. I want a plan for teaching it in seventy-five minutes to twenty-five students who have had one session on personal identity, no exposure at all to modal notions, and a firm prior that this is a question about biology, or about destiny, rather than about necessity.

So, first, the order: what has to be in place before the thesis even sounds like a thesis, and why each step depends on the one before it — which of the components (the gap between a thing's properties and its identity conditions; the idea of varying an individual rather than varying a world; the difference between what is causally settled and what is metaphysically necessary) can be presupposed at this level and which cannot. Second, the carrying example. The two I keep reaching for are an artifact case — a thing made from a different lump of matter, or the same lump differently arranged — and the well-worn case of a person of famous parentage asked whether they could have been born to a different couple. I suspect neither does the work I need. Tell me which, if either, earns its place, what the carrying example must have that they lack, and whether the donor-gamete case, or the neighbouring cases of children conceived with a third party's mitochondria or chosen from a cohort of embryos, should carry the session or would only turn it into bioethics. Third, the misconception map: I predict they will hear the thesis as fatalism, as a claim about which sperm in fact won the race, as a claim about genes rather than about individuals, and as a religious claim about souls. Where in the sequence should each of those be headed off, and what am I failing to anticipate? Fourth, the personal problem: my own family is the most vivid example available to me, and I do not trust my judgment about whether to use it. Is it a mistake, and what does it cost if it is? Fifth, what should I openly decline to explain at this level, and say that I am declining? And finally, one question to put to them at the end that would separate a student who has grasped the modal claim from one who has merely learned the slogan.
