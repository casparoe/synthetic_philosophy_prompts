I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on non-classical logic, and I want a worked example that isn't the Liar paradox or the usual "this sentence is false" routine, since everyone's seen those and just recites the stock moves. So: construct me a thought experiment to order, set somewhere completely ordinary — a courtroom, a kitchen, a lab bench, a family argument, whatever you like — in which there is a genuine case of a thing that both is and isn't F, or a claim that is both true and false, and where that double status isn't just asserted but actually falls out of the facts you've described. A few constraints that all have to hold at once: (a) no self-reference, and no sentence that talks about its own truth; (b) the "both" has to come from a source a reasonable person could take seriously — a real borderline, two equally authoritative but conflicting verdicts, an object with two conflicting but equally well-supported natures — not "assume P and not-P"; (c) a classical logician should be able to accept your description of the situation as accurate and still deny that anything is both true and false, so the disagreement is about the logic and not about question-begging facts — if the only way the classicist can resist is to declare the situation impossible, then say so, and say whether that's a fair move; and (d) keep the scenario itself to a paragraph.

Once you've built it, do the following. Name the crucial claim P and say exactly which truth-value the dialetheist gives it and which the classicist gives it, and why each is forced by their view rather than chosen. Then take the most humdrum inference in the world — from "P or Q" together with "not P," conclude "Q" — and show what happens to it in your case; I want the dialetheist to end up having to call this inference invalid here even though its normally fine, and I want the classicist to end up committed to the conclusion. So build the case so that Q is independently and boringly false, otherwise the classicist gets off too easy. Then walk through both verdicts in full, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the real work (my guess is the glut in P plus the idea that an argument is valid just when no situation makes all the premises true and the conclusion not true — but correct me if I'm wrong), say what happens to the whole example if you tweak it so P comes out consistently true or consistently false, and finish by giving the strongest principled reply a classical logician has against the setup — not a lazy "contradictions can't be true," but the best actual case they can make.
