I am a third-year undergraduate taking a course on formal epistemology and epistemic utility theory, and I also run the calibration desk for our university’s forecasting team. Every week team members submit probabilistic forecasts on a set of resolvable questions, and the tournament scores each forecast with a proper scoring rule—this season, a 50/50 mixture of the Brier score and the logarithmic score, though I want the procedure to say how it would change under a different proper rule. The stakes are low in the grand scheme, but they are high for us: a single incoherent submission can sink our team’s score, and I have to sign off on every submission, with the team captain able to override me only if she writes a paragraph explaining why. I have had one semester of probability but no measure theory, so I need you to turn accuracy-first epistemology into a reusable audit procedure that a student at that level can run in ten minutes on a set of credences before we submit. The procedure should be ordered questions with stopping conditions: what to check first, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure returns a flat no, where it returns a repaired credence, and where it stops and hands the decision back to the forecaster or to me because the issue is a judgment call rather than a matter of accuracy. I want it grounded in the actual machinery—proper scoring rules, accuracy dominance, the result that any incoherent credence function is accuracy-dominated by some coherent one, conditionalization, and the difference between a credence being merely permissible and being required—not in generic advice to consider the base rate. I do not need a reading list or citations; I need the procedure itself, with the hard cases it must handle and the places where it will misfire marked as such.

Then run it on three cases. First, a forecaster submits P(A) = 0.7, P(B) = 0.6, and P(A or B) = 0.5, and cannot see anything wrong. Second, a forecaster is torn between a base rate that suggests 0.55 and a friend who insists the answer is 0.8; she wants the procedure to tell her which to trust. Third, a forecaster refuses to give a point estimate and submits the interval [0.3, 0.7], arguing that precision would be dishonest. For each case, say what the procedure does, where it stops, and what the forecaster should do next. At least one of the three should produce an answer the forecaster will not like, and I want you to say plainly which step is doing the work and which step is a value judgment dressed up as procedure. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of epistemic situations it is silent on, and what would have to be added to it—perhaps a separate procedure for weighting sources, or for aggregating individual forecasts into a team forecast—before it could be a complete guide.
