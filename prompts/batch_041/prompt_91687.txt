I am leading a session for a reading group of non-specialists on the idea of a free-floating rationale—a reason without a reasoner—and I need a plan for how to explain it rather than an explanation. Assume the group has no biology beyond high school and no philosophy of mind. The case I want to carry the whole session is one I have invented to avoid the stock examples: a nocturnal moth that mates once, then flies upwind until it detects a volatile compound released only by a particular night-blooming plant when that plant is being eaten by a specific caterpillar; it then performs a fixed looping descent and lays its eggs on the plant's leaves, after which it dies without ever encountering its offspring. The moth's behaviour is correct or incorrect depending on whether the plant really is where its larvae can feed, yet nothing in the moth represents that fact or that goal. What has to be introduced first for that case to do its work—the difference between a cause and a rationale, the idea of a norm or standard, the notion of a function—and in what order? What must the case have and what must it lack for the idea to land rather than collapse into ordinary causal talk? Which misconceptions will the group predictably form—for instance, that a free-floating rationale is just an unconscious belief, that it is merely a manner of speaking, that it implies a hidden designer or a world-spirit, or that any biological function counts—and at which point in the sequence should I head each one off? Finally, what single question should I put to them at the end to see whether they can apply the idea to a new case rather than recite it, and what should I explicitly refuse to settle at this level?
