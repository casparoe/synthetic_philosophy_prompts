I am a high school junior preparing for an ethics bowl tournament, and our case concerns a patient we have been calling Mr. Ellery. Several years ago, when he was still fully lucid and had just received a diagnosis of early-onset Alzheimer's, Mr. Ellery signed an advance directive refusing antibiotics and any other life-prolonging treatment in the event that he should become unable to make decisions for himself. He wrote a short statement explaining that he did not want to live through the later stages of the disease. Now he is in a nursing home, moderately confused but not miserable; he does not recognize his old signature, and when he developed pneumonia he asked the staff, calmly and repeatedly, to give him the antibiotics so that he could go on living. His daughter says the directive must be honored because it was made by the real him. His son says the man in the bed is also the real him, and that we cannot treat him as a mere vessel for a document. Our coach says this is a classic dispute about the authority of the earlier self, but I think there is a simpler way to settle it. An advance directive is a promise, and promises are binding; the earlier Mr. Ellery was competent and the later one is not, so the earlier one has authority. Is that not the whole answer? If a competent person's considered instructions can be overturned by the requests of someone who is no longer competent, what is left of autonomy, and why would anyone bother to write a directive at all?

I suspect my reasoning must be missing something, because my teammates keep telling me that the word competent is doing too much work, and that the later request is not simply noise. But I do not see how a person who cannot understand his own diagnosis can have standing to override a decision made by someone who could. Could you explain whether the authority of the earlier self is really grounded in promise-keeping, or whether that is the wrong model, and what exactly would make the later Mr. Ellery's request either binding or irrelevant? I am especially confused about whether the answer changes if he had never written the directive but had simply told his family his wishes, and whether the fact that he is now calm and asking to live counts as evidence that the earlier self was wrong about what he would want. I would be grateful for a careful answer that does not just tell me to respect autonomy, since both sides seem to be doing that.
