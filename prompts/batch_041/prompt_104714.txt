I write long-form features on philosophy and the human sciences for a general-interest magazine, and for the better part of a year I've been reporting on a dispute that keeps restarting without ever resolving. On one side are philosophers who hold that a hypocrite — someone who blames another for a wrong he is himself committing — forfeits the standing to blame; on the other are those who hold that hypocrisy is a defect in the blamer but not a bar to blaming, and who suspect the first camp has confused being in no position to cast the stone with having no right to. I've interviewed partisans of both views at length, and the more I listen the more I suspect that a good part of the impasse is terminological, because standing is being asked to do at least three jobs at once: the epistemic warrant to judge (whether the blamer is placed to know what he's talking about), the normative authority to make a demand of someone and expect an answer, and the warrant to attach a sanction or penalty to the failure. Nobody I've spoken to denies the three come apart in principle; they simply keep sliding between them in argument. I want to commission something better than another round of point-scoring: the joint statement these two camps could actually sign if they sat down together in good faith, and I'd like you to draft it.

The document should do four things. First, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, about the structure of blame, about what standing is for, and about which of my three readings, if any, each side is really relying on. Second, the residual disagreements, each stated in wording that its own side would accept as fair — not a paraphrase that quietly concedes the point. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, and for each crux a concrete case both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance. I want one of those cases to be a well-known public figure who hectors others about a vice he plainly shares, and another to be someone who blames a practice — say, an industry's emissions — while drawing an income from it, since I think these are being run together and shouldn't be. That second case is why I'd like you to test a distinction of my own against both camps: between hypocritical blame, where the blamer merely does the same wrong, and what I've been calling complicit blame, where the blamer is implicated in producing or sustaining the very wrong he condemns. My hunch is that complicity does something hypocrisy doesn't — that it corrodes the authority to demand an answer while leaving the warrant to feel indignation intact — but I'd rather the statement report honestly if the distinction collapses under pressure than pretend it survives.

Finally, address the question I can't stop asking my sources: how much of this long quarrel is genuine and how much is two camps using one word for different things? If part of it is verbal, say which part and why; if none of it is, say what the real crux is and why the dispute can't be dissolved by a definition. And for each crux, note what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own position, not just against the other's — I'm told philosophers find that question unusually hard, and the honesty of the answer is the main thing that will decide whether the piece runs as written or gets killed by my editor, who thinks the whole subject is academics splitting hairs. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and keep it to a length I could actually print.
