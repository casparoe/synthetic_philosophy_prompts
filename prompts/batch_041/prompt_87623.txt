I'm putting together a session for a reading group on early modern idealism and I want to try something a bit odd. Imagine Berkeley got a week in 2025 — VR headsets, brain-computer interfaces, and the whole simulation-hypothesis cottage industry that's grown up around it. I want you to work out what he'd actually say, not the polite "he'd be amazed" version.

The interesting bit for me is how his master argument survives the trip. Roughly: you can't conceive of an unconceived thing, because the moment you conceive it, it's conceived — so there's no coherent thought of a world with nobody around to perceive it. Fine. Now hand him a simulation. Someone writes a world, runs it on a server, and then walks away or dies, and the code just keeps rendering trees in an empty valley. Nobody perceives anything. Is that the counterexample the master argument has been waiting 300 years for, or does he just say the simulation was never the kind of thing that could exist unperceived in the first place? And push him on it — the programmer did conceive the valley, so has he smuggled in a perceiver by the back door?

Second thing I want from him: the simulation hypothesis is usually sold as a rival to ordinary realism, like we might be in a fake world. My suspicion is Berkeley would find that framing almost boring, since for him the interesting question was never real vs fake, it was which mind is doing the perceiving. Does he think the sim argument is a genuine challenge, a restatement of his own position with worse vocabulary, or a confusion? If he ends up saying the sim's author is basically playing the role God plays in his system, is that a win for him or does it collapse the whole thing into "any sufficiently powerful mind will do"?

Third, and this is the one I keep going back and forth on: what does he do with an AI that tells you it's in pain or sees red? He doesn't have our notion of computation, so don't let him cheat by importing one — make him reason from what he'd actually have. Does an unperceived-but-active mind fit anywhere in his ontology, or is that just incoherent for him? And would he say we're wrong to ask whether it's conscious, the way he'd say we're wrong to ask whether the tree in the quad is still there when we shut the door?

Don't hedge everything into "he'd probably." Give me his actual positions, where you think he'd dig in and where he'd have to give ground, and flag any spot where the honest answer is that his framework just doesn't have the resources to say. No citations needed, obviously he can't check anything, I care about the reasoning.
