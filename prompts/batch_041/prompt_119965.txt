I'm a graduate student working on the ethics of autonomous systems, and I keep bumping into a distinction that I can state in the abstract but cannot make vivid, so I would like you to build me the examples that would. The background is familiar: an autonomous system does something with a bad outcome, and no human being satisfies the conditions we ordinarily require before holding someone responsible — no one knew what it would do, no one could have stopped it at the moment it acted, no one intended the outcome. The standard remedy is what I have been calling the tracing move: if no one was in control at the moment of the act, look backwards along the causal chain for an earlier act that was under someone's control and for which they were culpable — the choice to deploy the system, the decision to specify the objective as it was specified, the regulatory decision to permit it — and attach responsibility there instead. I want to know whether this move actually fills the gap or merely relocates it, and I want to see the difference in cases rather than in formulas.

So: give me one example of an autonomous-system case in which the tracing move plainly succeeds, where the ascription of responsibility to the person further back is not a stretch and the gap really does close. Then give me a second, equally concrete example in which the move appears to work but does not — the traced-back act is real, the person really did something, the causal chain really does run through them, and yet the resulting claim that they are responsible for the outcome is simply not true, so that what looked like a solution turns out to be a category mistake. Keep both examples specific enough that the causal chain can be inspected step by step: a determinate sort of system, a determinate harm, a determinate person whose earlier choice is the candidate. For the second case especially, tell me exactly which condition on responsibility fails and why no amount of further tracing repairs it.

My own hunch, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that the line falls at whether the traced-back act was the act that made the difference — that the deployer's choice has to stand to the harm roughly as the drunk driver's decision to drive stands to the crash, and that most of the tempting cases fail because the earlier choice only raised the probability of a harm that the system itself selected. If that criterion is wrong, or if it makes the second example impossible to construct without begging the question, say so plainly instead of producing something to fill the slot; I would rather be told that my distinction is confused than receive a tidy answer that quietly assumes it.
