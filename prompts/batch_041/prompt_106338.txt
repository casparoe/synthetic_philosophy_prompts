I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, so I may be missing something a philosopher would catch. A colleague in a reading group gave me the following argument, which he calls the sandbox discount, and I cannot tell whether it is a reductio of fanaticism about the far future or an overreach. The argument is:

(1) Fanaticism about the far future holds that because the far future could contain vastly more value than the present, we should be willing to accept small present costs for very small probabilities of influencing it.
(2) If we are in a simulation, then our actions affect at most the simulated world; anything beyond that depends on the simulators' choices.
(3) The simulation argument gives a non-negligible probability that we are in a simulation.
(4) Call the sandbox discount the multiplier by which the expected value of an action is reduced when its consequences are confined to a simulation whose continuation and whose valuation of those consequences are uncertain.
(5) Conditional on being in a simulation, the sandbox discount is not close to 1; simulations are often short-lived, purpose-specific, and indifferent to our long-term projects.
(6) So the expected value of far-future interventions is not astronomically large; it is at most the value of the simulated far future multiplied by a modest sandbox discount.
(7) Therefore fanaticism about the far future is dampened: we should not make large present sacrifices for tiny probabilities of a far-future utopia.
(8) The same discount applies to preventing an existential catastrophe, spreading worthwhile life, or any other long-term project.
(9) If the discount is strong enough to defeat fanaticism, it is strong enough to make long-term concern optional.
(10) That cannot be right; the possibility that we are simulated does not license indifference to the far future.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (1) and (3) if I can, but I want to know what I am paying.
