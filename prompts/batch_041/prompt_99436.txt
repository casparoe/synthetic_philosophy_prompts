I spent the better part of 35 years as a plant breeder — barley, mostly — and since retiring I've been working my way through philosophy of biology books, which is how I ended up caring about what "heritability" is supposed to mean. Here's what I want, and I'd rather have the spec than another essay.

Back in the 90s we had the same population of barley in two trials about 40 km apart, and the heritability of heading date came out around 0.85 in one and 0.2 in the other. Same seed, same genotypes, roughly the same measuring. Nobody at the breeding station thought this was a paradox. But when I read the human genetics literature everyone talk about "the heritability of IQ" or "the heritability of height" as if it were one number belonging to the trait, and then people on both sides of the nature/nurture fight use it to argue about schools, or about whether we can stop worrying about poverty, and I get the feeling the number is being asked to do jobs it was never built for.

So: before I read any more of this stuff, give me the desiderata. What does any adequate account of heritability have to deliver — the cases it has to get right, the puzzles it has to dissolve, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results from genetics and breeding it can't contradict. For each one, give me the case or consideration behind it, say whether it's non-negotiable or just a preferance of one camp, and flag any pair you think can't be satisfied at the same time — that last part is what I actually want most. Then, only briefly, how the main accounts on offer do against the list.

A few things I'd want on the list, so you know what I mean by in the weeds: my two trials above; what happens to h² when the environment is uniform for everybody (as I understand it the number goes to 1 no matter how environmental the trait is, which seems like it should embarrass somebody); the difference between the narrow and the broad sense and why a breeder cares about the first one; the fact that the SNP-based estimates keep coming out lower than the twin/pedigree ones for the same trait; and the trick where a number computed from differences within a population gets slid over to explain differences between populations.

Don't give me citations or a reading list — I can't look things up easily and I don't want to be told to read So-and-so. If a term of art is doing work, define it.

Format: I want it as a table, not prose. Columns: id | requirement (one sentence) | the case or consideration behind it | status (non-negotiable / camp preference — say which camp) | which of the main accounts it knocks out or strains. Then a second, shorter table of conflicts, columns: requirement ids that clash | why they pull apart | which one I should give up and why. Last, one line: the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates. If you want a one-paragraph gloss after the tables, fine, but the tables are the answer.
