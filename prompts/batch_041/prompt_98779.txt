I participate in an ethics reading group, and last week a friend who has actually read some of the contemporary literature gave us a summary of the particularism–generalism debate. He is usually reliable, and I do not know the literature well enough to tell where he is simplifying, but something about the summary struck me as too neat. Here is what he said: ‘Particularism is the view that there are no moral principles. Generalism says that right action is always determined by a principle, and particularism denies this: it holds that moral reasons are entirely context-dependent, so a feature like that an action would keep a promise can count in favor of it in one situation and against it in another, with no general truth about what promise-keeping favors. Since every moral principle has exceptions, generalists are forced to retreat to rules of thumb, and particularism simply takes this to its logical conclusion. The particularist therefore says that moral judgment is a kind of perceptual skill, like tasting wine or diagnosing a patient, that cannot be captured in words; moral education is a matter of training someone’s eye rather than teaching them rules. The standard objection is that without principles we could never justify a moral judgment or resolve a disagreement, and since particularists cannot answer this, particularism collapses into intuitionism—just saying “this action is right” and refusing to give reasons.’ I am inclined to think particularism is a bit silly, but I suspect that is partly because the summary makes it sound sillier than it is.

Please audit the summary sentence by sentence. Which parts would a careful particularist accept, which would they wince at as an oversimplification or a conflation, and which are just wrong? In particular, I would like you to separate: (a) the claim that principles are not necessary for moral thought or action from the claim that there are no true moral generalizations; (b) the holism of reasons—roughly, that a feature can have different contributory valence in different contexts—from the stronger claim that no feature has any general moral significance; and (c) the familiar objection that particularism cannot guide or justify from the conclusion that particularism is refuted by that objection. Does the summary’s final collapse-into-intuitionism claim follow from anything true in it? If the summary is unfair, what is the most defensible version of particularism that remains, and what would a generalist have to concede or deny to make the debate genuine rather than a misunderstanding? I would also like to know how much of this is settled among people who work on the topic versus genuinely contested—for example, whether the disagreement is metaphysical, epistemological, or primarily about the role of principles in moral education and deliberation—and please do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
