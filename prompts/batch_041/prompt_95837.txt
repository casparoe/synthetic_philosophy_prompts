I sit on the appeals board for my state's workers' comp system, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's vote on a case thats split us 2-1. The claimant was a school custodian who took a lungful of a cleaning solvent during a spill. The acute symptoms cleared up, but since then she reports headaches, dizziness, word-finding trouble and a growing intolerance of ordinary chemical exposures — perfume, fresh paint, the detergent aisle — and she hasn't worked in two years. Nobody on the board disputes that she's suffering or that she can't do her old job. What's disputed is whether she has a disease. Our statute says an occupational disease requires "a demonstrable pathological change in bodily tissue or function." Her expert, who is genuinely board-certified, says the mechanism is central sensitization — a durable change in how her nervous system handles chemical and pain signals — and concedes there's no lesion you could see on a scan. The employer's expert says the whole diagnostic category (her treating doctor wrote "multiple chemical sensitivity") is junk science, that her symptoms are better explained by conditioned avoidance and hypervigilance, and that a mechanism inferred only from the symptoms it's supposed to explain can't satisfy the statute.

My problem is that a few years ago we held in a different case that a claimant doesn't need a named diagnosis, only a demonstrated impairment, and I don't see how to square that with a standard that reads like it wants a visible lesion. One colleague says the statute is quietly incoherent: "function" only means anything against some norm about what bodies are supposed to do, so the board is really making a value judgment about whose life counts as broken, not a medical one, and should just say so out loud. The employer's lawyer, meanwhile, will argue that granting this opens the door to every stress and sensitivity claim in the state.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete disposition — affirm the denial, reverse and award, or remand with a test we can actually apply in the next case — and it has to be honest about the strongest consideration on the side you rule against, since half the board is there. 900 to 1200 words is fine.
