So I got into a two-hour argument at a party about the murderer at the door, and my friend (who is doing a PhD on Kant and isnt just making stuff up) wrote this out on the back of a receipt to show me the whole position collapses, and I've been chewing on it all week. Roughly what he wrote:

(1) Kant's answer is that you don't lie to the murderer. And the reason isn't anything about the murderer — it's that a lie wrongs humanity in general even when it wrongs no one in particular, it corrupts the very thing that makes truthfulness a duty in the first place. Perfect duty, no pile of consequences outweighs it. (2) But notice the murderer is the one who pays for the lie: he runs off in the wrong direction and misses his victim. So if the wrong of lying were the harm it does to the person you lied to, there'd be nothing wrong with lying here at all. (3) So the wrong has to live in the liar's will instead: I take his capacity to reason and use it as a tool, feeding him a belief so as to steer what he does with it — formula-of-humanity failure with extra steps. (4) Except feeding someone a belief in order to steer what they do is not something lying has a monopoly on. Hiding in the pantry with the lights off. Locking the door so he forms the belief that he can't get in and goes away. Telling him my friend moved to Bristol last year, which happens to be true, in a tone that implies he's long gone. Answering "why do you want to know?" and letting him infer whatever he infers. Every one of those works by shaping what the murderer believes and therefore what he does, and none of them is a lie. (5) So if (3) is the real reason lying is wrong, all of those are wrong for the same reason, and Kant has to tell me I may not even bolt my door against a burglar, since I'd be managing his beliefs about whether the door opens in order to get him to leave. And if instead you narrow it — the special thing about a lie is that it hands him a false premise, so what he does next isn't fully his own doing — then the wrong-making feature is just falsity as such, and every false statement is wrong even when it steers nobody: the lie the murderer doesn't believe, fiction, jokes, the fake-out in a card trick. (6) Either way the answer proves either nothing about lying in particular (a lie is just one more way of moving people around, like a locked door) or far too much (no concealment, no evasion, no privacy, no fiction, no self-defense). The murderer case was supposed to show that lying is specially wrong, not to take out half of ordinary life.

So: which step do I give up? It's got the shape I've learned to be suspicious of from years of reading blog arguments — a conclusion way bigger than the premise that's doing the work — but I genuinely can't find the seam, and I have a real pull to keep (3), since without it the murderer case is just rule-worship by someone refusing to look at consequences. If (3) is the problem, tell me what the wrong-maker is supposed to be instead, and price it for me: what do I lose if the wrong of lying is really a duty to myself, or is about keeping the practice of assertion trustworthy for everybody, or something else entirely? And if the chain is actually valid, just say so and tell me which premise is cheapest to drop, because I'd rather know the bill than keep pretending I've got a clever objection.
