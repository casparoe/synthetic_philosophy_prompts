I've been going back and forth on the Epicurean argument for years and I want to just get a number out of someone. The proposition I actually care about is this: there are harms I'll call orphaned harms -- harms to a person that obtain at no time during which that person exists -- and dying is one of them, so a person who dies can have been harmed by her death even though there is no moment, before or after, at which she is worse off. (I'm at maybe 0.35 on this myself but I dont trust it.) Give me your credence and decompose it.

Reply as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up:

1. Your headline number, plus one clause saying what you're taking the proposition to amount to.

2. The probability that the principle -- for something to be bad for you it must be bad for you at some time when you exist -- is a genuine constraint on well-being rather than a verbal artifact of how we report harms, with a clause on why.

3. The probability that the deprivation story, on which dying is bad because of the life you would have had, survives the fact that it compares you to a continuer who isn't you at any moment you could point to, with a clause on why.

4. The probability that the asymmetry is defensible -- death bad, the eternity of nonbeing before your birth not bad -- and that it supports the deprivation story rather than being an embarrassment the theory has to absorb, with a clause on why.

5. The combined number, which of items 2 through 4 it's most sensitive to, and the one consideration that would move it the most.
