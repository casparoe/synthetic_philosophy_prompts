You are a philosopher of science who has taught Kuhn for years and who designs classroom simulations in which participants run into a problem with their hands before anyone names it. Think carefully before you commit to a design. Open with a compressed brainstorm: sketch three or four candidate activities for the brief below, say for each what it would make vivid and what it would leave inert, then choose the strongest and specify it in full. Do not build the activity around a historical episode (phlogiston, caloric, Newtonian versus relativistic mass) — the conceptual apparatus must be invented for this session, so that nothing turns on prior reading.

The setting: an upper-level seminar, eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, on incommensurability. Assume they have not read Kuhn and should not hear the word in the first hour. What I want them to come away with is the force of the claim that two communities can each reason impeccably inside their own conceptual scheme and still have no neutral vocabulary in which to state what they disagree about — and then to feel how the obvious escape routes fail or cost something: a shared observation language, a translation manual, a principle of charity, the reflex of saying that the other side must really mean one of our categories.

Design requirements. You must invent two lexicons for the participants to work in, precise enough to be applied mechanically to a set of items, and hand each to one group only. Build in at least three structural features: one term in one lexicon whose extension cuts across two terms in the other; one term defined by a rule the other group cannot guess from the items alone (something with the flavour of a gerrymandered or projectible predicate); and a class of test items on which the two lexicons return conflicting verdicts for principled reasons rather than noise. Include a phase in which the groups are brought together on a joint task, and a phase in which one designated participant is permitted to become bilingual and act as translator — say exactly what that person discovers they cannot do, and what the others can nevertheless learn by immersion that they could not learn by being given rules.

Then give me: roles, materials, a timeline to the minute, a scoring or stakes scheme that needs no money, the precise moment the two lexicons collide, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the philosophical material — normal puzzle-solving inside a lexicon, anomaly, the shift from talk of meaning change to talk of the lexicon and local versus global incommensurability, the distinction between incomparability and untranslatability, learnability without translation, semantic versus methodological versus evaluative incommensurability, the Davidsonian objection that a scheme wholly alien to ours is incoherent, and the relativism charge that follows the whole business. Be explicit about which of these the activity genuinely produces and which it only gestures at.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be frank about the feature of Kuhn's view the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, that I am secretly holding a neutral description of the materials that neither group possesses, whereas Kuhn's claim is that for real paradigms no such description exists. Say what I should tell the student who spots this, and whether the objection is fatal to the activity, to Kuhn, or to neither.
