I am building a theory of epistemic value, and the central thing it must explain is the Meno intuition that knowledge is better than mere true belief. Before I commit to any particular account — reliabilist, virtue-theoretic, or otherwise — I want the full specification of what such an account would have to deliver. Write the desiderata for a theory of the value of knowledge over true belief.

By "desiderata" I mean the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the results it must be consistent with. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Make the list demanding: include the puzzles the standard accounts are built to solve, but also the constraints the standard accounts tend to ignore.

I specifically want the list to engage with the following, since this is where the pressure is:

- The road to Larissa: two travelers, one who knows the way and one who merely holds a true belief about it, arrive equally well; the theory has to say what, if anything, is worth more about the one who knows, and in what currency that extra worth is denominated.
- The swamping problem: if truth is the value that makes a reliable belief good, and reliability is good only as a means to truth, then adding reliability to an already-true belief seems to add no value. A theory that cannot meet this owes an account of where the extra value comes from.
- The trivial-truths worry: many true beliefs, whether known or merely believed, are worthless (the number of grains in a heap, the spelling of a word I will never use). If knowledge is supposed to be more valuable than true belief, the theory must either restrict the claim or explain away the apparent counterexample.
- Robustness: a case in which a knower and a mere true believer hold the same belief and act identically now, differing only in what they would have believed had things been slightly otherwise. Must the theory assign that difference value, and if so, what kind?
- A case I have added because I think it strains the usual answers: a person forms a true belief by deferring entirely to an institution or instrument whose reliability she has no way to check — a navigation system, or a scientific authority she cannot evaluate for herself. If she knows, the theory must say why deference transmits value; if she does not, it must say what knowledge requires beyond a true belief from a de facto reliable source.

Then, as part of the deliverable: flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied and say why; separate the requirements that are genuinely non-negotiable from those that are merely preferences of one camp; and name the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise popular theories.

Format the answer in American English, in exactly these sections and in this order:

1. "The job to be done" — one short paragraph, under 150 words, stating what problem the theory is solving and for what purpose adequacy should be demanded.
2. "The desiderata" — the bulk of the answer, numbered, each with a one-line statement of the requirement followed by the case or consideration that motivates it.
3. "Fault lines" — the pairs of desiderata in tension and why, as a short list.
4. "Non-negotiable versus camp-specific" — two short lists.
5. "The most exclusionary requirement" — one paragraph naming it and saying what it rules out.
