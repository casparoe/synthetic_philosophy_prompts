I'm a philosopher of maths — logic and probability, mostly — and I've fallen down a rabbit hole on heuristic probabilistic reasoning in arithmetic: the move where, since you obviously can't just compute whether some arithmetic statement holds, you pretend the relevant quantities are random and independent, and then read a probability off the model. I keep having fairly firm gut verdicts about when this is kosher and when it's a swindle, and I'd like you to tell me what principle (if any) actually captures them — and, less comfortably, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Please answer in British English, and don't be gentle with the ones that don't survive.

The verdicts, roughly in order of how confident I am:

- Treating a random integer near N as prime with probability about 1/ln N: I'm completely happy with this. It's my paradigm of a good heuristic, and the fact that it was later vindicated by a proof is part of why I trust it.
- Treating "n is prime" and "n + 2 is prime" as independent, so the expected number of twin primes up to N is about N/(ln N)², which diverges: I believe the conclusion (infinitely many twin primes), but I know the naive independence is strictly false — you need a correction factor coming from the small primes — and yet the qualitative verdict survives untouched. So "the model is exactly right" clearly isn't the bar I'm actually using.
- The same style of argument for Goldbach: the expected number of ways to write a large even N as a sum of two primes is about N/(ln N)², which grows without bound, so every large even number should have plenty of representations. I find this convincing even though it's open.
- Now the one that rattles me. Treat the Möbius function as though its values were independent random signs and you get a random walk, so |M(x)| should be on the order of √x, and in fact you'd expect |M(x)| < √x for every x — the Mertens conjecture. That's known to be false. Structurally it looks like the same move as the twin-prime one, and yet the outcome is the opposite.
- A milder version of the same worry: for primes in short intervals the naive Poisson/random model seems to get the fine structure wrong, over-predicting a regularity that people have shown is illusory, even though the model is perfectly good at coarser scales.

My tentative principle is this: such a heuristic is trustworthy to the extent that the arithmetic objects in question are genuinely "generic" — there's no hidden algebraic or arithmetic structure (a character sum, an unexpected correlation, a quiet conspiracy among the small primes) that the independence model is simply blind to. When such structure exists, you can sometimes repair the model with local correction factors; sometimes you can't, and the heuristic fails outright. Is that a real principle, or have I just restated "it works when it works"? Test it against the cases, say what it predicts for ones I haven't given (whether the digits of π are normal, whether there are infinitely many Fermat primes, whether the zeta zeros really behave like random-matrix eigenvalues), and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. If there's a cleaner principle that saves more of them, I'd much rather have that one.
