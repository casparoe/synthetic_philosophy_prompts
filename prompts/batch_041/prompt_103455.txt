"A Ulysses contract is any arrangement where you deliberately restrict your future options, named after Odysseus tying himself to the mast. Constitutional precommitment is the same trick applied to a whole society. The point is that your earlier self knows better than your later self, so the later self's objections don't count; if the later self would reject the arrangement, that just shows the arrangement worked. The purest form is an unamendable constitution, since any amendment procedure is already a compromise with weakness of will. And because the later self never consented to being bound, constitutional precommitment is basically undemocratic—really it's just 'precommitment laundering,' dressing up paternalism as self-government."

How much of this is fair? Go claim by claim and tell me what someone working on practical rationality or constitutional theory would accept, what is a common simplification, and what is just wrong. Finally, does his conclusion that an unamendable constitution is the purest form of precommitment follow from anything true in the summary, and does the "precommitment laundering" charge land? Don't give me citations or reading lists—I want the conceptual audit. Begin with your verdict in one sentence, then the audit, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
