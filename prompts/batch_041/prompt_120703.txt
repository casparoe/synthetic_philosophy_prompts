I have no academic training in philosophy. What I have is twenty years of genealogical research, done mostly for other people, and a growing conviction that the puzzle about chains of reasons that philosophers worry over is a puzzle about sourcing, and that I already handle it every day in a vocabulary I did not get from any book. I would rather you take the debate seriously enough to translate it than flatter me by declaring my habits to be philosophy under another name.

Here is the debate as I have pieced it together, without having read the literature, so correct me if I have it wrong. Foundationalism: the chain of reasons must terminate, and what terminates it are beliefs that need no support from further beliefs. Coherentism: it need not terminate at anything special, because a belief is justified by fitting together with the rest of what one believes, so the support runs in a large loop rather than down to a floor. Infinitism: it neither terminates nor loops, since every belief is backed by a further reason and then another, forever, and the impossibility of finishing is not a defect.

In my vocabulary the counterparts are these. A claim in a family tree is only as good as the record behind it, and records rest on further records: a transcription of a transcription of a parish register, a death certificate resting on what an informant believed, a census resting on whoever happened to be home that day. Three habits divide people who do this work. The first is to demand a primary record at the bottom of every chain, a document that speaks for itself. The second is to concede that most chains never reach such a record, and to treat a claim as established when enough independent records agree. The third is to keep asking where the last source got its information, knowing the question never ends, and to regard an unbroken but unfinished chain as respectable rather than defective.

Translate the three positions into that vocabulary, one at a time, as faithfully as you can. For each, say what comes across cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Two matters I want you to press on especially, because they are where I suspect the translation fails. First, in genealogy two sources that both descend from a single earlier source are one source; agreement among copies is worth nothing, and this is the first thing you learn. If coherentism is right, why is that not fatal to it? Second, when a chain of records runs back and back without ending, I still want to know whether the earliest item in it is true, and the endlessness tells me nothing about that — which makes me suspect that the relation I care about, the transmission of information from one document to the next, is not the relation the philosophers are arguing about when they argue about justification.

Then give me the residue report: what of the debate did not survive the move into my vocabulary, and is that because sourcing is missing a distinction it ought to have, or because the philosophers' regress is less substantial than it looks? Include in that report whether the three-way division itself survives, since I have never understood why a chain that does not end is supposed to be a problem in the first place. If my three habits do not line up with the three positions the way I think they do, say where I have gone wrong.
