I'm a PhD student in philosophy working at the seam between criminal-law theory and philosophical theology, and I need a satirical piece built on a position I keep circling: the argument associated with David Lewis that we do, in fact, already believe in penal substitution — that ordinary moral practice treats it as intelligible, and sometimes as admirable, for a blameless person who volunteers to bear the penalty owed by another, so that the doctrine of the atonement is continuous with an everyday conviction rather than a theological aberration. That is my own reconstruction of the position, not a quotation or a citation of anything; if it misstates the view, flag that in the closing note described below and work from the reconstruction regardless.

Write the in-universe text of a Voluntary Penal Substitution Program administered by a state department of corrections: a policy document with numbered clauses and a brief Q&A appendix addressed to members of the public, roughly 650 words. The register should be that of a genuine government form — neutral, procedural, faintly euphemistic, innocent of irony — and the comedy should come entirely from the substance. A specialist in the philosophy of punishment or of the atonement should recognize that the author understands the position; a general reader should find it funny without knowing why.

The clauses need to do real philosophical work, and should be deadpan throughout:

- Consent: must the substitute volunteer; whether consent can be given in advance or withdrawn mid-term; whether the voluntariness of someone who loves the guilty party is genuine voluntariness; what the department does with a substitute who recants.
- Desert: punishment is ordinarily thought to be deserved by the person punished, so what the program does about a substitute who deserves nothing — whether this is handled as an exemption, a waiver, or simply not mentioned.
- The standing of the guilty party: whether repentance is a condition, whether it matters to the validity of the substitution, and whether accepting a substitute undermines the offender's own accountability.
- What exactly is transferred: a penalty, a debt, a stigma, a quantity of suffering, a status. The document should have to commit to one of these somewhere in its fine print, and should do so with bureaucratic blandness.
- Deterrence and communication: what an available substitution says to victims and to future offenders, and whether the sentence still expresses anything once it can be served by someone else.
- The administrative afterlife: liability, insurance, whether the substitute's own dependents have a claim, what happens if the substitute dies before the term is complete.
- The theological boundary: a clause or Q&A entry that quietly presupposes a divine party to the transaction, without ever saying so directly.

The target of the irony is the asymmetry between how readily substitution is embraced as salvific in the theological case and how monstrous it looks in the penal one, or the reverse — the piece should leave a reader genuinely unsure which side is being mocked, without ever announcing that this is the point. The document must not smuggle in positions the doctrine would disown; it should be confident precisely where the underlying philosophy is not, since that gap is where the humour lives.

Constraints: no citations, no reading lists, no dates, no names of scholars, and no quotation of any real text. Any quoted material must be attributed to invented officials of the invented program. Keep the jurisdiction generic — do not invent facts about actual legal systems. Do not resolve the philosophical tension the piece raises.

After the piece, add roughly 150 words in your own voice, outside the parody: how confident you are in the philosophical claims the document commits you to; what you take the actual Lewisian claim to be and how sure you are of that reading; and the single point in all of this you are least sure of.
