I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind, and I am drafting the final exam: one 90-minute in-class essay, closed book, no internet access, students working from argument alone. I want the question to be about illusionism regarding phenomenal consciousness — the view that there are no phenomenal properties and that our impression of enjoying them is a kind of introspective error. My recurring problem is that students can fill a page by announcing that illusionists "deny qualia" and that critics find this "counterintuitive," then they have nothing left to say; the labels do all the work and the thinking stops. A colleague of mine, who is convinced the whole dispute is a verbal one dressed up in technical vocabulary, has been needling me about this for a semester, and I have come to think she is half right — which is exactly why I want the exam to land on that possibility rather than dodge it. So I have written a compressed argument for you to build the question around; sharpen the wording if you like, but keep its structure: (P1) If there are no phenomenal properties, then no state ever seems to its subject to have one. (P2) Illusionism holds that states do seem to their subjects to have phenomenal properties, though none of them do. (C) Therefore illusionism is inconsistent.

The question should require students to say which premise an illusionist ought to reject, to reconstruct as strongly as they can the reason for rejecting it, and then to confront the follow-up that does the real work: whichever premise they reject, they owe an account of what introspection gets wrong and of why that account does not either quietly reintroduce the very seeming the illusionist denies or collapse into a merely verbal dispute with a realist who is willing to apply the word "phenomenality" to the same something. A strong answer should commit to a specific position on that fork and defend it; surveying both sides and declining to choose should not earn full marks. Since students have no books or internet during the exam, the question must not require anyone to name theorists, cite texts, or date debates — it has to be answerable from the armchair, and any technical vocabulary must either be common knowledge at this level or be defined inside the question itself.

Please give me three things. First, the exam question, phrased so that it reads as a question posed to students rather than as a set of instructions to a writer. Second, a model answer of roughly 700–800 words, written at the level of a strong undergraduate in the seminar. Third, a marking scheme totaling 20 points that names the specific moves a top answer must make, with particular attention to the moves that separate an A from a B — including those that distinguish a student who has genuinely worked through the fork from one who has memorized the standard objections and stopped there. For each must-hit point, state the characteristic error that costs marks, and be explicit about how much latitude you are allowing on the final verdict, since I do not want to penalize a student for reaching an unfashionable conclusion if the reasoning holds up. Write everything in American English.
