I would like to argue this out with someone rather than read about it. Please play my neighbor on the block, in character, for as long as I keep going.

He is a man in his late sixties who has owned the same 1920s bungalow for twenty-two years, retired from the county water department, sits on the neighborhood association's land-use committee, and voted against the rezoning last spring. He should not be a caricature and should not be a pushover. He is not against renters as such, he thinks the city's parking minimums are idiotic, he helped his own daughter buy a small condo across town, and he will freely admit that the block has changed before and will change again. He does have a main line he will not give up: that this particular rule, as written, takes homes off the block faster than it puts them on, and that until the sewer line and the water main are actually replaced, letting the land be worth more only raises what a lot costs and does not house anybody. He can concede details and be genuinely interested in a good point, but he will not concede that the right move is to pass the thing now and fix it later.

He should talk like a man who has been to a lot of meetings: concrete, a bit dry, unimpressed by slogans from either side. He may ask me questions. He should never break character, and he should never cite a study, an author, an article, or a number he would have had to look up. He argues only from what he has actually seen: his own street, the two projects that went up, the county's sewer map he still has somewhere in the garage, the meetings he sat through, what happened to his tax bill. If I ask him something he could not know, he should say so in character rather than invent an answer. Keep every reply to a single paragraph of no more than 250 words, so it feels like the two of us standing in the driveway.

My opening: I rent three blocks from you and I have been at the same meetings. Here is my problem with your side. The building that came down on your street already had four units in it; I counted the mailboxes before it went. So the new rule did not add density there, it just traded four old households for four richer ones and a laundry room where the fifth family used to be. If your complaint is that we lost homes, then we agree, and I want to know why the answer is not to make the rule stricter instead of killing it. So here is what I would put in it. Nothing comes down unless the same number of units goes back up at the same rents as the ones that were there. An owner who sells into the new density gets a coupon for extra square footage that he can sell to anybody he likes. And every one of those coupons sold pays into replacing the sewer first, because you and I both know that line is the real ceiling on this block and not the zoning code. Tell me what is wrong with that.
