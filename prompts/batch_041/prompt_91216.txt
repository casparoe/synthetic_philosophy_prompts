I run pre-departure briefings for a mid-sized engineering consultancy that posts staff, and often their spouses and children, to overseas offices for assignments of two to three years. Roughly twenty families pass through my ninety-minute session each year, I am the only person who runs it, and I have no philosophical training and no time to supply any. What they want, and what I cannot currently give them, is an answer to the question that surfaces in the first week of every posting: which of the local ways of doing things do we actually have to adopt? Tipping, queueing, jaywalking, shoes at the door, how you address a senior colleague, whether you take a gift to a dinner, whether you hold your ground in something that is not quite a line -- these are what people write to me about. My instinct is that some of it does not matter at all, that some of it you simply must do, and that I have no principled way of sorting the two. I keep running into the awkward fact that a rule can be purely conventional and still be the local form that respect takes, so that ignoring it is not merely a faux pas; and the reverse fact, that a rule can carry real moral weight for locals, because of dependencies and relationships they have and my people do not, without being anything my people owe anyone. I also have a nagging worry about standing: my people will never be in a position to know whether a local practice is uncontested or is something a good part of the country quietly resents, and they are certainly in no position to reform it. And I have never worked out what to say about the family members, who have signed no contract with anyone.

Please write me a decision procedure I can hand out and walk through in the briefing. I want the questions to ask, in order, about a candidate convention; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a clear comply or a clear do-not-comply; where it hands the matter to a person -- the employee, the country manager, our legal team -- because the question is not one a procedure can settle; and where the sensible move is to route around the situation rather than take a side. It has to be usable by someone with no philosophy and about ten minutes of attention, it must be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it must survive the case where a local convention collides with the firm's own rules or with the law at home -- I am thinking especially of the gift and facilitation-payment customs, where I suspect the procedure is going to tell us to lose business. Open with a two-minute plain-language account of the one or two distinctions the procedure actually rests on, since I have to be able to state them myself; then the procedure; then run it on three cases: tipping in a country where cash left on the table is read as an awkward gesture rather than a kindness, a jaywalking statute that everyone including the police ignores, and the gift-to-an-official case above. For each case, give the verdict the procedure reaches and the step it turns on, and note where a reasonable employee might reach a different verdict without having misapplied it. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and which single step is most likely to be gamed by someone who has already decided what they want the answer to be.
