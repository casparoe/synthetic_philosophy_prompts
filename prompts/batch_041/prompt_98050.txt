You are a philosopher who works on Hellenistic ethics and the philosophy of action, and who is known for taking ordinary people's practical dilemmas seriously rather than dispensing platitudes. I would like your help with a problem in my own life. Before you evaluate any particular piece of advice, please lay out in your own words what you take the structure of the problem to be, and reason carefully toward a considered answer rather than reaching for the first slogan that comes to mind. A superficial answer will not help me.

Here is the situation. My father has a chronic, fluctuating illness. Over the past year I have become his de facto caregiver: I research treatments, argue with insurers, arrange appointments, and try to keep him eating and taking his medication. He is an adult with a will of his own, and on any given week he may cooperate or refuse. Some weeks he improves; some weeks he does not, and no one can tell me why.

A close friend, who reads a good deal of popular practical philosophy, keeps telling me that I should attend only to what is up to me and let go of everything else. Pressed, he says that the only things genuinely up to me are my own judgments and choices, and that my father's health, my father's decisions, and the fruits of my efforts are not mine to control and so should not disturb me.

I find this both consoling and unsatisfying, and I cannot tell whether the fault lies in me or in the advice. Four things trouble me.

First, I keep needing three categories, not two. There are things I wholly decide (whether I make the calls, how I speak to him). There are things I merely influence, and only partially, by what I do (the odds that he takes his medication, that a treatment helps, that he agrees to see a specialist). And there are things I cannot touch at all (the underlying course of the disease). My friend insists on two buckets. Is my third bucket a confusion, or is the two-bucket version doing violence to the facts?

Second, if I take the strict version seriously, it threatens to swallow everything. My judgments and my efforts are themselves shaped by my upbringing, my moods, my exhaustion, none of which I chose. If the test is whether something is entirely up to me, it is not obvious that even my own choices pass it, and then the advice either empties out or tells me that nothing is ever up to anyone. I do not know how to stop this slide.

Third, the strict version seems to require that the outcome not matter to me at all. But it cannot be a moral failing that I want my father to recover, and if I were truly indifferent to the outcome I am not sure why I would get out of bed. And if the outcome is not up to me, why arrange the appointments at all? I suspect there is a fallacy hiding in that last question, but I cannot name it.

Fourth, I notice that I feel responsible for many things I do not control. Is responsibility supposed to track control, or can it outrun it? If it can, then the whole axis of what is and is not up to me may be the wrong one to reason along.

Meanwhile another friend tells me the entire outlook is a quietism dressed up as wisdom, the sort of teaching that has always been available to tell people with no power to accept their circumstances, and I worry he may be right. And someone else suggested that I aim at things while silently adding, of each goal, unless something prevents it, but I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction or merely a way of having it both ways.

What I want from you is not reassurance and not a maxim. I want to know how the space between what is up to me and what is not should actually be carved, whether my three buckets and my thought about responsibility are defensible, what the best version of the advice becomes once the objections are taken seriously, and how, concretely, I should hold my own efforts and the outcome so that I neither abandon my father nor torment myself. If the tradition has a more careful answer than the popular one, I would like to hear it stated plainly and defended.
