I run operations for a regional grocery-delivery outfit — about 400 drivers — and last year I signed off on two separate pieces of software that I now can't stop thinking about. The first is a routing tool that builds each driver's whole day: stop order, time windows, and a target number of drops per hour. The second is a dashcam fatigue monitor from a completely different vendor that watches eye movement, beeps at the driver, and fires a "drowsiness event" onto the shift supervisor's dashboard. On paper they're unrelated — one schedules, one watches. In practice the router learned to pack days tight because tight days are what the metrics reward, the fatigue monitor started firing constantly, and our supervisors had been told in no uncertain terms that pulling a driver off a route early costs us money, so they learned to hit "acknowledge — driver advised to take a break" and move on. The dashboard even has a little button that clears every pending alert at once. Two weeks ago a driver on hour eleven of a twelve-hour day crossed the centre line and put a cyclist in the hospital. He's going to be okay, I think, but he's definately not walking for a while.

Now everybody has a story ready. The routing vendor says their model only optimizes inside the constraints we hand it and never told anyone to skip a break. The fatigue vendor says the alert fired fourteen minutes before the crash and their logs prove it. The supervisor says she followed the escalation policy, and that policy was written to keep vans moving. The driver says he'd been asking for a shorter route for a month and that he was scared to stop because the app docks your rating when you're late. And then there's me — I approved the rollout, I picked this router over a slower one because the demo was prettier, and I never once asked what the two systems would do to each other once they were both switched on. My insurer wants a name for who's at fault. My board wants the incident report to say "driver error, retraining completed," which feels to me like we're picking the guy with the least power and the most name to hang it on. But "the system did it" just seems like a way of saying nobody answers for a man in a wheelchair, and I can't stomach that either. I don't think the fix is more human oversight, because the humans were overseeing and it made things worse. I took one ethics class in college and all I remember is the trolley, which isn't helping. Is there a principled way to sort out who actually bears what here — not just who pays, but who answers and for what — and practically, what should go in this report and what do we change on Monday?
