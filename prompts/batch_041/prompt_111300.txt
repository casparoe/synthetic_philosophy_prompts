I'm a backend engineer, and an argument with a colleague has turned out to be more interesting than either of us expected. He claims that the model our team ships doesn't believe anything, and that when people say it "thinks the meeting is on Tuesday" or "wants to finish the sentence," they're using words that will look to our descendants the way "phlogiston" looks to us — a placeholder for something a mature science drops rather than explains. I half agree, and I can see why the comparison is seductive: phlogiston was introduced to account for combustion, it did real explanatory work for a while, and then a better chemistry came along that had no use for it; on his view "belief" is the same sort of posit, doing temporary work in our everyday predictions about people (and about models) until a serious theory of the brain takes over. What I can't follow is the second half of his claim: he says the comparison also shows that nobody believes anything, me included, and that the entire vocabulary of beliefs and desires is a folk theory on its way out.

I'd like an audit of how far the comparison actually carries. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what corresponds to phlogiston, to combustion, to the better chemistry that replaced it, and to the people who kept using the old word — then say where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks matter for the conclusion he's drawing and which are incidental. I'm especially interested in three things. First, whether the history he leans on is as clean as he assumes, and how much of his case rests on treating "belief" as a theoretical posit in the first place. Second, whether the analogy can support the claim about human believers or only the far weaker claim about the model, given that he seems to want one price to cover both. Third, what the analogy quietly assumes about what it takes for a piece of everyday talk to count as a theory that can be refuted or retired — that seems to be where most of the weight is sitting. If the comparison can't carry that weight, say what would do better and why; if it can, tell me plainly what I'd have to give up in order to accept it. American English, please, and no reading list — I want the argument itself, not pointers.
