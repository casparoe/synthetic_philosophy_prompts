I teach the social choice unit in our phil-of-econ course, and this year I want to run the 2011 UK referendum on the Alternative Vote as the running case study — so do the analysis for me and I'll pinch it for my lecture notes. Setup, since you can't look any of this up: after the 2010 general election the UK got a Conservative–Lib Dem coalition, and the coalition agreement promised a referendum on changing how MPs are elected — but on AV (ranked-choice voting in single-member seats, which is *not* proportional) rather than the proportional system the Lib Dems actually wanted and had to swallow losing. The referendum ran in May 2011 and AV lost by roughly two to one, about 68 to 32, on a turnout of 42%. The Yes side said AV would make MPs earn a majority in their seat; the No side said it was complicated, expensive, and unfair because some voters' ballots would count more than others'. I know the history — don't waste words on it. I want the theory worked over the episode and the episode worked back over the theory. The theory is Gibbard–Satterthwaite: on an unrestricted domain of strict ordinal preferences over at least three alternatives, any strategy-proof social choice function whose range covers every alternative is dictatorial. Please don't explain Arrow's theorem to me; assume I know it and skip the wind-up.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no intro and no wrap-up: (1) the mapping — who the players are, what the alternatives are, and whether GS even applies to the thing the public was actually asked to do, given that a referendum is a two-option choice; (2) what the theorem genuinely does explain about the campaign, including whether the No side's complaint that AV is manipulable and unequal is even an arguement against AV rather than against every rule on offer; (3) where it misfires — the things the theorem is silent on, like the coalition bargain that produced the referendum at all, the fact that both parties were eyeballing their own expected seat share under each rule, intensity of preference, uncertainty about consequences, and the status quo; (4) what the episode shows back about the theorem — confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated — and say which of its assumptions is doing the most work in your answer; (5) your verdict on that last question, plus how confident you are in the whole thing and the one claim you're least sure of.
