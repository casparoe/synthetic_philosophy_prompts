Write me a deadpan open letter, about 500 words, from a 17th-century philosopher to the mathematicians who keep rejecting his solution to squaring the circle — he should be recognisably Hobbes, so give him his own doctrine: demonstration is knowledge of the cause, we can only have science of things we make, geometry qualifies because we construct the figures, the algebraists pushing symbols around aren't producing knowledge at all, and the whole thing matters because he wants the same demonstrative certainty in his civil philosophy. Stay inside his position the entire way: every complaint has to be one he can actually make without contradicting himself, and he must never concede the point, admit he's wrong, or let on that he knows the game is up (he can quietly drop one construction and switch to a better one, but only the way he'd tell it). The jokes have to come from the actual structure of his argument — a theory of demonstration being asked to deliver a mathematical result, the demand that his critics refute him using only principles he already accepts, the slide from your proof doesn't convince me to your proof isn't a proof, and the suspicion that an Oxford professor of geometry and the new experimental society are ganging up on him for reasons that aren't mathematical. Dont quote anything and dont lean on real titles or dates, just the voice and the argument. Aim it so someone who's read a bit of Hobbes laughs and thinks it's fair rather than a cheap shot at a deluded old man, and someone who hasn't still finishes it seeing what's really at stake: what it would take for a philosophical account of knowledge to produce a theorem.
