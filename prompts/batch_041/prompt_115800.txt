Been rereading Berkeley's The Querist for something I'm writing and I keep getting stuck on the run of queries where he does the money-as-ticket thing. It's 1735, he's an idealist bishop, and out of nowhere he seems to be saying money is basically a token. The stretch I mean:

22 Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?
23 Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?
24 Whether the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty?
25 Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?
26 Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?

What I can't get straight is what kind of claim the ticket-or-counter line is supposed to be. Is it a metaphysical thesis (money has no intrinsic value, full stop), a semantic one (money is a sign, and signs are nothing over and above their role in a practice), or a policy recommendation about how we ought to treat it? Because in 24 he gives what sounds like a pretty robust account of value as a proportion between demand and plenty, and then 25 makes gold and silver and paper all equally tickets for recording that proportion — so a ticket for what, exactly? And then 26 says the denominations could survive the disappearance of bullion, which sounds like the credit/chartalist view way ahead of schedule. Is that downstream of his general theory of signs (language, vision, etc.), where signs don't have to be pictures of anything, or is it a seperate economic argument that just happens to rhyme with it? Also there's the earlier stuff about money being useful only so far as it stirs up industry (5, 6, 21) and the bit where he wants to create new wants in peasants (20) — is that in tension with treating money as a mere counter, or is that actually the point, that the counter's job is to keep the industry circulating?

Can you walk me through how these pieces fit, and in particular tell me whether the ticket view is meant as an analysis of what money is, or as a claim about what we should take it to be? No cites needed, I've got the text.
