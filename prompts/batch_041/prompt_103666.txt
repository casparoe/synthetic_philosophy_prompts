The question the prof wrote, 10 points:

"Distinguish an epistemic moral dilemma from a genuine (ontological) one. Then say whether genuine dilemmas are possible. You must address (a) the agglomeration principle — that if an agent ought to do A and ought to do B, then she ought to do A-and-B; (b) 'ought implies can'; and (c) whether the obligation that loses out is overridden or cancelled."

The student's answer:

"An epistemic dilemma is a case where you can't tell which of two obligations you should follow, even though one of them really does win. A genuine dilemma is a case where there really are two obligations in play and you can't do both. I think genuine dilemmas are possible. Sophie in Sophie's Choice has to choose which of her children dies, and whichever she does she's done something she shouldn't have.

The agglomeration principle says that if I ought to save one child and ought to save the other, then I ought to save both. Since ought implies can, and I can't save both, agglomeration would make it true that I ought to do something impossible, which can't be right. So anyone who accepts agglomeration has to say the two obligations can't both be in force at once, and that rules out genuine dilemmas.

I don't accept agglomeration. Kant held that duties could never really conflict, and Ross held that our duties are absolute, so neither of them leaves room for a genuine dilemma; the intuitionists are better on this. When one obligation outweighs the other, the weaker one is cancelled, so nothing of it survives. And you can tell it was a genuine dilemma because the agent feels guilty no matter which way she goes — that guilt is what shows the losing obligation was still binding on her. So genuine dilemmas are possible, but only when the two obligations are equal in strength."
