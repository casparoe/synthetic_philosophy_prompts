I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and next month I have to lead a single ninety-minute session in an undergraduate survey of philosophy in the Islamic world. The students are second- and third-year undergraduates; none of them reads Arabic, most have never encountered Avicenna, and the only remotely relevant thing they have met earlier in the term is Descartes' cogito, which I expect to be the principal source of confusion. They will have read a short excerpt in translation containing the flying man passage before they arrive. I do not want the argument explained to me. I want a plan for how I should explain it, and I will handle the text and any secondary reading myself.

Please give me the following.

1. The order in which the pieces of the session must come, with the dependency relations made explicit. I am fairly confident that opening with the scenario is a mistake and that something has to be in place before it, namely the question the passage is meant to answer, but I want the structure laid out rather than assumed, and I want to be told which piece cannot be moved later without breaking the rest.

2. The carrying example. Should the flying man scenario itself carry the session, or should something more mundane carry it first, with the scenario arriving later as the payoff? If the latter, specify what the stand-in case must have and what it must lack in order to set up the real one rather than pre-empt it.

3. The misconceptions, in the order in which they will actually arise, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I anticipate at least these: the reading of the scenario as an out-of-body experience, or as a report of what it is like to be disembodied; the assumption that the point is the soul's survival of death; the assimilation of the conclusion to the cogito, which is what the students will reach for because it is the only thing they know; and the failure to register the stipulations of the scenario, so that they picture a person with working senses, or a newborn, or someone in a sensory-deprivation tank, rather than a being made in a single instant with no contact with anything bodily. I also want them to arrive at the circularity worry themselves, the suspicion that the argument presupposes that the subject of the self-affirmation is a soul and not a body, but only after they can state the argument in a form in which the circularity is not obvious.

4. One distinction I want the session to leave them with. I read the passage as bearing on whether the soul is not the body, that is, whether it is a substance in its own right, and I take the further claims, that it is immaterial and that it survives death, to be additional steps rather than part of the same move. I want the students to end up able to identify which step is the contested one and to see why someone might hold that the argument establishes less than it is usually said to establish. Build the sequence so that this distinction is the destination rather than a remark appended at the end.

5. A single question to put to the class at the close that would show me whether they have grasped the shape of the argument rather than memorized its conclusion.

6. What I should refuse to go into at this level, and how to decline without making it look like an evasion.

Do not give me readings or a bibliography; I will supply those.
