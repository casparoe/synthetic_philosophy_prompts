I'm a science journalist — I mostly write about decision theory and AI, and the places where they bump into much older stuff — and I've got a half finished piece in my head about the fact that, formally speaking, information is never bad news. The result I keep chewing on is the one usually called Good's theorem, after I. J. Good, the Bletchley Park guy who also came up with the idea of an intelligence explosion: roughly, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, if the information is free, if the set of actions open to you doesn't change, and if your preferences over the outcomes don't depend on what you end up knowing, then the expected value of getting that information is never negative. The intuition is almost cheap, since you can always ignore it, so having it can't leave you worse off in expectation — but every one of those provisos is doing heavy lifting, and what I actually want to write about is a case where the provisos obviously fail.

Which is why I've been rereading Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, and I'd like you to read the play through this result — not as a cute illustration, but as a real test of it. Oedipus does the rational thing at every turn: he sends to Delphi, he summons Tiresias, he insists on the truth about Laius's death, and the truth is what destroys him; stay ignorant and he finishes his life as a happy king. So where exactly does the play dramatize the theorem, and where does it break it? My hunch is that the interesting failure isn't just that the news arrives late or costs him something, but that the information isn't inert. The very same facts — married to this woman, ruler of this city — are worth one thing to him before he knows and something unbearable after, which looks like exactly the state-dependent-utility case the theorem rules out, and maybe also like a case where the decision problem itself changes shape once the answer is in.

I don't want to just assert that and move on, though. Walk me through the scenes that matter — the quarrel with Tiresias, the messenger from Corinth, the shepherd, the final revelation, and whatever Jocasta and the chorus are doing while he investigates — tell me which ones push toward the theorem and which ones push against it, and be honest about whether the play is a counterexample to the theorem or just a dramatization of the gap between an ex ante expectation and an ex post catastrophe. And does it matter that his information-seeking isn't free, or is that a red herring given what the theorem actually claims? I want the piece to be right about the decision theory, so if my framing is sloppy somewhere, say so.
