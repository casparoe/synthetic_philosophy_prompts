I am at work on the distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, and I have grown tired of papers that select a favourite case and reverse-engineer an account to fit it. Before I commit to a position of my own I want the specification: the desiderata that any adequate theory of that distinction must satisfy — the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it must preserve, the explanatory work it must do, and the results it must be consistent with. State each requirement as a constraint on the theory rather than as a feature of any particular account, give in a sentence the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which merely express a commitment that a reasonable rival could reject.

Three things I want from the exercise above all. First, any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, with the conflict stated plainly; that result would be worth more to me than a tidy list, and I would rather see the inconsistency exposed than repaired by qualification. Second, which single requirement eliminates the most candidate accounts, and what manner of account it eliminates — I do not want a literature survey, so descriptions at the level of accounts that define manipulation by the manipulator's intention, accounts that define it by the mechanism of influence, and accounts that build the wrongness into the definition are what I have in mind. Third, whether the requirements can be met by an account that a regulator could actually apply, since I mean to use the theory in an argument about consumer protection, and a regulator can inspect neither a firm's intentions nor a consumer's cognitive processes.

The list must handle at least these cases: influence that proceeds entirely by true statements, as when a genuine deadline is displayed to hurry a decision; influence that recruits a bias rather than disabling a capacity, set against influence that degrades the capacity to deliberate, whether by fatigue, cognitive load, or intoxication; influence that no one intends, as when a feed's ordering shifts a user's beliefs in a direction no designer aimed at; manipulation a person arranges against themselves; manipulation the target consents to, as in a therapist's deliberately provocative intervention or a novelist's engineering of a reader's emotions; and the case of a target who knows the technique, resents it, and is moved by it anyway. The theory must also keep manipulation apart from coercion, from deception, and from rational persuasion without making the boundaries arbitrary; must say whether manipulation is a thick concept whose evaluative charge belongs to its meaning; must say whether the distinction is categorical or one of degree; and must not classify as manipulation every act of influence that succeeds.

Where two requirements pull against each other, say so in place rather than resolving the tension in a favoured account's behalf — I am interested in the shape of the constraints, not in a defence of one theory. No citations and no bibliography: this list is going into a seminar, and what I need is the requirements together with the cases that generate them.
