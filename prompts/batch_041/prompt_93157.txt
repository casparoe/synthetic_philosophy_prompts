The question was: "Intuitionists are often said to reject proof by contradiction. Distinguish the two argument forms that go under that name, say which one an intuitionist accepts and why, and explain what Brouwer's weak counterexamples actually establish about the law of excluded middle."

The student wrote:

"Intuitionism is the doctrine that a mathematical claim is true only when we possess a construction that establishes it; in particular, a proof of an existential claim must exhibit, or give a method for finding, an instance. This is why intuitionists refuse the argument form known as proof by contradiction. To prove A by assuming not-A and deriving an absurdity shows only that not-A cannot be the case; it gives us no construction of the object A is about. The refusal cuts both ways: the familiar argument that the square root of 2 is irrational, which assumes that it can be written as p/q in lowest terms and derives a contradiction, is not available to an intuitionist, who would have to give a positive construction instead.

Brouwer's weak counterexamples turn the scruple into a theorem. Take an open question — say whether some specified long run of digits occurs in the decimal expansion of pi. Define a real number whose n-th digit depends on whether that question has been settled by stage n. Since the question is undecided, we cannot decide the corresponding instance of 'A or not A'; and because the construction can be varied at will, the failure is not a temporary gap in our knowledge. Brouwer concluded, and intuitionists have held since, that the law of excluded middle is not merely unproved but false, and that for such propositions we may assert the negation of the disjunction.

The positive account is the BHK reading: a proof of A-and-B is a pair of proofs, one of each conjunct; a proof of A-or-B is a proof of one of the disjuncts together with an indication of which; a proof of A-implies-B is a method that turns any proof of A into a proof of B; and a proof of not-A is a method that turns any proof of A into a proof of an absurdity. One consequence is the disjunction property: if A-or-B is derivable in intuitionistic logic, then A is derivable or B is. Classical logic lacks this property, since it derives P-or-not-P without deriving either disjunct. It is worth adding that intuitionistic logic is not a rival system proving a different mathematics: adding P-or-not-P to it recovers classical logic, and the double-negation translation shows that classical reasoning can be simulated inside it."
