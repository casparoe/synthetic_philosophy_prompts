My study group has been going around in circles on this for two weeks and I think we've argued ourselves into something stupid, so I want an outside read. Here's the argument, written out the way we've been running it.

(1) Being alive is something a thing does, not something it's made of. If it were about the material, we couldn't even ask whether there's life under the ice on Titan without first checking the chemistry, and that's not how we actually reason.

(2) The doing comes in two parts: a living thing keeps itself going (takes in material and energy, uses them to hold off falling apart), and a living thing makes more things like itself, which vary, and the variation is passed on, which is why living things evolve.

(3) So X is alive iff X is a chemical system that maintains itself and belongs to a lineage that can evolve by natural selection.

(4) A fire maintains itself: it's a structure far from equilibrium that eats fuel and oxygen, holds a boundary against the air, and collapses when you starve it or drown it.

(5) A fire also belongs to a lineage: a spot fire lit by an ember thrown off the main front is descended from that fire; fires differ in how fast they spread depending on fuel and wind; whole lines of them go out.

(6) So a fire satisfies (3). A forest fire is alive.

(7) But a fire is the clearest case there is of something that isn't alive. Putting out a fire isn't killing, and there's nothing it's like to be a fire.

(8) So (1)-(3) is wrong, and we're back to "life" meaning the carbon-and-water stuff, and the virus question is a question about how we use a word.

I'd rather keep (1)-(3) then keep (8) — I think there could be life that isn't like us and I don't want to give that up — but I can't tell where the trick is. Is it (4)? Is it (5)? Is it something in (2)?
