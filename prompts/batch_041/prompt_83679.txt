I teach a 300-level seminar on concepts and analogy (14 students, 75 minutes) and I want one session on conceptual slippage in a microdomain — the thing Hofstadter's group built Copycat to model. The idea I want them to feel rather than hear: that describing a string is never neutral, that every description drags a halo of neighbouring descriptions along with it, and that committing to one reading is already deciding the analogy, so the answer comes out of the concepts you brought rather than out of the puzzle.

Design the activity: rules, materials, roles, a timeline. My rough instinct is a set of letter-string puzzles — you're shown one string turning into another, and asked what a third turns into — run so that two or three readings stay live at once and the group has to negotiate instead of compute. One mechanism I had in mind: give each student a single primitive concept to own (successor, sameness, reversal, leftmost, group-of-two, group-of-three), make the group's answer depend on which of those can build a description that covers the whole puzzle, then swap one element and watch the winning coalition collapse while a different concept takes over. But design whatever's better.

Then the debrief, mapping what happened onto the machinery: many partial descriptions explored in parallel, a temperature-like measure of how coherent the current reading is, slippage as the engine rather than an error. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — a real system holds thousands of competing descriptions at once and 14 people in 75 minutes can't, so say which part of the fluidity I have to supply myself.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run. One thing I'm worried about: they walk out thinking the lesson was just that puzzles are ambiguous. I dont want a room of relativists, I want them to feel that concept-application is constructive and that thats a feature.
