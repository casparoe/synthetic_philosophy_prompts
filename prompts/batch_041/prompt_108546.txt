For a seminar I am co-teaching on the border between semantics and pragmatics, I have been asked to stage a debate between two ways of explaining why slurs wound, and I would like you to write the comparison for me. The first view locates the wound in the word's meaning: a slur encodes, as part of its semantic content, a derogatory characterization of the group it names, which is why the insult is said to survive embedding under negation, conditionals, and reported speech, and why a speaker cannot simply cancel it with a subsequent disavowal. The second view locates the wound somewhere other than content: on this account the term's descriptive contribution is no worse than that of any ordinary group name, and the injury comes instead from a social norm governing who may use the word and in what circumstances, so that the same sound can be an insult in one mouth and a term of solidarity in another, and the insult is better modelled as a prohibition or a kind of act than as a piece of encoded information.

I am tired of the standard examples, so a colleague and I invented one for the class. In our case, two expressions are pronounced and spelled identically. One descends from an old insult coined by a pamphleteer for a group that never actually existed, so that nothing ever satisfied the term; the other is an ordinary occupational word borrowed from another language that happens to have collided with it. Speakers today use the sound to insult a real group with no connection to either history, and members of that group have begun using it among themselves as an affectionate in-group term. I would like the two accounts compared directly against this case — not summarized one after the other, but argued against each other. In particular: which account needs fewer patches to handle it; what would each say about whether the insulting expression has an empty extension, about whether the affectionate use is a use of the same word, and about whether the insult can be cancelled; and finally, what the case suggests about the relationship between a word's meaning and its history.
