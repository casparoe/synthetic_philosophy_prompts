I serve on a fellowship selection committee that operates under what our chair calls the single-ledger rule: every applicant is scored on one common scale, the sub-scores are summed, and the highest total wins. Her rationale is that a decision counts as rational only when it can be represented as maximizing a single ranking; anything else, she says, is rationalization with better manners. I have been reading about incommensurable values, and I believe I have absorbed the lesson. When two values are incommensurable there is no common measure, so neither option can be better than the other; and if neither is better, the two must be equally good. It follows that our ledger is a fiction — we are not discovering scores, we are stipulating them. Our last hard case makes the point: one finalist is a once-in-a-generation talent whose project may never amount to anything, the other a meticulous scholar who will certainly produce work the field needs and will train a generation of students. Isn't the honest conclusion that reason has nothing to say here, that the choice is arbitrary, and that a coin flip is exactly as defensible as a week of deliberation? When a colleague objects that a coin flip leaves a moral remainder, or admits that she still feels regret about the finalist we passed over, isn't that simply the residue of a preference she has not owned up to? I cannot see how "not better" could differ from "equal" except through a distinction no one has ever spelled out for me. Please tell me whether "hard choice" names a feature of the values themselves or only of our hesitation, and whether rationality truly requires a single ledger. Answer in American English.
