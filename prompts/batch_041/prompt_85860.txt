I am a high school student sitting on my district's student advisory committee for a new early-warning system. The system gives every ninth grader a risk score for failing to reach reading proficiency by the end of the year, and the district uses those scores to fill the limited seats in a reading-intervention seminar. The committee has been asked to say what it would mean for the system to be fair. A classmate and I drafted the argument below; I can follow every step, and I cannot accept where it ends. I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (8), and I suspect the real problem with the district's pilot is not arithmetic, but I cannot find the loose step by myself.

(1) A score is fair only if it is equally precise for every group it is applied to: among the students it flags in each group, the same fraction must actually go on to fail.

(2) A score is also fair only if it errs at the same rates for every group: the false positive rate (students flagged who would have succeeded) and the false negative rate (students not flagged who would have failed) must match across groups.

(3) For a score applied at a single threshold, precision is fixed by the base rate and the two error rates. If the base rate is b, the false positive rate is f, and the false negative rate is m, then precision = b(1 − m) / [b(1 − m) + (1 − b)f]. This is just Bayes's theorem.

(4) So if the false positive rate and the false negative rate both match across two groups, any difference in their base rates forces a difference in precision.

(5) Two groups of students will essentially never share a base rate. This holds for any partition we could name, including one with no history or meaning behind it — for instance, students whose surnames fall in the first half of the alphabet against those in the second half.

(6) Therefore no score can satisfy both (1) and (2) whenever the groups differ in base rate and the prediction is less than perfect, which is every real case.

(7) Since (1) and (2) are each part of what "fair" means, and no score can satisfy both, no score is fair with respect to any two groups; and because any two groups can be split this way, fairness here is impossible in principle.

(8) An impossible demand is no demand at all. So the committee should stop asking whether the system is fair, because "fair" names a mathematical impossibility rather than a standard anyone could meet.

Please tell me which step to reject. If the argument contains a flaw, I want the step named, an explanation of why it looked innocent, and an account of what rejecting it costs me — in particular, what I then have to say about the students the score gets wrong and about the alphabet-halves case, which I cannot believe is a case of injustice. If instead the argument is valid as it stands, I need to know which of (1) and (2) is cheapest to give up, and what is owed to the students who bear the remaining errors once I have given it up.
