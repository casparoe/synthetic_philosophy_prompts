I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, and I've somehow ended up on my deparment's social committee, which in practice means I'm the one writing the skit for the end-of-year dinner. This year the theme is "founding moments" and I've been paired with the constitutional law people, so I want to do something about precommitment — Ulysses contracts, entrenchment clauses, a polity binding its own future self. I've read Elster on it and I get the basic structure: the agent at T1 who can see the sirens coming and wants to be overruled by his T2 self. But what I actually find funny, and what I want the skit to turn on, is the regress — the binding only works because T1 is resolute, and T1's resolve is precisely the thing in doubt, so you end up needing a precommitment about the precommitment. Same shape in the constitutional case, where the amending power gets used to make amendment impossible, so the sovereign has to be two things at once: the one who binds and the one who is bound.

So: write me a short skit, three or four exchanges of dialogue, maybe 250 words, that plays on that rather than on a generic "haha Ulysses forgot to tie himself to the mast" joke. Set it at a fictional constituent assembly in some made-up republic, delegates drafting a clause saying certain clauses can never be repealed, and let one of them slowly notice what they're doing. The laughs should come out of the actual mechanics: the regress of self-binding, the awkward fact that the entrenchment clause itself has to be passed by an ordinary majority vote, the difference between an agent who's merely weak-willed and one who's bound by a decision he no longer endorses. If you can make the last line land on the Ulysses/crew asymmetry — he had to be sane enough to give the order and mad enough to need it — please do.

One thing though: I'm performing this in front of people who actually work on this stuff, and a couple of them will be annoyed if it's sloppy. So at the end, add a short note separating what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic — e.g. whether the regress point is the standard reading or something I'm just improvising, and whether anyone serious thinks diachronic precommitment is even a coherent notion in the first place, versus it being taken for granted.
