I am giving the toast at my sister's wedding this weekend, and I would like you to write it. She and her fiancé met eight years ago in a graduate seminar on evolutionary game theory; for the first two weeks the professor had the class play a repeated prisoner's dilemma tournament, and they were paired against each other in the last round, which had been announced in advance. He defected in that last round and still insists he was simply applying backward induction; she says he defected because he panics under pressure. Both versions are now family lore. About half the room will be academics—biologists, economists, a few philosophers—who will catch every game-theoretic reference, and the other half will be our aunts, uncles, and cousins, who will not.

I want the toast to be warm, funny, and about five minutes long, and I want the iterated prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation to do real structural work in it rather than serving as decoration. The speech should turn on the difference between the one-shot game and the repeated game: the one-shot temptation to defect, the way a known final round can unravel cooperation by backward induction, and the way an unknown horizon—a life together with no announced last period—changes what it is rational to do. It should also make something of the fact that real signals are noisy: a partner will sometimes perceive defection where none was intended, and strict tit for tat can then spiral into mutual punishment, so some form of generosity or forgiveness is needed for a cooperative equilibrium to survive. I would like indirect reciprocity to come in near the end—the idea that people cooperate with those who have a reputation for cooperating, even when they may never be repaid directly—because everyone who knows the couple says they are like that.

Finally, I want the toast to acknowledge, lightly, that evolution selects strategies by their payoffs rather than by their intentions, which is a strange and slightly funny thing to say at a wedding. Please write the toast. Do not make it a lecture; introduce the ideas through concrete details of their life, and make sure the non-academics are laughing and the academics are not wincing.
