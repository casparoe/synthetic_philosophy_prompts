I'm a philosophy professor, mid-career, and I've got a recurring irritation I'd like help pinning down properly. There's a colleague in my department — lovely man, works on formal epistemology — who has a habit, whenever anyone raises an objection to anything he says about rationality, of intoning 'accuracy first: the only thing that makes a credence better or worse, epistemically speaking, is how close it is to the truth', and then sitting back as if the case is closed. Most recently it was my turn. I'd raised the old chestnut about two agents with exactly the same credences, both equally close to the truth, one of whom got there by conditionalising carefully on good evidence and the other by consulting a stopped clock, and I asked whether accuracy-first really wants to say the two are epistemically on a par, and if not, where the difference is supposed to come from. He said 'they're on a par, accuracy is the only thing that matters, everything else is instrumental', and went off to get coffee. I don't think that can be right, but I can't put my finger on which of the things he might be claiming is the thing thats actually false, and I'd rather not splutter at him in the corridor again.

So what I want from you is a disambiguation. Take that slogan — accuracy first, accuracy is the only epistemic good — and lay out as precisely as you can the different claims it could be making, ranging from readings so weak that nobody could deny them, through the substantive and genuinely defensible, to the ones that are radical or just false. I'm especially interested in whether it's a claim about individual credences (this credence is good to the extent that it's close to the truth), or about the policies and methods that produce credences (this way of forming credences is good to the extent that it tends to produce accuracy), or about which epistemic norms are justified at all (only those that can be shown to serve accuracy), because I suspect his case-closed move needs one of those readings and quietly borrows its authority from another. For each reading I want a verdict — platitude, live controversy, or false — plus a note on which reading the person repeating the slogan probably has in mind, and whether that reading actually supports the dismissive use it was being put to. I know some of the names that get attached to these moves (veritism, the only-game-in-town argument, teleological accounts of belief and credence, the machinery of expected accuracy and dominance and proper scoring rules) and I'd rather you tell me which of them the slogan could be standing in for than assume I've got them all straight — but take the machinery itself as read, I want the taxonomy and the verdicts, not a tutorial.

One thing about the format, and I mean this: I know a menu of readings is crying out for bullet points, but please give it to me as continuous prose, no headings, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables — I'm going to read it out at our next meeting and the formatting always mangles when I paste it into my notes. And British English, please; I'm in a UK department and he'll be insufferable about an 'ize'.
