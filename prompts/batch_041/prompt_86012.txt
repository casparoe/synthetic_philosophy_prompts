I'm the discussant for the fourth meeting of a graduate seminar on the philosophy of pleasure, and my job is to open our session on Mill's *Utilitarianism* with a close reading of a single paragraph from the second chapter. I picked it because it seems to me to compress the whole difficulty my classmates have been calling the heterogeneity problem into four sentences, and because I have a nagging suspicion that I am reading it wrong. The paragraph is this:

> But there is no known Epicurean theory of life which does not assign to the pleasures of the intellect, of the feelings and imagination, and of the moral sentiments, a much higher value as pleasures than to those of mere sensation. It must be admitted, however, that utilitarian writers in general have placed the superiority of mental over bodily pleasures chiefly in the greater permanency, safety, uncostliness, &c., of the former—that is, in their circumstantial advantages rather than in their intrinsic nature. And on all these points utilitarians have fully proved their case; but they might have taken the other, and, as it may be called, higher ground, with entire consistency. It is quite compatible with the principle of utility to recognise the fact, that some kinds of pleasure are more desirable and more valuable than others.

What I want is help understanding it, though not in the way of a general summary of Mill's doctrine of higher pleasures — that I can get from the lecture notes and from any number of encyclopaedia entries. I want something closer to a line-by-line commentary, written for someone who has already read the chapter twice and is now stuck on details: what each sentence is claiming, what is being conceded and to whom (the first sentence, for instance, seems to be answering the charge that utilitarianism is a doctrine worthy only of swine), which sentences are doing argumentative work and which are merely transitional, and how the four sentences hang together as a single movement of thought. Three particular things puzzle me. First, the phrase "a much higher value as pleasures": what work is the qualifier "as pleasures" doing? If it means that the higher pleasure is more valuable precisely in respect of its pleasantness, then the claim appears to presuppose a single dimension — pleasantness — along which all pleasures, however otherwise dissimilar, can be ranked, and that is precisely what the heterogeneity problem denies, since the pleasure of drinking a glass of cold water and the pleasure of proving a theorem seem to share nothing but the name. A classmate of mine concludes from this that the phrase is simply incoherent and that Mill must be smuggling non-hedonic value in through the back door; I am not persuaded, but I cannot say what else the phrase could mean, and I would like you to set out both readings before you commit to one. Second, the contrast between "circumstantial advantages" and "intrinsic nature": Mill concedes that earlier utilitarians defended the superiority of mental over bodily pleasures on grounds of permanency, safety and cheapness, then says he could have taken "higher ground" with "entire consistency". Why is the higher ground the riskier one to occupy, and consistency with what — with the principle of utility as a criterion of right action, or with the thesis that pleasure is the only thing desirable as an end? Third, the closing "more desirable and more valuable": is that a normative claim, a prediction about what people in fact prefer, or a claim about what is worth preferring, and does any of those readings require a common measure that the heterogeneity problem says is unavailable?

I recognise that this is a great deal to ask of four sentences, so if you have to ration your attention, I would rather have a careful treatment of the first puzzle than a survey of the other two. And where you think I am importing a distinction that is simply not in the text, please say so plainly rather than accommodating me.
