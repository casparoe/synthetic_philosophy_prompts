Could you help me pin down where this argument goes wrong? I wrote it for a problem sheet on Zorn's lemma and the conclusion is obviously daft, but I cant find the dodgy step. Here it is:

(1) Let S be any set, and let P be the set of all proper subsets of S, ordered by inclusion.
(2) Take any chain in P. Its union is a subset of S, and since each member of the chain is a proper subset, the union is also proper, so the union is in P. Hence every chain in P has an upper bound in P.
(3) So by Zorn's lemma P has a maximal element M.
(4) M is proper, so pick x in S but not in M. Then M ∪ {x} is a proper subset of S strictly bigger than M.
(5) That contradicts M being maximal, so there's no such x, so M = S.
(6) But M was proper. So we've got a contradiction, and the upshot seems to be that no infinite set exists.

I'm pretty sure the blow-up is around (5)-(6), but that only tells me some earlier line is wrong. Which line do I give up, why did it look so innocent, and what does giving it up actually cost — what am I no longer entitled to do when I reach for Zorn's lemma? For what its worth, the same shape of argument is meant to work fine for maximal ideals of a ring, so I don't think Zorn's lemma itself is the villain.
