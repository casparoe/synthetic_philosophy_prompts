I sit on a working group drafting a binding fairness rule for automated decision systems in consumer lending and hiring, and after a year in the technical literature I have concluded that the literature is organised around a question we do not have. Each candidate criterion — demographic parity, equalized odds, calibration within groups, predictive parity, individual fairness as a Lipschitz condition on a task-specific metric, counterfactual fairness — is defended against a different background assumption, and the impossibility results get presented as proof that we must simply choose which of them to give up. That seems premature. Before we choose, I want the specification rather than the menu: write the desiderata that any fairness criterion would have to meet to be fit for a rule of this kind — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the facts about our setting it must be consistent with — each requirement stated together with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference of one camp, and marked as non-negotiable or as negotiable. A numbered list is fine.

Our setting imposes five constraints that I suspect most of these criteria were not designed for, and I would like the list built around them. First, the rule must be checkable by an outside auditor who sees only decisions and outcomes over time, not the model, its features, or its training data; a criterion verifiable only by inspecting the algorithm is of no use to us. Second, we cannot reliably record the protected attribute; it is partly inferred, and the inference errors correlate with the outcome we care about. Third, the label we would train on and audit against is itself a proxy — arrears rather than creditworthiness, supervisory ratings rather than performance — so the criterion has to be explicit about what it is a criterion about, and must not silently inherit the label's bias. Fourth, the decision changes the population it is applied to: advertising, recruiting, and repayment behaviour all respond to the policy, so the distribution on which a criterion is checked in year one is not the distribution it will face in year three. Fifth, the rule must be non-vacuous: satisfiable by some policy other than denying everyone, not satisfiable by degrading the better-off group to the level of the worse-off, and not satisfiable by meeting the constraint on the named groups while failing inside them.

Tell me where the first constraint conflicts with any requirement that a criterion track the actual decision procedure rather than its output, and identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied — in our setting or in general — and say what has to give; that part is what I most want. If the impossibility results usually cited generalise here, say which survive and which dissolve once the third constraint is taken seriously. Then, briefly, how each criterion I listed fares against the list, one line apiece, and which single requirement excludes the most candidates overall. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are where I need the help.
