I am working through Aristotle's Posterior Analytics and have hit a passage in Book I that I can follow sentence by sentence but cannot assemble into an argument. He writes:

"We suppose ourselves to possess unqualified scientific knowledge of a thing, as opposed to knowing it in the accidental way in which the sophist knows, when we think that we know the cause on which the fact depends, as the cause of that fact and of no other, and, further, that the fact could not be other than it is."

and then, a little further on:

"The premisses must be primary and indemonstrable; otherwise they will require demonstration in order to be known, since to have knowledge, if it be not accidental knowledge, of things which are demonstrable, means precisely to have a demonstration of them."

What I cannot see is where the necessity comes in, and in particular why the second sentence is supposed to follow from the first. The first tells me what unqualified scientific knowledge is — knowledge of the cause, of a fact that could not be otherwise — and the second tells me that the premisses of a demonstration must themselves be indemonstrable. I can see that if a premiss required demonstration, and its premiss required demonstration, and so on, I should never finish; but "I should never finish" is a claim about me and my finite life, not obviously a claim about knowledge, and Aristotle appears to be making the stronger claim. Nor can I tell whether "indemonstrable" here means "incapable of being demonstrated" or merely "not in fact demonstrated by us, here" — the two readings would yield very different arguments, and the sentence seems to slide between them.

I should like help with three things. First, an unpacking of the passage itself: what exactly the object of unqualified scientific knowledge is taken to be, what work the "could not be other than it is" clause does in the argument that follows, and what "primary" adds that "indemonstrable" does not. Second, an assessment of the argument as it stands: is the regress he is worried about a regress of demonstrations specifically — syllogisms whose premisses are causes, prior to and better known by nature than the conclusion — or is it meant to be a regress of reasons in some broader sense, and does the argument succeed on either reading?

Third, the question I actually care about. I am told that this passage is the founding move of foundationalism, and that its rivals are an infinite regress of reasons and a circle of them; I am also told that modern infinitism accepts an infinite regress and denies that anything is lost by it, on the grounds that a reasoner need not complete the series, only be able to produce the next member whenever challenged. That last move seems to me to depend on construing the regress as a regress of challenges rather than a regress of premisses, and I want to know whether the passage I have quoted can be turned against it. So: if the chain of reasons is infinite but each member is available on demand, has Aristotle's argument been met or merely evaded? Does it rest on a premiss that infinitism can simply deny — for instance, that justification must be completed rather than merely continuable — and if so, is that premiss stated in the passage or imported into it? And is the coherentist option the same structure as the circular demonstration he rejects, or is the holism of a coherentist system genuinely different from demonstrating a conclusion from premisses that include it? I should like the answers to be as specific as possible about which of these questions the quoted text settles, which it leaves open, and which turn on claims Aristotle does not make in the passage at all — including whether the argument survives at all once the relata are empirical beliefs rather than necessary truths.
