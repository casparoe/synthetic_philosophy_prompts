I am a PhD student in philosophy, TAing a graduate seminar on Frege and early analytic philosophy. Below is the essay prompt for the final assignment and one student's response. Please grade the response: give a letter grade first, then the point-by-point comments that justify it — which substantive claims are correct, which are not, where an inference does not go through, and what you would raise in office hours rather than penalize. Also note anything the student gets right in substance but supports with a bad argument, and anything that would have to be there to satisfy the prompt. Do not summarize the essay or give a general impression. Open with the grade in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the assignment.

Essay prompt: Frege's diary of 1924, recovered from his Nachlass and published only posthumously, contains antisemitic and anti-democratic remarks. Some commentators treat the diary as irrelevant to his philosophical work; others treat it as bearing on how that work should be read and taught. State the strongest version of each position, and defend one of them against at least one serious objection.

Student response:

The strongest case for irrelevance rests on Frege's own anti-psychologism. From the Grundlagen onward, and most explicitly in his 1894 review of Husserl's Philosophie der Arithmetik, Frege insists that logical and mathematical truths are objective and independent of any thinker's mental life. If that is right, facts about Frege's character cannot bear on whether his claims are true, so the diary is of biographical interest only. A second strand is chronological: the diary is from 1924, so it postdates the Begriffsschrift, the Grundlagen, and both volumes of the Grundgesetze, and cannot have shaped the logical work.

The case for relevance grants all of this about truth and adds two points. First, Frege's Platonism is a thesis about the subject matter of logic, not a license to ignore authors; one can hold that truth is mind-independent while holding that a thinker's commitments shape which questions he asks and which arguments strike him as compelling. Second, if the diary is evidence of commitments Frege held throughout, the chronological argument loses its force, since it shows only when the attitudes were written down, not when they were formed.

I side with relevance, though I would state it modestly. The diary does not falsify Frege's theorems; it bears on interpretation and on how we present the corpus. It is worth noting that Frege's anti-psychologism is standardly read as a response to Husserl's Logical Investigations, which suggests that a philosopher's polemical context can be philosophically decisive; Frege's political context deserves the same treatment. The objection I take most seriously is the genetic fallacy: an argument's unsavory motive does not make it unsound. I concede that, and rest my case on the interpretive claim instead.
