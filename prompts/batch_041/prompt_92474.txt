My friend is doing a reading course on modality and she's built an argument that's bugging me, because I can't tell if it's a cheap trick or a real problem. The setup: there's a view where you only ever exist in one world — this one — and talk like "I could have been a doctor" or "I could have been born somewhere else" is really about a different person in another world who's enough like you, your "counterpart," who is the doctor or who got born elsewhere. You never show up in any other world yourself. The rival view says no, it's the same you across worlds, just with different properties. Standard stuff.

Her argument:

(1) On the counterpart view, "I could have been a doctor" is true just in case some counterpart of mine is a doctor.
(2) A counterpart of mine is not me — I only exist in this world, so my counterpart is a distinct individual.
(3) So when that sentence is true, the person who is the doctor is not me.
(4) So the sentence, when true, is true in virtue of facts about somebody else.
(5) But when I say "I could have been a doctor," I'm talking about me and what's possible for me.
(6) An analysis that turns a claim about me into a claim about somebody else has got the subject wrong.
(7) So the counterpart view is false.

She says she's heard the obvious comeback — "the counterpart is just how you spell out the truth-conditions; it doesn't mean the sentence is about the counterpart" — and she thinks it fails, because if the counterpart is only ever a truth-condition device and never really me, then the view owes me an account of why my counterparts should matter to me at all, why "I could have been a doctor" is any more about my life than a fact about a stranger. Either the counterpart is me (and it can't be, per (2)) or modality stops being about us. She also throws in a kicker: run the same thing on "I could have been you," and the view says that's true whenever you and I are counterparts of each other, which she says makes it a sentence about two other people that's somehow true — a reductio.

I definately think the slippage is around (3)-(5) and the word "about" is doing something load-bearing, but i can't say exactly what. Which step should she give up? And what does the counterpart theorist actually have to say here — does saving the view cost anything, like giving up on the idea that modal claims are genuinely about us? Price it out for me.
