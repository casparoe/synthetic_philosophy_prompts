ok, comparative religion-and-politics seminar, and everyone keeps reaching for the same picture: church and state are two institutions and the healthy setup is a wall between them — state doesn't run the church, church doesn't run the state, and where the wall stands you get freedom. I want to stress-test it on the four cases we actually cover, because they dont line up: England has an established church (monarch at its head, bishops in the legislature) but almost nobody is coerced; Germany has no establishment yet the state collects a church tax straight out of your paycheck and the churches have public-law status; France is aggressively secular but the state actively manages religion — bans conspicuous religious symbols in schools, pays chaplains, and still runs the Alsace-Moselle concordat; the US is the wall metaphor's home turf yet has 'In God We Trust' on the money, legislative chaplains, and church tax exemptions.

So what maps onto what here? When people say 'wall', do they mean separation of two institutions, neutrality of the state toward its citizens, or non-coercion of individual conscience — and are those actually the same thing or do they come apart? Where does the wall picture break worst, and is there a case where the wall is missing but the thing we care about is present, or a wall that's intact and freedom still isn't? Tell me which disanalogies matter for the argument the metaphor is being made to do, and whether the conclusion people draw from it — more wall, more freedom — survives once they're on the table. If there's a better image, give me one.
