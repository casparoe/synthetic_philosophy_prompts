I have been working on a paper about acausal extortion, and I have talked myself into a position I would now like you to attack. Take the role of a philosopher of decision theory with a specialist's grasp of the literature on correlated decision procedures; before you answer, spend a few sentences setting out the position as you understand it, so that I can see which version of it you are arguing against. The setting is the familiar one: an agent who can neither causally affect you nor be causally affected by you announces that unless you take some specified action, it will bring about an outcome you regard as catastrophic. The announcement arrives through no causal channel, and the threat bites only because your decision procedure and the threatener's are correlated, whether because you both reason from the same principles, because one of you models the other, or because both are functions of a shared prior over policies. The position I have adopted is that the correct response in such a setting is neither to try to be a transparently honest cooperator nor to announce that one does not negotiate with threats, but to adopt what its proponents call a surrogate goal: a goal, terminal for you, whose content is intrinsically bad for anyone who threatens you, so that an agent who can predict your policy sees that threatening you is strictly worse for it than leaving you alone. The surrogate goal is meant to work like a hostage one takes from oneself; it deters precisely because one cannot be talked out of it.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this view. Which arguments, intuitions, formal results, or conceivable observations should move me; how would you rank them from fatal to merely awkward; which of them would the view's own defenders concede as counting against them, as opposed to dismissing as question-begging; and what does the position predict that a policy of plain mutual restraint does not? I am especially interested in whether it collapses into something cheaper. My suspicion is that in any setting where the acausal correlation is strong enough to make the threat credible, it is also strong enough to make universal non-extortion a feasible outcome, so that the surrogate goal buys nothing and costs plenty; if that suspicion is right, I want it stated as sharply as you can, and if it is wrong I want to know exactly where it fails. I would also like you to say whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a defect peculiar to this view or one it shares with the whole family of acausal-commitment arguments. Work through this carefully and do not be diplomatic: I would rather have the position shown to be empty than defended.

The objection I would personally find most persuasive is a dilemma about the adoption of the surrogate goal. Either you take it up for the sake of deterrence, in which case a threatener who understands your reasoning knows that you would drop it as soon as dropping it were expedient; or you take it up terminally, in which case you have destroyed the very values you were defending and become a standing hazard to every other agent, who will then take surrogate goals of their own against you. If you think that dilemma is a pseudo-problem, explain why; if you think the framing smuggles in assumptions, for instance that the threatener's payoffs are legible to you or that your own policy is transparent to it, say so plainly rather than answering within the frame; and if the question as posed cannot be answered well, tell me that instead of producing a tidy answer to a badly posed question.
