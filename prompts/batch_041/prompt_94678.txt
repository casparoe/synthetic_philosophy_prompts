Since retiring from experimental physics I have been trying, rather belatedly, to give myself a philosophical education, and the last several months have gone into the theory of choice. I have worked my way through the revealed-preference programme — Samuelson's project of reconstructing the consumer's preferences out of nothing but his market behaviour, the ordinalist prohibition on talk of quantities of satisfaction, and the no-interpersonal-comparisons rule that came with it — and then through the objections: Sen's argument that an agent defined entirely by the choices he makes cannot be credited with commitment or with the values he has reason to endorse, and the psychologists' demonstrations that a person's choices and his stated valuations of the very same objects can diverge systematically and in predictable directions. What keeps striking me, as a scientist, is how closely the identification of utility with choice resembles Bridgman's operationalism, the doctrine that a concept is nothing over and above the operations by which it is measured; my own field gave that doctrine up precisely because different operations for the same quantity turned out to disagree, and the choice-versus-valuation experiments look to me like the economists' version of that discovery. What I cannot decide is whether the moral is merely that choice is a fallible indicator of preference, or something stronger — that the concept of a person's good cannot be built out of choices at all.

It is with that question that I went back to a novel I first read decades ago, Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and to the Merchant Ivory film of it. Stevens, the butler at Darlington Hall, strikes me as an unusually exacting test case. He insists throughout his own account that a single value has governed him, the dignity of his profession; and his behaviour, taken strictly as behaviour, is remarkably consistent with that insistence — he keeps serving through the evening on which his father lies dying upstairs, he declines every personal opening Miss Kenton makes, and when she finally leaves he carries on exactly as before. There is even a small scene in which he is caught reading a sentimental romance and explains, with perfect composure, that he reads such books only to improve his command of the English language: a choice that reveals a preference his testimony flatly denies. What I would like from you is a reading of the novel, and of the film where the film's register of the unspoken — a face, a hand not quite taken — does something the first-person narration cannot, through the lens of revealed preference and the meaning of utility. Does the novel confirm the behaviourist identification, since Stevens's choices do line up with an intelligible preference ordering, or does it refute it? I would like you to press on four specific points. First, whether identifying utility with choice leaves the novel with nothing to mourn: if Stevens's choices consistently satisfy his preferences, then his life scores well by his own utility, and the elegiac close — the seafront bench, the stranger's remark that the evening is the best part of the day, Stevens resolving to make himself useful to his new employer — would be unmotivated. Second, what account of well-being would be required to make the novel's judgement coherent, and whether that account, whether a mental-state view, an objective-list view, or Sen's distinction between what a person chooses and what he has reason to value, can be had without the interpersonal comparisons the ordinalists forbade. Third, whether this is best described as a reversal between decision and experience — he gets what he chose and does not get what he wanted — or instead as the manufacture of a preference set by an ideology, so that the satisfaction of his preferences is precisely what ruins him; I want to know whether the economists' apparatus can express the second possibility at all, or whether it must file it under irrationality. Fourth, since everything we observe about Stevens is reported by Stevens, whether the novel is also a commentary on the observer's side of the behaviourist bargain: the choices are no rawer than the testimony, and both are shaped by the very ideal the theory is meant to measure.

Please keep the reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a theorem, and do not bend the theory to fit the novel. Where Stevens's case resists the framework, and where the framework has an answer that the novel's sadness simply ignores, say so plainly. I would rather have the argument than a string of quotations, which in any case I have no text at hand to check. End with a single sentence, on its own line, beginning "Verdict:", stating whether you take the novel to support or to undermine the identification of a person's utility with the pattern of his choices.
