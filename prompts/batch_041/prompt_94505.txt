Here's the question (worth 20, 5 per part):

Suppose we fix a space of tasks T, where each task t comes with a loss function L(·, t) scoring a policy π, with L bounded in [0, 1]. Let D be a distribution over T representing the tasks we actually expect at deployment.

(a) Give the standard argument for why scoring a policy by its average loss E_{t~D}[L(π, t)] is not enough to certify that the policy is safe.

(b) Give the standard argument for why the opposite extreme, sup over t in T of L(π, t), is also not useful on its own — i.e., why a pure worst-case criterion over an unconstrained T tells you almost nothing.

(c) Define the ε-robust loss of π as the max over distributions D′ with TV(D′, D) ≤ ε of E_{t~D′}[L(π, t)]. Is this a good middle ground between (a) and (b)? Say what it buys you, what it doesn't buy you, and what has to be true about T and ε for the number to mean anything.

(d) A classmate says: "none of this matters, because under optimization pressure the system will find the one task in T where it does something catastrophic, so any criterion that isn't literally worst-case over T is unsafe." Assess this.

And here's what the student wrote:

(a) The problem with average loss is that D is a guess. If deployment tasks drift even slightly from D — new users, new inputs, a new environment — then the average under D stops telling you anything about the average you'll actually get, and the failure shows up exactly in the tail: a policy can have an average loss of 0.001 and still have some specific task where it does something irreversible. And since training optimizes a proxy, pushing hard on the proxy moves the policy off D, so the better your training loss, the less the D-average is worth. So you need a criterion that doesn't hinge on D being exactly right.

(b) The opposite extreme is vacuous. If T is unconstrained then for any policy π there's a task t* defined as "the task on which π destroys everything," so sup over t of L(π, t) = 1 for every π, and the criterion can't rank anything. This is just no-free-lunch: averaged over all possible objectives every policy performs identically, so there's nothing for worst-case analysis to latch onto, which is why worst-case reasoning on its own doesn't give you a usable ordering of policies.

(c) ε-robust loss is a genuine middle ground. It only prices in shifts of size at most ε in total variation, so it protects you against tasks near D but not against a genuinely new kind of task that sits far away from D in TV. It also inherits the fact that TV is a blunt instrument — a shift of size ε can bundle a harmless perturbation together with a catastrophic one, so what you get is a bound over a neighborhood rather than a statement about any particular task. And both ε and the shape of the neighborhood are choices you make, not facts you discover: set ε small enough and the criterion collapses back into (a), set it large enough and it collapses into (b). So it buys you a quantifiable statement about a neighborhood of D, and nothing more.

(d) The classmate is overstating it. Optimization pressure is real — you don't get to pick which task the system lands on, and if there's any task in T where the catastrophic action is also the reward-maximizing action, a strong enough optimizer will find it. But that's exactly what regret bounds are for. If we can show the deployed policy has worst-case regret at most δ relative to the best policy in its class, then whatever it's doing, it's doing at least as well as anything else we could have deployed, so there's no mistake left to fix. And since the bound is on regret rather than on absolute loss, the guarantee doesn't depend on us guessing D right. So the classmate is wrong that you need literal worst-case over T — a regret guarantee is the thing that actually survives optimization pressure.
