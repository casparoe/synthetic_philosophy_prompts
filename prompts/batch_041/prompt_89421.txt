Ok so I'm a novelist — third book, historical, set in a made-up Gulf Coast town in the 1890s — and I've gotten myself into a problem that's less about prose than about method. My source base is four things. A sugar plantation ledger that lists people as prices and first names, no surnames. The county asylum admissions book, which has a column for cause. Two runs of the local paper on microfilm with a two-year hole in the middle after a flood. And a Black church whose records dont exist anymore because the church burned down. Everything I know about my characters' world comes through those four things, and most of what I need isn't in them.

An archivist friend keeps telling me a silence isn't one thing. She says there's stuff that never got written down because nobody thought it was worth writing, stuff that got written and then somebody decided not to keep it, stuff that's kept but sitting in a box nobody catalogued or filed under the wrong heading, and stuff that's in the record and was never believed — the complaint logged as drunkenness, the woman whose statement is filed as hysteria. Then she adds a fifth, which is the shape I impose when I sit down to tell it. She says each one calls for a different response, then refuses to tell me what the response is, because she says it's an ethics question.

So give me a procedure I can actually run, at my desk, with a folder, maybe fifty times over a draft. Put the questions in order. Say what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure simply stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision back to me — or to the family I've been talking to, or my sensitivity reader — because it's a values call and not a fact call. It needs to be runnable by my research assistant, who's 24 and has no history training, and by me at 11pm the night before something's due. Then run it on three cases: the unnamed fourteen-year-old girl in the ledger; the asylum entry where the cause is listed as grief; and the two-year hole in the paper, where I know from one offhand reference much later that something happened in that town and I have no record of it whatsoever. Include the case where your procedure tells me something I don't want to hear, and a short section on where the thing breaks down — the cases it can't settle and shouldn't pretend to.

One more thing, and it matters: seperate what's actually settled from what's one camp's view among people who know this stuff — archivists, historians of slavery, oral historians. There's a real fight about whether filling a silence is repair or just another theft, and I don't want one side of it handed to me dressed up as the consensus. If it's contested, say so, and tell me in plain terms what the disagreement actually is. Don't send me a reading list, I'm not chasing citations at this stage and I can't check them from here anyway.
