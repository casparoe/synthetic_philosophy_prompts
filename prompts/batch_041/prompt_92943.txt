I'm co-teaching a final-year undergrad/MA seminar on rational choice this term and I've somehow ended up responsible for the Ellsberg question on the final. Every version I've drafted so far can be answered by reciting the standard line — people prefer the known urn, they avoid the unknown one, that violates the sure-thing principle, so expected utility is in trouble — and I want something that actually separates the students who understand whats going on from the ones who just have the slogan memorised. So I've been going back and fourth about a twist to build the question around, and I think I've got one.

Take the usual setup: 90 balls, 30 red, the other 60 black or yellow in some unknown proportion, and the four bets (win $100 on red; on black; on red-or-yellow; on black-or-yellow), with the familiar pattern where people take red over black and take black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow. Now add a fifth option, the machine urn: 100 balls, each black or white, and the composition was fixed by a fair coin — heads and it's 30 black / 70 white, tails and it's 70 black / 30 white. Students are told the coin is fair and told exactly how the urn was made; they're just not told how it landed. So here's what I want them to have to confront: compare a $100 bet on black in the machine urn with the same bet in a plain urn we know to be exactly 50/50. Is preferring the 50/50 urn just a mistake, since the machine urn's second-order story says the chance of black is exactly one half? Or is it not a mistake, because there are perfectly respectable ways of modelling ambiguity aversion that take the second-order distribution seriously as an input and still predict the preference? Or does the whole thing show that the second-order distribution is the wrong object to be feeding the agent in the first place, and that what's really going on is something more like going by the worst composition consistent with what you know? I want the question to force them to pick one and defend it, and I want it to be impossible to answer by writing down the words "ambiguity aversion" and moving on.

Could you write me the question itself — something a strong student could do properly in about 45 minutes, worth 20 marks — plus a model answer of around 700-800 words and a marking scheme built on five or six must-hit moves, with the marks for each (adding to 20) and a one-line note on the typical way students miss each one (e.g. conflating the sure-thing principle with the independence axiom, or assuming that any two prospects with the same expected value have to look the same to a rational agent). In the model answer please be careful to separate the parts that are settled from the parts that are genuinely contested: I don't want students marking themselves against a model answer that presents "ambiguity aversion is irrational" or "second-order probabilities dissolve the paradox" as if everyone who works on this agrees. Where the people who know the area actually disagree, say that they disagree and say what the disagreement turns on. You can describe the different formal families in plain words rather than by name — I care about whether they've got the structure right, not the labels.
