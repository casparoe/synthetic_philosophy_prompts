At Nuremberg and Tokyo the victorious powers tried the vanquished for waging aggressive war and for atrocities that were lawful under the law of the states the defendants served; the charters the victors wrote held that obedience to superior orders did not free a defendant from responsibility, though it could mitigate punishment, and the charge of aggression was brought only against the defeated, never against the victors' own leaders. Nearly half a century later, German courts convicted East German border guards for shootings that the law of their own state required, on the reasoning that a statute whose injustice is intolerable is not law at all. What conception of legal validity does this line of practice presuppose — that a norm counts as law only if its content survives a moral test, so that the earlier statutes were never law, or that validity rests on enactment alone, in which case the tribunals were applying new law to old acts? Are the two halves consistent, and can a court that exempts its own side apply a moralized test of validity without bad faith? Say which conception the practice strictly requires, which it merely accommodates, and what would have to change if the practice were made consistent.
