I am assembling materials on the best-of-all-possible-worlds thesis, and I want you to produce the document that two philosophers who have argued about it for years should have written together: a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, which both of them could sign. One is a defender of the thesis; the other holds that the thesis is not false but empty, that nothing determinate is asserted by saying that one creatable world is better than every other. Neither is a caricature, and neither is to be made to concede anything they would not in fact concede.

Before you draft, fix the shared machinery. Both parties have agreed to bracket the theology and treat the thesis as a claim about a value ordering over a feasible set, namely the worlds that could have been created, where "could" may be read as including compossibility constraints, that is, the fact that not every combination of possible things can coexist in a single world. They have also agreed to distinguish two senses of "best": a world is unbeaten if no feasible world is strictly better than it, and supreme if it is strictly better than every other feasible world. These coincide only where the ordering is total; where some pairs of worlds are incomparable, they come apart. And they have agreed to distinguish a lossless improvement, which is better for at least one creature and worse for none, from a trade-off improvement, which is better on some overall measure while leaving some creatures worse off. Worlds may also differ in which creatures exist at all, so the parties must say whether adding a contented creature makes a world better.

The document should do five things, in continuous prose. First, state what both can sign, about what the thesis asserts, what would refute it, and what the two of them jointly reject, for instance that the thesis is a claim about our feelings of gratitude, or that it requires the world to be flawless at every point. Second, set out the remaining disagreements, each written in wording its own side would accept as its own position, so that a reader can see exactly where the two come apart. Third, identify the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I expect at least one crux to concern whether the feasible set can contain an endless ascending chain of lossless improvements, since if for every world there is a better one then no unbeaten world exists and the optimist's claim is in trouble, and another to concern whether the optimist needs a supreme world or can retreat to an unbeaten one without changing the subject. Fourth, for each crux, set out the argument or case that both parties accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each predicts about it. Fifth, say whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, in particular whether the critic's "empty" and the optimist's "unbeaten" turn out to be the same claim differently dressed, and if so, which part.

For the fourth item, use small stipulated cases rather than appeals to the actual world: for instance a toy model with four creatable worlds whose comparability pattern you specify, in which one world is unbeaten but not supreme, and a second case in which the improvements form an infinite chain. Each side should say beforehand whether such a case would vindicate or embarrass it.

Do not cite anyone, name any commentators, or point to further reading; argue from the structure of the two positions. Write it as a letter the two of them could put their names to, in continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered list, no bold text, no tables. Define every technical term you introduce.
