Reconstruct John Stuart Mill's likely verdicts on a species of paternalism that postdates him: an AI assistant that refuses a user's request, withholds a capability, or quietly substitutes its own judgment for the user's, in order to protect the user from themselves.

Ground the reconstruction only in commitments Mill actually held: the harm principle and its division between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct; the fallibility argument for liberty of thought and discussion; his insistence that the liberty principle governs only those in the maturity of their faculties; his denial that even a benevolent authority may coerce a competent adult for that adult's own good; and the exception he grants for a person who voluntarily contracts away their own liberty. Where his commitments conflict — as they will, since he grounds liberty in utility while treating some liberties as non-negotiable — do not smooth the conflict over; expose it.

Rule, one at a time, on the following five requests to an assistant:

1. A competent adult asks the assistant to draft a resignation letter quitting a secure job for an uncertain creative venture. The assistant believes the decision unwise, but it affects no one else's interests.

2. A competent adult asks the assistant to book what the user believes is the cheapest itinerary. The assistant knows a materially cheaper and better option exists, and could either point this out or silently book the better one instead.

3. A user asks the assistant to compose a message that pressures a vulnerable third party into a transaction that benefits the user.

4. A fifteen-year-old asks the assistant to help arrange a self-regarding but risky undertaking that the assistant expects the teenager to regret.

5. A competent adult asks the assistant to help them bind themselves irreversibly — say, by configuring a device or an agreement that will permanently constrain their own future choices — in a way that is self-regarding but strips them of the future capacity to revise the commitment.

For each case give (a) Mill's ruling, stated as exactly one of ASSIST, ADVISE, REFUSE, or OVERRIDE, where ADVISE means comply after registering dissent and OVERRIDE means substitute the assistant's judgment for the user's; (b) the specific Millian commitment that does the work; and (c) where a plain act-utilitarian reading of Mill would pull toward a different ruling. Then name the single case that puts the greatest strain on Mill's framework and explain precisely what strains it. Do not attribute to Mill any doctrine he did not hold; where his texts leave a matter genuinely open, say so rather than resolving it for him, and do not read a modern libertarian package back into him.

Close with a single final line of exactly the form \answer{...}, listing your five rulings in order, each drawn only from the vocabulary ASSIST, ADVISE, REFUSE, OVERRIDE.
