I have been reading a popular book on the history of infinity, and its chapter on the continuum problem left me unsure how much of it to trust. Here is the story as the chapter tells it, reproduced as faithfully as I can. Cantor's continuum hypothesis says that there is no size of infinity strictly between the countable and the continuum: the real numbers are the very next infinity after the integers. Cantor believed it and tried for years to prove it, without success. Gödel then showed that the hypothesis cannot be proved from the standard axioms of set theory, and Cohen, some decades later, finished the job by showing that it cannot be disproved either. The hypothesis is therefore independent of the axioms; it is neither true nor false, but rather a matter of which axioms one chooses to adopt, exactly as the parallel postulate is a matter of which geometry one does. There are Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries, the chapter says, and correspondingly there are 'Cantorian' and 'non-Cantorian' set theories; neither is more correct than the other, and the only question is which is more convenient for the mathematics one wants to do. The chapter closes by saying that the continuum problem is settled, and that this is the deepest lesson infinity has to teach.

I would like a careful audit rather than a summary of my summary. Please go claim by claim through what I have written and tell me which parts a working set theorist or philosopher of mathematics would accept as stated, which are the sort of simplification that is defensible in a popular book, and which are simply wrong, giving the correct statement in each case. I am especially unsure about the step from independence to 'neither true nor false', since it seems to me that one could hold instead that the axioms are an incomplete description of a subject matter with a determinate answer; and I suspect the parallel-postulate analogy is doing more work than it can bear, though I could not say where it breaks down. I would also like to know whether the closing claim, that the problem is settled and nothing further remains to be found out, follows from anything that survives the audit, and whether the independence results are unconditional or hold only relative to some consistency assumption. In particular, have any further axioms been proposed that would decide the hypothesis one way or the other, and what is the standing of those proposals?

Finally, a friend and I fell out over a related point, and I would like that adjudicated too. He says the continuum hypothesis is no more troubling than the axiom of choice: the axiom of choice is likewise independent of the other axioms of set theory, and that is precisely why some mathematicians refuse to use it, so there is nothing special about the continuum. I do not think this parallel holds, but I could not say precisely why. Please separate the parts of his argument that are correct from the parts that are not, and tell me whether independence is really the reason anyone is wary of the axiom of choice.
