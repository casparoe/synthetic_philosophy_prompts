My friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about the grandfather paradox and ended up talking past each other, so I would rather have a diagnosis than a verdict.

His position is that the paradox is a reductio. If I could travel into my own past, I could kill my grandfather before he had any children; then I should never have existed and could never have travelled, so backwards time travel is impossible. The paradox is not a curiosity to be explained away; it is the proof. My position is that the argument establishes only that a certain pair of events cannot both belong to one history — my grandfather's survival and his death at my hands — and that nobody ever supposed otherwise, so nothing has been refuted. What it rules out is a contradictory combination of events, not an action.

He does not accept this. His objection, which I think is the real pressure point, is that if I am standing in 1920 with a loaded rifle and a clear line of sight, something must prevent the shot, and 'something prevents it' sounds like impossibility; but if nothing prevents it, then I can do it, and we are back in the paradox. I said that nothing in the situation prevents the shot, while also thinking that the shot cannot happen, and he reasonably wanted to know which of those I actually meant. A related case we could not settle: I try to fire and the gun jams, or I slip, or a bird crosses the line of fire. He finds the idea that the universe arranges a jam absurd; I find it unremarkable, since whatever happens is what happens.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than endorsed, is that most of our three hours was a verbal dispute about 'can': he meant it in the sense of ability and opportunity — nothing in the situation stops the act — while I meant it in the sense of compatibility with what has already happened. If that is the whole story, the paradox dissolves and we were quarrelling over a word. But I suspect a substantive residue remains, and I want to know what it is. Candidates, which you should assess rather than assume: whether affecting the past differs from changing it; whether the disagreement is really about the metaphysics of modality, a single fixed history against branching histories, and if the latter, whether branching can even be stated without a second time dimension in which branches come into being; whether it turns on the semantics of counterfactuals whose antecedents are inconsistent with the actual past — what is true of the case in which I do pull the trigger — where neither the ordinary possible-worlds treatment nor the vacuously-true treatment looks right; whether the constraint is a logical ban on contradiction or a physical constraint on solutions of a theory that permits closed timelike curves; and whether the real issue is freedom, since an act that cannot succeed is an odd candidate for a free one.

Please diagnose the disagreement: verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading — one about the logic of counterfactuals and one about the structure of spacetime — and say what each side is actually committed to. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a decisive case, or a change of question — or an argument that none should be expected, and why. I am not looking for a reading list or citations.
