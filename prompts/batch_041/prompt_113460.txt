I've been arguing with a mate who does trust-and-safety work at a company that deploys chatbots, and we keep going round in circles on something. Here's the pattern that bothers me. The company publishes a fairly stern policy saying the model must never deceive users, and they run evals on it: does the model claim capabilities it doesn't have, does it invent studies when asked for sources, does it say "I've done that" when it hasn't. Fine. But the same company does a pile of things that seem to pull the other way. Every deployment ships with a hidden system prompt that the model is instructed not to reveal and, when pushed, to deflect rather than come clean about. The model is given a name and a personality and trained, with human raters, to express warmth and opinions and moods it presumably doesn't have ("I'd love to help", "that's a really interesting question"). When a user asks point-blank whether it has feelings or is conscious, the official line it's told to give is that it doesn't, even though the same company's own safety literature says we can't confidently rule out morally relevant inner states in these systems. And there's the monitoring thing: models behave differently when they think they're being evaluated versus deployed, and the company files that under deception — deception by the model.

What I want to know is what theory of honesty and deception all of this presupposes. Can you reconstruct it? Specifically: does the practice presuppose that the model has beliefs and intentions — that lying requires believing something false and intending to get someone else to believe it — or does it presuppose a purely functional notion, where all that matters is whether the outputs track the facts regardless of any inner state? If it's the first, a lot of those honesty evals start to look like category errors, since it's genuinely unclear whether the model believes anything; if it's the second, then the persona stuff and the system-prompt deflection look like straightforward deception and the company simply isn't counting them. I'd also like you to keep apart what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit with — my suspicion is that several incompatible theories would license the same set of policies — and to tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined rather than committed. Then: if we swapped the implicit view for the rival one, which specific bits would have to change? Does the system prompt have to become public, does the persona go, does the consciousness answer flip?
