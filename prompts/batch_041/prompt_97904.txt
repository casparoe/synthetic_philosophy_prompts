So I run a small reading group in my department on decision theory, and we've somehow blagged a decision theorist onto our podcast next week as a guest — someone who's made a bit of a career out of the absent-minded driver problem. You know the setup: a driver passes two identical intersections, can't tell them apart or remember having passed the first, and the payoffs run so that stopping at the second is what he wants, stopping at the first is the worst outcome, and sailing past both is merely middling. Her line, as I understand it, is that this isn't a cute puzzle about mixed strategies but the clearest proof that our whole picture of sequential choice quietly assumes perfect recall, and that once you drop that assumption you can't even keep the idea of one agent picking a plan — rationality sort of fragments into pieces. I want questions that actually make her earn that claim, not ones that let her re-tell the example for the fifth time.

The bit I keep getting stuck on, and which I'd like the questions to circle, is the self-consistency move: the thought that the driver should pick whatever strategy maximises expected utility given the strategy, so that his later self's choice and his earlier self's choice line up. When I raise this in the group, half of us say that's just expected-utility maximisation at the outset and that the driver's real trouble is that he can't execute it — which starts to look like a reductio of the whole approach — while the other half say the self-consistency requirement is a seperate rationality constraint doing all the work with no independent justification. I also have a half-formed suspicion (have the questions treat it as a suspicion, not as something settled) that the absent-minded driver and the sleeping beauty problem are the same puzzle in different clothes, both being really about self-locating uncertainty under memory loss, and I'd love something that forces her to say whether that's right and what it implies for causal versus evidential decision theory.
