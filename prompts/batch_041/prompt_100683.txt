After a long argument with a friend last night about forgeries, I've lost confidence that my own view is coherent, and I'd rather have it audited than defend it again half-blind. Here is roughly where I stand.

(1) Aesthetic value is fully determined by the perceptible properties of the object. Two objects that an ideal observer cannot tell apart have the same aesthetic value; nothing imperceptible can make an aesthetic difference.

(2) A perfect forgery is nonetheless less aesthetically valuable than the original it copies. Even if the substitution could never be detected, something is genuinely lost when the forgery takes the original's place.

(3) What the original has and the forgery lacks is the creative achievement it embodies — that it is the product of the artist's own act of creation rather than a reproduction of a result.

(4) The artist's intentions do not fix the meaning or the value of a work; once made, the work stands on its own.

My friend insists these cannot all be true at once. I suspect he is right about at least one pair, but I cannot tell which; and he also pressed me with a case I could not answer, namely a forgery that demanded far more skill and effort than the lazy original it copied. Please tell me which of the four are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each give the cheapest revision that would restore consistency together with what that revision would cost me.
