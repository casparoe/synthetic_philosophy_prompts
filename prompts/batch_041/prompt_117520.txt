I took up philosophy of science after thirty years in an atomic physics laboratory, and for the past year I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on chance and credence, where the reference class problem will not go away. As I understand it, the difficulty is this: to assign a probability to a particular event — this patient's recovery, this coin's landing heads — one must place the individual in some class of similar cases and read a frequency off that class; but any individual belongs to indefinitely many such classes, and the frequencies need not agree, so it begins to look as though there is no fact of the matter about the single-case probability at all. What has struck me is that the participants who want to insist the problem is merely technical — that there really is a correct probability and we have simply to identify the right class — keep reaching for an analogy from physics, and I confess I have begun using it myself. The analogy runs as follows. Every physical event has a complete description, namely the full microphysical state of the system; once one has that, there is no further question as to which description is *the* description, since the coarse-grained, macroscopic accounts are merely lossy summaries of the complete one. So, the argument continues, there must likewise be a maximally specific reference class, the analogue of the complete state, and the correct single-case probability is the one determined by that class; the familiar classes — age, sex, smoking history — are just coarse-grainings, and the fact that they disagree no more threatens the objectivity of chance than the fact that the temperature of a gas is a coarse-grained summary threatens the objectivity of the molecular description.

I should like you to audit this analogy rather than defend or attack the conclusion directly. Set out the correspondence explicitly, and a small table is welcome: what in the statistical case plays the role of the complete state, of a coarse-grained description, of the coarse-graining map itself, and of the privileged status that the complete description is supposed to enjoy. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care most about — which of the breaks are idle and which actually bear on the use to which I put the analogy, namely licensing the claim that a principled, non-pragmatic choice of reference class exists. I am already suspicious of one thing and would like you to say whether it is the decisive break or whether I have misidentified it: the maximally specific class would appear to be the class containing only this individual, whose frequency is 0 or 1 and so tells us nothing, whereas a complete microphysical state is maximally informative; if that is right, the analogy may point away from the conclusion rather than toward it. Finally, tell me what a better analogy would be, if there is one. I have heard the reference class problem compared to the problem of selecting which factors are the relevant ones in a causal explanation, and to the choice of a prior, but I have no idea whether either comparison is any improvement. Do not bother with references or a reading list; I shall chase those down myself, and I want the argument rather than the scholarship. Keep the whole reply to at most 800 words, and put your verdict in the first two sentences.
