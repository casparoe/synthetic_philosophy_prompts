I run the philosophy club at my high school — about 15 of us, we get one hour after school on Tuesdays. Last week our advisor said something that's been bugging me. She said philosophers fight over whether things really have "dispositions," like sugar being soluble or a glass being fragile, or whether that's just a fancy way of saying what usually happens when you mess with them. The obvious fix is to say "this glass is fragile" just means "if you drop it, it breaks." Then she gave us two cases that seem to break it. Case one: a glass that's normal and fragile, but a wizard enchanted it so that the second you drop it, it turns to rubber and bounces — it never breaks when dropped, but you'd still swear it's fragile. Case two: a glass that really would shatter if dropped, but it's sitting in a force field that catches it every time, so it never breaks. In the first one the test itself wrecks the thing you're testing; in the second, something outside just blocks it. She called the first a "fink" and the second a "mask" and moved on, and honestly most of us nodded without feeling the difference at all, me included.

So here's what I want: design a 50-minute activity for us that makes us actually feel this problem, where we have to do something and run into it ourselves instead of just hearing about it. I want us to hit the wall where the neat if-then definition falls apart before anyone says the words fink or mask out loud. We've got a classroom, basic craft junk (paper, tape, marbles, plastic cups, a few boxes), and no budget. Tell me how it should run, what the debrief should pull out of what happened, what's definately going to go wrong the first time I try it and how to roll with it, and be honest about what the activity can't show — the part I'll just have to tell them myself. Also we're mostly 15-17 and a couple of them will try to game any rule I give, so factor that in. Write it to me like an email, normal prose, no headings or bullet points or tables, just paragraphs.
