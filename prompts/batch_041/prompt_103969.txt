A friend of mine, Mara, has lived for years with a chronic pain condition. The pain is not dangerous, but it is constant, and it has slowly reorganized her life around the few intense, absorbing pursuits she can still manage — she climbs, badly and stubbornly, and that stubbornness is more or less who she takes herself to be. She has now been offered a treatment that would end the pain. Its side effect is unusual but well documented: patients do not become flat or diminished; they are reliably reoriented. They care as strongly as before, about different things, and tend to find their old absorbing pursuits faintly puzzling — not distressing, just no longer theirs. Mara is not depressed and does not want to become less engaged with the world. She wants the pain gone, and the two cannot be pried apart.

Her brother tells her to settle it by asking which life she would rather live, the one she has now or the one after treatment, and to take the treatment if the answer is the second. Her doctor tells her the opposite: that no one standing where Mara stands can answer that question, and that she should stop treating this as a matter of weighing outcomes at all. Neither answer satisfies her, and neither satisfies me.

There is a further feature that neither of them has reckoned with. The treatment can be undone. A second course, scheduled in advance and administered a year later, restores her original outlook, so that she would never have to decide to go back while she is changed. Her brother thinks this makes the whole thing safe. I am not so sure: during that year, the changed Mara will act, and the Mara who returns will inherit whatever the changed one did.

She has asked for my advice, and I have none to give her. What should I tell her?
