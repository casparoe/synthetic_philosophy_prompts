I retired from the lab two years ago — forty years in soil chemistry, mostly — and I've been going to a small philosophy discussion group at the library to catch up on everything I skipped. Last week was on knowledge, and two of the regulars, Maya and Dev, went back and forth for an hour and I couldn't tell who was winning. Can you referee it move by move, the way I'd referee a disputed measurement? Neither of them has any formal training either, so judge the arguments as they actually came out, not as some textbook version of them.

Maya: if you know something, then it's true. So if evidence against it shows up later, that evidence is by definition pointing away from the truth, which makes it misleading evidence. And you're entitled to ignore misleading evidence. So knowing really does entitle you to wave off whatever comes along to contradict you. She said that isn't arrogance, thats just what knowing amounts to.

Dev: he said that can't be right, because then a knower would be sealed off — she could never have a good reason to give the belief up, since any reason to give it up would count as misleading. Worse, he said, take two people who believe the same true thing, one of whom knows it and one who doesn't (she got there by luck, or for a bad reason). They can be sitting on exactly the same evidence and look identical from the inside. Maya's rule tells them to do different things with the next piece of contrary evidence. So whether it's rational to ignore evidence would depend on something neither of them can see. He said that makes "rational" a word for being lucky.

Maya: she said he was confusing being rational with being able to prove you're rational, and that plenty of things we call reasonable depend on facts we can't check from the inside. She also said she never claimed a knower can't change her mind — only that until she's actually persuaded, the contrary evidence isn't a reason to lower her confidence.

Dev: he said then a "reason" is something you can only identify in hindsight, after you already know how it turned out, and a should that depends on how things turn out isn't a should at all.

So: who's right on which move, where does each of them go wrong, and is there something they're both missing — some way of stating Maya's starting point that doesn't have Dev's consequence, or a way for Dev to block the argument that isn't just shrugging and saying knowledge isn't the kind of thing that can do that work? I'd definately also like to know whether they're even using "evidence" the same way.
