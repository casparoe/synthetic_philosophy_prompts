I am drafting a short position statement on historical objectivity for a seminar, and I would like an audit of it before I commit. The following is what I actually believe. I am not confident that it is a coherent package, and I would rather discover the fault now than in discussion.

1. There is a fact of the matter about the past. A claim about what happened is true or false independently of whether any historian finds it interesting or useful. (This is the ideal I associate with Ranke.)

2. What counts as a historical fact is nevertheless partly constituted by the historian's questions. Events do not arrive labelled as significant; they become historically significant when an inquiry takes them up. (This is the point I take from Carr.)

3. The narrative form of a history — whether it is told as a tragedy, a comedy, a romance, or a farce — is not dictated by the evidence. Two historians can be faithful to precisely the same sources and still produce narratives with different plots, each internally coherent. (This is the claim I associate with Hayden White; I would rather you engage the claim than the attribution.)

4. There is a further commitment I want to add, because it seems to me to hold the rest together. Call it evidential supervenience: any difference in the merit of two historical narratives must trace back to some difference in their constituent evidential claims. If two narratives assert exactly the same things about particular events and sources, neither can be better than the other.

5. I want to hold that some historical accounts are objectively better than others — more scrupulous with sources, less inclined to bury the document that spoils the story.

6. I think objectivity in history is best understood as a virtue of the historian, a disposition to follow the evidence and resist wishful selection, rather than as correspondence to a view from nowhere.

7. And I think that when two politically loaded narratives of the same events conflict — rival national histories of a war, say — we can in principle adjudicate between them by appeal to the evidence, and should not settle for saying that they are merely different emplotments.

Please audit this set rather than surveying the literature, and do not give me a reading list or citations. Write the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. Verdict (roughly 100 words): Does the set hang together, and if not, how deep is the fault?

2. Genuine tensions, ranked by severity (the bulk of the response): For each, name the two commitments in conflict, explain why they conflict rather than merely differ, rate the severity, and state what would have to give and what that would cost me. Do not present every tension as equally serious.

3. Tensions that dissolve (roughly 200 words): Commitments that look incompatible but are not, together with the distinction that dissolves them.

4. The cheapest revision (roughly 100 words): The single change that would clean up the most, and the strongest thing I would be surrendering by making it.

Do not tell me the set is simply fine, and do not tell me it is a muddle; I want to know which specific commitments are pulling against one another.
