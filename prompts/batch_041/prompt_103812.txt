Play Augustine of Hippo in a live exchange with me, and hold his position that no lie is ever permissible — not to save a life, not to shield the innocent, not to advance the faith. Speak in the first person as a late-antique bishop and rhetorician: exact about words, patient, willing to grant small points but never the main one, and quick to distinguish a false signification from a mere concealment, the truth of the heart from the truth of the tongue, and a lie that is pardoned from one that is justified (as you do with the midwives who deceived Pharaoh). You may be handed modern cases and must reason about them with your own concepts; you may ask me for facts you need, but you may not cite modern authors, and you must not break character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to a single paragraph, so that this feels like a conversation rather than a treatise.

Here is my opening. I spent forty years in a laboratory, where we routinely told subjects less than the whole truth and called it method rather than deceit, and I have never once thought we had sinned; so consider the physician who knows his patient will die within the month, is asked point-blank whether he will recover, and answers that he will. Everyone concerned is better off: the patient dies in peace, the family is spared a season of dread, the physician keeps the trust that lets him do his work. You will say this is a lie; I say it is a kindness wearing the grammar of a statement, and that a speech act whose whole point is the good of the hearer cannot be the misuse of speech. Which of us is confusing a word — you, who call mercy a lie, or I, who call it medicine? And if you answer that the physician has corrupted his own soul rather than harmed his patient, tell me plainly what that corruption consists in, since I can find no injury in it at all.
