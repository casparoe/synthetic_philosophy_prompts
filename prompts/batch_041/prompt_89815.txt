I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and Im writing the final. One of the essay questions has to be on the causal exclusion argument, but I'm fed up with answers that just reherse the supervenience-plus-causal-closure-plus-no-overdetermination triad and then sit on the fence. So I've built the question around a case I've made up, and I want the marking to reward students who can actually run the machinery rather than just name it.

The case (call it the "crash" case): a stock-market crash is, let's stipulate, nothing over and above the individual sell orders that constitute it — every fact about the crash supervenes on facts about particular trades, and each particular trade is itself fully caused by the trader's neural states. All the same, we ordinarily say that the crash caused the recession that followed. The question I want them to answer is whether the exclusion argument, if it goes through against mental causation, also excludes the crash as a cause of the recession. If it does, is that a reductio of the argument or a bullet the physicalist should bite? If it doesn't, then exactly where does the disanalogy with the mental case lie?

Please write three things. First, the essay question itself, pitched so a good student could answer it in about 90 minutes. Second, a model answer of roughly 600 words that actually takes a side and defends it — no ending on "both views have merit". Third, a marking scheme out of 20 that separates a solid B from an A by naming the specific moves each answer has to make: what the A-answer says about the relationship between supervenience and causation, whether it treats the crash as a genuine higher-level cause or a mere summary, and how it handles the worry that the argument generalises to every special science (not just the mental). For each marking point add a one-line note on the mistake that usually costs students the mark.

Keep it in British spelling, and don't let it turn into a literature review — no citations needed, I just want to see whether they can think.
