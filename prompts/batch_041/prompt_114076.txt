Although I work as a software engineer and have never studied philosophy or constitutional law, I have ended up in a long argument with a colleague about secession, and I would like a careful comparison rather than a verdict by intuition. The two of us seem to be operating with two different pictures of what grounds a right to secede, and I cannot tell whether they are rivals or whether one is simply a special case of the other. On the first picture, which I will call the remedial one, a group may leave the larger state only when it has suffered serious and persistent injustice there — its language suppressed, its votes reliably outvoted, its autonomy promises broken — and secession counts as a last remedy, available only after internal self-government has been tried and has failed. On the second picture, the primary one, a group that constitutes a distinct nation with a settled and durable desire to govern itself may leave simply because it so chooses, without first having to prove that anyone wronged it. Please compare these two pictures directly: what each one says about the central cases of the other, what each one has to concede, and in what respects the remedial picture is special — that is, whether it is really a narrower version of the primary one or a genuinely different kind of claim. Please be concrete, since the debates I follow in the news about Scotland, Catalonia and Quebec tend to slide between the two without noticing.

The reason I want the comparison done properly is a problem my colleague and I could not resolve, and which, as someone who spends his days on recursive data structures, I suspect is a recursion with no base case. Both pictures quietly assume that the group which secedes is the same as the group which decides, and that its territory is simply given. But suppose a region votes to leave, and inside it a sub-region with its own distinct identity and language votes either to stay with the larger state or to leave the region in turn. The remedial picture now has to say who the wronged group actually is, and why the sub-region's grievance, if it has one, does not count equally; the primary picture now has to decide whether the primary right it grants to the region is also held by the sub-region — and if it is, secession fragments without limit, while if it is not, the larger state's refusal to let the region go seems to have been justified all along by exactly the same reasoning. I would like you to run both pictures through this nested case, say precisely what each has to give up in order to handle it, and tell me which of the two survives the case better and why. If the honest answer is that neither handles it without an arbitrary stopping rule, say so, and describe the least arbitrary stopping rule you can find.
