I retired two years ago after a career in experimental chemistry, and I have spent much of my time since then trying to acquire the philosophical education I never got. I have just finished a popular book on moral psychology, and one passage in it has been nagging at me. The author, summarizing what he presents as the settled verdict of his field, writes:

"For most of Western history, ethics rested on a single assumption: that people have characters — stable dispositions to behave well or badly that they carry with them from one situation to the next. Psychology has now put that assumption to rest. Study after study has found that the best predictor of whether a person helps a stranger, cheats on a test, or defies an authority is not what kind of person he is but the situation he happens to be in: whether he is in a hurry, whether he has just found a dime in a pay phone, whether anyone else is watching. In the famous prison experiment, ordinary students were turned into sadistic guards by nothing more than a uniform and a role. The ancient ideal of training oneself to be brave, honest, and just, so that one acts that way no matter what, turns out to be a fiction. Character traits, where they exist at all, are so local and situation-bound that they cannot bear the weight virtue ethics places on them. Some philosophers have tried to save the theory by saying that a virtue is really a skill at reading situations; but a skill that works only in the situation it was learned in is not what the tradition ever meant by a virtue. Since the whole theory is built on the existence of these traits, the empirical evidence leaves it without a foundation."

My instinct, after a lifetime around data, is that if the experiments are as robust as he says, anyone doing ethics has to reckon with them. But the passage never quite tells me what has been refuted, and I have learned enough to be suspicious when a conclusion about how a theory stands is presented as the direct consequence of a body of experiments. What I would like is an audit, not a lecture. Specifically:

1. Go through the passage claim by claim. For each, tell me which parts a philosopher working on virtue ethics would accept as fair, which are the ordinary simplifications a competent popularizer is entitled to, which are misrepresentations of what virtue ethics actually holds, and which empirical assertions are doing the real argumentative work rather than adding color.

2. Mark every point at which the author slides from a claim about how people behave to a claim about what there is, or from a description of behavior to a verdict on a normative theory, and say how much weight each slide has to carry.

3. Tell me what a defender of virtue ethics would have to concede, what she could reasonably deny, and whether any of her denials would be ad hoc.

4. Most important: does the closing inference — that virtue ethics is left without a foundation — follow from the parts of the passage that survive scrutiny, or does it require a premise the author never states? If the latter, name the premise and say whether it is true.

I do not need citations or a reading list; I am reading for my own edification and would rather have the argument than the references. Please write your answer in American English.
