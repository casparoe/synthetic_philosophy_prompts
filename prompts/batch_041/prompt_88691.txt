I've somehow been talked into giving the welcome address at my town's naturalization ceremony next month. I got sworn in myself eight years ago, so the mayor's office decided I was the obvious pick, and I said yes before I thought it through.

Here's my problem. I don't think becoming a citizen of a particular country creates any special moral obligations to the other people who happen to live there. I think you owe a stranger in another country basically the same things you owe the person next door, and a passport doesn't change that. But the whole ceremony is built on the opposite idea. The mayor's office even sent me a draft of talking points and one of the lines is roughly "from today, your first loyalty is to your fellow citizens."

The audience is going to be about forty people taking the oath, from fifteen or so different countries; a bunch of their relatives, including little kids and a couple of grandparents who speak almost no English; three local officials who'll be expecting the patriotic version; and my mom, who thinks borders are a scam and will be in the front row giving me a look the entire time.

Can you write me the speech? Five minutes, spoken, no philosophy jargon, nothing that needs a footnote. I don't want to lie about what I think, and I don't want to lecture forty people on the most emotionally loaded day of their year, either. It should actually take a position instead of saying "both views have merit." And it has to land for the kids and the grandparents, not just the officials. There's also a local tradition where, as each person gets their certificate, the speaker says one short line to them individually — so give me a few options for that too, something that doesn't contradict the rest.

If there's a way to honor what they're doing without pretending a border makes a moral difference, find it. Also, keep it casual — I'm not a professor, I'm a dental hygienist, and if it sounds like a term paper they'll know I didn't write it.
