I serve on the board of trustees of a mid-sized family foundation that is midway through its five-year strategic review, and I need to circulate my written recommendation before the March meeting. The decision looks narrow on paper and has turned out to be unexpectedly hard.

Our charter instructs us to "improve the lives of as many people as we can reach," and until this cycle nobody had pressed on what that means when the number of people we reach and the quality of the lives we reach pull in opposite directions. We are choosing between two five-year strategies in the same region, at roughly the same cost:

Strategy A scales up child-survival work — vaccinations, oral rehydration, malaria nets. Our modelling suggests it would save on the order of several thousand lives over five years; those children would grow into adults whose lives are, by any measure we would apply to ourselves, worth living, though in conditions that remain materially poor and constrained.

Strategy B redirects the same money to girls' education and family planning. It leaves the population smaller than A would, but the people who exist are healthier, better educated, and more likely to live lives of the kind we would want for our own children.

The program staff have put working numbers in front of us: Strategy A yields more welfare if you simply add up lives across the region; Strategy B yields a higher average. Two trustees have dug in on opposite sides. One says the children under A are obvious beneficiaries, so declining A is choosing to let them die for the sake of a tidier average. The other says you cannot add lives up like that — that a life of health, agency, and education and a life of bare survival are not the same currency, and that a policy which maximizes the sum while lowering everyone's prospects has lost the plot. The finance chair has floated a 60/40 split as a compromise, and I genuinely do not know whether that is a real option or a way of not deciding.

I should flag a personal stake, since it is why I am writing this rather than abstaining: I grew up in this region, and I am alive because of a program very like Strategy A. In the terms of this argument, I am one of the "extra" people.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend one course of action concretely, with a time frame and any conditions or safeguards, and it should say plainly what to do about the split proposal. It should engage the population question head-on rather than hiding behind cost-effectiveness tables, and it should give the strongest version of the case against my recommendation rather than a straw man. Where the two sides are trading on different senses of "better," say so explicitly, and be honest about which parts of the comparison we simply cannot know in advance.
