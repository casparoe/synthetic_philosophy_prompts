I have been reconstructing, for a seminar on early modern philosophy of mind, the reasoning by which Descartes concludes that animals are mere machines, and the reconstruction now troubles me, because I cannot see how to stop it from proving far more than Descartes would want. Here is the argument as I have set it out.

(1) Speech — the flexible use of words or other signs to give expression to one's thoughts — is the only sure sign of thought.
(2) No non-human animal possesses speech in this sense; the sounds of a parrot and the gestures of an ape are produced by mechanism, not by thought.
(3) Therefore animals afford us no evidence of thought.
(4) A being that affords no evidence of thought, and whose behaviour can be explained wholly as a machine, is to be treated as a machine.
(5) The behaviour of animals can be explained wholly as a machine.
(6) Therefore animals are machines.
(7) But each of these steps bears on other human beings no less than on animals. Of another person's thought I have no direct awareness; what I have is his speech.
(8) Speech is a sign of thought, not thought itself; another person's speech is therefore, in the relevant respect, outward behaviour — no different in kind from a dog's bark.
(9) Therefore, by the same reasoning that makes animals machines, I have no evidence of thought in any human being but myself.
(10) So Descartes's case for animal automatism, if it is sound, delivers solipsism about other minds — a conclusion Descartes would never accept.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a summary. Which step, if any, is the defect: an equivocation between two senses of a term, a premise that must be surrendered, or a step that is valid only under a reading the earlier premises do not license? Explain why the offending step looked innocent, and then price the repair — what Descartes must give up, in his account of animals and in his account of how we know other minds, to escape the conclusion. I would prefer, if it can be had, to keep premise (1). Answer in at most 400 words.
