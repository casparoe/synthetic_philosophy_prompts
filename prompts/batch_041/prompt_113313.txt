I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether an individual act can be wrong when it makes no difference to the harm, and I came away with a set of verdicts I cannot reduce to anything tidy. His position is clean: if your act changes nothing about who gets harmed, there is nothing to criticize, and the guilt people carry about meat, flying, and voting is confused. He keeps calling it the inefficacy problem, as though the label were the argument. I don't believe him, but I can't say precisely where the mistake lies. Here is what I actually think, case by case.

1. My vote in a state that will be called 70 to 30 for the candidate I oppose, no matter what. Staying home: not wrong. Voting: not wrong. No obligation either way.

2. My vote in a state polling at a dead heat and likely to be decided by a few hundred ballots. Staying home: wrong.

3. A supermarket chicken. The store's order for the week is fixed, its future orders are set by a formula no single customer can move, unsold birds are thrown out, and no individual purchase, mine included, changes how many chickens are killed. Buying it still strikes me as somewhat wrong — a real twinge, though nothing like the wrongness of wringing a chicken's neck myself.

4. A chicken a friend has already cooked for a dinner party and will otherwise discard. Eating it strikes me as entirely fine.

5. Flying across the country for the funeral of a close friend. The plane is half empty and would fly regardless; my seat adds a little fuel burn. Not wrong at all, and I feel no twinge.

6. Driving around for an hour on a Sunday for the sheer pleasure of it. It makes no measurable difference either. Also no twinge — though I notice I cannot say what separates this from the chicken in (3).

7. A river that is poisoned once a thousand barrels of waste go into it. Twelve hundred of us each dump a single barrel the same afternoon, so the river would be just as poisoned if any one of us had stayed home, mine included. I think every one of us acts wrongly, and I do not think the ones who happened to fall near the threshold are any worse than the rest.

The best principle I can state — an act is wrong when it belongs to a class of acts whose combined effect is seriously harmful and the act is not backed by a weighty enough reason, even if the act on its own changes nothing — condemns the Sunday drive in (6), which I do not accept, and seems to leave (1) and (7) pointing in opposite directions. I would like the principle, or a small set of candidates, that actually fits these verdicts, with the case that forces each clause. Please also tell me what it predicts for three cases I have not listed: a vote in a race close enough that my side might lose by a handful, though my own ballot will not be decisive; buying a used coat from a shop that also sells new fur, where my purchase touches only the used rack; and a case where the river needs a thousand barrels, exactly nine hundred ninety-nine others have already dumped, and I am the one who tips it. Finally, if no single principle can hold all seven verdicts, tell me which one I should give up, and why that one rather than another.
