I teach a 200-level undergrad seminar on decision theory (18 students, one 50-minute block) and next week's session is the St. Petersburg paradox. The problem is that they've all seen the Wikipedia version, so if I put the lottery on the board and ask what they'd pay for a ticket, I'll get "the expected value is infinite" in the first thirty seconds, someone will mutter "but I'm risk averse," and we'll be done in five minutes having felt absolutely nothing. I want the reverse: I want them to commit to numbers in public, with something riding on it, and then have the ground move under them. What I want them to walk out seeing is that the paradox isn't really a puzzle about what the ticket is "actually" worth — it's a collision between two things they'd have sworn were platitudes, namely that a gamble is worth what a market will pay for it, and that the value of a compound gamble is fixed by the values of its branches, and that they can't keep both. I don't want to say that sentence to them. I want them to arrive at it and then look uncomfortable.

Here's the shape I've been sketching, and I'd like you to build it into something I can actually run. Act one: everybody gets an index card and writes two numbers privately — the lowest price at which they'd sell their ticket and the highest price at which they'd buy a second one — and then we run a market on the whiteboard, post a price, count hands, adjust, repeat until it clears. My guess is it clears in the single digits, but don't rig it; if it clears high that's interesting too. Then I play the casino, with a finite and publicly posted bankroll (I'll write an actual number up there — the department's photocopy budget, or the contents of a jar of coins I bring in), and I offer to buy tickets at the clearing price. Act two is the part I care about most: I want the class themselves, at the board, to work out that the lottery is just a 50/50 between two dollars and a doubled copy of itself, hence that its value V has to satisfy V = 1 + V, and then I want the market price still sitting next to it, five or six dollars, both of them things the room endorsed twenty minutes apart. Then a capstone: have them work out how big a bankroll a casino would need before a fifty-dollar ticket is even fair, so they can feel how much of the whole thing rests on an infinitely deep vault — I think it comes out around a quadrillion dollars, which is a nice number to have on a whiteboard. And at some point I want one of them to actually sit in the casino chair with the jar of coins and run the game for a few minutes while everyone watches the payoffs come in at two, four, eight and basically nothing else, ever.

So: a materials list, the exact rules I read out, a minute-by-minute timeline for the fifty minutes with a realistic amount of slack in it, a payoff scheme that doesn't require real money (tokens, points, a shared snack pool, whatever — but it has to feel like they're risking something), and a debrief that maps each stage onto the pieces of the problem rather than just summarizing the paradox at them. Also tell me what will definately go wrong the first time and how to handle it — I'm already worried about the kid who posts a deliberately absurd price to be funny and wrecks the market, and about the whole thing collapsing into "well obviously no casino would offer this," which is true and boring. And be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce: with eighteen people and a coin I'm never going to see a payoff above about sixty-four dollars, so the tail that drives the entire problem stays theoretical no matter how well the game goes, and I suspect the one genuinely interesting formal move — replacing money with a utility that grows like the log of money so the value comes out small and finite — has to be told to them rather than felt. Tell me which bits I just have to supply from the front, and how to do it without deflating everything they just did.
