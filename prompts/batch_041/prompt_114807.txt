I am a novelist, and I have spent the last several years writing a book set in the East India Company's London office in the 1850s. My protagonist is a fictional junior clerk in the Examiner's department, the office that drafted the despatches sent to India; he copies official correspondence by day and reads On Liberty by night, and the novel is, among other things, about what it does to a person to serve two masters like that. The historical society that runs the annual dinner for people interested in the Company's history has asked me to give the after-dinner speech at their conference this year. The room will be split down the middle: half are historians of empire and Mill scholars who know the India House material in detail, and half are general readers who know Mill only as the author of On Liberty and perhaps Utilitarianism. I want a real speech, about eight minutes, warm and slightly wry, with a serious core—not a book pitch, and not a lecture.

The speech should turn on the tension between Mill's liberalism and his career administering colonial rule. I want it to engage the fact that the harm principle is bounded: it governs the exercise of power over members of what Mill called a civilized community, and his utilitarian framework left room for despotism over peoples he considered backward, at least as a temporary instrument of their improvement. I do not want the speech to resolve that tension by calling him a hypocrite or by excusing him as a man of his time. I want the audience to feel the contradiction in their bones. So build the speech around one concrete thing—a fictional file of despatches, the physical ledgers of the office, a scene from the novel in which the clerk reads a draft that authorizes coercion—and let the argument emerge from that thing rather than from a list of points. The philosophical idea should shape what can and cannot be said: the speech cannot simply celebrate Mill's liberalism without the imperial shadow, and it cannot simply condemn him without taking seriously the theoretical structure that made his position coherent to him.

Do not put any direct quotations from Mill or from historical documents into the speech; paraphrase his positions in your own words, and if you need a concrete example, use a scene from the novel and make clear that it is fiction. No citations, no reading lists, no bibliographies. The speech should be respectful of the Indian members of the audience, some of whom may be descendants of people subject to the policies Mill helped draft, without turning them into props or the evening into a tribunal. It should land for the experts without losing the general readers. End with a question or an image that leaves the audience unsettled rather than a tidy moral. Please write the speech in my voice, first person, as the novelist.
