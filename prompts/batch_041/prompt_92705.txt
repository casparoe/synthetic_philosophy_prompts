My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about which of the patterns we casually invoke are actually in the world and which are conveniences of description, and I came away with a list of verdicts that I cannot get to line up. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they express.

We were using the vocabulary of the three stances — physical, design, intentional — as a way of describing what we do when we predict a system, and the quarrel was over when a stance is licensed rather than merely handy. Here is where I ended up.

A hurricane is a real thing and not a convenient fiction. It has edges, it does work, and its persistence is not an artifact of how I describe it.

A chess engine is predicted best by treating it as wanting to control the center and dreading a weak kingside; reasoning about its search from the design stance predicts its moves worse. All the same, I will not say the engine wants anything. The intentional description is a shortcut, not a fact about the engine.

A bacterium swimming up a sugar gradient: I am content to say it wants the sugar, though I would not say it represents the sugar.

An ant colony is a real agent with real interests. No ant represents the colony's foraging policy and no ant is in charge, yet the colony is the thing that lives or dies, and the intentional stance beats the physical one for predicting it.

When someone says the market expects a rate hike, I think that is true, and that it is the best available predictor of the next move. When the same person says the market is afraid, I think that is a manner of speaking with no predictive content of its own.

A species is real, even though its boundaries are drawn by us and dissolve under a different species concept. National character is not real, even though the same statistics will cheerfully report it.

A cell type turned up by a clustering algorithm: the cluster boundary shifts when I re-cluster by a different method, yet the cells inside it go on behaving differently from their neighbors under every assay I run. I call that a real kind.

An output gap — any quantity defined as the difference between what happened and what a model says would have happened without shocks — I call an artifact of the measuring rod rather than a feature of the economy.

A feature learned by a neural network that no one can name, which keeps doing the same work when it is transplanted into a new task: real, and I am willing to call it a representation.

The agreeable, hedging voice a language model adopts under a given system prompt: not a persona, just the prompt's shadow, even though I can predict the model's outputs better by describing it that way.

Two pairs here trouble me. The colony and the market both lack a controller, and I want the colony to be an agent and the market not to be one. The transplanted feature and the prompted persona are both learned and both stable enough to predict with, and I want one to be a representation and the other to be nothing.

My friend's proposal, which he credits to a philosopher he is fond of, is that a pattern is real just to the extent that describing the system in those terms compresses it — buying more predictive power than the description costs — and that the stance which compresses best is the one that is true of the system. Taken seriously, that seems to hand the chess engine its wants and to erase my colony-market distinction, since both descriptions compress and neither has a controller, and I am not willing to pay either price. My own hunch is that a pattern is real when it survives intervention, when I can push on the system in the ways the pattern itself specifies and get back what it promised, but I cannot tell whether that is a principle or just a restatement of the cases I already like.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, sorts these verdicts? State it precisely enough that I can apply it myself, identify the case that forces each clause, and be honest about whether it saves every verdict or has to overturn one; if it needs epicycles to save all of them, give me the simpler version and name what it costs me. Then apply it to cases I have not raised, including at least one where I would not have guessed the answer. And settle the one I keep flip-flopping on: whether a regime type — democracy, hybrid, autocracy — is a real kind, given that it compresses and predicts, that its boundaries are contested, and that the labels get redefined every few decades. I do not need the literature; I need the principle.
