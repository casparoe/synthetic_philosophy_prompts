I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough that I keep a private ledger of my gut verdicts on thought experiments, and the page on collective minds has finally filled up. What I want from you is the principle, if there is one, that these verdicts add up to — or, if they do not add up, an honest account of which one has to go. The cases, in the order I wrote them down:

1. A single worker ant, alone, away from the nest: no mind. It is a small chemical automaton and I feel no pull towards saying otherwise.
2. A mature leafcutter colony, a few million strong, with fungus gardens, waste heaps, castes and trunk trails: yes, a mind. Aunt Hillary, as I first met her in Hofstadter. She gets hungry, she chooses between foraging sites, she can be startled, and she holds a route in memory for weeks. Something there is representing the world and acting on the representation, and I do not think I am being fanciful.
3. My own brain, which is after all a colony of a hundred billion cells: yes, obviously — and I take this to show that there is nothing absurd in (2). The mind is not the cell; it is the organisation.
4. The same colony with the queen removed and replaced by a device that reads the pheromone field and dispenses exactly what she would have dispensed: still yes. Nothing has winked out; the organisation is intact.
5. Now replace the workers one at a time with silicon chips that behave identically. I say Aunt Hillary survives, at least until very far in. I cannot say when she would stop, and I am suspicious of any answer that names a sharp threshold.
6. A murmuration of starlings, or a school of fish: no. Beautiful, but there is no one home; the birds track their neighbours, and nothing represents anything to anything.
7. A slime mould finding the shortest path through a maze: no, though it unsettles me, because it looks closer to deciding than the flock does.
8. A termite colony: I want to say a thin, dim version of yes, but I would be embarrassed to defend the difference from (6).
9. A corporation: no. It is a legal fiction; every decision it "makes" is some human's decision or a vote among humans, and no new level of representation appears. I am uneasy here, because (2) and (9) look structurally alike — both are collectives whose members exchange signals.
10. A market, or a city: no, for the same reasons as (9).
11. Two colonies of the same species joined by a bridge, so that they share trails and food: my gut says one mind, not two — but I cannot say why, if the bridge is the only difference.
12. The whole colony simulated, ant for ant, on a computer: I want to say yes, the same mind; but I notice that this is doing a great deal of work, and that I cannot tell whether it follows from (3)–(5) or collides with (6).

My tentative principle, which I do not trust, is this: a system has a mind when its global state both represents the world and causes the behaviour of its own parts, and when that state cannot be decomposed into the states of the parts without loss. I offer it mainly so that you can break it. Test it against the cases above; say what each clause is really buying, and whether the clause that saves (2) and (9) together is the same clause that saves (6) and (7) together. Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I have not listed — a colony split in half and later re-merged, and a colony of humans drilled into the sort of rigid hierarchy an army has — and, finally, which of my twelve verdicts the resulting account cannot accommodate, so that I know what I would have to give up.

Please give me the principle, or a small set of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself. I care less about a verdict for every case than about knowing where the real joint is: if my ledger is incoherent, I would rather find out which entries are doing the damage than paper over them with a distinction I cannot state.
