I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in a city that maintains an Office of Prodigies, a small bureau whose clerk must decide which reported wonders are to be entered into the public register and which are to be dismissed as fraud, error, or merely natural. The register has two columns: wonder, for an event for which no natural cause is known, and sign, for an event taken to carry a divine message. The clerk works alone, has at most a day for each report, and must defend every entry to a bishop who signs the register and who has political as well as theological interests. I have written several scenes in which the clerk must rule, and I cannot make them consistent without a procedure.

Please write me a decision procedure the clerk can follow, grounded in the classical philosophical argument that a miracle is a violation of a law of nature and that testimony for such a violation faces a special burden of proof. I do not want a summary of that argument; I want it turned into something a clerk with no philosophical training can run on a report. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a refusal to certify, and where it must hand the decision to the bishop because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must say how to treat reports that depend on a single source, how to treat a naturalistic explanation that arrives after certification, how to treat two rival reports that cannot both be true, and how to treat a report whose certification would have consequences for a political or doctrinal dispute. It should distinguish between an event's occurrence and its significance, so that a naturalistic explanation may remove the wonder without removing the sign, and it should say what the clerk is to do when the evidence is genuinely mixed. It should also be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as tests.

Then run the procedure on two reports. First: a statue of a local saint is said to have wept during a drought; hundreds saw moisture on the statue, and the report has already been entered as a wonder. A naturalist later shows that a certain insect, common on old stone, secretes a fluid that can look like tears, and the statue is infested. Second: a child's fever breaks after a relic is applied; the witnesses are the mother, a neighbor who is the mother's brother, and a physician who arrived after the fever broke and who is known to disbelieve in relics; the priest who applied the relic has been promised a donation if the child recovers. In each case, tell me what the clerk should enter, what the bishop should decide, and what the clerk should write in the margin. I expect the procedure to give me an answer I will not like in at least one of these cases; do not soften it to protect the plot. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and where I should stop asking it to decide and instead let the characters disagree.
