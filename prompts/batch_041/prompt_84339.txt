I'm a backend engineer with zero philosophy background, and through a series of bad decisions I'm now on the board of the community mesh network co-op in my neighborhood -- we run wifi relays off rooftops, about 200 households in range. Special meeting Thursday to adopt new bylaws, and we've hit a wall I did not see coming: nobody can agree on who gets to vote.

Here's the mess. Our current rule is just "members vote," and membership means anyone who shows up and pays $20 a year. That was fine when it was 12 of us with drills and a ladder. Now we've got: ~140 paying households; a bunch of people using the free open SSID in the two public housing blocks, some of whom use it as their only internet and have never signed anything; a retired guy who's hosted the main uplink relay on his roof for six years and pays nothing; two landlords who host relays and want a say in the rooftop equipment rules plus the right to hand membership to whoever rents the unit; some people inside the coverage footprint who aren't connected at all; and the city broadband office, which gave us a grant and now thinks it deserves a seat.

Whichever rule we pick Thursday decides who's in the room for the bylaws vote, so the rule about who decides is itself getting decided by whoever we let in. That's the part that's definately frying my brain. Someone on the mailing list said this is a whole thing in political philosophy and sent a link I didn't read, but I need something I can actually bring to the meeting.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate Wednesday night. It should recommend one concrete membership rule, say exactly who votes on adopting that rule and in what order (my instinct is we need a two-step thing, but you tell me), and lay out the reasoning in a form my neighbors can push back on. Most of the board leans toward paying households only -- it's clean, it's roughly what our grant paperwork implies, and they'll argue that anyone who wants a vote can pay twenty bucks. Take that position seriously instead of waving it off, but if you think it's wrong, say so plainly and tell me what to do instead.

Two things I care about. Don't give me a reading list or cite papers -- half of us won't read them and I can't check them anyway. And tell me how confident you actually are in the recommendation, and name the single point in your argument you're least sure about. I'd rather know where the soft spot is than get a memo that sounds equally certain about everything.
