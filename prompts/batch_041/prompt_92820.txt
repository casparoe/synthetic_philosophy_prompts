I subscribe to a geopolitics newletter that's been running a long argument over the last few issues and I honestly can't tell how much of it is real. I'd like you to audit it for me.

The gist, as fairly as I can put it: there's a thing called the outside view, which the newsletter credits to Kahneman and the planning-fallacy work, and it says that when you're forecasting something you should stop reasoning from the details of your own case and instead ask how the class of similar past cases turned out. Apply it to empires and the move is: ignore the particulars of American politics, ask how long empires last, and the answer comes out to roughly 250 years from founding to collapse. The US dates itself to 1776, so we're due about now. The newsletter draws two morals. First, when the inside view and the outside view disagree, the outside view wins, because it's anchored in data instead of in vibes. Second, that's exactly what makes it objective: a base rate is just what happened, whereas the inside view is the forecaster's story. It closes with the thought that once you see it this way, the fall of empires stops looking like something anyone decides and starts looking like a lifecycle, a structural inevitability.

I'm inclined to buy the general shape of it — I do think forecasters are overconfident about their own cases — but "we're due" makes me uneasy and I can't articulate why. So: go through it and tell me which parts someone who actually works on forecasting or on historical base rates would accept, which are the popularizer's simplifications, and whether anything here is just false. Is the thing he's calling the outside view the same thing as a base rate, or is he running two ideas together? And does the inevitability conclusion follow from whatever survives the audit? If the honest version of the argument is weaker than his version, tell me how much weaker — and what it would look like to use the outside view properly on this question instead.
