For the past term I have been sitting in on a reading group on the metaphysics of mind, and one member — a doctoral student finishing a chapter on representational theories of consciousness — has a move he makes whenever the conversation drifts toward the felt character of experience. Consciousness, he says, is a user illusion: the brain runs an interface, and that interface is the mind. The comparison he draws is with a desktop computer. What you see when you look at the screen — windows, folders, a cursor, a trash can — is a simplified, purpose-built representation of an underlying process that is nothing like it: registers, address spaces, magnetic domains. Nobody who has looked at the machinery mistakes a folder for a place where papers actually live, and asking what a folder is made of, or whether the trash can is really empty, is a category mistake rather than a hard research question. So too, he says, with the self, the stream of consciousness, the redness of red: these are features of the brain's interface with itself, and the questions philosophers call the hard problem — what it is like to be this system, whether the interface is like anything for anyone, whether the machinery could have run the same interface with nobody home — are the trash-can question all over again. The phrase, I gather, is not his invention: it comes from interface design at Xerox PARC and was picked up for consciousness by Tor Nørretranders and, more influentially, by Daniel Dennett. My question is whether the analogy can bear the weight he puts on it.

I would like the audit done properly rather than gestured at. First, the correspondence table: what in the computer case plays the role of the brain, the interface, the icons and folders, the user, the designer, the code, the act of clicking and dragging, and the fact that the desktop is useful to someone; and what in the consciousness case each of those is supposed to be. Second, where the mapping breaks — and this is the part I care about — separating the breaks that matter for the specific conclusion he wants (that the hard problem is confused, that there is no further fact of the matter about experience beyond what the interface reports) from the mere friction we can wave through. Third, whether that conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy has been carrying it all along; if the latter, say exactly which feature of the source is doing the work. Finally, if the comparison is a poor instrument for this purpose, what would be a better one — something in the same family that keeps whatever is genuinely useful in the intuition without importing what the desktop imports. I have a suspicion that the comparison does its work backwards and presupposes the very thing at issue, but I do not trust the suspicion and cannot say where it goes wrong. No reading list, please — I have the books; I want the argument.
