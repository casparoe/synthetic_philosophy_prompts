I just switched into an MA in philosophy after a law degree and got thrown into a seminar on expressivism, which means I'm presenting on the Frege-Geach problem next week and I'm panicking a bit. I think I've got the basic shape of it: "lying is wrong" has to mean the same thing when I assert it as when it sits unasserted in the antecedent of "if lying is wrong, then telling my little brother to lie is wrong" — otherwise the inference from that conditional plus "lying is wrong" to "telling my little brother to lie is wrong" is just an equivocation and not a valid argument. And the expressivist who says the meaning of "lying is wrong" is the expression of a non-cognitive attitude toward lying can't obviously say that about the antecedent, since nobody in that sentence is actually expressing disapproval of lying, they're only supposing it. Fine, I definately get the sketch.

What I can't get a grip on is what the actual conceptual issue is once you go past the sketch. My supervisor keeps saying this is really two different problems that people run together — one about how a word can keep its meaning in a context where the attitude isn't expressed, and one about why certain inferences are valid or certain pairs of sentences inconsistent even though nothing in them is truth-apt, which is supposedly a problem about the logic of attitudes rather than about compositionality — and that the second one shows up much more sharply with negation than with conditionals. So: if "lying is wrong" expresses disapproval of lying, what exactly does "lying is not wrong" express? It can't just be the absence of disapproval, because someone who simply doesn't disapprove of lying isn't thereby disagreeing with me; and if it's meant to be some higher-order attitude (disapproval of the approval of lying, or whatever), then it's no longer clear that my "lying is wrong" and their "lying is not wrong" actually contradict each other — they just have different second-order tastes. Could you explain, as concretely as you can, what the expressivist actually has to show here, which of these two problems is the real one, and whether the standard fixes — higher-order attitudes, saying "wrong" picks out a property after all, etc. — solve the thing or just relocate it? I'd especially like to understand why negation is supposed to be harder than the conditional case, because on my reading they look like the same move.
