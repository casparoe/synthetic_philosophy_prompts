I teach a final-year undergraduate option on toleration and free expression, and I'm writing the exam. I want one essay question on the paradox of tolerance that can't be answered by reciting Popper's line about unlimited tolerance destroying itself — most of my students can do that in a paragraph and then they stall.

Here's the angle I want. A colleague of mine has been arguing in seminars that the whole thing only looks like a paradox because people run together three seperate things: (a) tolerance as an absolute principle, in which case it really is self-undermining and the paradox is genuine; (b) tolerance as a defeasible policy adopted for reasons — peace, truth-tracking, autonomy — in which case there's no paradox at all, just the ordinary limit that every defeasible commitment has; and (c) tolerance split by audience, where the citizen is required to tolerate the intolerant as people and as opinion-holders, while the state isn't required to extend them the same institutional protections, since protection is a policy whereas persons are the object of a duty. She says (c) dissolves what's left of the problem. I think (c) either does no work or just relabels (b), but I dont want to bake my answer into the question. She hasn't published any of this, so don't go hunting for a source — treat it as a proposal the students have to assess.

So: write the essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme. The question should force a position on whether the paradox is a genuine paradox, a limit case, or a piece of rhetoric, and make them say what would have to be true for each of those readings to be right. It should also make them notice that the paradox is usually stated about opinions and their expression, whereas the case doing most of the rhetorical work — a party that wins power and then abolishes the contest — is a case about institutions and actions, where ordinary constitutional self-defence may do all the work and the paradox adds nothing. And it should make them confront the sincerity problem: the paradox bites hardest against open, declared enemies of tolerance, but the practically dangerous case is the one who keeps quiet until it's too late, and no principle can read insincerity off a manifesto.

Things a first-class answer should have to do (build the scheme around these, but add or drop points if you think I've got the crux wrong):

- say precisely what makes something a paradox in the strict sense, and show that the Popperian move is a withdrawal of tolerance in a particular case rather than a limitation of it — which means it needs a criterion;
- say what's lost if tolerance turns out to be defeasible (the neutrality that made it attractive, I suspect — it becomes one value among others and has to be grounded in something like autonomy or truth-seeking);
- take the two-audience distinction seriously and locate its weak point;
- address the demarcation and abuse problem: who decides who counts as intolerant, on what evidence, and how do you stop the licence being used against unpopular but harmless views;
- say whether the paradox does any independent work in the institutional case;
- say something about the announced-versus-concealed asymmetry and what it implies for whether the criterion can be aim-based or has to be action-based.

They've had lectures on Popper and on the interwar militant-democracy idea, so they know the standard material; the question needs pitching so that recalling it gets you a lower second at best. Time allowed is 55 minutes, so keep the model answer no longer than it needs to be. Total marks 25, and I'd like the scheme to separate a first from an upper second by naming the specific moves each band must contain — not just "good analysis". Also a one-line note per point on how candidates typically miss it, and a short list of two or three hard errors that should cap the mark (I've had scripts argue that the paradox shows tolerance is incoherent full stop, which I take to be a plain misreading). No citations needed — they have the texts, I just want the reasoning.
