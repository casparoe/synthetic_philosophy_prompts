**Question.** (a) Imagine a patient whose neurons are replaced one at a time, over several months, by silicon chips each of which exactly replicates the input–output profile of the neuron it replaces, so that at every stage the patient's behaviour, reports and test scores are indistinguishable from before. (i) Should we hold that the patient remains conscious throughout? (ii) Should we hold that the patient at the end is the same person as the patient at the start? (b) What, if anything, does the case tell us about the principle that experience is invariant across systems sharing the same fine-grained functional organisation? (20 marks)

**Script.**

"(a)(i) Yes. Each chip does exactly what the neuron it replaces did, so the fine-grained functional organisation of the system is unchanged. By the principle of organisational invariance, experience is unchanged too. Chalmers gives the decisive argument here: his dancing qualia argument asks us to suppose that experience dims while behaviour stays fixed. The subject would then carry on behaving normally and would sincerely deny that anything had changed when asked, which is absurd, so experience cannot have dimmed. His fading qualia argument makes the parallel point for qualia that are inverted rather than dimmed. The zombie objection is not a serious worry, since we know from our own case that this kind of organisation produces experience. A biological chauvinist could still insist that silicon cannot support experience, but that is an extra premise with nothing behind it.

(ii) Not clearly, and this is where Parfit's combined spectrum does the work. As we move along the physical spectrum from an ordinary brain to an all-silicon system, psychological connectedness and continuity thin out along with it, so at the far end neither personal identity nor Relation R survives. What matters on Parfit's view is Relation R rather than identity, so the silicon patient is at best a very good replica of the original rather than the original surviving. But no single chip can be the one that destroys the person, so identity must be preserved at each step, including the last. Either way the practical upshot is that we should treat the silicon patient as the original and not as a bereavement.

(b) The case supports organisational invariance, because functional organisation is held fixed while the substrate varies. It also shows that identity is not what matters, since if it were we would have to say that a person can die by degrees, which nobody believes. It is worth noting that this is not the same as teletransportation, where the original is destroyed and a copy made: that is a duplication case, and duplication is not survival for either copy."
