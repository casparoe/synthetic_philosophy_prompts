I work for a mid-sized union local — about 1,400 members across four warehouses and a call centre for a regional grocery distributor — and my job is basically to be the research person who helps shop stewards think about automation. Our master agreement has a technology clause: the company has to consult us 45 days before any new machine or system goes live, which in practice means we get a binder, a slideshow, and a meeting where everyone panics and asks me the same question in different words — do we fight this, trade it away, or try to shape it? I've been answering case by case and Im tired of it. I want a procedure a steward can actually run.

The theory I want it built on, and tell me if I'm mangling it before you build anything on it: Marx's point isn't that machines are bad, it's that the same machinery does different things depending on who owns it and what the point of running it is. Machinery as such replaces labour and could shorten the working day; machinery as capital gets brought in to capture the gain, and in that setup it tends to throw people out or push them into worse jobs, take the know-how off the shop floor and put it inside the system, intensify whatever work is left, and weaken the people who remain because there's a queue at the gate. The distinction I find most useful is between capital taking over a labour process it finds already there — the boss owns the shop but the work is still done our way — and capital remaking the labour process in its own image, which is usually what machinery is. Also the older point that the same technical advance that immiserates under one set of arrangments would shorten hours under another, so the procedure has to be able to say that the machine is fine and the deal is the problem, rather than just yes or no on the technology.

So: give me a procedure, not advice on one case. The order of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops dead with a no, where it hands off to the members because it's a values question rather than a fact question, and which steps are just judgment wearing a lab coat. It has to fit on one page, use no theory words in the output (stewards will stop reading), take an afternoon at most, and land on one of five recommendations: endorse, endorse with conditions, bargain it hard, oppose, oppose and escalate. Then run it on the three proposals actually sitting on my desk. (1) Robotic palletisers in the dry goods warehouse; the company says 22 jobs go and the rest get easier. (2) An AI dispatch scheduler that would take route decisions away from the four dispatchers who currently make them by feel. (3) Powered exoskeletons for the freezer crew that would cut the injury rate a lot, but the company wants to tie them to a 6% headcount reduction and a higher picks-per-hour target. My gut says fight all three. I suspect the procedure won't agree with me on at least one, and I want to know which one and why. And tell me straight what this procedure can't do — where it will give a confident answer that's wrong.
