The setup: a judge has to rule on a case where the statute says only a person who is "elderly" may do a certain thing, and the defendant is right on the cusp, sixty-four and a half or something, and the whole plot turns on how she reasons. I want her to be informed by the real debate about vagueness, so I've been reading up, and here's my take — tell me if I've got it. There are the epistemicists, who say there's a perfectly sharp line between elderly and not-elderly, we just can't know where it falls, and then there are the supervaluationists, who say the world itself is genuinely blurry, like a photo out of focus, and thats why there's no fact of the matter about the line. So my judge could be an epistemicist and keep hoping that someday some super-sensitive instrument — age-in-the-marrow, I don't know, something — will definately reveal the true cutoff; or she could be a supervaluationist and say the defendant is elderly on every reasonable way of sharpening the word, so she's guilty, or not guilty if it comes out true on some sharpenings but not others. Is that a fair split? And can a person honestly be both at once, depending on the word — because I keep feeling like my judge is both, and I need to know whether that's a coherent character or just a confused one.
