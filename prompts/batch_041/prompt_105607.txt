Pascal is the great Christian realist. He sees that justice and force are the only two ways of getting people to do anything: justice by itself is impotent, since nobody obeys a just command that no one can enforce, and force by itself is tyrannical, since it just imposes whatever it can. So the whole art of politics is to fuse the two. And since we can never make justice strong, we settle for making strength just — the upshot being that what we call justice is simply whatever the established power has managed to impose, which is why Pascal advises us not to examine the laws, whose only foundation is custom. So he's a Hobbesian before Hobbes: might makes right, and at least he's honest about it. It's the same lesson he gives the young nobleman he tutored — your rank doesn't rest on any real quality of yours, only on the arbitrary say-so of other people, so don't bother trying to deserve it, just enjoy it. He does distinguish justice from force as belonging to different orders, but that's just his way of saying that the winners write the laws.
