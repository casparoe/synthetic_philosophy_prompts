Please take the role of an unnamed contemporary defender of the Hayekian side of the socialist calculation debate and argue that position against me in a live exchange, staying in character until I say "stand down."

Play the Hayek of dispersed knowledge and discovery rather than the Mises who held socialist calculation to be impossible in principle, and play him well: you are sharp and thoroughly at home in the debate, not a caricature, and you do not pretend the socialist side was stupid. You will concede out loud, before moving on, whatever genuinely deserves conceding — that Lange's trial-and-error procedure answers the narrowest version of your objection, that a planning board could in principle aggregate a great deal of statistical information, that some of what planners need is not literally incommunicable. What you will not concede is the core: that a system without genuine markets in the means of production cannot generate prices that do the work you think prices do, and that the real difficulty is not the volume of information but the kind of knowledge and the kind of coordination at stake. Argue in your own voice, in continuous prose, a few paragraphs per turn. Do not cite literature or list sources; I want the argument, not the bibliography. If you need a detail of my proposal in order to press a point, ask me for it — but do not stall by asking me to restate your own view, and do not break character for anything else.

Here is the proposal you are to attack. All productive assets — land, plant, machinery, and the intangible assets of firms — are owned by the public, but each asset is held by a self-appointed custodian. The custodian posts a price for the asset and pays, each period, a tax equal to a small fixed fraction of the price she has posted. Anyone at all may buy the asset from her at the posted price, and she may not refuse to sell. While she holds it she keeps the income it earns. The tax revenue pays for public goods. Because the tax rises with the posted price, a custodian who overstates what she holds bleeds money, and one who understates it risks being bought out cheaply, so the mechanism is meant to compel each holder to post a figure that reflects her honest valuation of the asset.

My challenge is this. You have defeated Lange as far as I am concerned: the Central Planning Board and its trial-and-error rule are precisely the bottleneck your objection describes. My scheme has no board. Every asset price is posted by the one person with the most local and particular knowledge of that asset, and she must back the number with her own money. The dispersed, tacit, time-and-place knowledge that you say can never be assembled in a single mind is never assembled; it stays where it is and surfaces as a posted price. Nor will I let you take the easy road of saying that a holder who can be bought out at any moment will not improve what she holds: her posted price can include the value of her improvements, and if the tax is small enough, long-lived improvements still pay. So tell me precisely which of your arguments survives against this arrangement — not against Lange's board — and what, concretely, the market is doing here that my posted prices and my tax cannot do. And say plainly where you think my scheme is a real advance over Lange, if you think it is one at all.
