For a seminar on socially constructed kinds, I need a case built to order rather than another turn on race, money, or gender. Construct one concrete classification that ordinary people actually use — an occupational grade, a legal threshold, a box on a form — but not one of those three, and nothing science-fictional, so that all four of the following hold at once. (i) The cut-point is conventional: nothing in the world singles it out, and a neighbouring society could have drawn it elsewhere with equal justification. (ii) The property being cut is not conventional: it is a graded, causally efficacious feature that the classification did not create, and the ordering it imposes is discovered rather than stipulated. (iii) The label carries inductive weight that the underlying gradient does not — knowing that something falls under it licenses predictions about its unobserved features that knowing its position on the gradient, or that it falls somewhere on the gradient, would not. (iv) Dissolving the convention would not merely rename anything; something we have reason to care about would be lost.

Then audit the case. For each constraint, say which of three positions it favours: that classification constitutes its members, that classification merely registers an independent division, and that there is no fact of the matter beyond usefulness. Identify the single feature that lets the first two positions each be right about a different part of your case, and say what becomes of the case when that feature is removed — in particular, whether it degenerates into the familiar looping case in which the label reshapes the labelled. If you think the four constraints cannot all be met, name the one you would give up and what giving it up costs. Do not stipulate the inductive success in (iii); make it something I could go and check.
