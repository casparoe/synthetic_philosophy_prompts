I'm a PhD student in biostatistics preparing a case for a methods seminar on the replication crisis, and I want one that runs the standard story backwards. Construct a realistic study, a real experiment someone could actually run in a lab, in which two analysts examine exactly the same dataset under the same likelihood and reach opposite conclusions about the effect, with the Bayesian playing the credulous role: the frequentist, applying a pre-registered test at the conventional five percent level that fails to reject, concludes that the effect is not established and should not be built on until it replicates, while a Bayesian using a prior that a fair-minded reader would accept as legitimate (informative rather than vague, fixed before the data are seen, derived from evidence independent of this dataset, and not a spike at zero) arrives at a posterior that licenses a confident claim that the effect is real. Supply the numbers, meaning sample size, observed effect, and prior, and check that they are internally consistent, since the case has to be verifiable rather than merely described.

Then work it through: the verdict each framework's own rules deliver and why; the single feature of the case doing the work and whether removing it makes the two analysts converge; how a defender of the frequentist would attack the case and how the Bayesian would answer; and what the case does or does not show about the claim that the replication crisis is a p-value problem that a switch to Bayesian methods would mitigate. The verdicts must fall out of the two frameworks applied to the numbers, not out of one analyst being careless or the prior being chosen after the fact; a case that works only because the Bayesian is cheating fails the specification. Keep it at the level of a first-year PhD reading group, with no measure-theoretic fine print, and do not lean on particular published studies.
