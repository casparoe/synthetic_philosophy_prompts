I am a novelist, and I have been asked to contribute an essay to a volume on science fiction and the philosophy of mind. I want to read William Gibson's Neuromancer through the lens of John von Neumann's views on the brain -- not the popular image of von Neumann as the father of the digital computer, but the more cautionary argument of his late work, where he suggests that the brain is not a digital computer in any straightforward sense: that it mixes digital and analog processes, relies on statistical coding and redundancy, achieves reliability from unreliable components, and may be organized on principles quite different from the machines we build. My hunch is that Gibson's cyberspace, his deck, and his AIs, Wintermute and Neuromancer, are saturated with a digitalism that von Neumann himself would have regarded as a category mistake about the brain, and yet the novel also has moments -- the body, addiction, the meat, the flatlining -- that complicate that digitalism.

I would like a reading that takes both the novel and von Neumann's views seriously. Where does Neuromancer dramatize a von Neumann-style brain, and where does it assume a clean computationalism he rejected? Which scenes should I focus on -- the matrix, the sense/net, the ending's union of the two AIs -- and does the novel end up vindicating von Neumann's caution or refuting it? I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the secondary literature; I want the argument. And if the novel does add something to the philosophical debate, I want to know what it is: what does it show about the brain, or about our desire to escape it, that von Neumann's argument alone cannot?
