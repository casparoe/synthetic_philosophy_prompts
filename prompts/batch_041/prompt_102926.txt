I'm taking a metaphysics seminar this term on identity and individuation, and we have just finished a unit on the identity of indiscernibles: Leibniz's principle and how it differs from the indiscernibility of identicals, the familiar worry that the principle is either trivially true or false depending on whether properties of identity and difference are admitted into the base, and Max Black's thought experiment of two iron spheres in an otherwise empty universe, matching in every intrinsic respect, whose only apparent difference is that each lies some distance from the other. For my final paper I have to read a film through one of the debates from the course, and I have chosen The Prestige, Christopher Nolan's 2006 film. I would like you to write that reading.

The film gives me two related cases. Robert Angier builds a machine that, at each performance, produces a second man exactly like him; one of the two ends up locked in a tank of water and drowns, and the survivor takes the applause. Alfred Borden has been living his entire public life as one half of a pair of identical twins, who alternate in the role and divide the private life between them, one married to Borden's wife and the other in love with his assistant. There is also the trick with the cage of birds, in which one bird is killed and a child in the audience weeps, having grasped that the dead bird was one of a pair and the brother of the one still in the cage.

I want the paper to do two things at once. First, I want the film read as a Black universe populated by persons: Angier's nightly duplication presents us with two things that appear to be indiscernible, and the question of what makes them two rather than one is precisely the question Black raises. The obvious line is that they are distinguished relationally — one has the property of having just been produced, the other of having been Angier all along; one is in the tank, the other on the stage — and that this is exactly the move a defender of the principle makes when she observes that Black's spheres are two miles apart. I want to know whether that move works here, and what it costs. If Angier's duplicate really is Angier, then drowning him is self-harm; if he is not, it is murder; the film seems to require that he is at once indiscernible from Angier and distinct from him, and I want you to say whether that is a coherent thing to require.

Second, I have been developing a distinction of my own, and I would rather you test it than assume it. I call two things biographically discernible when they match in every intrinsic respect but differ in what has happened to them, and I have been wondering whether the bird-cage scene offers something further still: what reveals the two birds as two is not any property either one has, but the fact that one can die while the other goes on, so that distinctness shows up as the possibility of separate fates. I want to know whether that is a genuine third criterion alongside qualitative and relational difference, or whether it quietly presupposes the very numerical distinctness it is supposed to explain.

So: where does the film dramatize the debate rather than merely illustrate it; where does it trade on a confusion between the question of what makes two things two and the question of what makes one person the same person over time; and where does it resist the reading, so that an honest critic has to concede the film is doing something else? Close by saying what the film contributes to the debate that argument alone does not. Write it as continuous prose at the level of a good seminar paper, and do not worry about citations or quotations — I will supply those, and I do not need you to reconstruct any of the dialogue.
