I am a novelist, and the book I am finishing turns on a state sentencing commission whose two most senior members have been arguing, in memoranda and in the press, for eleven years. The first is a judge who holds that a sentence exists to give an offender the treatment his wrong has earned, and who believes that a system which sets sentences by their expected effects has already stopped treating the offender as a moral agent. The second is a criminologist who holds that punishment is a state practice like any other and must answer for what it does in the world, and who believes that the retributivist's scale is theology dressed as arithmetic. I have read enough of both camps to know that I will write a dishonest book if I stage this as a contest between a man who loves justice and a man who loves spreadsheets. So I want the document the two of them would have produced if they had been made, by a grant, a deadline, and their own fatigue, to sit in a room until they had written down what they actually agree on, where they actually part, and what would actually settle it.

The collaboration has to be conducted on the terrain of institutional design rather than first principles, and I have invented three pieces of machinery for the book that it must work with.

The two-stage scheme. A desert table assigns every offense a censure rank and a severity interval: a floor and a ceiling, in months. A risk-and-need instrument may choose any point within the interval and may attach supervision and program conditions, but may not move the sentence outside it.

The separate ledger. Collateral consequences — disenfranchisement, registries, restrictions on housing and employment, immigration consequences — are booked on a separate ledger and are not counted as part of the punishment.

The censure-without-custody track. Open only where the floor of the interval is zero, it discharges the censure by public acknowledgment, restitution, and a period of supervised liberty.

What I need is the joint statement itself, in four parts.

First, a numbered list of the claims both would sign: about what punishment is for, about what the state may and may not do to a person, about which of the three devices above each can accept and on what terms, and about what the two of them jointly reject in their own camps — a retributivist and a consequentialist can agree that certain things done in their names are indefensible, and I want those on the record.

Second, a table of the disagreements that remain. For each: the disagreement in wording neutral between them; then the retributivist's own formulation and the consequentialist's own formulation, each written so that its side would accept it as a fair statement of its position; then the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them; then the test, case, or argument both agree would bear on that crux; and then, recorded in advance, what each side predicts the test will show. I would rather have four disagreements done this way than twelve done loosely.

Third, a section separating the real disagreements from the verbal ones. If any part of the quarrel is about what a word means rather than about the world, say which part and why, and say what the two of them would have to stipulate in order to make the dispute testable.

Fourth, a section on what would change each side's mind, and whether there is anything that would change both. If there is nothing, say so plainly; that is itself a finding.

Two suspicions of my own that I would like tested rather than assumed. I suspect the deepest crux is whether desert is cardinal or only ordinal — whether there is any fact of the matter about how much more an offender deserves, as opposed to merely which of two offenders deserves more, and, if desert is only ordinal, whether it can fix a floor and a ceiling at all. And I suspect the second crux is whether hard treatment is the necessary vehicle of censure or merely the conventional one, which is precisely what the third device puts in question. If either of these is not the crux, discard it and put the real one in its place.

Constraints. Name no scholars, cite no works, and supply no reading list; the two of them have an old contempt for arguments that lean on authorities, and in the novel the absence of footnotes in the commission's minutes is a running joke. Every claim must stand on its own reasoning. Draw both of them as serious people: the retributivist is not to be a caricature of severity, nor the consequentialist a caricature of managerial cruelty, and each should be able to make the other's strongest case better than a partisan would like. Keep the residual disagreement one that a reader who has followed the whole document can feel as a loss rather than a technicality. It will be printed as an appendix with their two signatures at the foot, so it should read as a document rather than as an essay — and I need to know exactly how far these two can walk together before they must part, because the last scene of the book depends on it.
