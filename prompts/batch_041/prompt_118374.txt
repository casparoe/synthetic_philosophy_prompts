I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, chiefly in the areas of probability and decision theory, and I have been trying to work out what Frank Ramsey would have made of a family of problems that arose only after his death in 1930. Ramsey's treatment of preference and degree of belief — the idea that a person's subjective probability can be measured by the odds at which she will bet, and that her preferences can be represented by a utility function — is usually read as a coherence argument. If her preferences violate certain axioms, she is vulnerable to a sure loss; if her degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, she is vulnerable to a Dutch book. Ramsey himself seems to have regarded these axioms as a kind of logic of consistency, a normative ideal rather than a description of how people actually think, and he was careful to note that the betting definition of degree of belief runs into difficulty when the stakes are large or when the person takes pleasure in the gamble itself.

What he did not, so far as I can tell, consider is the case in which a person's preferences are perfectly consistent at every moment, but change over time in a way that an outsider can predict and exploit. Consider an agent whose preferences are time-indexed in the following manner. At any given moment she prefers a smaller reward sooner to a larger reward later, but the rate at which she discounts the future is not constant: she discounts the near future steeply and the distant future gently. Thus at time t she prefers ten dollars today to eleven dollars tomorrow; at time t+1 she prefers eleven dollars tomorrow to ten dollars the day after; and so on. A broker who knows this can offer her a sequence of trades, each of which she accepts because each is an improvement by her lights at the moment of choice, and yet after the sequence she is poorer than she began. This is a money pump, but it does not rely on intransitivity of preferences at a single time. It relies on the fact that the agent's preferences at different times are inconsistent with one another.

My question is what Ramsey would have said about this. Would he have regarded the agent as irrational? Or would he have regarded the money-pump argument as failing to establish irrationality, on the grounds that the agent is never choosing anything she does not prefer at the time of choice, and that rationality is a property of choices rather than of preference profiles across time? Ramsey's own framework appears to be static: it asks whether a set of preferences at a time can be represented by a utility function, and whether a set of degrees of belief at a time can be represented by a probability function. It does not obviously say anything about whether preferences at different times must cohere with one another. But the money-pump argument is dynamic, and it seems to many people to show that diachronic inconsistency is a defect. Where exactly would Ramsey have drawn the line? In my own field we are accustomed to distinguishing the consistency of a state from the consistency of an evolution; I am trying to understand whether Ramsey's coherence arguments are about states or about evolutions.

I would also like to know what he would have said about a related case. Suppose the agent is aware of her own future preference reversals and can bind herself in advance — for example, by placing money in an account she cannot access until a certain date, or by making a binding commitment to a broker. Is the binding itself rational, on Ramsey's view? Or is it a kind of irrationality, a refusal to be governed by one's own future preferences? And what if the binding is only partial, so that the agent can still be pumped for a smaller amount? Does Ramsey's framework give any guidance about how to weigh the value of being governed by one's future preferences against the value of being protected from them?

There is also a mirror-image case that interests me. Suppose the agent's preferences are consistent at every time and consistent over time, but she is offered a sequence of trades that is bad for her at each stage, yet good for her overall — for instance, a sequence that requires her to accept a small loss now in order to gain a larger benefit later, where the later benefit is only available if she accepts the earlier loss. A resolute chooser would accept the sequence; a sophisticated chooser, who anticipates that she will not follow through, would reject it. Would Ramsey have regarded resoluteness or sophistication as the rational response? And does his framework even have the resources to distinguish them, given that it seems to be built around choices at a time rather than policies over time?

Finally, I am curious about a case that seems to me to push the idea further. Suppose the agent's preferences are not merely time-indexed but are preferences over her own preferences. She prefers to become the sort of person who prefers eleven dollars tomorrow to ten dollars today, and she is willing to pay a broker to change her preferences. Does Ramsey's framework allow preferences over preferences at all? If it does, how does it handle the fact that the agent's preferences over her preferences may themselves change over time, generating a hierarchy of pumps? If it does not, what is the principled reason for excluding them?

I am not looking for a reading list or a survey of the secondary literature. I want to know what Ramsey would have thought, and in particular how he would have distinguished the static coherence argument from the dynamic money-pump argument, and where he would have located the boundary between rationality as consistency at a time and rationality as consistency over time.
