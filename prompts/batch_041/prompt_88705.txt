I'm assembling the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on truth and the metaphysics of language, and I want a single essay question on deflationism that resists the essay I keep receiving: a recitation that deflationists deny truth is a substantive property, that correspondence theorists deny this, and a paragraph of throat-clearing on each side. What I want instead is a question that makes students actually test whether the deflationary packages on the syllabus can deliver two things they appear to owe us, and then commit to a verdict. The two pressure points I care about are, first, the generalizing role of the truth predicate — how a device that merely lets us ascend semantically can express blind generalizations such as the claim that everything a given speaker asserted is true, and whether that role covertly requires propositions as objects or a genuinely property-like notion of truth after all; and second, the normative role — in what sense truth can be the norm of belief and assertion if it is not a substantive property, and whether the standard deflationist move of reading the norm off the equivalence schema actually earns its keep or simply relocates the problem. I would like the question to require students to take up both, to consider whether truth must do explanatory work (for instance in explaining the success of action) or whether the deflationist can explain that demand away, and to end with a defended position rather than a survey.

The question should be pitched so that it can be answered in seventy-five minutes and should tell students the essay is worth twenty-five marks. It must not be answerable by naming the varieties of deflationism — redundancy theory, disquotationalism, minimalism, prosententialism — so please build the crux into the question itself, in a way that names the specific commitments students have to reconcile rather than gesturing at "the debate." I would also like the question to leave room for students to distinguish the positions: an answer that treats all deflationisms as one thing should be visibly weaker than one that says which package survives the two pressures and why.

Please give me three deliverables. First, the exam question as it would be printed. Second, a model answer of about six hundred words that does the work rather than summarizing it — stating the equivalence schema and the generalization function precisely, showing where the alleged need for propositions or for a substantive property actually bites, articulating the normativity problem and at least one serious deflationist reply together with its cost, and reaching a considered verdict. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five built around five must-hit points, giving each point its marks and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students those marks; and then a short separation statement naming the specific moves a top-band answer must contain that a merely competent answer will not, so that the grade boundary is defensible.
