The audience is not a general philosophy crowd: there are two econ grad students who work on Arrow-style impossibility results and treat any solution that just restricts the domain of admissible preferences as cheating; one Rawlsian who thinks rights are trumps and will be suspicious of anything that makes them conditional; one republican who cares about non-domination and will ask whether my proposal gives people real control or just the appearance of it; and one feminist philosopher who works on adaptive preferences and will probably say my filter is either paternalist or useless. The chair is a game theorist who likes crisp formal statements and hates hand-waving. So I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper.

My actual line is this: the paradox only bites if we treat a right as unconditional decisiveness over a pair of alternatives. I want to replace that with what I'm calling conditional decisiveness: a person is decisive over a pair only when both alternatives are in their protected personal sphere and their preference over them is not itself shaped by the social outcome at issue. I formalize it as a two-stage procedure. First a laundering filter screens the preference profile, throwing out preferences that are other-regarding or adaptive in a specified sense; then a liberal choice function runs on the filtered profile. I claim this escapes Sen's proof because the domain restriction is endogenous to the procedure rather than an exogenous limit on what preferences count as admissible. I also want to say the filter is not paternalist because it only targets preferences whose content depends on the very decision being made, but I'm not sure that line survives contact with the feminist in the room. I'm definately not a formal methods person, so Ive kept the sketch informal and there's no appendix to work from.

Prep me for the objections I'll actually get from this crowd. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. At the end, tell me how confident you are in your ranking and which single reply you're least sure of. Keep the whole thing under 900 words, use headings and bullets, and don't pad it with stuff I already know.
