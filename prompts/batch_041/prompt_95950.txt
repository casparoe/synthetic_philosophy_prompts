You are an expert in decision theory, self-locating belief, and anthropic reasoning, and you have designed a good many classroom simulations for philosophy seminars; I want you to work as both the theorist and the activity designer here. Think carefully before committing to a design, and begin by brainstorming several candidate mechanics rather than settling on the first one that runs; I would rather see three sketches and a reasoned choice than one polished plan. Work from what you know about anthropics — I am not asking for citations, a reading list, or pointers into the literature.

The setting: I run a reading group on decision theory with twelve participants, mostly philosophy graduate students plus a few mathematically comfortable outsiders. Some have met the Sleeping Beauty problem; none has thought about self-locating belief in any depth. I have one ninety-minute session, a seminar room with a whiteboard, envelopes, index cards, and a bag of poker chips (no real money), and I am the only facilitator.

What I want them to feel in their hands rather than their heads: the difference between the self-sampling assumption (reason as if you were randomly drawn from the observers who actually exist in your reference class) and the self-indication assumption (reason as if you were randomly drawn from all possible observers, weighted by the probability of their existing), and the move that makes the second look absurd — the armchair argument that the world must contain vastly more observers than we thought, because a world with more of them makes your own existence more likely. The last is the one usually called the presumptuous philosopher. I do not want them to hear either name before the debrief.

Constraints on the design. First, do not hand me Sleeping Beauty with the labels filed off; I have seen that activity and my group is too small for it to bite. I would rather the multiplicity be generated by a copy room — a machine that runs some number of copies of the same person — than by awakenings, and I want the copying to be real enough that the payoff structure, not my narration, does the work. Second, it must be runnable with twelve real people who cannot actually be duplicated, so be explicit about what stands in for what: who or what counts as a copy, what the coin decides, who knows what and when, and how the scoring is settled at the end without my having to lie about the outcome. Third, I want two acts, not one: a first in which participants discover that the multiplicity itself pulls their betting odds around, and a second in which someone must stake chips on how large the world is with no observations at all, and must defend that stake to the room. Fourth, nothing in the scoring rule may quietly presuppose that one of the two assumptions is correct; if the design cannot avoid that, say so and tell me exactly where it leaks in.

Give me: the brainstormed alternatives and why the two you reject fail; the chosen activity as a minute-by-minute run sheet, with materials, the exact bets or choices, the payoff rule in chips, the reveal or reveals and their timing, and what goes on the cards as opposed to what I say aloud; then the debrief, mapping each moment of the activity onto the two assumptions and onto the presumptuous philosopher's move; then the failure modes you predict — I expect at least someone to insist that a copy is not really them, someone to reduce the whole thing to a probability puzzle, and someone to complain that the payout rule has already decided the question — with what I should do at the moment each arises; then an honest account of what this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words; and finally one closing question I can put to the group that would show me whether they can distinguish the two assumptions rather than merely recite them.

On the epistemics: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as the consensus — in particular, do not imply that either assumption is the standard view, or that the presumptuous philosopher is a decisive reductio rather than a live disagreement, and flag it explicitly if the label for the self-indication assumption has been used in more than one way, since I would rather have the ambiguity marked than smoothed over. State plainly which formulation of each assumption you are working with, and flag any step in your own design that a careful critic would reject.
