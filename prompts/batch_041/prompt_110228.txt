Regulators in the US and elsewhere put a dollar figure on saving a statistical life by looking at how much extra pay workers demand to take riskier jobs, so the number comes from choices people actually make in the labor market. But when they need to value something with no market -- a cleaner river, a species that doesn't go extinct -- they just ask people survey questions about what they'd be willing to pay, and then they drop that number into the same cost-benefit table as the wage-premium number, as if the two were the same kind of currency. What does that practice presuppose about utility? That it's just whatever people choose, that choices reveal a stable and well-ordered preference ranking, that preferences are interpersonally and inter-good comparable? Does the practice actually need those assumptions, or could you run the same tables with a totally different theory of welfare? And which parts of it quietly rule out the idea that people can be mistaken about their own good -- or is there a tension in the fact that the same agencies get nervous about hypothetical bias in surveys while treating market choices as gospel?
