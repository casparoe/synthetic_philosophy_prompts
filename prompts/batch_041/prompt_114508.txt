I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about a squabble between decision theorists over how we should handle risk — and I've definately hit a wall trying to get the two sides straight in my own head.

The theory everyone keeps invoking is risk-weighted expected utility, the one the philosopher Lara Buchak worked out. Here's my rough grip on it, tell me if I've got it wrong. You take an option's possible outcomes and line them up from worst to best. The worst outcome always counts in full. Then each step up, from a worse outcome to a better one, only gets counted in proportion to a risk function that you feed the probability of landing at least that well off. If that function is the plain straight line you're back to ordinary expected utility; if it's brutal enough you end up with maximin, where all you care about is the floor.

Now a friend of mine, who prices catastrophe bonds for a living and reads a lot of philosophy on the side, says all that machinery is overkill. He's got a rule he calls the floor rule. His version: work out the ordinary expected utility of the option, work out the utility of its single worst possible outcome, and then score the option as a weighted average of those two — hanging a weight, call it lambda, somewhere between zero and one, on the worst outcome and one minus lambda on the expected utility. Lambda is just how much he cares about not getting wiped out; set it to zero and you're back to expected utility, set it to one and you're a maximin guy. He reckons this beats Buchak's because it's simpler, and because the worst case does real work instead of having some funny curve draped over it.

Could you compare the two for me properly? What I actually need to know is whether his rule is a genuine rival to hers or already somewhere inside her theory wearing a cheaper suit. Does it buy the things the risk-weighting people are after — the intuition in the Allais cases, where people cheerfully take the sure thing in one pair of bets but gamble in another pair that differs only by a common slice of probability; the demand that if one option is at least as good as another no matter how things shake out, you shouldn't prefer the worse one; and the worry about being the sucker in some betting scheme thats a guaranteed loss? And if his rule does snap somewhere, I'd love a concrete pair of gambles where the two rules disagree and his answer is the one that looks nuts. Please don't just tell me hers is more general and leave it there — I want to know what actually breaks, and what his rule is blind to that hers isn't.
