My little brothers call-up papers came last week and the house has gone a bit mad about it — my dad thinks its the last thing that actually makes you a citizen, my mum thinks it's just forced labour with a flag on it, and my brother mostly wants to know whether you can fail the medical on purpose. I'm the economist in the family (IO and mechanism design — screening, participation constraints, public goods, incidence, that toolkit), so I keep translating everyone into my own language mid-argument, and some of it goes through cleanly and some of it really doesn't.

So here's what I want. Take the case *for* conscription and armed neutrality — the citizen-army / nation-in-arms thing — and restate it in my vocabulary. Concretely: the Swiss and Finnish version where every man is liable and the civilian alternative is deliberatly longer than the military one so it isn't a free exit, and the Swedish version where a screening test picks out only a small slice of each cohort. And I don't mean the standard economic critique of the draft, I already know that one (implicit tax on the young, allocates by lottery rather than comparative advantage, invisible in the budget, lands hardest on the people with the fewest outside options). I want the *other* side put into my language: what is 'a duty owed to your fellow citizens rather than to the state' in terms of participation constraints; what is 'the whole society is the defence' in terms of a commitment device or a deterrence equilibrium; what is 'you can't buy your way out' in terms of a deliberately non-price allocation rule; what is 'everyone has skin in the game, so we don't go to war lightly' in terms of aligning the incentives of the people who decide; what is neutrality as a status rather than a policy choice.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which bits come out as recognisable economic claims with the same force? Which come out recognisably economic but thinner or subtly off — I suspect 'the army should be the people' collapses into something much weaker about agency costs, and that the militia idea (which in Switzerland runs all the way up to the part-time parliament) doesn't survive at all? And which have no counterpart whatsoever — I keep bumping into things like the duty being owed to other citizens rather than to the state, or the claim that a shared experience everyone has had is valuable in a way that has nothing to do with what it produces, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's content or just rhetoric that can't be cashed out.

Do the reverse as well: what does my toolkit see that the civic argument can't even state?

Then the verdict. Is the residue a hole in economics, or a sign the civic case was vaguer than it sounded? And one more thing that's been nagging me: three countries, three pretty different mechanisms — universal liability with a longer civilian alternative, selective screening, and so on — but roughly the same civic rhetoric draped over all of them. Is that because the civic argument is genuinely mechanism-independent, or because it's doing no real work and the mechanism gets picked on other grounds entirely? Don't be polite about it.
