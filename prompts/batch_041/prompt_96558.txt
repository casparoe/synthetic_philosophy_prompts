I'm designing a six-session interdisciplinary seminar, and the framing essay that goes with it, on the non-mathematical afterlife of Gödel's incompleteness theorems. The audience is philosophy graduate students plus a handful of mathematically literate outsiders from computer science and law, so the material needs to be precise without being technical. My aim is not to survey the uses of the theorems; I want the seminar to end with a defensible view about which of those uses are worth anyone's sustained attention. So: rank the main lines of work in this area and defend the ranking.

Here is the menu I have assembled so far. Treat it as a starting point rather than a complete list, and tell me what I have missed and what should be struck.

1. The argument from human mathematical insight: for any consistent formal system of the relevant kind there is a true sentence it cannot prove, yet we can recognize that sentence as true; therefore the mind is not such a system. A common reply is that the argument delivers only a disjunction — either the mind is not a consistent formal system, or it cannot know that it is — so the advertised conclusion is much weaker than it sounds.

2. Self-trust in formal agents: an agent that rewrites or improves itself and trusts only what it can prove cannot prove the soundness of its own proof system, so "provably safe self-modification" is harder than it first appears, and the same machinery constrains any project of a system certifying its own reliability.

3. Undecidability results in the sciences: for instance, results suggesting that whether a quantum many-body system has a spectral gap is undecidable in general, and the older undecidability results for tiling problems. Does this tell us something about the limits of physics, or only about the formal encodings we happen to choose?

4. Law and legal reasoning: the analogy between an undecidable sentence and a "hard case," and the claim that a legal system cannot be both complete and consistent. Is the analogy load-bearing, or is it rhetoric dressed in logical notation?

5. Set theory and the philosophy of mathematics: whether independence results such as the continuum hypothesis are instances of Gödelian incompleteness, what "true but unprovable" amounts to, and whether the situation favors pluralism about set-theoretic universes or a single intended universe. The trap here is running together independence, incompleteness, and undecidability.

6. Humanistic and popular appropriations: the use of "Gödel shows there are limits to reason, knowledge, or formalization" in literary theory, theology, and popular science. Is anything salvageable, or is this all metaphor?

For each line, tell me: whether the theorem is actually doing the work or merely decorating a claim that has independent motivation; whether anyone would hold the target claim if the incompleteness theorems had never been proved; how tractable the question is for people who are not specialists in mathematical logic; how many other questions depend on getting it right; and how neglected it is relative to its importance. Then give me a total order, make the trade-offs among those criteria explicit, and say what argument or finding over the next year or two should move an item up or down the list.

I want the reasoning rather than the list, and I don't need names of researchers or a reading list. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic; do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and flag clearly where you are speculating or going beyond what the theorems themselves establish.
