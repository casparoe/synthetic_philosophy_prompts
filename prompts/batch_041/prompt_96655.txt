I cover science and ideas, which is a fancy way of saying I'm the guy my editor calls when a hospital ends up in court over pulling a ventilator. So I've read more of this literature than is good for me, and a transplant surgeon I drink with laid out an argument last month that I still can't shake. Roughly:

(1) You're dead when your body has irreversibly stopped working as one integrated whole, as opposed to a bag of parts held together from the outside.
(2) A brain-dead patient -- no brainstem, no drive to breathe, no consciousness -- meets that description. Whatever their body still does, the vent and the drugs are doing it for them.
(3) So brain death is death, which is why the law in most states says so and why we can take the organs.
(4) But irreversibly only ever means irreversible with the medicine we happen to have. There's nothing about a dead pancreas or a scarred heart that makes its failure irreversible in principle; we just can't fix it yet.
(5) A diabetic who'd be dead by morning without insulin has lost a function her body can't perform and can't get back on its own. Same for the dialysis patient. Same, honestly, for anyone on a feeding tube.
(6) If needing a machine to do a job your body can't do makes the loss irreversible, then those people are dead too.
(7) They definately aren't, and I'm not filing that story.
(8) So something in (1) through (5) has to go, and I don't think it's (7).

Two things make me think the crack is somewhere specific. First, a colleague insists the whole fight is really about the difference between irreversible and permanent, and points out that in donation-after-circulatory-death protocols we wait five minutes after the heart stops and then call it death -- five minutes is not irreversible by any biological standard and everyone in the room knows it. Second, there are now programs where, after death is declared, they put the donor on a circuit that restores blood flow to the abdominal organs and sometimes gets the heart going again, which seems like exactly the thing we just said couldn't happen.

So where does this break? I'd like to keep (7), and I'd like to keep the transplant program. Tell me which premise to give up and what giving it up costs me -- and if you think the chain is airtight, say which of those two I have to lose.
