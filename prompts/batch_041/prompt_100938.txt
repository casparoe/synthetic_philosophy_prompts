I am preparing a two-page research proposal for a one-year writing fellowship in epistemology, and I would like you to develop a half-formed idea of mine into a properly specified project.

The idea is this. Two theses about higher-order epistemic standing are usually treated as near-equivalents: the KK principle, on which knowing that p implies knowing that one knows that p, and the luminosity of knowledge, on which knowing that p implies being in a position to know that one knows that p. I suspect the two come apart once one presses on what the phrase in a position to know is actually doing. The standard anti-luminosity argument runs a sorites-style series against the claim that a condition which can be gained or lost by imperceptible degrees is one that its possessor is always in a position to know they possess; it is commonly read as showing that knowledge is not luminous and, by the near-equivalence, as thereby threatening KK. I want to argue that the argument in fact refutes only one reading of the in a position to know clause — a margin-for-error reading — and that on a safety-style reading KK survives it. From there I want to say what, if anything, the anti-luminosity argument does establish about the epistemic status of our own credences and about higher-order ignorance, that is, whether failing to know that p puts one in a position to know that one fails to know it.

Please turn this into a proposal that states the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explains why the existing positions on KK and on anti-luminosity leave that question open; sets out the approach I would take and why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives, such as simply stipulating a safety reading of in a position to know or rejecting the margin-for-error premise outright; describes what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project's failing; and gives an honest assessment of whether the whole thing is too large or too small to fill a year. Assume no empirical component and no literature review by citation — I will supply the references myself. Write it as continuous prose suitable for a fellowship panel rather than as bullet points.
