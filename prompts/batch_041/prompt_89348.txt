I am a sixth-form history teacher, and for the past few months I have been working my way through Peter Turchin's structural-demographic account of secular cycles — the thesis that states oscillate between integrative and disintegrative phases, and that the disintegrative phase is produced by the interaction of popular immiseration, elite overproduction, intensifying competition within the elite, and the consequent strain on the state's fiscal and coercive capacity. I came to it sideways and rather unwillingly: a colleague in my department has taken to announcing that we are living through the opening of the next disintegrative phase, and I want to know whether the machinery behind that claim can bear any weight, or whether it is a vocabulary for describing decline after the fact. So I should like the model tested, hard, on a case I know well.

The case is the revolutions of 1848, and specifically the contrast between France and Britain. In France the February revolution brought down the July monarchy, the June Days followed within months, and Louis-Napoleon was elected president and then seized power at the end of 1851. Britain faced pressures that look comparable — industrial distress and unemployment, the Irish famine, the repeal of the Corn Laws and the party crisis it caused, a Chartist movement that staged its great petition and its mass meeting at Kennington Common in the spring of 1848 — and produced no revolution at all; Russia produced none either, and both outcomes need explaining, not just the French one. I do not need the events narrated back to me. What I want is the mapping: which actors, prices, grievances and institutions in these two countries are supposed to fill the model's variables, and where the fit is a stretch. I have also been trying to make 'elite overproduction' do some work rather than merely naming it, and my crude proxy is the ratio of aspirants to established positions — men with legal training, literary ambitions or expectations of commissions and offices, set against the posts that the state and the professions could plausibly fill. My own guess, which I should like you to assess rather than adopt, is that emigration does much of the work in the British case and that the model underplays it, since Britain could export surplus population and surplus sons to America, the colonies and the Indian service in a way France largely did not; tell me whether that is a mechanism inside the model or an escape hatch I am smuggling in to save it.

Then turn it round. What does the contrast show about the theory — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? I am sceptical in one particular way, and would rather you engaged with the scepticism than reassured me: the model appears to predict breakdown only where we already know breakdown occurred, and I cannot see what observation would count against it. If you think that charge is unfair, say what would falsify the theory; if you think it is fair, say which of the components is doing the real explanatory work and which are decorative. Keep the counterfactual discipline as well: Britain in 1848 is a non-event, and a non-event is the hardest thing for a model of this kind to explain, so do not let it slide into 'the pressures were simply weaker' without saying what did the relieving. Open with a single-sentence verdict on whether the model earns its keep on this case, then give the analysis, and finish with the one question the 1848 contrast poses that the theory's defenders would find hardest to answer. No more than 850 words, and no bullet points.
