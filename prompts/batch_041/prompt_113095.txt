(0) Background: earlier in the evening someone had been paraphrasing a pop-science thing about how the incompleteness theorems prove no computer can match a human mind, or maybe that there can be no complete theory of everything, one of those. (1) Ada — my roommate, reads a lot of this — said the shape of it is basically right: in any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic there are true statements the system can't prove, so truth always outruns proof, and since a mind or a brain would have to be some such system, no machine captures what we can do. (2) Ben pushed back on the details. He said the theorem only applies to systems that are consistent, effectively axiomatized, and rich enough to express arithmetic; and by the second theorem any such system can't prove its own consistency, so if Ada's mathematical beliefs form a consistent, effectively axiomatizable system then she can't establish her own consistency either — and without knowing a system is consistent you can't know its Gödel sentence is true, so where is the asymmetry supposed to come from? (3) Ada said it doesn't have to come from her own case: hand her any particular machine, and if that machine is consistent then its Gödel sentence is true and unprovable in it, while she can see that it's true — so for every machine there's a truth it misses. (4) Ben said that only works when the machine is somebody else; when the machine is you, you're in exactly the same position, and the argument needs you to be able to certify the consistency of the very system you're being compared against — including yourself — which is what the second theorem says you can't do. (5) Ada said fine, maybe she can't certify her own consistency, but that's a limitation she shares with the machine; what she can do is outrun any system she's handed. (6) Ben said then she's just assumed she isn't a system, which is the conclusion and not a premise, and that the mechanist can always say the mind is a machine whose axioms aren't effectively enumerable, or one that can't verify its own consistency — neither of which the theorem rules out. (7) Ada said that's a hollow victory: a machine whose axioms can't be listed by any procedure isn't a machine in any sense anyone cares about, and a machine that might be inconsistent would mean she could be wrong about arithmetic, which she isn't willing to grant.
