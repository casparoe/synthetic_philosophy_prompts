I am a novelist, and the climax of my current book is a negotiation: two half-sisters dividing what is left of their father's business, one of them caring chiefly about the land and the other about the cash. I want the quarrel about fairness in that scene to be intellectually serious, so I have been reading up on axiomatic bargaining theory. I can state the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution well enough: given a feasible set of utility pairs, a disagreement point, and the ideal point — each player's best attainable payoff — it selects the point on the segment running from the disagreement point to the ideal point that lies on the boundary of the feasible set, which is also the point at which each player's gain is the same proportion of the gain they could have hoped for. Nash's solution, by contrast, maximizes the product of the two players' gains over the disagreement point.

What I am after is the conceptual issue underneath, not the formalism. Please take the role of a philosopher-economist who works on the foundations of cooperative game theory and is also a patient teacher of non-specialists, and think carefully before you commit to an answer. Begin by brainstorming, in a few lines, what each of the standard requirements is protecting: Pareto optimality, symmetry, invariance to how utility is measured, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and individual monotonicity — the thought that if the feasible set expands without lowering either player's best attainable payoff, neither player's share should fall.

Then explain, as one connected issue rather than a checklist, what is really at stake in the choice between these two solutions. I would especially like you to address: why Nash's solution can punish a player for an enlargement of the feasible set, with a concrete numerical example; why no solution can satisfy both independence of irrelevant alternatives and individual monotonicity once Pareto optimality, symmetry and scale invariance are also required, and what intuition each of those two axioms is protecting; whether the ideal point is a legitimate anchor for a fairness judgment, given that it is jointly unattainable and that it shifts whenever the disagreement point shifts; whether equal proportional gains quietly presupposes interpersonal comparisons of utility, or whether scale invariance makes it innocent of them; and what becomes of the whole rationale when the feasible set is not convex, so that the point on the disagreement-to-ideal segment may not be feasible at all.

Where a claim is genuinely contested, say so and give both sides rather than asserting a consensus, and mark clearly when you are reconstructing an intuition rather than reporting a result. Close with two or three sentences on what my sisters are really disagreeing about when one of them finds the Nash answer fair and the other finds the Kalai–Smorodinsky answer fair.
