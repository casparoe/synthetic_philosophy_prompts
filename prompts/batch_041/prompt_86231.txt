I teach a graduate seminar on identity and individuation, and I want to assign Christopher Nolan's The Prestige (2006) alongside the debate over the identity of indiscernibles. The film presents two cases that pull in opposite directions. Borden is a pair of identical twins who share a single public identity and a single life, alternating as the magician and as his assistant Fallon; they are indiscernible to the public, and perhaps to everyone except each other, though Sarah's tragedy is that she seems to feel the difference. Angier's machine produces duplicates that are, at the moment of duplication, qualitatively identical to him — same body, same memories, same intentions — and each night one of the two is drowned in the tank beneath the stage while the other takes the applause. Angier confesses that he can no longer be certain which of the two he is.

I want a reading that uses Max Black's two-spheres thought experiment — two iron spheres in an otherwise empty universe, alike in every quality, which Black takes to show that the identity of indiscernibles is false — to illuminate what the film is doing, and that uses the film to put pressure on the philosophical debate in turn. Some questions I would like addressed: Does the film treat the twins as numerically two but qualitatively one, and if so, in what does their two-ness consist? Does the fact that one loves Sarah and the other Olivia make them discernible in a way that vindicates the principle, or does it merely show that their histories and relations differ, which is not the kind of intrinsic qualitative difference the principle's defenders need? In Angier's case, are the duplicates two beings or one being at two locations? If they are qualitatively identical, does the film suggest that the question of which one is really Angier is empty — and is that a vindication of Black or a reductio of the principle? What role does Angier's loss of certainty about his own identity play here? And what should we say about the ethics of his nightly killing: if the duplicate is indiscernible from him, is killing it murder, or is it something else, and does the principle bear on the answer?

I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: not a plot summary with philosophical labels attached, and not a philosophy paper that uses the film as a mere illustration. Where does the film resist the philosophical reading, and where does the philosophy fail to capture what the film shows? Conclude with what, if anything, The Prestige adds to the debate that Black's spheres and the subsequent literature do not.
