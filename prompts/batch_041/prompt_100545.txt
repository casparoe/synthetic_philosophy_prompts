I run a small decision-theory reading group out of a Discord that's mostly refugees from LessWrong who then drifted into econ or cog sci, and I want to build a "which decision theory do you actually hold?" quiz we can all take and then argue about. I'd like you to take the role of someone who's genuinely expert here — decision theorist who's also spent way too much time in those circles — and think carefully before you write anything; maybe brainstorm to yourself first about where the real fault lines are, then give me the finished instrument.

The topic is the Allais paradox and the independence axiom. Please don't just hand me the textbook lineup of "Savage, prospect theory, done" — that's the thing everyone's already seen. I want the quiz to sort people by what they actually say about cases, not by which label they like, so every question has to be a concrete choice between two gambles phrased so the taker can't tell which answer maps onto which position.

Call it nine questions, each a concrete pair of options, and each one probing exactly one thing. I need these specific cases in there, in whatever order makes the logic flow. First, the basic Allais setup, but split into three separate questions: what would you pick in the moment, what would you wish you'd pre-committed to before you saw the options, and what do you think is rationally required of you. I suspect those three come apart for a lot of people and that's half the point. Second, take one option and describe it two ways, once as a compound lottery and once as a single-stage one, so we can see whether somebody's violation tracks the description or survives it. Third, an expansion case, two options that differ only in a branch that's identical on both sides, which is the sure-thing principle in its most naked form. Fourth, a case where the option that flunks the axiom is one you'd pay a small real cost to keep choosing — I want to separate "I slip up sometimes" from "I'm running a policy I'd defend."

Fifth, here's a case I made up and I'm curious whether it works: a lottery where the common consequence you're being asked to ignore isn't your own payoff but whether some third party gets a benefit — you know, independence but with someone else's welfare riding on the branch you're supposed to treat as irrelevant. I want to know whether people who are otherwise strict expected-utility types suddenly stop being strict when the thing being cancelled is moral rather than monetary. If you think that case is broken or doesn't isolate anything, say so and fix it rather than padding around it.

Then give me the scoring key: the answer patterns that map onto plain Savage-style expected utility with the independence axiom fully swallowed; onto a risk-weighted view where you keep something like an expectation but reweight how much the bad branches get to matter; onto the descriptivist line that your choices just reveal reference-dependence and loss aversion and that this is a fact about you, not a sin; onto the view that the axioms are partly constitutive of having a preference at all, so a violation means you never really had the preference; onto the Dutch-book pragmatist who only cares about not being pumpable and will conform to whatever avoids that; and onto the flat deflationist who thinks they just have inconsistent tastes and doesn't see why that needs fixing. Flag any two of those leaves the quiz genuinely can't tell apart, and don't paper over it — if prospect-style and risk-weighted can't be separated without a case I haven't given you, tell me which case I'd need to add.

Build the thing from the logical space first, the axes that actually cut between positions, then drop the named views into the resulting cells, and tell me which cells come out empty and why — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's ugly, or just because nobody's bothered to hold it.
