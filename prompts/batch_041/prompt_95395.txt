I am assembling a seminar unit on time for an upper-level undergraduate course, and this year I want to pair Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five with Kurt Gödel's rotating-universe solutions to Einstein's field equations. I have a decent but not expert grip on the physics, and I want a reading I can hand to students without misleading them.

The Gödel material, as I understand it, runs roughly like this. His 1949 solution describes a rotating, dust-filled universe in which the worldlines of matter curve back on themselves, so that in principle an observer could set out in a rocket and arrive at her own past. Gödel took this to be more than a curiosity. In his philosophical gloss on the result he argues that relativity had already stripped the world of a privileged, world-wide present: simultaneity is observer-relative, so there is no objective set of events that are happening at once, and without such a set there can be no objective lapse or flow of time. What remains is a static four-dimensional whole, passage belongs to our experience rather than to the world, and the upshot, which he connected to Parmenides and to Kant's idealism about time, is that time in the relevant sense is ideal. I am not confident about how much weight each step of that argument will bear, and I would like the reading to say honestly which step is doing the work and which is an assumption.

The novel appears to reach the same conclusion from the other direction. The Tralfamadorians tell Billy Pilgrim that all moments are equally real and permanent, that the dead are not gone but alive in other moments, that they see the whole of time the way we see a range of mountains, and that this is why they can be untroubled by death. Billy's own experience seems to confirm the doctrine from the inside, since he lives his life out of order, sliding from one moment of it into another without regard to when it happened. The novel's form makes the same gesture: it moves around in time rather than forward, and every death in it, from the firebombing of Dresden to the deaths of individual soldiers, is followed by the narrator's flat little refrain.

What I want is a reading of the novel through this philosophical material, doing the following, roughly in this order.

First, say precisely which view of time the Tralfamadorian doctrine amounts to. Is it block-universe eternalism, is it Gödel's ideal time, is it something else again — and does it require the strong claim that passage is an illusion, or only the weaker claim that there is no privileged global present? This matters for everything downstream, because those two claims come apart.

Second, test the fit instead of assuming it. Where does Gödel's argument genuinely underwrite what the Tralfamadorians say, and where does the novel need something Gödel would deny? I am particularly interested in Billy's sliding between moments: his consciousness occupies one moment and then another, in some order, and that looks like a moving present of experience — does the novel smuggle back in exactly the objective now that Gödel's argument is meant to eliminate? I would also like the reading to separate eternalism from determinism. The Tralfamadorians know how the universe ends; Gödel's argument, as I read it, concerns passage rather than whether the future is fixed. Does the novel conflate the two, and does the conflation matter for the reading?

Third, say where the novel resists the reading. Is it endorsing the doctrine, or using it as a consolation that a badly traumatised person needs, whose comfort may come partly from not being true in the way it claims? Which scenes should I put in front of students — the zoo on Tralfamadore, Billy watching the war from outside it, the aftermath of Dresden, the narrator's own attempts to write his book — and what does each of them show? I would also like the Tralfamadorian novels, which are read all at once with no sequence, treated as part of the argument rather than as decoration: the book proposes a form for the view it describes, and I want to know whether that form delivers the view or merely gestures at it, and whether the refusal of sequence is a claim about time or only about storytelling. Finally, if all moments are permanent, then Dresden is permanent too; I would like some account of whether the novel's serenity about death is consolation or evasion, and whether the book knows it is both.

Fourth, end by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the argument alone does not — what a reader gets from Vonnegut's version of the block universe that Gödel's essay cannot give, and what the novel gets wrong that a philosopher would want to correct.

About a thousand words, in sections. I know the novel and the philosophy well enough that I do not need a bibliography, a reading list, or long quotations; argue from the text and from the positions themselves, and flag it clearly wherever you are unsure of a fact about Gödel or about the physics.
