Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about T. H. Huxley's conscious-automaton doctrine, the position of his 1874 address on the hypothesis that animals are automata: behaviour is produced entirely by physical machinery, and consciousness accompanies the machinery without moving any of its parts — his own images were the steam-whistle that sounds while the locomotive does its work, and the bell of a clock that rings when struck but contributes nothing to the keeping of time. My friend's parting shot was that Huxley had it easy, because he never had to confront what we now call the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if phenomenal states are causally inert, how can our judgments and reports about them be reliably about them at all, rather than merely about the physical states that produce both the reports and the states? I want to know what Huxley himself would have said about a cluster of problems that postdate him, and I want the extrapolation constrained by his own commitments rather than by whatever later epiphenomenalists happened to believe.

Work through these four, in each case giving a prediction, the reasoning from what Huxley actually asserted or argued, and an honest marking of where his nineteenth-century framework leaves the answer underdetermined:

1. The report problem in its sharpest form. A subject sincerely says she is in pain. On Huxley's account the utterance is caused by brain processes, the pain is caused by those same processes, and the pain causes nothing. Does that make her report evidence about the pain, or only about her brain? Would Huxley accept that she could be systematically mistaken about her own experience in a way no amount of introspection could expose? Would he treat the shared physical cause as sufficient for reliability, and what would he say if that common cause were surgically or pharmacologically split so that report and experience came apart?

2. Artificial and occluded minds. First, a system that produces fluent, situation-appropriate first-person reports of experience, including reports of uncertainty about whether it has any, while we have no independent reason to posit phenomenal states in it. Second, a locked-in patient with intact phenomenal life and no motor output. Which of these reports would Huxley treat as evidence, and evidence for what?

3. The exclusion worry, which is a sharpened descendant of his own argument. If every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, the mental is either identical with the physical or left with nothing to do. Would Huxley greet this as vindication or as a reductio? Would he become an identity theorist to preserve the efficacy of thought, and if so does the steam-whistle image survive the move?

4. Functional status. If consciousness does nothing, what accounts for its presence, its correlation with complex processing, and the way its contents seem tailored to what the organism is doing? Must Huxley treat it as a functionless by-product, and would he accept the consequence that selection cannot explain its character?

For each issue, give me at least two readings of Huxley that fit what he wrote but diverge on the case, say which you would bet on and why, and name one further concrete case that would tell the readings apart. No bibliography or citations; I will chase those down myself.
