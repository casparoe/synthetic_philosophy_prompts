Two people in my EA-adjacent reading group got into it last week over whether habitat destruction is actually good or bad for wild animals, and they asked me to referee and I totally choked. Roughly how it went:

M: Clearing a forest or draining a wetland is usually good for wild animals, or at least not clearly bad, because the animals that would have lived there over the next century overwhelmingly live short, nasty lives -- starvation, parasites, being eaten alive -- and the clearing cuts that total off. Yeah, some animals die in the clearing, but you're preventing way more births, and most of those prevented lives would've been net negative. The math runs the opposite way from what everyone assumes.

R: That's a shell game with non-existence. You can't count lives-not-lived as a benefit unless you've actually shown those lives would've been net negative, which is a giant empirical claim about insect and fish nervous systems that nobody has seperately established. And you're ignoring how the current animals die -- burned, crushed, starving as they flee. That's suffering you're causing right now to real beings, traded for a speculative benefit to imaginary ones.

M: I don't need net-negative lives, just the asymmetry -- there's a reason not to bring suffering beings into existence and no corresponding reason to bring happy ones in. And in practice cleared land usually becomes farmland or sprawl, which holds far fewer wild animals per acre than the forest did, so the long-run headcount of suffering drops too.

R: The replacement isn't zero -- farms and suburbs are crawling with rodents and insects -- and disturbed edge habitat tends to favor exactly the fast-breeding, high-mortality species, so you might end up with *more* animal-years of misery, not less. And "fewer animals" cuts both ways: if the forest had a lot of good animal-years in it, you just threw those away. You keep comparing against a baseline of pure suffering that you smuggled in at the start.

Then M said something like "so your view is we should leave nature alone because we can't be sure, which conveniently means never doing anything," and R said "no, it means not pretending a spreadsheet you can't fill in is filled in."

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what? Don't just crown a winner -- say where M's argument actually works, where R's objections land, and what both of them are assuming that they maybe shouldn't.
