Ok, so heres something I keep chewing on and I cant get a clean answer out of the secondary stuff I've read.

Take the Genealogy of Morality, mainly the second essay, where Nietzsche does his thing about guilt coming out of the creditor-debtor relation, so that the moral concept of guilt is a later, moralized version of an older, more material concept of debt, and where bad conscience is what you get when cruelty gets driven inward because the instincts can no longer discharge outward. The part I care about is that he treats guilt as a historical artifact, not a fixed piece of human hardware. But he also seems to think that same inward turn is what gave us the soul, the interior, the capacity for responsibility, the kind of individual who can make promises and stand surety for himself. So it's not obviously something you'd want to just delete.

Now the posthumous part. He died in 1900, never saw psychopharmacology, never saw the therapeutic culture we swim in. So imagine something he never had to face: a cheap, safe, widely available drug (or a neurofeedback protocol, whatever, the delivery doesn't matter) that reliably turns down guilt, meaning the self-directed I did a wrong thing feeling, by maybe half, while leaving shame, meaning the I've been seen as lesser feeling, basically intact. Suppose there's a real movement to hand it out, not as a party drug but as therapy and as moral enhancement. And suppose there are also bioethicists today arguing the opposite, that we should engineer more guilt into people so they cooperate better, while a counter-movement says guilt is mostly a pathology we should be treating.

I want Nietzsche's view, and I want you to derive it, not vibe it. Specifically:

1. Would he read the guilt-dampening option as finally escaping the ascetic ideal and the slave revolt in morals, or as the deepest symptom of it, herd man now outsourcing his own conscience to a molecule?
2. Is there a tension in the genealogy itself here? He calls bad conscience a sickness, but he also treats it as the pregnancy of something higher, and he seems to think the way out is not removal but a new redeeming type of human. Does that make him an opponent of chemically deleting guilt, or a supporter? Which way does the text actually cut?
3. How would he draw the guilt/shame line in this scenario? He seems more at home with a shame-based, agonal morality than with a guilt-based, universalist one. Does that mean he'd welcome the drug, since it prunes guilt and leaves shame? Or does his admiration for shame depend on shame being tied to rank and self-overcoming, which the therapeutic culture also dissolves?
4. What would he make of the proposal to increase guilt for the sake of cooperation? Is that the herd's morality completed, or something else entirely?
5. Where does he have to bite a bullet he clearly wouldn't like, and where does the text simply underdetermine the answer so you're guessing?

Please separate what actually follows from his explicit commitments from what you're inferring off his temperament, and tell me the one question you'd need to settle to pin his answer down, if there is one.
