For the better part of a year a reading group I sit in on has been working through the literature on coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation, and I have reached the point where I doubt the participants are disagreeing about anything the arguments could settle. The shape of it is this. One side treats the standard Dutch book and money-pump constructions as establishing something normative: an agent whose preferences and credences cannot be represented by an expected-utility function is, for that very reason, defective, since the constructions exhibit sequences of transactions that lead her, by her own lights, to a position she counts as worse. The other side grants the mathematics and denies the moral. Its members argue variously that the constructions show only that such an agent is exploitable by a well-informed counterparty; that they presuppose a completeness condition, and perhaps continuity or the definability of preferences over lotteries, which ordinary agents need not meet; and that they trade on a conception of rationality as internal coherence among attitudes which is itself a substantive commitment rather than a neutral premise. Two details from our sessions keep returning. One of us presses a variant in which the agent is never offered the whole sequence, only ever one step at a time, with no opportunity to look ahead or to reconsider, and insists that nothing has been shown to be wrong with her attitudes, only with the situation she was placed in. Another observes that the same machinery would convict any consistent goal-directed system, a thermostat included, and asks whether that shows the argument proves too much to be about rationality at all.

My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading: one is a question about consistency requirements internal to an agent's representable attitudes, the other about how an agent ought to respond to reasons, and neither side's arguments can reach the other's question. What I want is a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on the merits. Set out the strongest form of each side's case as it actually runs, and then tell me what kind of disagreement is doing the real work: a verbal dispute over the words 'rational' and 'requirement'; a disagreement about which cases count as the data, the idealised agent with complete preferences versus the ordinary agent with gaps and cycles; a hidden formal question, such as whether the representation theorems can be had without completeness or whether a pump can be rebuilt for agents whose preferences are incomplete; or a clash of starting points, rationality as coherence versus rationality as responsiveness to reasons, that no argument on either side should be expected to move. If my hypothesis misidentifies the fault line, say so and locate it properly.

Finish by telling me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a formal result, a restriction of the original claim to a narrower class of agents, or an argument that the question was ill-posed from the start. A well-argued negative verdict — that the debate cannot be resolved in the terms in which it is conducted, and why — would be more useful to me than a survey that keeps the peace.
