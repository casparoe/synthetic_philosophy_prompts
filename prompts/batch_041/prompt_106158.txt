I am a final-year undergraduate taking a jurisprudence option in which the whole of the second term is given over to the Hart--Devlin debate on the legal enforcement of morality, and I have to write an essay that uses a single case to test both positions against each other. Please note at the outset that I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list or citations; I want the argument, not the apparatus.

The case is the Spanner prosecutions, and in particular the decision of the House of Lords in R v Brown. I shall set out the salient facts here so that nothing turns on looking them up. A number of men were convicted under sections 20 and 47 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861 for consensual sadomasochistic acts carried out in private over roughly a decade. The activities came to light during a police investigation into something else; none of the participants complained to the police, and the injuries, though real and sometimes severe, were sought and enjoyed by those who received them. The Lords, by a bare majority of three to two, held that consent is no defence to assault occasioning actual bodily harm or to malicious wounding. The majority reasoned that society is entitled to protect itself against a culture of violence, that acts of this kind are degrading to body and mind, and that they carry a risk of corrupting the young; the two dissenters thought that private, consensual sexual conduct ought to be left outside the criminal law and that repugnance or moral disapproval is not a proper ground for creating a new crime. The applicants then went to Strasbourg and lost: the European Court of Human Rights found no violation of the right to respect for private life, treating the question of how much physical harm consenting adults may inflict on one another as one for the state to settle within its margin of appreciation, and observing that many men were involved and that recordings had been made and circulated.

I want a case analysis that runs in both directions, from theory to case and from case back to theory.

First, take the two positions at their strongest. Devlin's view, as I understand it, is that the immorality of conduct gives society a prima facie right to legislate against it, subject to countervailing considerations of principle and of expediency, because a society is held together by a shared morality and the loosening of that morality endangers the society itself; and that the measure of what the law may prohibit is what the ordinary reasonable person would find intolerable. Hart's view is that the immorality of conduct as such is no reason to criminalise it, that the harm principle does the real work with only a narrow room for paternalism and for offence, and that we must distinguish positive morality, which is whatever a group happens to accept, from critical morality, the standards by which we appraise it. Having stated each, tell me who or what counts as the society whose morality is said to be at stake in this case, and how each theorist would identify it.

Second, sort the majority's reasoning into its parts. Is it legal moralism, or is it paternalism, on the ground that consent cannot authorise harm to oneself; or perfectionism, on the ground that the law may uphold valuable forms of life; or an objectivist account of harm, on which these acts damage the participants' well-being whether or not the participants experience them as damaging? Say which of these readings best fits the judgment, which of them survives Hart's objections, and where the judgment is merely confused between them.

Third, use the patchwork of the law of assault as a diagnostic. Consent is a defence in surgery, sport, tattooing and horseplay, and in one later case a man who branded his initials on his wife's buttocks at her request had his conviction quashed, yet consent was no defence here, or in duelling, or in prize-fighting. Does that pattern track harm, or does it track the social value of the activity, and if the latter, what does that show about whether this is really a case about legal moralism at all?

Fourth, assess a distinction I have devised for the essay and am not sure survives scrutiny: positive legal moralism, on which the bare immorality of conduct is itself a reason to criminalise it, and negative legal moralism, on which immorality is not itself such a reason but removes or weakens a reason that would otherwise exist, for instance by defeating a claim that the conduct falls within a protected sphere of autonomy. Which of the two judgments, the Lords' or Strasbourg's, fits which of these better, and does the distinction illuminate the disagreement or merely redescribe it?

Fifth, treat the later decriminalisation of homosexual conduct in England and Wales as a rough natural experiment for Devlin's claim that moral permissiveness damages the social fabric. What does it do to that claim, and can the claim be restated so as to be immune to such evidence, and if so, is it thereby unfalsifiable?

Finally, tell me whether the case confirms, complicates or refutes each position, and what it exposes that neither theorist anticipated. I am particularly interested in the questions on which the debate seems to be silent: about consent, about disgust, about whose morality is being enforced, and about whether a criminal law of this kind can be administered without bias against unpopular minorities.

Please write throughout in British English, in a formal register, and argue rather than summarise. Do not supply citations or recommend reading; where a name is needed, use it only because the argument requires it. Be explicit wherever the framework runs out and the case has to be described in terms neither theorist supplied.
