I work in patent law, and I have come to suspect that a philosophical question is hiding inside a legal one. Two features of the system pull in opposite directions, and I cannot tell whether the tension is superficial or deep.

On one side, the entire point of a patent is to reward whoever got there first. Rights attach to the first applicant, and the law treats the invention as a discrete act performed by an identifiable individual — a moment of arrival that belongs to somebody. On the other side, the system refuses to grant a patent for anything that would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the art. That person is a legal fiction: a composite of everything already known in the field, stipulated to be incapable of creativity but always capable of the next mechanical step. So the law insists at once that inventions are the achievements of particular people and that a large class of them are things anyone competent would have done anyway.

I want to know what theory of discovery this practice presupposes. Does the priority rule require us to believe that discoveries are not inevitable — that if the first inventor had never lived, the thing would have gone unmade — or can it survive on a thinner view, that discoveries are inevitable but credit should still track who actually did the work? Does the obviousness standard commit the system to a picture in which most of the landscape of invention is determined by the existing state of the art, with only rare genuine leaps? And are these two commitments consistent, or does the practice quietly hold a great-inventor view and a discoveries-are-social-products view at the same time? Cases of simultaneous invention — the calculus, natural selection, the telephone — are usually trotted out as curiosities. I want to know whether they are anomalies the practice must explain away or evidence that its implicit theory is simply false.

Reconstruct the theory the practice actually needs, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if we replaced that theory with the view that invention is inevitable. Tell me how confident you are in each claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
