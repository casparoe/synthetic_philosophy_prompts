I recently took over the second half of an upper-level seminar on science and pseudoscience after the colleague who designed it left mid-term, and I have to write the final exam from scratch. The topic has become uncomfortably personal: my own background is in computational statistics, and a philosopher in my department has taken to arguing at faculty meetings that model-based epidemiology and machine-learning prediction are not genuinely scientific, on the grounds that no single observation can ever refute a probabilistic model. I would like the exam to make students confront that charge instead of reciting the standard Popper-versus-Kuhn narrative.

Please write one essay question for a 45-minute in-class final, together with a model answer of roughly 650 words and a marking scheme. The question must be organized around the following materials, which I intend to hand to the students verbatim as part of the question, so that they cannot simply reproduce a textbook treatment.

A colleague proposes what I will call the liability test for demarcation: a practice is scientific to the extent that it (i) specifies, before seeing the relevant data, an outcome that would count as a refutation of its central claim, and (ii) does not shield that claim from such an outcome by adding or revising auxiliary assumptions, except where the new auxiliary is itself independently testable.

Case A: a pre-registered epidemiological model that outputs continuous risk scores for a population. Its predictions are probabilistic, so no single dataset refutes it, but its analysis plan and calibration targets were fixed in advance, and it can be shown to be badly miscalibrated on held-out data.

Case B: a practitioner of a fringe energy therapy who publishes one sharp, checkable prediction — that a sealed sample of water treated by her device will differ measurably, within an hour, from an untreated control — and who offers to submit to a blinded test.

Ask the students to determine what the liability test says about each case, whether the test is a strengthened version of Popper's criterion or a criterion of a different kind altogether, whether clauses (i) and (ii) can come apart, and whether the whole project of locating a single criterion of demarcation is well-conceived. The crux I want to probe is whether they recognize that clause (ii) is doing work that no purely logical criterion of falsifiability can do, and that it may convert the test into a methodological or even social norm rather than a demarcation line. Students may invoke Popper, Duhem, Quine, Kuhn, or Lakatos if they wish, but the question should be fully answerable without naming anyone.

For the model answer, I want a serious attempt at the crux, not a survey: it should work through both cases, state where the liability test agrees with and diverges from a falsifiability criterion, and give a verdict on the single-criterion project. The marking scheme should consist of five must-hit points, each with the marks it is worth (totaling 20) and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong, followed by an explicit statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer — naming the specific moves each must contain. Assume the students have read Popper and Kuhn but nothing more specialized.

Finally, close with a short note stating how confident you are in the model answer's central philosophical claims and identifying the single point in it you are least sure of.
