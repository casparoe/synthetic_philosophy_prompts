Something thats been bugging me about how AI systems get evaluated, and I think it's a philosophy-of-mind thing in disguise. The way a system earns the label intelligent or conversational is basically behavioral: you put it in a blind chat against a human, judges guess which is which, and if they can't reliably tell, the lab announces human parity and everyone acts like a line got crossed. That's the Turing test with different branding. But the same researchers, often in the same breath, will say no amount of chat behavior settles whether the thing understands anything — it might be a very good next-token predictor — and that the exercise mostly measures how gullible the judges are. Meanwhile the original proposal wasn't really a pass/fail gate at all, more a statistical thought experiment about an interrogator's error rate.

So what does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of mind: is it behaviorism, or a weaker claim that behavior is the only evidence we could ever have, or something functionalist about conversational role? Which rival views does it quietly rule out — say, that understanding requires internal states we could in principle inspect, or that what matters is consciousness and consciousness is untestable? Are the two halves consistent — can you make a behavioral test your official criterion while insisting behavior can't settle the question? Distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it's underdetermined. Then: if you replaced the presupposed view with the rival one, what would actually change — do the competitions and parity announcements stop, or just get renamed? And does it matter that many of the same people happily accept a dog's behavior as evidence of a mind? Finally, if a standards body started certifying assistants as conversant only after fooling judges for ten straight turns, would it inherit the same commitments or add new ones?
