I spent my working life as a geologist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more appetite than method. One question I keep returning to is Molyneux's problem: a person blind from birth learns to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch, then gains sight, and is asked to tell which is which by vision alone. Over the years I have formed verdicts on a set of variants, and I would like to know what principle, if any, underlies them. I am not asking for a literature review or for citations; I want the principle stated precisely, tested against the cases, and honest about where it fails.

My verdicts are these.

1. The classical case. The person has had no visual experience, gains sight, and is shown the two objects. I think they cannot match the seen cube to the felt cube, or the seen sphere to the felt sphere, at better than chance.

2. Size without shape. The same person, blind from birth, has learned by touch to distinguish a large sphere from a small sphere. When they gain sight and are shown the two, I think they can match large to large and small to small almost immediately. Size transfers; shape does not. This is the pair that most troubles me.

3. Shape after calibration. The person from case 1 is given several weeks of sighted life, handling objects while looking at them. Tested again, they can match cube to cube and sphere to sphere. The deficit is not permanent.

4. Late blindness. A person who had normal vision until age six, then lost it for forty years, regains sight. I think they recognize the cube and the sphere at once, or nearly at once.

5. Active versus passive. A person blind from birth, just after gaining sight, is allowed to handle the objects while watching their own hands, and is then tested by vision alone. I think they succeed. If the same person is only allowed to look, without ever having touched the objects while seeing them, they fail. Active exploration seems to do work that passive viewing cannot.

6. The case I keep going back and forth on. A person blind from birth learns shapes through a tactile-vision substitution device -- a camera that drives a grid of vibrating pins against the skin, so that they can read the spatial layout of objects. They become fluent with it. Then they gain sight. Can they recognize the cube and the sphere by sight alone? Some days I think yes, because the device has already given them a spatial code that crosses modalities. Other days I think no, because the device is not vision, and what they learned is still a tactile mapping. I would like your principle to settle this, or to explain why it cannot.

My tentative principle is this: what transfers across the senses is whatever they encode in a common abstract format, and vision and touch share a code for coarse spatial properties such as size and number, but not for shape, because shape is fixed by the specific geometry of edges and surfaces that each sense builds in its own way. Early visual experience lays down the visual shape code, which is why the late-blind person in case 4 recognizes shapes at once, and why the person in case 3 can acquire the mapping with experience. But I cannot get this principle to explain case 2 without calling size coarse and shape fine, which sounds like a difference of degree rather than kind. And it leaves case 5 mysterious: if active exploration succeeds immediately, then the mapping can be established without weeks of calibration, which undercuts the calibration story.

- The substitution-device case (about 200 words): what your preferred principle predicts for the case I cannot settle, and how confident that prediction should be.
- Cases I did not list (about 250 words): two or three further cases your principle speaks to, with its verdicts, chosen to test the principle at its edges.
