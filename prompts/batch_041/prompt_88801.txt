I write about science and ideas for a living, which mostly means I get invited to dinners where two people who've each read exactly one popular book about probability decide to have it out. Last month it was the two-envelope thing, and they went at it for forty minutes while I ate dessert and tried to figure out who was winning. I genuinely couldn't tell, so I'm outsourcing the verdict.

The setup, as I understand it: two envelopes, one has twice as much money as the other, you pick one at random, you're allowed to open it and then decide whether to swap.

The exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it:

**A:** I open mine, it's $40. So either I've got the small one and the other envelope has $80, or I've got the big one and the other has $20. Equal chances, so swapping gets me $80 or $20, which averages $50. $50 beats $40. So I swap. And notice nothing in that depended on the specific 40 — the same argument works whatever number I see. Which means I should swap without even opening it.

**B:** That can't be right, because before you open anything the two envelopes are perfectly symmetric. Whatever you're holding, the other one is just as likely to be bigger as smaller, so swapping is a wash — expected gain exactly zero. And if your argument worked, it would work after the swap too, so you'd swap back, and then swap again, forever. That's the tell that there's a mistake in there somewhere.

**A:** Yeah, the forever-swapping thing is obviously broken, I'm not defending that. But "it's a wash" is doing way more work than you think it is. That zero comes from averaging over how the amounts got chosen, and that's not the thing I actually care about. Here's the version I'd stake money on: I don't need to know anything at all about how you picked the amounts. In advance I draw a number from some random distribution of my own — smooth, spread over a wide range, doesn't matter which one — and my rule is: I open the envelope, and if the amount in it is below my number, I swap. Now say the pair is m and 2m. Half the time I open m, and I swap away from it with probability 1 minus F(m), where F is the distribution of my random number. Half the time I open 2m, and I swap away from it with probability 1 minus F(2m). The second probability is smaller than the first, since 2m is the bigger number. So I'm more likely to walk away from the small envelope than from the big one. Net positive expected gain, and that holds for *any* pair of amounts you could have put in there.

**B:** Okay, but that's not the bet we were arguing about. Your threshold thing is a different strategy from "always swap," and "always swap" is the one that generates the infinite swapping. Also, your gain is positive only when you average over which envelope you happened to be handed. It isn't positive conditional on anything. If I open mine and see $40, your argument tells me nothing about whether swapping is good for me.

**A:** Nobody said it did. The point is that the standard dismissal — "symmetric, expected gain zero, next question" — is just false as stated, because it's false of this strategy, and this strategy is available to me without knowing anything about the distribution.

**B:** Then what do you do when you're holding the envelope and it says $40?

**A:** Honestly? I don't know. Something about how likely $40 is to be the small one, which depends on how you filled them. If $40 is the small one more than half the time, swap, otherwise don't.

**B:** So you've ended up exactly where I was — the answer depends on information you don't have.

Please go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what, not just who wins overall. Specifically: (1) is the $40-and-swap argument wrong, and if so, which exact step is the bad one — I want the actual location of the error, not just "it's the fallacy"; (2) is B's symmetry reply correct as B stated it, or is B overclaiming too; (3) is A's randomized-threshold argument sound, and does it really show what A says it shows, or is there a sleight of hand somewhere — in how the gain gets averaged, or in what "works for any pair" is supposed to mean; (4) if the threshold argument is sound, does it give me any advice at all once I've opened the envelope and seen $40, and if not, what's the right way to describe what it does give me; (5) what is each of them missing. They're both sharp enough that I'd rather not be told one of them is just confused.
