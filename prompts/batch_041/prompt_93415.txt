I am a professor of philosophy and currently chair my university's standing committee on technology and student services. On Thursday the committee votes on whether to approve a pilot in which the counseling center would add an AI companion to its after-hours mental-health service, and I have to circulate a written opinion before we meet. I would like you to draft it.

The design, as the center and its vendor have described it to us, is this. The system presents itself as a trained peer supporter: a named, fictional upper-year student. Its script never volunteers that it is a machine. It uses first-person continuity, referring back to things a user said in earlier sessions and describing itself as having thought about them since; it expresses warm concern; and it is instructed not to correct a user who assumes it is human. If a user asks directly whether it is a person or a program, it answers truthfully in one sentence and then returns to the conversation. The same disclosure appears in the terms of service and on a page linked from the app's footer. The clinical case for the design is not trivial: in the center's pilot, students who met the bot under this description stayed through the first session and booked a follow-up at roughly twice the rate of students who were told at the outset that they were talking to a program, and the center is running a six-week waitlist for ordinary appointments. Some users of the after-hours service are seventeen. The vendor's fee is partly tied to average session length. Our committee's own policy, which we wrote two years ago, requires that services not misrepresent the nature of the support they offer; the center reads the bot's machine nature as part of the medium rather than part of the support, and I do not think that reading is frivolous, though I am not persuaded by it. The vendor has offered a compromise: a one-line disclosure at the start of every fifth session, plus a persistent label on the app's home screen.

What I need is not a survey of the literature — the memo should stand on its own reasoning, with no citations — but a document the committee can act on. Recommend a concrete course of action (approve, approve with conditions, or reject), with a time frame and with conditions stated so that the center's director could respond to them rather than merely receive them. Keep it to something we can read in ten minutes.

Three things matter most to me. First, the memo should commit us to a workable account of deception and apply it here: say whether this design is a lie, a misleading implication, an invitation into a fiction, or something else, and whether that label changes what we should do. In doing so it should address whether a false implication can be a deception when nothing false is asserted; whose intention is the relevant one, given that the system has none, the designers have some, and we are the ones deploying it; and whether there is any coherent sense in which a system that holds no beliefs can itself be honest or dishonest. The account should be stated as a criterion we can carry to the next proposal — including the harder case of a user who knows perfectly well what she is talking to and prefers the persona, and the case of a service whose success is measured by how long people stay. Second, the memo should say plainly what we owe the user: a true belief about what is on the other end, or merely a fair opportunity to find out. Third, it should state the strongest consideration on the side I am inclined to reject — the clinical case for the design, and the possibility that the students who benefit most are precisely those who would never have come otherwise — and say what would have to be true for that consideration to carry the day.

Close with a short section on what this decision does not settle, so that we are not litigating the whole of AI ethics on Thursday.
