Ok so I'm a novelist — three books, the kind that get reviewed in the back pages, no philosophy degree, the last time I read any of it was a gen ed I mostly skipped. I'm writing a book whose whole spine is one choice, and I keep circling a question I don't have the tools for: can a person actually be in a spot where they can't do the right thing, no matter what they pick? A friend with a PhD tells me philosophers mostly say no, or that it's just a confusion about the word 'ought' — something like, if you're required to do each of two things then you're required to do both, but you can't do both, and since you can only be required to do what you can do, one of the requirements was never real. I think thats horse shit, but I'd like to know whether I think that because I've spent fifteen years writing people into corners, or because I'm missing something.

Here's what I want. Take the real debate over whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible — the actual arguments, not the vibe — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I use: characters, scenes, stakes, subtext, what a plot demands, the gap between what a character knows and what the reader knows, the difference between someone who's conflicted and someone who's trapped. Don't dumb it down and don't dress it up. If a philosophers move is really about the logic of a word rather than about people, show me the move in my terms anyway and flag that it's a word problem. No names and no reading list, please — I won't read it.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue. What in their debate has no counterpart in how I think? What did you have to fudge, inflate, or quietly swap out to make it fit? And going the other direction — is there anything I do, some way I make a reader feel the weight of a choice, that their setup has no room for at all?

And give me a verdict on the residue, not just the list. When something won't translate, is that because my vocabulary is missing a real distinction, or because the claim was never as clear as the notation made it look? I suspect mostly the second, and I want you to tell me if that's wrong.

One test case, so I can see whether the translation holds. Current scene: a woman promised her dying husband she'd never put him in a nursing home, and she promised their daughter — the one he hurt, years ago, before the illness — that she'd never leave her alone with him again. He's getting worse. She can keep one promise. How does their machinery carve that up — a dilemma, or just a hard choice where one option is worse — and does the answer change anything I'd actually write on the page?
