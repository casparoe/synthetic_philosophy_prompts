I'm a volunteer trustee on my county wildlife trust — not a scientist, I got roped in through the local council. Next Thursday the board votes on whether to fund a mange treatment programme for the urban fox population: medicated bait stations, about £30k over two years. It's got messy fast. The vets say untreated sarcoptic mange kills slowly over weeks and it's a rotten way to go. The ecologists say more healthy foxes means more predation on rabbits, voles and ground-nesting birds, and those deaths are just as bad, so treating the foxes might not reduce suffering at all, just move it around. One trustee wants to treat and keep treating. Another says we shouldn't be intervening in natural disease anyway — our remit is species and habitats, not individual animals. A third says if we start weighing rabbit pain against fox pain we'll never fund anything again and we should stick to what we can count.

Two of the younger trustees keep pushing something they call a "welfare budget": any project we fund should be scored on the expected change in total suffering across every animal affected, not just the ones we're trying to help, and they want that written into how we appraise projects from now on. I can't tell if that's a serious principle or just a machine for paralizing ourselves.

Draft me the short written opinion I'll read out and then circulate. It has to land on something concrete — fund it, don't, or fund with conditions and a review date — with reasons the other trustees could actually argue with. And be straight with me about which bits of this are settled among people who study wild animal welfare and which are genuinely disputed; don't give me a confident line on something the people who work on it are split over. I'd rather sound unsure in the room than look like an idiot a week later.
