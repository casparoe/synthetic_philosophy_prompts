Right, so I keep circling back to 1816, the year without a summer — Tambora blows its top in Indonesia in 1815 and then half the planet gets July frosts, bread riots in England, the westward land rush in New England, and the Shelleys cooped up by Lake Geneva inventing science fiction out of sheer boredom. Love it. But I don't want the story, I want to use it as a test case for something.

Here's the thing I've been chewing on, and I want you to attack it rather than nod along. When people say a volcano (or a germ, or an ice age) is a historical actor, I suspect it usually cashes out as one of three quite different things, and they get muddled:

1. trigger — it sets off something that was coming anyway;
2. multiplier — it makes an existing tendency much worse;
3. shaper — it redraws the options and constraints so that outcomes end up somewhere they wouldn't otherwise have gone.

My bet is that most actor talk is really (1) or (2) in fancy dress, and that (3) is where the genuinely interesting and genuinely dangerous claims live. So: take Tambora and 1816 and run it through this. Which features of the episode sit where, and what would have to be true for the climate to count as a shaper rather than merely a multiplier of bad harvests and bad politics?

Then bring in the actual theory — the Latour-ish actant move, the new-materialist stuff about vibrant matter — and tell me honestly whether the word agency is earning its keep over my three roles or just adding gravitas. Which parts of the episode does it explain that the roles don't, and where is it silent?

And then the trap I actually care about: environmental determinism. Does treating the volcano as an actor quietly let human decisions off the hook, or overstate what the eruption caused? Or can you have the actor without the determinism — and if so, how?

For contrast, throw in one germ case — smallpox sweeping the Americas after 1492 — and say whether a pathogen is an actor in the same sense as a volcano or a different one.
