I teach a third-year seminar on the emotions, and every year we end up in the same place: I set out the three families — emotions as feelings, as judgments, as perceptions — and by week nine the students have collapsed them into each other, or decided the whole dispute is verbal. This year I want to close the last session with a poem instead of a summary, something I can read aloud in about a minute, and I'd like you to write it. A pantoum please: sixteen lines, four quatrains, with the closing stanza ending on the poem's first line so the whole thing loops back into itself — no first line and no last, which is roughly the point I want made about the question of what comes first.

The episode should be small and I'd rather you kept to it: you're walking home, a dog barks from behind a gate you didn't know was there, and you flinch before you've worked out what the noise even is; ten seconds later you know perfectly well it's a friendly spaniel, and the flinch is still there in your chest, and the dog is still, somehow, a thing to be got past. Second person, so that whoever's listening is the one at the gate — that way the reader is both the person having the thing and the person theorising about it, which is the only position from which the three views get any grip.

The formal job is the whole job: the repeated lines have to be the argument. Any line that comes back should be readable, in one of its appearances, as a claim about the body; in another, as a claim about what you think the situation is; in another, as a claim about what the situation is like. So write them so that none of them settles anything — someone who thinks emotions are feelings should be able to read the whole poem as hers, and so should a judgment theorist, and so should someone who thinks the fear is a perception of the dog's being dangerous. I especially want the line about the flinch still being there after you know better to return a third time; that's the case that makes the judgment view sweat, and I want it in the poem without it being argued for. No technical vocabulary (emotion, appraisal, valence, arousal, intentionality, content) and no names — if it only works once the views are labelled, it's failed. And dont resolve it: I don't want a last stanza telling me the truth is somewhere in the middle. I want to walk out still not knowing whether the flinch is a sensation that gets interpreted, a judgment that gets felt, or a way the dog looks.
