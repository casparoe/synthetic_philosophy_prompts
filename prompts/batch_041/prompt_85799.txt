I spent the better part of forty years as a laboratory scientist — measurements, controls, the occasional retraction — and in retirement I have been reading philosophy of mind, chiefly to test a suspicion that my old field's handling of consciousness was methodologically naive. I have read enough now to see that the dispute I care about is not really about consciousness at all; it is about method, and the two sides have been talking past each other for decades. I would like you to draft the document that an adversarial collaboration between them could actually produce and that both parties could put their names to. I am not asking for a verdict on who is right.

The two camps are these. Camp A defends heterophenomenology, the method associated with Daniel Dennett: a science of consciousness proceeds by taking the subject's utterances as data to be explained, building up the subject's heterophenomenological world from them, and staying officially neutral about what, if anything, those utterances reveal about experience itself. Camp B defends disciplined first-person methods of the kind that train subjects in introspection or phenomenological description, and treats the resulting reports not as behavioural output to be explained but as the primary access to the very phenomenon under investigation.

Both parties concede that the other's programme has produced real findings and that neither side's data are fabricated. What they cannot agree on is what the data are data about.

Please produce the following. First, the bottom line, in a single sentence, up front: the residual disagreement stated in its most exact available form. Second, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign — about what counts as data, about what a report is, about what both agree remains unexplained, and about what neither is committed to. Third, an exact statement of each surviving disagreement, each phrased in wording its own side would accept. Fourth, the cruxes. I take the leading candidates to be: (a) whether heterophenomenology is a method or a metaphysical thesis in disguise; (b) whether a report is an output of the system to be explained or the datum that fixes what is to be explained; (c) whether experience can outrun report — I have in mind no-report designs, such as binocular rivalry where perceptual alternations are read off from eye movements or pupil size rather than a button press — and, if it can, whether the heterophenomenological world excludes such experiences by construction; (d) what would count as a trained observer being wrong, and how one distinguishes refined discrimination from trained expectation; and (e) whether the two programmes are competitors or sequential stages of one enterprise. Revise this list if my reading is off, but do not drop a crux merely because it is hard. Fifth, for each crux, the case, experiment, or argument both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction, recorded in advance. Where a disagreement turns out to be about words rather than substance — I suspect the quarrel over whether the heterophenomenological world should be called a fiction is one — say so plainly and give the translation that dissolves it.

Format: a short unnumbered preamble, then the numbered agreed claims, then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, each side's formulation of its own position, the shared test, and the two predictions. Do not exceed 800 words, table included. I have no use for citations or a reading list; I want the argument, not the bibliography.
