I belong to a reading group that has been working through Rawls's theory of justice, and we keep snagging on a point that I suspect is more than a technicality. One of us insists that the difference principle — the requirement that social and economic inequalities be arranged so that they are to the greatest advantage of the least well off — amounts to nothing more than maximin applied to persons: make the worst-off individual as well off as possible. I don't think that is right, and I want a case that shows the two coming apart, not by cheating but out of the theory's own machinery, so that I can settle the argument properly rather than by trading intuitions. So please construct me a thought experiment to the following specifications. It should be a realistic policy choice in a mid-sized liberal democracy — a reform to labour-market, tax, or welfare arrangements — with exactly two options on the table. Both are to be assessed using a single, fixed index of primary goods (real income will do), and the assessment is to be made behind the veil of ignorance, by parties who do not know which social position they will occupy. Under the first option, the representative member of the least-advantaged social group — say the median of the bottom income quintile, the sort of person Rawls's own procedure would treat as the benchmark of disadvantage — is better off than under the second. But a small minority at the very bottom, a long tail lying below that median, is worse off under the first option than under the second. The second option keeps that tail above a decent minimum while leaving the representative least-advantaged person slightly worse off. I want the gap between the representative and the tail to be wide enough that the verdicts are robust rather than knife-edge, and I want the divergence to fall out of the theory rather than being stipulated.

After the scenario, do the following. State the verdict that the difference principle, as Rawls sets it up, delivers, and the verdict that maximin over actual persons delivers, and explain for each why it delivers that verdict. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work — I take it to be the fact that the theory compares representative members of social groups rather than the worst-off individuals themselves, together with the assumption that raising the position of the representative worst-off person raises everyone below as well — and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed: does it collapse back into agreement between the two readings, or not? Then play the defender: how would someone who thinks Rawls's procedure is correct resist my case — for instance, by arguing that the least-advantaged group should be defined so as to include the tail, or that the assumption just mentioned is empirically safe, or that the parties behind the veil would not choose the second option anyway — and how strong are those replies? Please write the whole answer in American English.
