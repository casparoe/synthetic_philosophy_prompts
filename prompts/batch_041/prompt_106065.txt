Okay, let's do a role-play for my seminar on rational choice. I want you to play a committed defender of imprecise probabilities -- credences come as sets, it can be perfectly rational to sit at "somewhere between 0.3 and 0.7" about a single event and never collapse to one number, and that's a feature, not a bug you're embarrassed about. Hold that line. You can concede that imprecision costs you something somewhere, but never that the whole idea is irrational, and dont bail out into "well, in the limit a good Bayesian converges..." unless you've actually earned it. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep it to a paragraph or two a turn so it feels like a real exchange rather than a lecture.

My opening move. I hand you an urn with 100 balls, red or black, and I tell you only this: the number of red balls is either 30 or 70, and I'm not saying which. No looking inside. So your credence that the first draw is red is the interval [0.3, 0.7]. Fine, no argument there. Now I start drawing with replacement, and it comes up red, red, red, red, red -- say twenty reds in a row. A plain Bayesian just shrugs and says the 70-urn is looking good, updates, and after twenty reds he's very nearly certain. You have to tell me what happens to *your* interval now. And here's the squeeze. If it narrows toward 0.7, then your vaunted imprecision was just ignorance that any Bayesian would have washed out too -- you were a Bayesian all along, just too coy to write the number down. But if it doesn't narrow, then you've built yourself a credence that twenty pieces of evidence, all of it about the very question you claim to be undecided on, can't touch -- and that starts to look less like epistemic humility and more like a refusal to learn. So: which horn do you take, and why isn't this a reductio of the whole program?
