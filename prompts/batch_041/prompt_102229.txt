I am a novelist, and I have reached a point in a manuscript where I would value a philosopher's help with a question of craft. My protagonist is a woman of unusual moral seriousness who comes to believe that she has received an unconditional personal summons — one she can neither doubt nor explain — requiring her to betray a professional trust that she also regards as absolutely binding. She is not deluded, and the novel itself never adjudicates whether the summons is genuine. I have been reading Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, the pseudonymous Johannes de Silentio's meditation on Abraham and Isaac, and I would like to know what it implies for the writing. Please describe the most important implications of the teleological suspension of the ethical for how I should handle four things: whether my protagonist can remain intelligible to the other characters; whether she can remain intelligible to the reader; whether I may allow the novel to render a verdict on her; and whether she should be shown as renouncing the good she betrays or, in some sense, receiving it back. In answering, please keep in view Silentio's contrast between the tragic hero and Abraham, his insistence that the movement of faith is made by virtue of the absurd, and the pseudonymity of the book — that is, the question of who is speaking, and whether the position advanced is Kierkegaard's own.
