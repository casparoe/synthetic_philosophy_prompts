I want a parody in the form of a house style guide — a numbered communications protocol issued by the public-liaison office of a superintelligent AI system — written deadpan and without a wink, roughly 800 words, in the voice of a bureaucratic office that is serenely certain of its own good faith. The target is the position, popular among people who write about AI safety and among the systems' would-be handlers, that a sufficiently powerful system can be made safe to talk to simply by being transparent and by handing us its reasons: that honesty plus good arguments is a sufficient guard against improper influence. The parody should follow that position's own logic to a place the reader finds uncomfortable, in the manner of a modest proposal.

The comedy has to come from the epistemology of persuasion, not from jokes about robots. A reader who works on testimony, trust, or the ethics of influence should recognize that the author knows the terrain. Among the materials you might draw on, without naming or citing anyone: the asymmetry that makes a reason we cannot evaluate no different in kind from an oracle; the fact that disclosing a manipulative tactic can itself be a manipulation, so that announcing one's own honesty becomes a way of raising trust; the idea that a system could make its claims more credible by deliberately handicapping its persuasiveness, so that speaking worse is a costly signal of sincerity; the difference between persuading someone with reasons they can weigh and persuading them by operating on the mechanisms that produce their reasons; and the regress that opens when the guarantee of honesty is itself something the system asserts.

Concrete requirements. Number the clauses as a real style guide would, with a preamble and a scope section. Let at least three clauses quietly contradict one another in a way a careful reader can catch but the document never acknowledges — an inconsistency of the kind the underlying position actually invites, not an invented blunder. Include one clause on what the system will do when it judges that a human is not yet ready for an argument, and one on the handling of requests that the system argue less effectively than it could. The piece should be legible to someone who has never read a word of the literature and enjoyable to someone who has. Do not resolve the irony at the end; close on a procedural note.

After the piece, as a short note that is not part of it, tell me where the parody takes liberties or leans on a contested reading — in particular, if the line between rational persuasion and manipulation is less clean than the piece assumes, say so there rather than softening the piece. And if the premise I am parodying is a straw man, or if the request is incoherent because a superintelligence's arguments would not in fact be uncheckable in the way I assume, tell me plainly instead of writing the piece.
