I am in the final year of a philosophy degree and taking a seminar on mind and machine that keeps returning, with rising irritation on all sides, to one question: whether it is correct to say of a large language model that it believes anything. We have read the standard material, and the discussion has not moved since the third week. I would like you to diagnose the impasse rather than settle it, and I would rather have your reasoning about the shape of the disagreement than a summary of the positions.

Here is the stand-off as it actually appears in the room. On one side are the instrumentalists, following Dennett: to attribute a belief is to adopt a predictive stance, warranted when it lets you anticipate and compress the system's behaviour better than a lower-level description would, and carrying no commitment to inner representational states. On this view, the question whether the system really believes anything is either a category mistake or a request to adopt a stance we have not yet shown to be useful. On the other side are the realists, in a couple of flavours. Some hold that belief requires something like genuine representation with the right causal and inferential profile — sensitivity to evidence, revision in light of it, integration with the agent's other commitments — and that current systems exhibit at best the appearance of this. Others hold that the instrumentalist move is a dodge, since it makes belief-attribution unfalsifiable and evacuates the concept of whatever made it worth having. A third group, mostly the empirically minded, thinks the question is simply handed over to interpretability research and behavioural probing.

My difficulty is that the two main camps appear to agree on all the facts about architecture and behaviour that anyone could establish, and to disagree about what those facts are facts about. The dispute therefore looks immune to evidence, which is why I suspect it is not one disagreement but several. My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis — is that the word "believes" does three jobs in our practice and that both sides assume the three travel together: (i) licensing prediction and compression of behaviour; (ii) marking the system as a member of the rational or normative order, the kind of thing whose states can be assessed for consistency and warrantedness; and (iii) fixing a locus of answerability, something you can address, hold to its commitments, or blame. The instrumentalist answers (i) and treats the rest as rhetoric; the realist answers (ii) and (iii) and treats (i) alone as insufficient for the word. If that is right, part of the impasse is verbal and part is not, and the interesting part is the part that is not.

I would like you to test that hypothesis against what each side actually says, rather than against my summary of it. Is the disagreement verbal — a decision about how to use a word, where either decision is defensible and little turns on the choice — or do the sides mean the same thing by belief and genuinely contradict one another? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, for instance whether the attributed states perform the inferential labour by which belief-attributions are supposed to earn their keep: whether the state is revised on evidence, whether it constrains what else the system will assert, whether it survives irrelevant changes of framing? If a system failed such a battery, would that refute the realist or merely fail to impress the instrumentalist? And what should we make of the fact that these systems avow beliefs in fluent first person — is that testimony, a trained output, or a further datum that neither side can use without begging the question? I am also suspicious that the debate keeps sliding between a predictive question and a practical one about moral status and responsibility, and that some of the heat comes from the second masquerading as the first.

What I want is a diagnosis with reasons: which kind of disagreement this is, or which mixture, why the parties cannot see it that way, and whether their apparent agreement on the facts is genuine or a truce. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a testable claim, a formal result, a decision to stop using one word for three jobs, or a change of question — and be honest about the possibility that no progress is available here because the disagreement is ultimately about what a theory of belief is for. If my three-jobs hypothesis is wrong or trivially true, say so and replace it with a better account of the structure. Please do not give me a reading list; I know the seminar materials and will supply references myself.
