Ok, I'm writing a seminar paper on the wrong kind of reasons and before I start I want to check that my own view actually hangs together, because I have a feeling it doesn't and I can't see where. Roughly what I believe:

(1) reasons for attitudes are the basic kind — reason to believe, reason to want, reason to admire — and reasons for action come downstream of those; more generally I'm a reasons-firster, I think "ought" and "fitting" get explained in terms of reasons rather than the other way round.
(2) what makes a consideration a reason of the right kind for an attitude is that it bears on whether the attitude fits its object — fittingness is what does the normative work.
(3) demon case: if a demon threatens to torture me unless I believe that 2+2=5, the threat gives me no reason at all to believe it, not even a weak one that just gets outweighed.
(4) parallel case: if he threatens to torture me unless I desire a saucer of mud, I think the threat really does give me a reason to desire the mud — a reason to have the desire, not just a reason to bring the desire about.
(5) same for admiration: threaten me unless I admire his painting and I've got no reason to admire it.

My instinct is that (4) is fine because wanting isn't answerable to the world the way believing is, so the demon can get a grip on my desires in a way he can't on my beliefs. But I also think its perfectly rational to take a pill that makes you believe you'll succeed at something, and I can't tell whether that's a problem for (3) or a different kind of thing altogether. Can you tell me which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, how bad each one is, and what the cheapest way out would be? I'd rather not find out in the seminar that I've been helping myself to something.
