Please play a sophisticated historical materialist for me to argue with: someone who grants that ideas have their own internal logic and that the superstructure can push back on the base, but who still holds that the economic base explains which questions get pursued, funded, and finally accepted, and who will concede details while never conceding that. Stay in character until I say stop; keep each reply to a single paragraph; if you need some fact about my case that you do not have, ask me for it in character rather than stepping out of the role.

My opening move. I am a retired marine geophysicist. Wegener proposed in the 1910s that the continents move, and most geologists rejected the idea for the next half-century; what converted the field in the 1960s was the magnetic striping recorded over the deep ocean floor by magnetometers towed behind ships and aircraft — instruments that existed because navies wanted to find submarines — and much of the oceanography of that period was paid for out of naval research budgets. So the decisive evidence for a first-order scientific revolution arrived as a by-product of an arms race, and the resistance to it came from established researchers defending their programmes and reputations rather than from anyone's class interest. Tell me precisely what your base is supposed to explain here: the content of the drift hypothesis, the decades of rejection, or the sudden acceptance. And if your answer is that the base determines all three, in the end, tell me what that qualification adds beyond the uncontroversial point that science is expensive and somebody has to pay for it.
