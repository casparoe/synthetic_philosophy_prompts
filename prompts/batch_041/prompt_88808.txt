I'm in a small reading group that has somehow spent eight months arguing about whether generations are real, and at this point I honestly can't tell if we're disagreeing about anything or just re-enacting the same three moves. I'd like a diagnosis of the arguement itself rather than a side to join.

The three positions, as fairly as I can state them:

Dana comes from the old sociology-of-knowledge line: a generation is a real social formation, not a bucket. A cohort of people who come of age during the same upheaval, share a formative experience in their late teens and early twenties, and recognise each other as having that in common — that's a social kind, the way a class or a profession is. She'll happily admit the birth-year cutoffs are arbitrary, but says vagueness at the border doesn't make the kind fake. Nobody thinks the boundaries of 'the working class' are crisp either.

Marcus does quantitative social science and thinks this is all fog. The only thing you can actually put in a model is cohort membership, and cohort is just period minus age — it's an exact linear function of the other two — so any 'generation effect' you estimate is an age effect, a period effect, or the residual that shows up after you impose some assumption to break the collinearity. Pick a different assumption, get a different generation effect. And the labels themselves (why does Gen Z start in 1997 and not 1994?) come out of marketing shops and then get laundered into surveys.

Priya thinks we're both essentialists and that 'are generations real?' is a malformed question. Once a population gets sorted into a generational category and told about it, it starts behaving like one: the category loops back into the thing it was supposed to describe. So generations are made, and made things can be causally efficacious, and 'real kind' was never the interesting predicate.

Every meeting goes: Marcus asks Dana for one identified cohort effect; Dana says identification is the wrong demand, because a generation was never supposed to be an independent variable you could hold fixed while wiggling something else; Priya says they're really arguing about whether categories have to be discovered rather than made, and Marcus says fine, then show me the making, with numbers; and we all go home.

My hypothesis — and I might be wrong — is that this is two questions wearing one hat. One is about explanation: what would it even mean for a birth cohort to be a cause, as opposed to a label we slap on a bundle of age and period effects? The other is ontological: are these kinds real or stipulated? And I suspect the collinearity point is doing double duty as a technical argument for an ontological conclusion it can't actually carry. There's also a whiff of something political underneath — the generational frame is very convenient if you want to explain away a political difference as kids being kids.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? A verbal dispute about 'generation' versus 'cohort'? A substantive one where somebody has a hidden empirical commitment? Two groups answering different questions under one heading? Or bedrock intuitions about what a social kind has to be, that neither side can argue for? Is the identification problem the real crux or a red herring? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a different question altogether — or should we just accept that it won't move?
