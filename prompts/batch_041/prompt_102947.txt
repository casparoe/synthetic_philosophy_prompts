There is a difficulty for consequentialism that goes by the name of cluelessness. I would like a clear account of the problem, and then an assessment of one proposed way of defusing it that I will state in my own terms.

The problem, as I understand it, is this. A consequentialist is meant to choose the action with the best total consequences. But the consequences of most actions ramify without limit, and the comparison between two candidate actions is often dominated by effects so numerous, so remote, and so tangled that no one could assign them even a sign with confidence. The difficulty is supposed to run deeper than ordinary ignorance about the future: it is not merely that we lack data, but that the quantity the theory tells us to maximize may have no determinate sign for the comparison we actually face.

Now the response. Imagine someone grants all of that but adds the following. The trouble comes only from insisting on the total. A sensible consequentialist will instead adopt a decision rule that looks only at consequences within some finite horizon -- effects on identifiable people over a few generations, or effects foreseeable with reasonable confidence -- and will treat everything beyond that as noise. The far-flung effects, being uncorrelated with which action one chooses, are as likely to favor one option as the other; so disregarding them cannot systematically bias the choice, and it spares the agent an impossible calculation.

I would like you to do three things. First, explain intuitively what makes the original problem serious -- what it is about the ramification of consequences that generates a threat to the theory, as opposed to a familiar and tolerable limit on foresight. Second, distinguish two kinds of cluelessness: the kind that additional information would relieve, and the kind that additional information would not relieve, and say which of the two the horizon response is actually addressed to. Third, evaluate the horizon response itself. In particular, does the appeal to symmetry between far-flung effects hold up, or does it assume something about how those effects are distributed -- for instance, that they cancel rather than accumulate in a common direction -- that the consequentialist has no right to assume? If the symmetry argument fails, say whether any repair of it succeeds, or whether the cluelessness simply remains.
