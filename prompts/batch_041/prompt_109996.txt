For two evenings now I have been arguing with a close friend about a plan he is about to bet his business on, and I am writing to you because I lost the argument and cannot reconstruct why I still believe I should have won it. He runs a nine-person architecture practice in which three people, himself included, make the judgments clients actually pay for. He intends to grow to forty without hiring anyone else of his own caliber. The plan is this. He will write down how he makes the hard calls — when a room needs more daylight before it stops feeling mean, when a client's stated wish should be overruled because it will make the building worse, how much of a budget to hold back for the problem nobody has seen yet. New hires will then be trained on a few hundred of his past decisions together with the reasons he attached to them, until they can produce, on unfamiliar projects, what he would have produced; they will be tested by being handed projects he has already completed and having their answers compared with his. Where a judgment is too large for one person, it will be cut into parts small enough for one person, with a more senior colleague checking each part and joining them back up; that checker will be trained the same way, from the decisions of someone above him, and so on up the line. He says the arithmetic is irresistible: one good judgment-maker plus a process that reproduces him is worth ten of him, doing it twice is worth a hundred, and the practice can therefore be as good as he is at any size. He has already promised six new hires that it works. He adds that medicine, the law, and the army have run on something like this for centuries.

I could not say what I thought was wrong, beyond a general sense that something gets lost and that the loss stays invisible until it is expensive. So I would like you to settle the argument for me. If he is right, tell me what would have to be true of him, of the training, and of the checking for the thing to survive to the third and fourth generation rather than quietly degrade. If he is wrong, tell me exactly where the rot sets in: whether judgment of this kind resists being cut into pieces small enough for one junior to handle, whether imitating a person's decisions leaves behind the reasons that made those decisions good, whether a mistake made at one level is the sort that gets compounded rather than caught by the level above, and whether matching his answers on projects he has already finished tells us anything at all about the projects he has not seen. Then tell me what to say to him on Thursday, when he wants my money and my blessing.
