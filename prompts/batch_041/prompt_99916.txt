You are a poet with a working knowledge of microeconomics and the economics of information, writing for readers who know the theory of information cascades well enough to catch a mistake. Before you draft anything, jot a short plan — a few lines mapping the features of the model onto the mechanics of the poem — and only then write.

I want a pantoum about information cascades and rational herding. The setting: an open-plan office where a single shared dataset may contain a systematic error. The staff learn of it one at a time, and each in turn must decide whether to raise the alarm aloud. The first two have each actually inspected the numbers and have a private reason to be worried; everyone after them is inferring only from the fact that no one before them has spoken. Let the speaker be one of the later arrivals — someone who can feel their own private doubt being quietly overruled by the silence around them.

The form is a pantoum: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain recur as the first and third lines of the next, so every stanza must carry forward the previous stanza's later half, and a closed pantoum folds its last lines back into its opening. Use that machinery to do the argument — the recurrence of lines should be the recurrence of other people's actions, so that by the middle stanzas the speaker's own private signal is being crowded out by lines they did not choose. Five to seven quatrains; rhyme as you like, or not at all.

Three things the poem has to get right, because they are the whole point and a generic poem about "following the crowd" will fail them:

1. The conformity is rational, not stupid. Each person is right to discount their own signal once enough public actions point the other way; the unease comes from sensible updating producing a false consensus. Make a reader feel the good sense of staying quiet.
2. The cascade is fragile, not stable. It rests on the actions of the first few, and a single new public signal can shatter it. Let the form break exactly once, at the moment the cascade does — one place where the pantoum's rule of recurrence is violated, so the fracture in the poem is the fracture in the consensus.
3. Keep the distinction the theory turns on: herding is merely correlated behavior, whereas a cascade is a situation in which it is optimal to ignore one's own information. The poem should let a reader see which of these the office is in.

Do not name any economists, studies, or dates, and do not cite anything; work from the model itself. No line should sit in quotation marks unless it is the speaker's own speech.

After the poem, add a short prose note of roughly 200 words in three parts: (a) where the poem idealizes the model — for instance, what it assumes about sequential decisions, common priors, or discrete signals that a real office would not deliver; (b) which reading of the theory it leans on and where that reading is contested, including if it treats "cascade" and "herd" as closer than they are; and (c) what would change the poem's verdict — which consideration or piece of evidence would make you write the speaker's situation differently. Be candid about the weakest joint in the reasoning, and do not hedge the poem itself into mush to protect it.
