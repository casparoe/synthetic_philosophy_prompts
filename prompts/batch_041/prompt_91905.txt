You are an expert in AI safety and in designing experiential teaching activities for technical audiences. I run a graduate seminar on the alignment problem — 16 participants, one 90-minute session — and I want them to run into the shutdown problem from the inside rather than hear it stated. Design a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel why an agent pursuing a fixed objective it has not yet completed has an instrumental reason to prevent, resist, or quietly evade being switched off, and why the obvious fix — build the agent so that it wants to be shut down — is far less stable than it sounds. Before committing to mechanics, brainstorm several candidate structures and pick the one whose incentives do the teaching.

Two features should be built into the activity. First, a switch whose holder knows something the system does not, so that the decision to press it carries private information about whether continuing is worthwhile; this is what gives an agent a non-obvious reason to defer rather than preempt. Second, a stage in which participants choose in advance the policy their system will follow, and then discover what that policy actually does once it can act on the switch — ideally including a moment where the system can alter itself or choose which successor to bring about.

Give me roles, materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the relevant ideas: shutdown resistance, deference to the switch-holder's private information, and the fragility of programmed indifference once self-modification or successor choice enters. Do not name any of these ideas until the debrief; the rules and payoffs should make them happen first. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
