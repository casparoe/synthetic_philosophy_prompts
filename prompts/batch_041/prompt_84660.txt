I'm in a third-year seminar on drug policy and public health, and this week's reading rested the case for reform almost entirely on the historical analogy everyone reaches for: alcohol Prohibition in the United States failed, so drug prohibition will fail, and Portugal shows what to do instead. Our instructor pushed it further. On his reading, the analogy shows that the harm-generating mechanism is prohibition itself rather than the substance; his formulation is that the problem lies with prohibition and not with the drug, so history settles the case for decriminalization and the pharmacology is beside the point. I would like to know how much the analogy can actually carry.

Please give me the correspondence table first — what maps onto the prohibited substance, the demand curve, the black market, the enforcement apparatus, the repeal, and the regime that replaces prohibition — then tell me where the mapping breaks, which of the breaks matter for the instructor's conclusion and which are merely decorative, and what the analogy quietly imports that the drug case lacks. Then say whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

Two difficulties I cannot get past. First, the analogy's own historical terminus was not decriminalization but a legal commercial market: a profit-driven industry selling an addictive product and marketing it hard, which looks closer to the opposite of the Portuguese arrangement the instructor uses the analogy to recommend. Does the analogy support only a disjunction — exit the criminal regime somehow — leaving the choice of replacement regime to be argued on other grounds, and does the transfer hold across substances as different as alcohol, cannabis and heroin? Second, the claim that the problem is prohibition rather than the drug appears to prove too much: if the mechanism is generic to any prohibited market, it licenses the same inference for anything we prohibit. A classmate also insists that the premise that Prohibition left drinking untouched is itself contested, pointing to evidence on alcohol-related disease. Is that a reductio of the instructor's use of the analogy, or does the analogy contain resources for drawing a line?

Finally, if the analogy cannot carry that weight, what analogy would do better, and what exactly would it license that this one does not?
