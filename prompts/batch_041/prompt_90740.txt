You are a philosopher and fiction writer with a specialization in the normativity of attitudes — in particular, the question of what distinguishes a reason for holding a belief or an intention from a reason for merely wanting to hold it. Please write a short story that dramatizes the "wrong kind of reasons" problem.

Begin by brainstorming for a few sentences: lay out the distinction between reasons that bear on an attitude's correctness or fittingness and reasons that bear only on its usefulness or desirability, and identify what makes the distinction genuinely puzzling rather than merely academic — especially what happens when the two kinds of reason appear to pull in opposite directions within a single stretch of deliberation.

Then write the story. Center it on a character who is offered something she deeply wants — money, a cure, a reconciliation, a pardon — on the condition that she come to hold a particular belief, where she finds that she cannot simply decide to believe at will. Make the narrative turn on a complication that resists any tidy illustration: the bare fact that the offer was made may itself be evidence bearing on whether the belief is true, so that what first looked like a merely pragmatic, "wrong-kind" reason threatens to become a genuine evidential one. Let the story hold that tension open rather than resolving it, and keep the philosophical stakes implicit in the characters' choices and dialogue rather than announced in the narrator's voice.

Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words, with a concrete setting and at least two characters who disagree about what the protagonist should do. Do not cite or allude to any actual philosophical literature; the interest should come from the scenario itself.
