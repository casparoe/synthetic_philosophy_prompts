Draft the joint statement that a hedonic monist and a hedonic pluralist could both sign after a real adversarial collaboration on the heterogeneity of pleasure. The monist holds that every episode deserving the name pleasure shares a single common property — one thing that makes it a pleasure rather than a pain, whether that thing is a distinctive felt quality, a hedonic tone, or a functional role — and insists the one thing is genuinely present. The pluralist holds that pleasures share no single common property: pleasure names a family of states bound by overlapping resemblances, and every attempt to isolate the one thing either gerrymanders the cases or says something so thin that it excludes clear pleasures or admits clear pains. Treat both as fully competent; neither is to be handed the easy win, and neither is to be talked past.

Anchor the collaboration in the following shared case-file, and take the cases as stipulated rather than as empirical findings:

1. A subject reports that every experience has the same neutral feel — a flat hum — yet she still prefers some activities to others, pursues them, and says she wants them. Does she feel pleasure?
2. A creature whose hedonic wiring is inverted relative to ours: the sensation we get from biting a lemon, mildly unpleasant for us, is for it the vehicle of intense delight, and the sensation of being stroked, delightful for us, is for it mildly unpleasant — though both describe the sensations' non-hedonic qualities (sour, ticklish) identically.
3. A person who seeks out and savors sensations that, considered as bare bodily feelings, are painful — the burn of chili, the ache of a long climb, the sting of cold water — and who calls the same feeling unpleasant when it arrives unbidden.
4. A pill that makes whatever one is currently undergoing feel good without altering the experience's content in any other respect.
5. Experiences that are, at one and the same time, pleasant and unpleasant: bittersweetness, ambivalent nostalgia, the laughter that hurts.

A third colleague proposes that pleasure is best treated as a determinable with many determinates — as color stands to red and blue — so that there need be no common phenomenal stuff, yet the kind remains real. Say whether this dissolves the dispute, relocates it, or merely relabels it.

Produce, in this order: (a) a numbered section of agreed claims — about the phenomenology, about how the word pleasure is and ought to be used, and about what would count as having found the common property if there is one — each claim doing real work rather than merely restating the others; (b) a table of the genuine disagreements, each side's position phrased in wording that side would accept; (c) the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each crux a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down; (d) an explicit verdict on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and which are substantive, with reasons.

Then append a short closing section, What would move us, giving for each side the single argument or finding that would lead it to abandon its position, and a candid note on where this document is most likely to fail — above all, where you suspect you have drawn the line between genuine and merely verbal disagreement in the wrong place.

Argue from the cases and considerations themselves; include no citations or references to published work.
