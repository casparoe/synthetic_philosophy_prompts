I am a first-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I keep meeting the same conversational move. Someone will say, in effect, that Darwinism is a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable scientific theory, and then treat that as having settled whatever was actually under discussion. I have seen it used three quite different ways in the last month: to dismiss a demand that a particular adaptationist story be backed by evidence; to argue about whether evolutionary biology belongs alongside physics as a science; and, most awkwardly, by a friend from my old church who takes it to show that design and selection are on an equal footing because neither can be tested. The line is usually credited to Popper, and I have heard that he later partly softened it, though I have never seen the details and I do not want to build anything on that hearsay. What I need is not a ruling on what Popper really meant, but a careful map of what that one sentence could be claiming, so that I can tell which version is being invoked on each occasion and whether it genuinely does the work it is being made to do there.

Please lay the readings out as a menu, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and give a verdict on each. Among the candidates I would want covered: that the principle of natural selection, taken by itself and apart from any particular application, is unfalsifiable because it collapses into something close to the claim that survivors survive; that it is unfalsifiable only in the innocuous way any high-level framework is, and that the charge therefore costs evolutionary biology nothing; that it is unfalsifiable in principle, which is a genuine defect; that it is perfectly falsifiable in form but that its central claims have not in fact been subjected to serious test; that it is metaphysical in a technical and respectable sense, namely a body of unfalsifiable but still criticisable background assumptions that generate testable problems; that it is metaphysical in the pejorative sense, untestable and therefore not science at all; that the slogan is really a claim about the logical form of a historical science as opposed to a nomological one; and that it is a remark about heuristic fertility, or about what can usefully be taught, rather than about truth or scientific status. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and say which of my three conversational contexts it would actually support. I would also like you to say, separately, what difference it makes if Popper eventually allowed that selection under a sufficiently specified problem situation might be predictable after all — whether that concession defuses the slogan, narrows it to one of the readings above, or leaves the more radical readings untouched. Please do not give me a reading list; I want the argumentative structure, not the literature.

End the whole reply with a single line of the form \answer{...}, containing the short label of the one reading you judge both most defensible on its merits and most likely to be what people are actually reaching for when they repeat the slogan.
