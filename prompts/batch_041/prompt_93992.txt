Ok so I've been chewing on the dispositions literature for a while now, finks, masks, mimics, the whole menagerie, and I've landed on a view I'd like you to try and take apart.

Here's my position. The conditional analysis (x is disposed to M when C iff x would M if C) is basically right, and the counterexamples everyone keeps lobbing at it aren't counterexamples at all. They're just cases where we've been sloppy about indexing. My claim is that every disposition ascription is implicitly indexed to two classes of circumstances: a trigger class, i.e. the circumstances in which the manifestation is supposed to happen, and a non-interference class, i.e. the circumstances under which nothing is allowed to get in the way. Finks are cases where the trigger itself alters the base; masks are cases where something outside the non-interference class is doing the work; mimics are cases where the manifestation happens but not via the route the ascription picks out. Specify both classes properly and the biconditional comes out true every time. So: no counterexample, just a more careful formulation. Call it the two-class view if you need a name for it.

What I want is a map of what would count against this. Not the stock objections, I already know those. Specifically:

- Are there cases the two-class view genuinely can't describe, rather than just describe in an unfamiliar way? Which ones, and why do they resist the indexing move?
- The triviality worry. It looks like specifying the non-interference class requires already knowing what disposition we're ascribing, and if the classes can be gerrymandered freely then the biconditional is true by construction and unfalsifiable. Is there a non-arbitrary restriction on legitimate classes, or does the view just have to eat this? If it has to eat it, is that fatal or tolerable for a view of this kind?
- Mimics feel like the real threat to me, since they run the conditional in the other direction. Does indexing actually help there, or am I just relabelling?
- Does the view force dispositions to be extrinsic/relational all the way down? If so, what does that cost me, in terms of the intuition that fragility is intrinsic, and in terms of individuating properties?
- What does it stand or fall with elsewhere: counterfactual truthmakers, laws, the dispositional/categorical distinction, whether a pure dispositionalism is still viable?

Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a committed defender would concede as counting against them rather than dismiss. If it turns out the view is well insulated, say so plainly and tell me whether thats a problem.
