I am setting the final examination for an upper-level seminar on Plato's ethics and political philosophy. The exam is a single long essay, written in ninety minutes, and I want it to turn on Plato's treatment of drunkenness rather than on any passage my students will have rehearsed. A question that simply invites them to summarize the Athenian's proposal that a well-regulated drinking party serves as a test of courage would let them coast on lecture notes, so I want the crux to be sharper than that.

What I want the question to force is a confrontation between two roles that wine plays in the Laws, which I would like the students to reconstruct and name for themselves. In the first role, wine is a diagnostic instrument: because it loosens shame and inflates confidence, it lays bare the drinker's settled disposition, so that a sober observer can read off what the drinker is really like — an unmasking, akin to exposing a man to danger in order to see whether he is in fact brave. In the second role, wine is an educational instrument: the Athenian folds convivial drinking into his broader account of education as the early and lifelong training of pleasure and pain, so that a rightly conducted symposium is itself a schooling in moderation, not merely a window onto it.

The question should press students on whether these two roles can be held together. If the point of drinking is to reveal a character that is already fixed, then the drinking party is a test and not a training; if the point is to shape character, then what the drinker displays is not his settled self but a self still under construction, and the diagnostic reading begins to look unstable. I want students to reconstruct the distinction in their own terms, decide whether Plato can consistently hold both roles, and defend that decision against the strongest objection from the other side.

Please write, in order: (1) the essay question itself, stated so that it names the tension without resolving it; (2) a model answer of about 600 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20, built around four or five must-hit moves that separate an A from a B answer, naming for each move the specific thing a strong answer does and the specific way a weaker answer tends to miss it. The question should not require students to cite, quote, or bring in any secondary literature; it must be answerable from a close reading of the relevant books and from reasoning alone.
