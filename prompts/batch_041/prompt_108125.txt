I recently took a job at a small development charity, I write grant applications and evidence summaries, and the more I look at what we actually do the less it hangs together. Officially we're institutionalists: the whole pitch to donors is that poverty is caused by bad institutions, that geography and culture are mostly excuses, so we fund rule-of-law projects, anti-corruption work, property rights. But the actual grantmaking runs on RCTs — cash transfers, deworming, bednets — and whenever I ask whether one of the governance projects actually worked, the answer is some version of "you can't randomize a legal system." So what are we presupposing here? That there are two separate kinds of causal claim, local and macro, one of which gets tested and the other of which is just known? That "institutions" and "geography" are distinct additive factors rather than the same thing counted twice (bad geography produces bad institutions produces...)? And what about the standard move of instrumenting a country's institutions today with its colonial settler mortality — does using history that way presuppose that history is basically a natural experiment we can read off like a trial, and if so does that survive the complaints I keep hearing that the instrument is broken anyway? Please take the practice apart: what view of causation, of evidence, and of what a "fundamental cause" is does it commit us to, which rival views does it quietly rule out, and what's the strongest thing an anti-institutionalist — or someone who thinks we simply can't know why some nations are rich — could say we're smuggling in? Also, honestly: is the split between our donors' theory and our grantmaking actually incoherent, or can you hold both without contradiction? I'd like to know what's strictly presupposed versus what we could drop without changing anything we do.
