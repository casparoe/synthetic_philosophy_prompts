I'm a third-year student in a joint philosophy and economics programme, and I'm currently in a graduate seminar in decision theory and formal epistemology that is co-listed with the economics department. I've been asked to prepare the handout for the week we take up the value of information, and the professor wants something students can actually work through during the break rather than a summary to read. I'd like you to take the role of someone who has taught this material for years and has thought carefully about exactly where the standard argument does and does not go through — not someone reciting a literature. The standard argument, the one our notes call Good's theorem, is that for a Bayesian agent who maximizes expected utility, information that costs nothing can never lower her expected utility, because she is always free to ignore it and act exactly as she would have without it. I can reproduce that proof on an exam. What I want from you is the other half of the picture, as a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree that takes a reader from a short series of commitments she can answer for herself to a named position on the question 'can information ever be bad for the person who gets it?'. Please build it out of the logical space rather than out of a list of authors — decide first what the genuine fault lines are, and let the labels come at the leaves. To be clear about my purposes, the distinctions I care about are (i) whether the agent is a standard expected-utility maximizer at all, (ii) whether her utility depends on what she believes as well as on what happens, (iii) whether her preferences or her menu of options shift when she learns something, (iv) whether the theorem's escape hatch — that she can simply ignore what she learns — is genuinely available to her, and (v) whether we are asking about her own welfare or about the welfare of everyone affected. Every branching node should be phrased as a question with a concrete answer, so that a reader can work her way down without already knowing which position she is heading for.

Please thread a specific set of cases through the tree, since I want to use them as the questions at the nodes and as illustrations at the leaves: the patient deciding whether to be tested for an untreatable condition she would rather not know about; the juror who knows she would decide differently if she saw the evidence that has been ruled inadmissible; the football supporter who has recorded the match and would enjoy it less if she learned the score first; the taster whose pleasure in a wine drops once she learns its region; the investor who buys a forecast that tells her nothing about whether the asset is worth more to anyone, only about who will be holding it when; and the person who has been told in advance that she will not be able to stop herself from acting on whatever she learns. The leaves should include, at a minimum: the unrestricted reading on which information is never bad for the agent, full stop; the view that only the cost or the timing of acquisition can make it bad; the view that it is bad because utility itself depends on belief; the view that it is bad because preferences or the option set are not stable across learning; the view that the theorem simply fails once we drop expected utility, for instance for an agent who goes by the worst case or who minimizes regret; the view that observing is itself an act with evidential significance, so that the premise about ignoring the signal is beside the point; and the view that information can be bad for the collective even when it is good for each individual, say by destroying the risk-sharing that made everyone better off. I am also happy to be told that one of these is a non-position: if the right answer is that the question is confused until the utility, the option set and the time of evaluation are fixed, make that a leaf and defend it.

Format: nine to fourteen nodes, six to nine leaves, each leaf carrying a name (or 'unnamed' if nobody occupies it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, say so and tell me whether that is because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; where two leaves cannot be separated by any question in the tree, flag them rather than papering over the gap. One house rule for the handout: no names, dates, or reading lists anywhere in it, and no bibliography — I can't check attributions and I don't want the tree to be answerable by recalling what somebody famous said. If a position has a standard label, give me the label and define it in your own words; if it has none, invent one and define it. Before you draw anything, spend a moment laying out the logical space explicitly, so I can see the questions you considered and rejected. And please commit: at the end, tell me which leaf you actually think is the correct position, why the others fail, and how confident you are in that verdict. If you conclude that two of my required leaves are the same position in different clothes, say which and merge them; if you think the whole exercise only has an answer relative to a fully specified decision problem, take that line and defend it rather than hedging.
