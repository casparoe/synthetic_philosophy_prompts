For a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my own position before we take up the incompleteness theorems, and I'd rather discover the fault lines myself than have them discovered for me. The request comes at an awkward moment, because I've spent the past month arguing with a friend who works on machine learning about whether Gödel vindicates or embarrasses the idea of machine intelligence, and I've noticed that the answers I give him don't always sound like they come from the same person. So here is where I've landed, and I'd like you to tell me whether it hangs together. (1) I'm a realist about arithmetic: arithmetical sentences have determinate truth values independent of what anyone can prove. (2) I take the incompleteness theorems to be, at bottom, claims about provability rather than about truth — they establish that in any consistent, recursively axiomatizable system strong enough to represent arithmetic, provability and truth come apart, and nothing more metaphysically dramatic than that. (3) Following from (2), I think the philosophical mileage commonly extracted from the theorems is largely spurious; in particular, the argument that a mind able to appreciate the truth of a system's Gödel sentence must thereby transcend any machine — the argument usually associated with Lucas and Penrose — seems to me a mistake. (4) I'm a computationalist about the mind: I see no in-principle obstacle to a machine possessing our cognitive capacities. (5) But I also think human mathematical practice is essentially open-ended — at any stage we can recognize the soundness of further reflection principles and move to a stronger system, so that our practice is never captured by any single formal system, which is why I say the incompleteness theorems do not really apply to us. (6) And I hold that we do know arithmetic to be consistent, even though the second theorem rules out a finitary consistency proof in Hilbert's sense; our assurance is reflective rather than formal.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these six actually pull against one another, which pairs only look inconsistent but in fact sit together comfortably, and for the genuine conflicts, how severe are they, what is the cheapest revision that would restore consistency, and what would that revision cost me? Please order the real tensions by severity rather than offering an undifferentiated list, and tell me which single change would clear up the most.
