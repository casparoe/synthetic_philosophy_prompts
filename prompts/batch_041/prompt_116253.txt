My ethics class has been stuck for weeks on the toxin puzzle. A perfectly reliable predictor tells me tonight that it will pay me a million dollars if and only if I genuinely intend, tomorrow, to drink a vial that will make me violently ill for a day; the payment depends on the intention alone, and actually drinking gains me nothing. One half of the class holds that the intention is simply unavailable to me: I can want the money, and I can wish I were the sort of person who would drink, but I cannot intend an act I know to be pointless merely because intending it would pay, any more than I can believe something for a cash reward. The other half holds that the intention is plainly available, since people talk themselves into all sorts of states and a hypnotist could manage it, and that if it is available the case exposes a real gap between what it is rational to do and what it is rational to intend. A few hold that the case cannot be set up at all. Nobody has moved, and I suspect the stalemate is not really about the psychology, though I cannot say what it is about.

Diagnose it. Is this a hidden empirical dispute about what we can bring ourselves to intend; a conceptual one about what an intention is and whether a payment can be a reason for one; a normative one about whether rationality assesses single choices or whole policies; or two parties answering different questions under a single name? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a finding, a change of question — or why none should be expected.
