I write a column on science and ideas, and I keep meeting the same argument from people who want the rough-and-tumble of online argument left alone: the internet is a marketplace of ideas, and the remedy for bad speech is more speech, because in a market the better product drives out the worse. The metaphor is not decoration in that argument; it is the load-bearing premise. It is why they conclude that no gatekeeping, no platform intervention, and no regulation is needed, and why they treat calls for moderation as a kind of price-fixing. I would like an audit of the metaphor rather than another round of approval or denunciation.

Begin with the correspondence, set out explicitly. What plays the role of the buyer, the seller, the good on offer, the price, the profit motive, the invisible hand that sorts quality from junk, the regulator, and the bankruptcy that clears out the bad actors? Then tell me where the mapping fails. I am interested in which of the failures matter for the work the metaphor is being asked to do — licensing the conclusion that more speech is sufficient — and which are merely decorative differences that a defender could wave away. In particular, I want to know what the market picture quietly imports that the information ecosystem lacks: a buyer who can inspect the goods, a price that aggregates dispersed private knowledge, a payoff for being right, a repeat-purchase discipline on sellers, and an assumption that participants are at least trying to sell something true rather than merely to win.

Two attempts to patch the metaphor are circulating in my notes, and I would like you to say whether either of them rescues it or instead concedes that it was never a market at all. The first comes from an economist I interviewed: the information ecosystem, he says, is a market in which quality cannot be inspected before purchase and often not after, so the bad drives out the good — a market failure, not a market. The second comes from a media critic: speakers should be treated like manufacturers, who bear liability for the harm their defective products cause, so that the cost of a false claim falls on the person who made it rather than on the reader. If the second is right, does the marketplace metaphor still support the conclusion it was invoked to support, or has it quietly become an argument for regulation?

Finally, address the question my readers keep raising about the other side: whether the people making the bad claims are arguing in good faith. Does the market metaphor presuppose good faith, or does it claim to work regardless of it? If a large share of the sellers are not truth-tracking at all, does the invisible hand still sort, and if it does not, does the failure indict the metaphor or the participants? Close by saying whether the marketplace is the wrong vehicle for the thought, and if so what would be a better one.
