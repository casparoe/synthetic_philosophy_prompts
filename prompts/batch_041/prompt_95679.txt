I spent the weekend re-fighting with an old friend the argument we have been having for years, and we got nowhere. He was a conscript in a war he came to think was unjust, and his position is that a soldier who fights in an unjust war is not doing anything wrong: he does what he was ordered to do, the other side's soldiers are in exactly the same position, and the point of the laws of war is that both sides get treated alike, so that everyone is a little safer. My position is that contributing to an unjust threat is wrong, that duress can excuse the conscript without justifying him, and that excusing and justifying are different things — so the two sides are not in the same boat, however pitiable both are.

What is maddening is that we agree on every case we try: the draftee, the volunteer who joined for the tuition money, the soldier who knows the war is unjust and could desert and doesn't, the defender on the other side whose town is being shelled. We agree about who should be punished, who deserves sympathy, what the law should say. Then he makes a move I cannot answer: he says the moral equality of combatants was never a claim about individual wrongdoing at all, but a convention — something we adopt because of what happens to people when we don't — and that I am applying a standard of deep moral fact about who is liable to be killed that the convention was never trying to track.

My hypothesis is that we are not really disagreeing about whether the conscript acts wrongly. I suspect we are answering different questions under one name, and that the word doing the damage is "wrong": he is asking what rule we should hold one another to, I am asking what is true of the man. But it may instead be that we disagree about which facts count as evidence about morality at all — he treats the possibility of armies functioning as data, I treat intuitions about individual cases as data — or that this is bedrock and nothing will move it.

Diagnose it for me, in American English, with no reading list or citations. Structure the answer as follows: "The verdict in brief" (about 100 words, your diagnosis stated flatly); "The two positions, restated" (about 200 words, saying each of us back more carefully than we said ourselves, and correcting my framing if it is wrong); "The candidate diagnoses" (about 500 words, running through the possibilities — a verbal dispute, two questions under one name, a disagreement about which cases count as evidence or about what moral theory is for, conflicting bedrock intuitions, a hidden empirical or conceptual question doing the real work — and citing what each of us actually said that supports each one); "The diagnosis I would defend" (about 400 words, choosing one and arguing for it); and "What would count as progress" (about 250 words, on whether a distinction, a change of question, or a new case could move us, or whether nothing should be expected).
