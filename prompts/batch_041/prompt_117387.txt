For thirty-odd years I built and stabilized precision instruments — diode lasers locked to high-finesse cavities, a dilution refrigerator whose temperature loop I never entirely trusted, a few interferometers that taught me the difference between a system that is quiet and one that is merely slow — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than method, lately in the philosophy of action. I have just come across Kavka's toxin puzzle for the first time, and I find that I can hold it in my head only by translating it into the language I actually think in: state variables and initial conditions, open-loop versus closed-loop policies, setpoints, latching and hysteresis, metastable configurations, objective functionals that are not stationary in time, and the distinction between the plant and the controller. I would like you to perform that translation properly, and then tell me what did not survive it. Restate the puzzle itself, and then the principal replies to it — the resolute line I have seen associated with Gauthier, the reply that one simply cannot sincerely intend an action one is certain one will not perform, and the suggestion that the puzzle reveals something about the agent's character or dispositions rather than about the rationality of any particular act — each one rendered as faithfully as you can manage in the vocabulary of someone who designs feedback systems, without quietly smuggling in my framework's assumptions and calling the result a translation. Keep the vocabulary honest in particular with respect to commitment: in my trade that word means something that is enforced by hardware, because anything that can be unlatched eventually will be, and I want to see whether the philosophers' sense of it survives contact with that suspicion. For calibration of my fluency: I am comfortable with the bench-level and engineering version of this vocabulary — gain, phase margin, dead time, limit cycles, bang-bang control, why a bistable element is what you reach for when you need a decision to stick — but I am not a mathematician, so no measure-theoretic or category-theoretic apparatus, and no formal proofs I would have to take on faith.

Then give me the residue report, and treat the residue as the interesting part. I want to know which pieces of the puzzle and of the replies map onto my vocabulary cleanly, which map only if I accept a distortion (and what the distortion costs me), and which have no counterpart at all. Four things in particular I would like you to sort out. First, whether the intention formed at midnight is best rendered as a parameter of the objective functional — something that changes what counts as the optimum at noon — or merely as an estimate of what the controller will do at noon, since those two readings seem to give opposite verdicts and I suspect the philosophers slide between them. Second, whether anything in the debate requires a single agent spanning both times in a sense that a non-stationary objective function cannot capture, or whether the insistence on one deliberator across time is doing work that my framework has no room for. Third, what to make of the claim that one cannot intend what one is certain one will not do: is that a dynamical impossibility, a design constraint on admissible controllers, or a norm with no dynamical reading whatsoever — and if the last, what kind of thing is it? Fourth, whether the billionaire is best understood as an observer running a predictive model of my controller, and whether that framing dissolves the puzzle or merely relocates the difficulty.

Finally, give me a verdict rather than a survey. Is the residue evidence that my vocabulary is missing something that matters, or evidence that the original puzzle is less deep than it looks — an equivocation between a causal sense of commitment and a normative one, propped up by a picture of the self as a single deliberator rather than a sequence of decision points? My own instinct, after a career of building instruments, is that the puzzle is really about commitment devices and that the philosophy is an attempt to obtain the effect of a latch without the latch; tell me whether that instinct is an artifact of the translation I have imposed, or whether it is tracking something the philosophical literature has genuinely left unresolved.
