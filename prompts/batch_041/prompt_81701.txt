I just got roped into the grant-allocation committee at a small local foundation -- we've got about $200k to split among food banks, a legal aid clinic, and a couple of youth programs, and I've been arguing that we should care more about helping the worst-off applicants than about maximizing total benefit. Somewhere along the way I realized I've basically become a prioritarian: the worse off someone is, the more a marginal improvement to their life counts, morally speaking. Now I want you to try to talk me out of it. What would count aginst prioritarianism? I don't just mean utilitarians disagree -- I want the actual defeaters: cases, formal results, theoretical commitments that a prioritarian has to accept and that might turn out unacceptable. Specifically, does the leveling-down objection, which I know is usually aimed at egalitarianism, have a version that bites prioritarianism? If prioritarianism is a weighted-sum view, it seems like it can never say it's good to make the better off worse off without helping the worse off -- but is that a feature or does it mean it can't capture what's wrong with inequality at all? What happens if the weighting function is very concave versus barely concave -- does it collapse into egalitarianism or utilitarianism, and is either collapse a problem? Are there cases where prioritarianism gives an answer that even a prioritarian should find intuitively repugnant, or where it's indeterminate which policy it recommends? And be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if any pattern of judgments can be accommodated by tweaking the weighting function, is that a bug? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be -- fatal, serious, merely awkward -- and tell me which ones a committed prioritarian would themselves concede count against them, versus which they'd just dismiss. I'm especially interested in anything that would make me abandon the view for something that treats inequality itself as bad, or for something that focuses on relationships between people rather than just individual welfare levels, or even for plain old utilitarianism.
