I hold the following view about the longevity of empires and other large political units, and I want to know how much it is exposed. The view is that imperial duration is approximately memoryless: conditional on a polity having already survived a century, its chance of dissolving in the next one is about the same as it was for a newly founded polity. Age is not a predictor of collapse. Read through the outside view — the practice of forecasting a unit's future by locating it in a reference class of comparable units and reading off that class's survival distribution — the view yields a nearly flat hazard for any contemporary great power, so that the question of how much longer it will last is answered without regard to how long it has already lasted. I take this to be a genuine rival of two alternatives: a senescence view, on which the hazard rises with age as institutions rigidify and commitments accumulate; and a Lindy-style view, on which durable entities become more, not less, likely to persist, so that expected remaining life grows with age.

What would count against the memoryless view? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, and distinguish what the view's own defenders would concede from what only its critics assert. In particular, I would like addressed:

- Which observations would discriminate among the three views, and how much can be settled from duration data alone.
- Whether a flat aggregate hazard could be an artifact of unobserved heterogeneity within the reference class: if some polities are robust and others fragile, and the fragile ones exit early, the surviving population grows more robust over time, which could depress the aggregate hazard relative to the hazards of the units that compose it and let a genuinely aging process present as a memoryless one. A mixture of constant hazards, conversely, is just what a declining aggregate hazard looks like.
- Whether the class is even the right unit of analysis: is empire a natural reference class, or a family of classes indexed by fiscal, military, or legitimating structure, such that the choice among them is disciplined by nothing but fit?
- Which repairs a defender can make — restricting the domain, re-drawing the class, retreating to a claim about one subtype — without the view collapsing into a tautology, and which repairs would amount to abandoning it.
- Whether the outside view is the right instrument here at all, given how small, non-stationary, and outcome-defined the class turns out to be.

Close with a verdict: once the reference class is specified, does the memoryless view predict anything its rivals do not, or does it dissolve into arithmetic about mixtures? I am not asking for a reading list or citations; where evidence would be needed, describe what it would look like and how it would discriminate.
