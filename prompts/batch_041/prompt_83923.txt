I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science working on the foundations of inductive inference, and I need the most important implications of a particular pair of facts about the Solomonoff prior — not an exposition of the prior, and not a survey of the literature.

Here is the setup I want you to work from. Fix two universal reference machines U and U′. The invariance theorem tells us that the ratio M_U(x)/M_U′(x) between the two priors is bounded above by a finite constant for every string x. Call that constant the encoding premium for the pair. Because the premium tracks a shortest-description length, it is not computable in general, so an agent cannot determine from the inside how far her prior is from a counterpart's. Now consider two agents who agree on everything except their reference machine — same hypothesis space, same likelihoods, same data — and two rival hypotheses h₁ and h₂. Define the settling time for h₁ versus h₂ as the number of observations after which the two agents' posterior odds for h₁ over h₂ agree to within a factor of two, assuming the data are generated by one of the two hypotheses and that they are eventually distinguishable. (If you think a different idealization of the comparison is more apt — for instance, comparing the agents' full posterior distributions rather than a single pair of odds — say so and use it instead.)

My question: what are the most important implications of (i) the finiteness of the encoding premium and (ii) its non-computability, taken together with the further fact that hypotheses assigning identical likelihoods to every possible data sequence are never separated by any amount of data, for the claim that simplicity is a guide to truth and for how parsimony comparisons should be treated in the practice of theory and model choice? I am already persuaded of the invariance theorem, so do not re-derive it; I want the downstream consequences, including the ones that cut against the usual line that Solomonoff induction vindicates Occam's razor. Where an implication bites only for resource-bounded agents rather than ideal ones, or holds only under extra assumptions, flag that explicitly. No citations or reading suggestions are needed.
