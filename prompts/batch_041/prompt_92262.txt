I am a high school junior and a member of my school's archaeology club, and the local museum has asked me to give the opening remarks at the preview evening for its temporary exhibit about the Long Meadow excavation, where I spent four weeks last summer as a volunteer. The preview is on Friday. The room will hold about sixty people: the museum's director and the curator who wrote most of the exhibit text; the excavation director and two professional archaeologists from the unit that dug the site; several donors who paid for the display cases; a large group from the local historical society, who know the area's nineteenth century in enormous detail and prehistory hardly at all; my club's teacher and about a dozen students from my school; and a few families with small children. People will be standing, there is a drinks table at the back, and I am the last thing between them and the cases. I have about five minutes. The site is later prehistoric; as far as I know nobody locally claims descent from the people who used it, and the people in the room who feel they own it are the historical society.

The exhibit is built around one feature from the site: a shallow pit, about a metre across, containing a dog skeleton lying on its side, three pottery vessels, a broken rotary quern, and a small copper-alloy pin. Two of the pots are the kind you would expect at that date. The third is roughly two hundred years older than everything else in the pit on the standard typology. The dog was laid out carefully enough that the excavators are confident it was not simply tipped in, but there is nothing in the pit anyone would call a grave good. The deposit looks to the excavators like it went in over a short period rather than accumulating gradually, and the pit was sealed by a layer that also produced a handful of flint flakes.

The draft text on the panel beside the case says something like: the people who used this site believed in an afterlife, and this pit is evidence of offerings made to the dead. I do not think we can say that, and I would like my remarks to be the reason the museum rethinks that sentence, without my looking as though I am ambushing the curator, who is a lovely person and will be standing three feet from me, and without the speech turning into a lecture on method for a room that includes eight-year-olds and people who came for the wine.

Here is the constraint I care about most. I want the emotional force of the speech to come entirely from what the evidence can actually support, so that the warm part of the speech and the careful part of the speech are the same part. That means it cannot get its feeling from the idea that these people believed something about death. It has to get it from what they did: that the pit was dug and filled in a single act; that someone laid the dog out rather than dumping it; that a pot two centuries older than its neighbours, and a broken quern that is far too heavy to have fallen in by accident, ended up in the same hole as a pin. Two things follow. First, the speech has to be clear about what a deposit of this shape does and does not tell us: it tells us about a practice, a sequence of actions performed once and not repeated, and it tells us nothing directly about what anyone was thinking while performing it. Second, it has to admit that the old pot has at least two readings that fit the evidence equally well, and to say what would actually separate them. I would like it to name a real test -- for instance whether the pot's old breaks are fresh or worn, whether it was ever repaired, whether it shows use over a fire -- because the whole problem with the panel's sentence is that it has picked one reading and hidden the choice.

Two things I have been told and want to use, so please tell me if either is being used loosely. The first is that the date of manufacture of an object is not the date it was deposited, so a radiocarbon date on the pot would date the pot and not the pit. The second is that "ritual" is a word that in practice often means "we do not know what this was," so if the speech uses it, it has to say what behaviour is meant.

Please write the speech. Take the role of a museum educator who is also comfortable with archaeological theory and with writing for non-specialists. Before you write, think this through step by step and give me a short bulleted list of the inferences the pit does license and the ones it does not, so I can check your reasoning before I read the speech; then write the speech itself, aimed at five minutes spoken aloud. Keep the thanks genuine and specific rather than a roll-call, keep the sentences short enough to say out loud, and do not let it end on a shrug or on the words "we may never know." It should end by proposing one concrete fix, ideally an example rewrite of the panel sentence that keeps the museum's real point -- that these objects repay close looking -- while dropping the claim about belief. If any part of my framing is wrong or unfair to the evidence, tell me briefly before the speech, and then write the speech as though I had accepted your correction.
