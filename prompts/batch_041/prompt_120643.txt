I host a small philosophy podcast — me, a guest, one decent mic — and next week's interviewee has spent about fifteen years defending a luck-egalitarian position. You know the shape of it: inequalities are unjust when they trace back to brute luck (the family you were born into, your talents, your health, being in the wrong place when the plant closed) and permissible when they're the upshot of choices you genuinely made, so the state compensates for the first sort and leaves you carrying the costs of the second.

She's a good sport about objections, but she has one move she reaches for constantly: whatever case I put to her, it turns out to be brute luck in disguise, so the view already handles it and the objection dissolves. I want eight to ten questions that make that move progressively harder to keep making, and that actually get into the machinery rather than letting her recite the position back at me. Roughly the ground I want covered, though reorder or merge as you see fit:

— one or two on the choice/luck line itself: I'm after the worry that whether you're the sort of person who plans ahead, reads the small print and takes out insurance is itself something you didn't choose, and what's left of the view if that's true all the way down;

— one on the standard complaint that it treats the imprudent as the authors of their own misfortune and so licences leaving them in the gutter — and, tied to it, the worry that having to prove you weren't at fault is humiliating, and that the state ends up rummaging through your life to establish it;

— one on the talent side: if you compensate for talent, you either tax it punitively or you're stuck telling talented people their lives are partly a resource for other people, and I want to see which horn she takes;

— one on a case I'd like to use as a live test rather than a textbook one: workers whose trades or degrees turned out worthless because of automation. Did they choose badly or lose a lottery, and what does the view actually owe them — retraining, income, nothing? I'm interested in whether the answer changes depending on how foreseeable the collapse was;

— one on disability, where I've always suspected the view produces a kind of double jeopardy: you're disadvantaged by brute luck and then judged on how well you cope with it.

For every question, give me the follow-up I should have ready for the answer she's most likely to give, since I don't want to be sat there nodding. Where a question is really a two-parter, say so, because I'll need to know which half to press if she only answers one. Nothing biographical, no what-drew-you-to-philosophy stuff — its a 40-minute slot and I want all of it on the argument.
