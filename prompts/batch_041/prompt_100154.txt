My colleague — tenured, card-carrying long-term consequentialist, total view, lives for the far future — has been giving verdicts in our grad seminar and I've started keeping score. Her record so far:

- Pulling a toddler out of a pool: required. Obviously.
- Voting in a national election: allowed, but she refuses to call it required. She says she can't tell you her one vote does any good and won't pretend otherwise.
- Recycling a single bottle: morally arbitrary. She'll do it if the bin's closer.
- Torturing a cat for fun: forbidden.
- Having a child: allowed. She says the objection that you can't know how their life will turn out is a regress that would forbid everything.

I've wondered some days whether she's secretly a quietist who thinks cluelessness levels everything into the permissible, but the pool and the cat put paid to that.

Now a student asks her about a new one and I want to call it before she does. The case: taking a job at a startup building a cheap clean-energy device. Near-term it's a clear good — cheaper power, fewer deaths from indoor smoke. But the student, and my colleague, think the long-run effects are genuinly unknown and could plausibly be bad — faster growth, more consumption, maybe worse.

So what's her verdict — required, forbidden, or optional — and, more to the point, what rule has she actually been running? I can see at least three that fit every verdict above: (a) judge acts by their foreseeable consequences and just ignore the unseeable far future; (b) require an act only if it comes out well across every plausible long-run scenario, and call it optional otherwise; (c) let the far future dominate, so the verdict just tracks the long-run expected value. Tell me what each predicts about the new case, which one I should bet on, and which further case would actualy tell them apart. And if the record really doesn't settle it, tell me which principle she'd be buying with each verdict.
