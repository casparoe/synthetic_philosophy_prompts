For a reading group on the ethics of self-killing, I have been working through Hume's essay "Of Suicide" — the one he kept out of print during his lifetime and that appeared only after his death — and I recently came across a summary of it in a popular history of ethics whose author I have unfortunately forgotten. I came away fairly convinced, which is exactly why I want a second opinion before I repeat any of it: I would like you to audit that summary claim by claim.

The summary runs roughly as follows. Hume's aim, it says, is to show that suicide is morally permissible, and he clears away three obstacles in turn. Against the religious objection, Hume argues that God governs the world through general and immutable laws rather than by special interventions, and that since those laws leave human beings in possession of their own faculties, suicide cannot be a violation of divine providence: God has, in effect, delegated the management of our lives to us. Against the social objection, Hume argues that a person who takes his own life does no positive harm to anyone else — he merely ceases to be useful — and the summary adds that Hume even treats suicide as potentially beneficial to society when it removes someone who has become a burden. Against the personal objection, the summary says Hume observes that no one discards a life that is still worth living, so the common fear that suicide is a tragic mistake is unfounded. The summary concludes that the essay is the founding document of the modern permissive position on suicide and that its central claim — that ending one's life is simply one more exercise of the liberty we have over our own persons — is now more or less common ground. The line about society benefiting from a suicide felt off to me even at the time, but I could not tell whether that was the book's embellishment or something Hume actually held.

I would like you to go through this claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of Hume would accept, what is a popular simplification, and what Hume would actually deny. I am also curious whether the summary's framing — its suggestion that the essay is a straightforward manifesto for individual liberty — survives contact with how Hume actually positions the argument, and whether anything about the essay's own qualifications, or its fate as a suppressed text, should change how I read it. Please tell me how confident you are in each part of your assessment, and single out the one point you are least sure about. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would explain it to me over coffee — no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
