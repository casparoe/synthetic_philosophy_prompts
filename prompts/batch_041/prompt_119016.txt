I am a third-year philosophy student currently taking a course on perception and epistemic justification; we have just finished a unit on whether prior belief can alter perceptual experience, and whether that in turn alters the epistemic standing of the beliefs we form from experience. For my term paper I need to argue from a case of my own rather than from the examples everyone reaches for, so I would like you to construct one to specification. I should warn you that I will be checking the case against each specification individually, because in my experience the cases people invent in this area satisfy the headline constraint and quietly ignore two or three of the others.

The case should be ordinary and everyday — a kitchen, a classroom, a hospital corridor, a bus — with no apparatus, no psychophysical stimuli, and nobody running an experiment. What reshapes the subject's experience must be a belief of hers, not a desire, a mood, an emotion, or an expectation about what would suit her, and I would rather you avoid the well-worn wishful-seeing shape. The effect must be genuinely perceptual: the content and the phenomenal character of the experience change, not merely the subject's judgement, her confidence, or her willingness to say something out loud, and the scenario should include some diagnostic that shows this — a downstream task she has no stake in, an action she gets wrong, a reaction of surprise when the belief is later exposed as false. The effect must not be redescribable as attention, as memory colour (the colour a familiar object is expected to have), or as perceptual learning (the way training changes what an expert can make out), so say in passing what is being held fixed in order to rule each of those out. Please keep it out of vision: we have spent the whole term on colour and size, and I would like a case in audition or in touch. Finally, the belief doing the penetrating should be unjustified — its grounds should be visibly bad — while the subject is not at fault for holding it and has no inkling that it is shaping what she perceives; she was told it by someone she had every reason to trust, or absorbed it too early to have ever had occasion to question it.

What I want the case to show is that the perceptual belief she ends up with is not justified, and I want that verdict to fall out of a view about the source of experiential justification rather than out of the stipulations of the scenario. So after the scenario, set out what a dogmatist — someone who holds that an experience as of P gives immediate justification for believing P — must say about her situation, and what someone who holds that the justificatory power of an experience depends on how that experience came about must say, and show that the two verdicts really do come apart. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed. Check the case against the two ways I worry it might collapse: it must not turn out to be merely a case in which the subject has a defeater, that is, some reason of her own to distrust the experience, since then both views would agree she is unjustified; and it must not turn out to be merely a case in which the penetrating belief is well founded, since then both views would agree she is justified. Finish with the strongest line of attack open to the side that comes out worse — I expect that to be the dogmatist, and I expect the move to be either that what you have described is really a judgement rather than an experience, or that the subject has a defeater rather than a downgraded experience — and tell me what I would have to add to the case to close that gap. I do not need citations or a reading list; I want the case and the reasoning.
