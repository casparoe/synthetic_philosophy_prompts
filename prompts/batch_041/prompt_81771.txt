Every second Saturday I volunteer at a repair café — a church hall, a long trestle table, people arriving with kettles, lamps, bicycles, the odd seized bottom bracket — and last month our coordinator introduced a target: items repaired per session, to be reported to the funder. I think this will eat the thing it is measuring, but every time I make the argument I end up saying "it will get gamed," and he says "then we'll fix the gaming," and I lose. So for the volunteers' meeting I would rather read a poem than restate the objection, and I want the poem itself to be the objection: it should show, in its own machinery, the four separate ways a good measure stops tracking what it was chosen for. First, the measure is only a noisy signal, so pressing on it presses mostly on the noise. Second, the correlation holds across the range we happened to observe and gives out past the edges of it. Third, the measure is a downstream effect of the thing we care about rather than a cause of it, so intervening on it moves nothing. Fourth, once the number is visible to the people it describes, they begin playing to it. Keep all four in the café — the same table, the same queue, one Saturday morning — so that the stanzas read as a single session rather than four separate fables.

Formally: four quatrains, one per failure, in the order above, each quatrain closing on the same refrain — a single short line, the kind of phrase that would fit on the sign-up sheet. The refrain may not change by so much as a word from stanza to stanza, and yet it must not be doing the same work in each: by the end I want a reader to see that one identical sentence has passed from description to prediction to instruction to verdict, that the measure was quietly promoted from report to order and nobody remembers voting. Then the refrain once more, alone, as the poem's last line. The poem should not use the words proxy, metric, target, KPI, Goodhart or gaming; if it needs to name the failure, it has failed. And I do not want it smug — the number did get more people through the door and more kettles out of landfill, and I want that honestly in there, because the reason the argument is hard to win is that the measure works, for a while.

Make it a poem and not a parable with line breaks: something in it should be available only to verse — a rhyme that lands a beat wrong, a line that arrives late, a turn that happens where a turn has no business happening. It will be read aloud to fifteen people who are not philosophers, so it should carry at around a minute, and it should not sneer at the coordinator. Anyone who has thought seriously about measurement should be able to identify all four failures without being told which stanza is which; anyone who has not should leave with the church hall, the queue, and a feeling that something just got quietly rearranged.
