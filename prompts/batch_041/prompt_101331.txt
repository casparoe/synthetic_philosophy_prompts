My friend Dana and I got into it for like two hours last night about Aumann's agreement theorem and I still don't know who came out ahead, so I want to write down what I actually think about a bunch of cases and see if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent. Her line was: if two people are both rational Bayesians, started from the same prior, and it's genuinely out in the open between them that they disagree, then they can't stay disagreeing -- one of them has to move. I said that's a fact about a toy model and real people disagree forever, and she said that just means the real people are breaking one of the assumptions. Which assumption, though, and when is breaking it fine? She also threw in something about how you only need it to be *almost* common knowledge for you to *almost* agree, which sounds to me like it makes the whole thing useless for any actual conversation, but maybe I'm misremembering what she said.

Anyway here are my gut calls. (a) Two forecasters, same training, same model, same data, same prior, each knows the other's number, one says 60% chance of rain and one says 30%, and it just stays like that. I think that's an unstable situation -- somebody's made an arithmetic error or is posturing, it can't be that they're both perfectly rational and it simply persists. (b) Two doctors, one from a tradition that treats this disease as basically genetic, one from a tradition that treats it as basically environmental, same patient, same test result, each fully aware of the other's read. I think it's completely fine for them to disagree forever, neither is being irrational, because they walked in with different priors and there's no god's-eye fact about whose prior is right. (c) A climate scientist vs. a guy who's read alot of blogs, each knows the other's position. I think the guy just has to move to the scientist, or near it -- not because of any theorem, just because he should defer to someone who's actually looked at the thing. (d) Me and a friend arguing about whether a film is good: I don't expect convergence at all and I don't think that's a failure of anything. (e) Two people who've only swapped conclusions, never reasons -- I think it's fine for them to sit there disagreeing, but once they actually lay out their reasoning, somebody should move. And (f), the one I keep flip-flopping on: two philosophers, or two mathematicians, who've hashed out every argument, same evidence, both sharp, both know each other's view and each other's arguments cold, and neither budges for twenty years. Sometimes I think that's just irrationality with extra steps, at least one of them has to be fooling himself. Other times I think it's obviously possible and totally normal, and that Aumann just doesn't apply because they aren't even carving up the world the same way, or because 'same prior' is doing all the work and you can never actually be sure you have it. Notice (a) and (f) look like the same setup to me and I give them opposite verdicts, so that's probably where the trouble is.

The closest thing I've got to a principle is this: you should move toward someone's credence to the extent you think they know better than you, split the difference if you think you're equals, and stay put if you think you know better. Does that get all of a through f? I suspect it wrecks (b), because the doctors are equals and I still don't want them splitting the difference, but then I need to know what to do with (b) instead. If the honest answer is that the theorem only bites when you're certain of each other's rationality and you can basically never be certain, then say that, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. And tell me what your account predicts for a case I haven't listed: two people who agree on the credence down to the decimal but disagree about whether the question even makes sense in the first place.
