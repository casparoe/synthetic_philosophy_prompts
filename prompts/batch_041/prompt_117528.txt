I'm teaching a 400-level seminar on philosophy of psychiatry for the first time — the guy who normally runs it handed me his syllabus and the last five weeks are basically all Hacking, which I'm happy about but also mildly terrified of, since half my students are psych majors who think they already know what social construction means. The final is a 90-minute in-class essay, and my dept requires every essay question to come with a model answer plus a marking scheme, which is the part I'm stuck on. What worries me is that my students can all recite that Hacking has a looping effect story and that classifications make up people — I've graded enough response papers to know they've absorbed it as "psychiatric categories are made up, therefore fake," and they'll write that no matter what I ask.

So I want to build the question around a bit of a fake case, to force them past the recitation. I'm going to invent a proposed diagnosis for a hypothetical next edition of the manual — let's call it adolescent motivational collapse, criteria all self-reportable and trivially findable online — and I'm going to have a colleague in the scenario, Dr. Reyes, argue that a category like that can never be scientifically respectable, because once the criteria are public the patients will start presenting to match them. She proposes a criterion she calls loop-invariance: a category is legitimate only if its symptom profile and prevalence hold steady whether or not its criteria get publicized. I want students to argue about wether that's the right lesson to take from Hacking, and they have to do it with his actual machinery — interactive kinds, transient mental illness, the ecological niche business — instead of either just agreeing with Reyes or waving "but the brain is real" at her. Could you write the question (setting up the case in a few sentences is fine), a model answer of around 600 words written at the level I'd want from my best student, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, with a one-line note on each about how students typically get that point wrong? I'd rather the model answer actually takes a side and defends it than lays out both sides, and if it ends up leaning on homeostatic property cluster accounts of kinds, that's fine, they've read Boyd.
