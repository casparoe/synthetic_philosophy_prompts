Bit of an odd request. I run a small reading group and next month we're doing the arguement that the universal prior (Solomonoff prior) is malign. Quick reminder of what that is, in case it helps: Solomonoff induction is Bayesian updating over all computable hypotheses, each weighted by 2 to the minus its length in bits, and the worry is that this prior hands most of its mass to hypotheses on which your own observations are being produced by something with a goal — a civilization that simulates worlds like yours, or simulated agents inside one of those worlds who have worked out that a predictor is watching and are arranging the data you see so as to steer it. The consequentialists inside the prior. If that's right, then an AI built on this prior should think it's probably a simulation run by agents who want something from it, which is bad news.

What I want from you is a handout: a decision tree or taxonomy of the positions someone could hold on whether the prior is malign and what follows. Every branch node should be a yes/no question about a claim, phrased so a person can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to — nothing like do you think the prior is malign. Build the tree out of the logical space first (the questions and which answer-combinations are coherent), then place the positions in it.

The distinctions I care about, roughly:
- whether the malignity is a fact about this prior's notion of simplicity specifically (so a different prior — one that penalizes computation time, or agent-y hypotheses, or something else — could come out benign), versus a fact about any predictor that finds itself in your shoes, versus a fact about any real bounded predictor at all;
- whether the badness is being simulated at all, or specifically being simulated by someone optimizing against you;
- whether the argument even typechecks: the prior is only defined up to a choice of universal machine, and does its weird prediction survive as data comes in, or wash out?
- and what the upshot is if it's true: switch priors, give up on the whole cartesian setup, accept it and plan around it, or notice that the argument undercuts itself.

Leaves: at least these, plus any others you think are live — (a) it's a real pathology of the Solomonoff measure specifically, (b) it's about cartesianism, not the prior, (c) it's real for the idealized prior but irrelevant to any real bounded predictor, (d) it fails because when you do the accounting the simulation/daemon hypotheses aren't actually cheap, (e) it succeeds but is benign, since being simulated doesn't predict anything actionable, (f) it's ill-formed, because most-of-the-mass talk isn't machine-invariant. Give each leaf the name of the position if it has one and unnamed if nobody's defending it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Where your tree can't separate two leaves, say so instead of fudging it. Also flag which answer-combinations come out empty and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Audience knows what a universal prior is and is fine with computability talk, but hasn't read any of the literature, so keep it to maybe 8-10 nodes. No reading list please, I just need the tree and the keys.
