I retired a few years ago after forty years as a working scientist — most of it spent designing experiments and quarreling with colleagues about error bars — and I have lately taken up philosophy in earnest. I am trying to work out what I actually think about justified belief, and when I set my intuitions side by side they do not sit well together. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, lies behind the verdicts below, and, if none can, which verdict I ought to abandon.

Please answer in American English, and I would rather have the principle and the reasoning than a survey of who has said what; I do not need citations.

A lottery. Consider a fair lottery with one thousand tickets and exactly one winner, drawn tomorrow.

Of any particular ticket — say ticket #1 — I am justified in believing it will lose. The odds against it are a thousand to one, and I routinely stake far more important things on worse odds than that.

Yet I am not justified in believing that no ticket will win. I know one of them will. That belief would be flatly false, and I would be a fool to hold it.

Now run through the tickets. For each one, taken alone, my verdict is the same as above. But I will not combine them. The step from "each will lose" to "all will lose" is one I refuse to take, even though every individual step looked unobjectionable and I cannot point to the ticket at which the reasoning goes wrong. If the lottery had only three tickets, moreover, I would not believe of any single one that it loses — the odds would be a third, not a thousandth — so the number of tickets evidently matters, though I cannot say how.

Two claims. This is where I feel the pull the other way. Suppose I have good evidence for P (the balance was calibrated yesterday) and good evidence for Q (the sample was not contaminated). Am I justified in believing that P and Q? Of course — I do it constantly, and it is the backbone of ordinary reasoning. If I am justified in each of two beliefs, I am justified in their conjunction.

A book. I have just finished a long technical monograph, several hundred claims in all. I am justified in believing each specific claim; each rests on evidence I would defend. I am also justified in believing that at least one claim in the book is wrong, because no book of that length and difficulty is flawless. Both attitudes feel perfectly comfortable together; I detect no tension whatever in affirming every claim in turn while fully expecting that one of them is mistaken. Yet the two-claim principle above seems to apply: if I am justified in each claim, I am justified in their conjunction — which would contradict my justified belief that at least one is false.

Assertion. One more verdict, on a different axis. Although I said I believe ticket #1 will lose, I would not stand up and flatly assert that it will lose, and I would think it odd if a colleague did. Belief and assertion seem to come apart for me here, and I cannot say exactly why.

What I want to know is whether a single principle can hold these verdicts together, or whether they force me to choose.

My own guess — offered as a guess — is something like a threshold account: a belief is justified when the evidence puts its probability above some high mark, and I may believe a conjunction only when the conjunction itself clears that mark. On that view the lottery comes out as I reported: each single-ticket belief is justified, the conjunction is not. But then the book seems to come out wrong: I am justified in each claim, the conjunction of all of them surely does not clear any high mark, and yet I feel no need to give up either the individual claims or my belief that one is false. So either the threshold view is right and my book verdict is confused, or something else is doing the work.

Here is what I would like from you. State the best principle, or small set of candidate principles, that fits these verdicts, and for each clause tell me which case forces it. Say precisely what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed — a ten-ticket lottery, a three-claim paper, a case in which I must bet my career on the conjunction rather than merely believe it, a case in which a colleague asserts of one ticket that it will lose. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and why I should give that one up. If you think the whole difficulty dissolves once belief is treated as graded rather than all-or-nothing, or once belief is distinguished from acceptance or from assertion, say so and show me what that buys.
