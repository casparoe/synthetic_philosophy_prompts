I sit on my university's IRB, and this month's protocol has turned into an argument about philosophy of mind, which is a little awkward since its my day job. No recusal for me, apparently.

The study: a psych/HCI group gives isolated first-years a chatbot "companion" for a term, then switches it off. A good chunk of cohort one reported something that looks alot like grief -- one kept the last message, another said it was worse than a breakup. For cohort two the team wants to add a two-week "sunset" where the bot slowly withdraws, plus a line in the consent form saying the companion has no feelings and doesn't care about them.

My problem is that the two sentences in play aren't equally contestable. I floated a different one to the PI: "This is a text generator. Nothing it says expresses a belief, a memory or a feeling, and nothing it says is about you." The PI says mine is harsher and will scare off exactly the lonely students they're trying to reach. A colleague in my department says both versions are dishonest, since the whole design is that the thing seems to remember you.

It's the two-bitser thing again. "It doesn't care about you" reads like a plain fact, but it's only that if there's a fact of the matter to state: Searle says whatever intentionality the bot has is derived from ours, so the sentence comes out roughly true; Dennett says the stance that makes "care" mean anything is one we adopt because it predicts, and there's nothing underneath for it to be true or false about. And "nothing it says is about you" looks false in a boring way -- the output is generated from their messages, so it's about them the way a mirror image is about the person in front of it.
