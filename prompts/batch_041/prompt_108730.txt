I retired a few years ago after thirty-five years as a cell biologist, and I have been trying to make up for lost time by sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group at the local university. Last week the group took up the logical positivist A. J. Ayer and his attitude toward the question of life after death. I knew the outline beforehand: Ayer held that statements about surviving the death of one's body do not carry the kind of meaning that factual statements carry, and he was for most of his life an atheist and a materialist about the mind. What made the session lively was that Ayer, late in life, had a cardiac arrest and afterward described an experience during the interval when, by the medical criteria, he was dead — light, a sense of presence, being told to return — an experience he could only render in the vocabulary of the afterlife. Two members of the group, Marta and Dev, got into an argument about what that episode shows about Ayer's philosophy. I could not decide who had the better of it, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general verdict. Here is how it went, as best I can reconstruct it.

Marta opened: The episode is a refutation of Ayer's own position, and a particularly neat one. He said that talk of an afterlife is meaningless because it cannot be verified. Then he had an experience that he could describe only in the vocabulary of the afterlife. If the only vocabulary that fits the experience is the vocabulary he had ruled out as meaningless, then the ruling-out was wrong. The principle cannot survive contact with the very case its author underwent.

Dev answered: That conflates the content of an experience with its interpretation. A brain in distress produces experiences, and the fact that the experiencer reaches for religious imagery tells us about the stock of images available to him, not about whether anyone survives death. And the decisive fact is that Ayer himself, once he recovered, did not treat the episode as evidence for survival. If the man who had the experience, and who was in the best position to weigh it, drew no such conclusion, then citing his case against him is odd.

Marta replied: Appealing to what Ayer concluded is close to begging the question. He had a career invested in the answer, and his interpretation of his own experience is not neutral evidence about what that experience was evidence for. And the deeper point stands: his report is a counterexample to the verification principle applied to himself. Something happened to him that he could not state in the language of observation and physics. If the principle cannot accommodate the reports of the person who underwent the event, it is not a criterion of meaning; it is a rule about what a philosophical program is willing to discuss. And once you concede that the only thing ruled out is the final metaphysical clause, the principle has no bite, because nobody claims to have observed his own survival. He claims to have had an experience that he interprets that way. If the principle only forbids the conclusion and never the report, then it is a house rule about how to describe the same events.

Dev came back: Those are two different things again. The principle is a thesis about what makes a sentence meaningful, and Ayer's report is a set of sentences. Most of them — I saw a light, I felt a sense of being summoned — are reports of experience, and reports of experience are exactly what a positivist can admit. What the principle excludes is the further claim: and therefore my mind survived the death of my body. The experience is data; the inference to survival is the metaphysical add-on. The case does not embarrass the principle; it illustrates its intended use. On the no-bite objection, the scope point cuts the other way. If the metaphysical clause is a conclusion rather than a report, it needs an argument, and the argument is exactly what Ayer said it would be: an inference from a brain event to a survival event, and the inference is not licensed by the data. Also note the asymmetry. If Ayer had come back a believer, you would be citing that as confirmation. The fact that he came back an atheist is being discounted as bias. That is a double standard. And there is a further point he himself made: even if something persisted through those minutes, it would not be Ayer surviving. Survival requires that the person continue, and a person is not a momentary episode of experience. A disembodied consciousness that remembers my life is not obviously me, and no experience it has could settle whether it is me.

Marta answered: I am not discounting Ayer's conclusion as bias; I am saying it is not decisive either way, and neither is the report by itself. What is decisive is that the case shows the first-person perspective is not capturable in the third-person terms his philosophy allowed. The line you keep drawing between experience and interpretation is not as clean as you make it. A person who reports a light and a summons is not reporting raw pixels; the description is already shaped by the categories he has. If the description is theory-laden, then you cannot quarantine the theory and keep the data.

Dev closed: Then we are not really arguing about meaning; we are arguing about inference to the best explanation. Grant that the report is theory-laden. The theory-laden report still has to be explained, and the best explanation is a distressed brain, because that hypothesis is independently supported and the survival hypothesis is not. The episode is evidence that consciousness can occur in a badly damaged brain. That is the opposite of what you need.

Please go through this exchange move by move. For each move, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. I am especially interested in four things: (a) whether Marta's first move really is a refutation of Ayer's position or only a rhetorical point; (b) whether Dev's appeal to Ayer's own conclusion is legitimate or question-begging; (c) whether Marta's no-bite objection is fatal to the verification principle or only a limitation on its scope; and (d) whether the disagreement would be better recast as a dispute about inference to the best explanation rather than about meaning. Tell me also what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall. I do not need reading recommendations; I need the arguments sorted out.
