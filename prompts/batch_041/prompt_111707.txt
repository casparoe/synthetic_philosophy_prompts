I'm on our town's conservation comission, and we've got a beaver dam that's flooding a culvert and part of the road. The beavers have been there about three years. We have to vote next Tuesday on whether to breach the dam or install a flow device that keeps the water level down. I don't have a philosophy background, just alot of meetings, but I've been reading up on this on my own.

Here's the thing. Two of the members keep saying we can't just remove the dam because the beavers built it for a reason and the wetland needs it. They say the dam has a purpose even if the beavers don't think about it. My friend who did a PhD in philosphy said that's called free-floating rationales -- reasons without a reasoner -- and that it's a real position but also a tricky one. I think he's right that the beavers aren't sitting there reasoning about flood control, but I also think the dam really does do something for the wetland. So I'm stuck.

I have to write the commission's opinion before the meeting. Can you act as a philosopher who works on functions, purposes, and environmental ethics? First brainstorm the strongest arguments on both sides for me in a few bullets. Then write the opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should seriously engage the argument that the dam has a purpose even though no one is doing the reasoning. Don't give me citations or a reading list. Keep the final opinion under 650 words, and make it something the other commissioners can actually vote on.
