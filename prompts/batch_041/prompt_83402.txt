A national archive has recently completed a mass digitisation programme covering the surviving records of the metropolitan department that administered a nineteenth-century colony. In an internal planning paper, a colleague argues that the archive's power over the historical record is best understood not as a single phenomenon but as five distinct silencing mechanisms, each operating at a different stage in the passage of a trace from the world into written history. Her taxonomy is a stipulation rather than a summary of any published scheme, so treat the definitions below as authoritative for the purposes of this task. She defines the mechanisms as follows.

Inscription silence: the ordinary routines of administration never generated a document about a given person, place, or activity, so no destruction was required for the trace to go unrecorded.
Appraisal silence: documents were generated but were subsequently selected out, destroyed, or never transferred to the archive.
Description silence: documents survive in the archive, but the finding aids, catalogue entries, and index terms used to describe them are drawn from the vocabulary of the record-creating institution, so the traces cannot be reached by anyone who does not already think in that vocabulary.
Preservation silence: documents survive and are described, but decay, illegibility, format obsolescence, or the cost of conservation means that only an arbitrarily small subset of them becomes usable.
Reception silence: documents survive, are described, and are usable, but prevailing historiographical conventions assign them no evidential weight, so they are read and then set aside.

Please rank these five mechanisms from the one that mass digitisation of the kind the archive has just completed reduces most to the one it reduces least, and account for each placement in a short paragraph. Where you judge that digitisation aggravates a mechanism rather than mitigating it, say so and explain the mechanism by which it does so. In answering, treat the question as concerning each mechanism's actual effect on the record available to historians, not merely how convenient digitisation makes the archive to use. Finally, identify the single mechanism you take to be least likely to be noticed at all by a competent researcher working from inside the archive, and explain what makes that silence self-concealing.
