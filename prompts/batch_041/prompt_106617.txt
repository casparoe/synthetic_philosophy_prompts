Alright, I'm in a political philosophy reading group and we keep having the same fight about "cancel culture" without ever agreeing on what would even count as a good theory of the thing, so I want to back way up and do this properly. Put on your hat as someone who works on punishment theory and the philosophy of law — I care less about a verdict on whether cancelling is good or bad than about what a theory of social sanction-as-punishment would have to deliver to be adequate at all. So give me the desiderata. What phenomena does it have to explain, which cases does it have to get right, which distinctions can't be collapsed, and what does it have to stay consistent with — e.g., the fact that informal sanctions have no procedure, no authority, and no appeal: does that make them a different kind of thing from legal punishment, or the same thing minus the paperwork?

Let me seed some cases, because I dont want a list floating free of them: (a) a magazine editor declines a piece by a writer whose views she finds repellent and doesn't announce why; (b) a hundred thousand people coordinate online to get that same writer's unrelated book deal killed; (c) a university disinvites a speaker after a petition; (d) a private employer fires an employee over something he said on his own time; (e) a small scene — a band, a reading series — quietly stops inviting someone, no announcement, no reason given, and he just... stops being there. I also want the theory to say something about a thing I've started calling sanction-laundering: the institution that does the firing insists it's only responding to "the community," so nobody owns the decision and nobody can be blamed or petitioned. And a second one, the unowned sanction: when a thousand people each do something individually blameless (each just declines to platform, each just buys elsewhere) and the aggregate is devastating, is there a punisher at all? Your desiderata should have something to say about whether a theory is even allowed to answer that with a flat "no."

Before you start listing, brainstorm for a bit: what are the candidate requirements people actually press on such a theory (proportionality, due process, the right not to be associated with, the difference between a sanction aimed at a person vs. at an idea, the act/omission line, whether consequences count as punishment when they're just the foreseen upshot of many private choices), and which of those are genuine desiderata versus just the prejudices of one camp — the civil-libertarian camp wants procedure, the you're-not-owed-a-platform camp wants no procedure requirement at all; is either demand a real constraint on a theory, or just a policy preference dressed up as one? Then write the list, each requirement with the case or consideration behind it, mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a camp, and — this is the part I most want — flag any subset you think can't be jointly satisfied. I have a suspicion that "must be proportionate" and "must not require a central authority to rank harms" can't both hold, but tell me if I'm wrong.
