Over the summer a friend lent me one of those pop-epistemology books — the author's a journalist, not a philosopher, but its very readable and I've been half-taking it at face value. There's a chapter on contextualism I'm now suspicious of, and I'd like to know how fair it is before I repeat any of it in a tutorial. What it says, roughly:

'Contextualism is the view that whether you know something depends on how much is at stake. If nothing much is riding on it, you can know on pretty thin evidence; if a lot is riding on it, the bar goes up. It's really the same idea as what some philosophers call pragmatic encroachment, just a different label for the same thesis. This makes knowledge a relative notion: two people with the same evidence can both be right, one saying you know and the other saying you don't, which is why critics call the whole thing relativism. The evidence for it is our intuitions about high- and low-stakes cases — you know your keys are on the table, but you don't know you won't win the lottery — plus the fact that we blame people more for acting on flimsy evidence when the stakes are high. The big objection is that it makes knowledge too easy to lose: if something purely practical, like a bet, can take your knowledge away without touching your evidence, then knowledge stops tracking anything epistemic at all. That's why the view has mostly been abandoned and everyone has gone back to plain old invariantism.'

I've definately got a hunch that the relativism bit is off, it doesn't match anything we've done in seminars, but I can't tell whether the rest is fine or whether I'm about to embarrass myself. Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion, roughly in order of how much it matters — and where the book is just stating one side of a live disagreement rather than making a mistake, say so, since that's a different thing.
