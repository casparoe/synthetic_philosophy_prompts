I am drafting a position statement on the nature of the left–right divide for a political theory workshop, and I would like a consistency audit of my commitments before I circulate it. The set I intend to defend is this.

(1) The distinction is real. Calling a position or a person left or right tracks a genuine and durable disagreement, not merely an artifact of party systems, media framing, or historical accident.

(2) What the distinction fundamentally tracks is equality. The left is unified by opposition to unjustified hierarchy and by the demand that social relations be made more equal; the right is unified by the acceptance or defense of existing hierarchies.

(3) What actually holds each side together, however, is not a shared doctrine but a shared social epistemology: a characteristic pattern of whom each side trusts and whom it suspects. The left is disposed to distrust concentrated private power and to credit the testimony of those who bear its costs; the right is disposed to distrust concentrated state power and the pressure of the crowd, and to credit established institutions and those who bear the costs of disruption.

(4) The labels are coalitional and historically contingent. No essence is shared by all left positions across time; the same label has picked out quite different commitments in different eras.

(5) Mixed positions are coherent. It is entirely possible to be left on economic questions and right on cultural ones, or the reverse; the divide is a bundle of axes rather than a single line, and such combinations are not confused but common.

Please sort these into the pairs that are genuinely in tension and the pairs that only appear to be, and be discriminating rather than flagging everything. For each genuine tension, state how severe it is, exactly what it commits me to, and the least costly revision that would remove it. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most while costing the least.

Finally, test the set against three cases and say, for each, which of my commitments do the classifying work and whether the set manages it without strain: the classical conservative who wishes to preserve existing institutions and hierarchies; the market radical who is on the right yet hostile to established institutions and wants to dismantle them; and the technocratic egalitarian who is on the left yet wants to concentrate state power in order to level social relations.
