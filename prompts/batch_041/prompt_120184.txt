i just started a part time gig at a small natural history museum, mostly writing label text, and the exhibit we're putting up in the spring has a big wall timeline running from the Pleistocene to now with the Anthropocene as the last chunk of it. the designer wants a date printed on the wall and i've been volunteered to defend whatever date we use, which has turned into a running argument with my flatmate, who did a geology degree and keeps telling me the whole question is dead because a committee of stratigraphers voted the epoch down a year or two ago (they'd proposed some lake in Ontario as the reference site, and then the thing went up the chain and got knocked back, and there was a whole procedural row about how the vote was handled). so before i write a word i want the spec rather than an answer, i want to know what i should even be asking of a proposal about when the Anthropocene started.

what i'm after: the list of requirements any serious candidate answer has to meet, with the case behind each one, and a view on which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. so: the start dates people actually put forward (the early-agriculture one, the 1610 low point that's supposed to come from forests regrowing after the conquest of the Americas, the 1780s steam-engine date, the 1950s bomb-test fallout) and what each of them does and doesn't deliver; the distinctions a proposal can't be allowed to collapse, like a unit defined by a single point in one rock or sediment sequence vs. a stretch of time, an isochronous marker vs. a phenomenon that spreads across the planet at different speeds, the base of a stratigraphic unit vs. the onset of the human influence it's meant to name, and a formal geological unit vs. the loose word humanists use; the rules it has to play by if it wants to be a geological unit at all, i.e. what the stratigraphic bodies demand of a boundary and of a candidate site, the chain of votes from the working group up through the subcommission and the commission and the union, and the supermajority thresholds involved; and what it has to be consistent with in the actual record, meaning what's readable in sediments and ice, how far back each signal can be detected, and how long a marker would still be findable by geologists a few million years from now.

then the part i care about most: flag which of these requirements can't be satisfied at the same time. my suspicion is that picking the cleanest, most globally synchronous marker forces you onto a date so recent that the concept becomes useless to the historians and artists who actually use the word, and if that's a real conflict rather than me being confused, say so and say what has to give. i'd also like a view on which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidate dates out, and on the who-decides side: what a defensible decision procedure would have to look like given that the term leaked out of geology into everything else -- is it a problem that the only votes that counted were cast by geologists, and could a process be set up that was legitimate for the other users without giving them a veto over the stratigraphy? i don't need you to score every contender, i'll do that in the label copy, i just want the criteria and the conflicts. a table would be fine if it helps.
