In the philosophy of technology, the claim that artifacts have political properties is usually defended by pointing to particular designs that plainly serve particular interests — a structure built too low for certain people to pass beneath, a machine only a certain kind of worker can operate. I would like you to explain a distinction that such examples leave untouched.

Call an artifact's political character load-bearing when some social arrangement depends on that artifact in the sense that the arrangement would not persist, or would have to be reconstructed, were the artifact removed or redesigned. Call that character decorative when the artifact merely expresses or advertises an arrangement that would survive the artifact's removal. Please do the following, treating conceptual clarity as more important than breadth of reference:

First, state precisely what the load-bearing/decorative distinction is meant to capture, and explain how it differs from the more familiar contrast between a technology that is flexible with respect to social arrangements and one that is held to be inherently political.

Second, confront the strongest objection to the distinction — that removing an artifact never leaves the surrounding arrangement untouched, so that the question of whether an arrangement would survive the artifact's removal has no determinate answer — and say whether the distinction can be repaired or must be abandoned.

Third, assess whether the distinction strengthens or weakens the determinist thesis that technology follows an internal logic of its own rather than being steered by social choice.

Where you need a concrete case, construct one rather than appealing to famous ones.
