I'm a grad student rep on my university's endowment commitee, and something about how we make decisions has been bugging me. Last year we voted to divest from fossil fuels. The arguements that won the room were basically: even if selling our shares doesn't reduce a single ton of emissions -- we sell to someone else, who sells to someone else -- we still shouldn't be profiting from the industry or lending it legitimacy. Fine. But then a proposal to get the university to stop buying beef for the dining halls died, and the exact opposite reasoning got used. The chair said our purchasing wouldn't change how much beef gets produced nationally, students would just eat it somewhere else, so it'd be pure cost for zero benefit. Same committee, same month, two opposite standards for what counts as "making a difference."

So my question is what each of these decisions presupposes about how one person -- or one institution, which is sort of a big person -- contributes to a collective harm. Divestment seems to presuppose that you can be implicated in a harm through what you participate in or endorse, even when your own marginal contribution is exactly zero: the wrong is in being part of the thing, not in what your particular slice does. The beef decision seems to presuppose the opposite, that the only thing that matters is the counterfactual difference you make, and if that's zero, or gets displaced onto someone else, there's nothing to talk about. Are those two actually compatible, or are we just being inconsistent? If you're a consequentialist about this, is there any way to make divestment come out as required without giving up the standard that killed the beef proposal? And if you go the other way, and participation itself is the thing that matters, doesn't that make every one of us complicit in something enormous all the time -- and then what do we do with that?

I'd also like to know whether either decision actually needs the view I'm attributing to it, or whether there's some third theory that licenses both and I'm just missing it. And does the fiduciary thing change anything? The endowment is legally set up as a pot of money to be invested for future students, so we had to make a deliberate exception for divestment and call it a values decision. Does treating the fund as a tool for a goal, except when it's a vehicle for our identity, presuppose some particular view of what an institution is for?

Last thing: what would have to change about how we run these meetings if we took the beef standard seriously everywhere? Would we just stop doing anything, since most of what a university does has no marginal effect on anything?
