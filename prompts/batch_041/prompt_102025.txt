Our philosophy club has spent the whole spring term on punishment and the state, and I have been asked to close our last meeting with a poem, because our teacher would rather end on something that can be read aloud than on another round of summaries. I have been working through Camus's essay against the death penalty, the one from 1957 that is usually translated as "Reflections on the Guillotine," and the sentence I cannot get out of my head is the one where he calls capital punishment "the most premeditated of murders," adding that for the punishment to be a genuine equivalent, the murderer would have had to warn his victim of the day on which he meant to kill him and then hold him at his mercy for months. What he is describing is something the murderer never does and the state always does: it tells a man the date of his own death and makes him live inside that date until the date arrives. I want a poem built on that asymmetry rather than one that merely mentions it, and I would like the form to carry the argument the way a villanelle's returning lines carry a thought that will not leave.

So here is what I am after. Write the poem in two halves of seven lines each, describing the same events in the same order, with the second half reusing the first half's line-endings in the same order, so that nothing in the second half can escape the shape the first half gave it. Put the first half entirely in the future tense — the waiting, the counted days, the guard's steps, the cell, the sound of something being tested somewhere in the building, whatever you choose — and the second half entirely in the past tense, so that the reader has to undergo the whole thing twice and realizes, at the turn between the halves, that the waiting was already a death. Let the first half's lines be closed and regular, each one ending exactly where it ends, like a clock, and let the second half's lines run on into one another without stopping, so that the poem breaks its own order at the moment the man is broken out of his. The voice should be the condemned man's, and in the last two or three lines I want him to turn and address the man who killed him, because that is the strangest thing in the essay: the one who killed him got to be sudden, and the state gave him months instead. If there is room, he might also notice that in a world without belief the state is not handing him over to any judge, only putting him away, which Camus contrasts with older religious societies where the condemned still had someone to be delivered to. End there, with the state's calm orderliness visible as the more terrible of the two deaths, and please do not let it slide into a sermon against the death penalty or a plea for sympathy with the murderer — Camus grants the case for the penalty before he refuses it, and I want the poem to keep that difficulty rather than resolve it. After the poem, add three or four sentences telling me which of these constraints is doing which piece of work, so that I can say something to the club before I read it.
