I'm a novelist, and I've been reading a lot of Mill's late stuff for a novel I'm writing—the Autobiography, the Chapters on Socialism, the Land Tenure Reform Association papers—and I've got this idea for a satirical piece for a fictional Victorian periodical called The Utilitarian Review (the conceit is that its a magazine that somehow survived into the present, still edited by the ghost of a disappointed Benthamite). The piece I want you to write is a mock Prospectus and Rules of Association for a utopian socialist community, circa 1870, but written by a consultant who is a close reader of Mill's late views. The joke should be that the fine print of the community's charter quietly takes positions on all the worries Mill had about socialism in his last decade: whether a cooperative can preserve individual spontaneity and the experiment in living; how to handle the unearned increment of land and the idea that the raw material of the globe should be commonly owned; the danger of a despotism of society replacing the despotism of capital; the problem of incentives and the impossibility of an absolute equality of remuneration; the position of women; and the question of whether socialism is a probationary stage or a final destination.

The satire should be legible to someone who knows the late Mill and funny to someone who doesn't—the humor should come from the actual tensions in his views, not from invented ones. About 700-900 words, formatted as numbered clauses, with a preamble and a few whereas clauses. Don't quote Mill directly; paraphrase his positions in the voice of a fussy Victorian lawyer. At the end, add a short author's note (150 words or so) saying where the piece takes liberties with Mill's actual views or leans on a contested reading of them, and what argument or consideration would make you think the satire was aiming at the wrong target.
