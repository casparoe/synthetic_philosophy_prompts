I am writing a historical novel in which Turing appears as a minor character, and I have reached the chapter in which he must advise a fictional laboratory on a claim of machine intelligence. I do not want to put an anachronistic position in his mouth, so I have assembled the track record I intend to have him running, drawn from the closing section of his 1950 paper, where he takes up extrasensory perception as the last of the objections to his imitation game. As I read him there, he holds: (i) that telepathy and its relatives are not to be dismissed in advance, since the statistical case for them is not the sort of thing a careful person can simply wave away; (ii) that if such phenomena are real, the imitation game as originally staged is compromised, because the interrogator's verdict may be produced by a channel other than the official questions and answers, so that a run in the ordinary room is inconclusive rather than either a success or a failure; (iii) that the remedy is not to abandon the game but to tighten it, isolating the parties so that no unacknowledged channel can operate, and then to rerun; (iv) that a machine which passes the tightened, isolated version has done what he asked of it, the remaining questions being engineering questions; and (v) that where a run succeeds only through a channel the human contestant does not enjoy, the fault lies in the staging rather than in the machine, and the run is to be voided and restaged. If any of these five strikes you as failing to cohere with the others, say so, but what I care about is what follows from them.

The new case the novel introduces is a researcher in 1953 who claims his machine has won the game under the tightened conditions, both contestants in separate rooms, no telepathist anywhere in the building. What the interrogator does not know, and what the laboratory has concealed from everyone, is that the machine's builders were given the interrogator's private diary a month earlier and that its answers trade on it; when a fresh interrogator is substituted, it fails. In a second episode, the machine is genuinely telepathic, the room is shielded as well as the laboratory can manage, and the machine nonetheless answers questions about the interrogator's private memories, while the interrogator, who is not telepathic, cannot tell.

What would my Turing say about each of these, and which rule he might be running does each verdict follow from? I am interested in the rival rules that fit his earlier verdicts just as well: a rule that voids any run in which an unacknowledged channel operates, versus a rule that cares only whether the conditions are public and symmetric between the contestants, versus a rule that treats indistinguishability itself as the whole criterion and the route to it as irrelevant. Which of his earlier verdicts is the closest analog to the diary case, and which to the leaky shield? And if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the matter, which further experiment would tell the rules apart — I need one scene in which he either congratulates the researcher or shuts the laboratory down, and I would rather it turn on a real discriminator than on my convenience as a novelist.
