I run a 90-minute graduate seminar on idealized models of reasoning, with about 18 students, and I want an activity that makes them feel why something like a human computation hierarchy, or factored cognition, is a natural idealization of deliberation, and where that idealization quietly cheats. The point must land through the mechanics rather than a lecture: they should run into the decomposition and its failure mode before either is named, and only get the vocabulary in the debrief.

The core is a deliberation tree. Divide the room into a hierarchy: a single root node, two or three levels beneath it, and eight to twelve leaves. Each participant is one node. The only permitted channels are between a node and its immediate parent and children; nobody sees the whole tree, siblings never talk, and the root never addresses a leaf directly. Messages are capped, say ten words each and at most three exchanges per edge, and answers must be short: yes, no, a number, or a single phrase. The task should be a decision with one correct verdict that requires several independent lines of reasoning to converge — a verdict about what to do or believe that needs, for instance, an alibi check, a motive check, and a physical-evidence check, each itself two or three inferential steps. Prepare the material so each leaf holds one fact or one inference rule, internal nodes combine their children's answers, and no single sheet contains a chain long enough to yield the verdict alone. The root announces the verdict after a fixed twenty-five minutes.

Then run the twist. In a second round, before the tree is rebuilt, plant a single error: designate one leaf to return a confident but wrong answer to its first query, or corrupt one supplied fact. Nothing marks it as wrong locally, and the group must still deliver a verdict.

The debrief should map moments to claims: leaves as bounded human-scale sub-tasks, internal nodes as deliberative steps, the tree as factored cognition; the bandwidth caps as the reason deliberation cannot be one big act; the root's verdict as the composition of sub-answers rather than anything the root reasoned through itself, so the ideal reasoner is nowhere in particular. The planted error should carry the main philosophical weight: the idealization assumes each sub-agent answers correctly, and the activity shows that composition is only as trustworthy as the weakest unverified step, that checking a step is itself a task, and that verification can cost as much as solving. Close by asking whether a factorization could be built that verifies itself, and what that would demand.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it. I expect participants to bypass the tree by whispering across the room, some nodes to sit idle because their information turned out to be redundant, the tree to become lopsided so the root is a time bottleneck, and one student to try to solve the whole thing alone. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the idealization concerns a reasoner of unbounded depth with perfect sub-agents and no resource limits, so a finite classroom cannot display the limit behavior or the self-referential case where deliberation includes reasoning about its own decomposition. Say which parts the facilitator has to supply verbally instead.
