so I'm not a scholar or anything, I just read a lot on my own, and I've been slowly working through this old medieval book in translation, the Guide of the Perplexed by the rabbi Maimonides. Theres a chapter late in the third part about the animal sacrifices in the temple and it knocked me sideways. His idea, as far as I can follow it, is that God never actually wanted the sacrifices. The whole system was a concession. Everyone in those days worshipped by killing animals at altars, the Israelites grew up with nothing else, and Maimonides says you can't move people from one extreme to the other all at once, so instead of abolishing the practice the law took what everyone already knew how to do and turned it around to point at the one God, with new rules and a new meaning laid on top of it. So the sacrifices were the thing that phased the sacrifices out. The bit I keep chewing on is that the aim was to erase the traces of idolatry without confusing people by taking away the only kind of worship they'd ever known. I found that wierd and kind of devastating.

What I want is a poem about that, and I want the form itself to do the work. Three sections:

1. Start strict. Rhymed quatrains, a steady beat you could chant, and one line that comes back at the end of every stanza like a piece of liturgy, something about the animal or the smoke or the altar.
2. Then let it slip. Keep the same shape for a while so you can feel it straining, but let the rhymes go off, slant or false, let the meter stumble, and bring the ritual line back changed. Same words, different weight.
3. Then free verse. No rhyme, no regular beat. The ritual line should still be there somewhere but worn down to a fragment, or turned into a question.

By the end it should read like something that used to be a ceremony and isnt one anymore. Thats the point, the poem should do the weaning the chapter is talking about.

Voice: someone inside it. The person holding the animal, or the priest, or somebody in the crowd watching the smoke, not Maimonides explaining himself afterwards. They can suspect something, but they shouldnt be handed the answer, because part of whats sad is that they think theyre doing exactly what they were told.

Keep it plain. No title with the word sacrifice in it if you can help it, and please dont turn it into a sermon about how religion evolves. I'd rather it be a poem that someone who never heard of Maimonides would still feel, and that someone who has read the chapter would recognize as getting it right: the gradualness, the concession, the sense of God meeting people where they are and using what they already have. And dont resolve whether hes right about it. I want the poem to stay inside the idea instead of judging it.
