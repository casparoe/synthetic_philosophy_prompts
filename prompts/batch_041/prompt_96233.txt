I'm in my final year of a taught master's in Technology and Public Policy, and I'm taking an elective called 'Frontier AI: Safety, Strategy and Governance'. The convenor has asked the two student representatives to draft a specimen examination question for the revision pack, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, so that the cohort can see what a strong script looks like rather than merely what a correct one contains. I've volunteered, but I keep producing questions that a well-read student can dispatch by reciting the standard sequence — capability race, therefore coordination, therefore treaty — and the convenor has warned me that anything answerable that way is worthless as an assessment. What I need from you is one essay question, pitched at taught-master's level and answerable in about forty-five minutes at roughly seven hundred words, that cannot be discharged with that sequence, together with a model answer of that length and a marking scheme that separates a first-class script from an upper-second-class one by naming the specific moves each must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks. The examination is closed-book and students will have no internet access, so please do not require citations, reading lists or bibliographies; the question must be answerable from conceptual understanding alone.

To help you aim it properly, here is the apparatus the module has built up, which I'd like the question to trade on. We distinguish the alignment tax — a technological claim, namely the capability, compute or time cost of a given safety measure relative to an unconstrained alternative — from what the convenor calls the competitiveness constraint — a strategic claim, namely that an actor who bears the tax unilaterally loses ground to actors who do not, so that restraint is punished unless everyone restrains. The point I want students to confront is that these two claims are logically independent, and that most public argument slides between them. We also use two further conditions in seminars. The decisiveness condition: whether any one actor's decision to bear the tax actually changes the outcome, or whether the race is lost or won regardless of what that actor does. And the assurance condition: whether a safety practice, once adopted, can be verified by others at reasonable cost — because a measure that is cheap to claim and expensive to check creates a commitment problem quite separate from the size of the tax. To make this concrete, suppose a lab is weighing an auditing and oversight regime that would cost it, say, a fifth of its pre-training compute and would meaningfully reduce a class of severe risk; suppose a rival lab will not adopt it; and suppose a regulator exists that could mandate the regime across the industry but cannot verify compliance cheaply. I would like the question to force students to say which combination of tax size, decisiveness and assurance is actually required for the competitiveness constraint to do the work its users want it to do — for instance, to license either unilateral restraint or its opposite — and to notice where the argument equivocates or becomes self-fulfilling, since the perceived tightness of the constraint depends on what actors expect one another to do. A good script should also engage with the possibility that some safety investment raises capability rather than taxing it, and with what follows for the prescription if the binding obstacle turns out to be assurance rather than cost.

Please write the essay question itself, then a model answer of about seven hundred words, then a marking scheme out of twenty-five marks built around five or six must-hit points with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong; please also give me one short paragraph of guidance on what separates a first from an upper second, and flag any way in which my own framing of the question may be smuggling in a conclusion the students should be free to reject.
