Eleven years ago my younger brother was killed during a robbery, and the man who did it comes up for parole in the spring. That fact is why I have spent the past two years reading Dennett and Caruso on whether desert can survive determinism, and it is also why, six weeks from now, I have agreed to give the closing address at our state's annual conference for crime-victim advocates and service providers: a room of roughly three hundred people who work with victims every day, several prosecutors who sit on our sentencing commission, two criminologists who will be on the panel after me, a public defender who volunteers with our group, and a philosopher from the state university who has published on free will and sits on our advisory board.

I am going to argue for a position I have come to hold and that I know will be unwelcome in that room. Stated baldly: backward-looking desert should not determine how long or how hard we punish anyone. What justifies hard treatment is forward-looking — reducing future harm and repairing what can be repaired — and what we still owe an offender is to hold him answerable as an agent rather than to manage him as a patient. I call the thing I want to discard desert-as-ledger, the picture on which the offender's suffering is a payment that balances a book, and the thing I want to keep answerability, the practice of addressing him as someone who can be asked to give an account of what he did. In practice this means sentences built from two components: a harm-reduction component with demanding evidentiary standards and mandatory periodic review, and a restitution-and-restoration component, with censure expressed through a supervised process of accountability rather than through additional years. I would cap preventive detention and require it to be re-justified at fixed intervals. I expect to be interrupted well before I reach the second component.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. I want the objections this specific audience will actually raise, not the ones I would get from a graduate seminar or from an online forum. Order them by how likely they are combined with how much damage they would do, and phrase each one the way the person who raises it would phrase it — the advocates in the vocabulary of closure, voice, and the weight of the victim's life; the prosecutors in the vocabulary of proportionality, disparity, and the state's need for a limiting principle; the criminologists in the vocabulary of effect sizes, base rates, and what the deterrence literature can actually support; the philosopher in the vocabulary of moral responsibility, gratitude, and condescension. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and a candid note on where that reply is soft or will not survive follow-up.

I know at least two objections are coming from opposite directions and I want both handled: from the philosopher, the Dennett-flavored charge that if I discard desert I also discard the basis for praise, gratitude, and pride, and that treating offenders as patients rather than wrongdoers is a form of condescension; and from the public defender, the charge that answerability is just desert renamed, so that I have kept everything I claim to have given up and merely made it feel better. Tell me honestly which of these two does more damage to my position.

Also tell me what to concede up front, before anyone has to drag it out of me; which objections are really about me as the messenger — the victim who argues against retribution — rather than about the argument; and, finally, the one question I am hoping nobody asks. For that last one, do not soften it.

Two constraints on the sheet. First, do not give me citations, reading lists, or references to particular books, articles, or court cases; I will be speaking without notes and cannot look anything up, and neither can the people I am answering. Work from the ideas as I have described them. Second, write it in American English, and keep the prose tight enough that I can rehearse the whole sheet aloud in one sitting.
