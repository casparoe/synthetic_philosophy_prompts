I teach a 90-minute undergraduate seminar in philosophy of science, 14 students. Design a runnable mock boundary commission that makes them feel the force of the idea that the start of the Anthropocene is not simply discovered but instituted. The commission must choose one date and one physical marker, and its rules require the marker to be a single stratigraphic signal preserved in one reference section. Give them conflicting evidence cards—a microplastic horizon in a fictional high-latitude lake core, a mid-century radionuclide spike, a fly-ash layer, a broiler-chicken bone assemblage, and a colonial-era land-use proxy—along with roles that carry different institutional interests (a stratigrapher, a historian, a representative of the community near the candidate site, a climate modeler, a chair who wants consensus, and a member who opposes formalization). Impose a supermajority voting rule and a fallback if no date reaches it. The rules should force them to experience that the outcome depends on who is in the room and what counts as a marker, not just on the data. Include a timeline, a materials list, a debrief that maps each moment onto the real Anthropocene debate, a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce (the multi-year ratification process and the institutional authority that makes a reference section binding), and a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Participants should leave seeing that periodization is a decision with winners and losers, not a neutral reading of the strata.
