I am a novelist, and for the better part of a year I have been writing a book whose central drama turns on a borderline case — a woman who has received so many successive neural grafts that the question of whether she is still the person who signed the contract becomes, to everyone involved, unanswerable — and the longer I live with the manuscript, the more I suspect that my own intuitions about such cases are not the ones I would have predicted, and that my writing group's intuitions are not the ones they would predict either. So for the reading group I host — six or seven intelligent non-philosophers plus one lapsed graduate student who will otherwise hijack the evening — I want to build a decision tree that sorts people into the actual positions on vagueness rather than into the labels they would volunteer. It should begin from something nobody in the room denies: that "bald," "heap," "tall," and my own "the same woman" all admit of borderline cases. From there, each branch should isolate a single commitment — whether a borderline case has a fact of the matter that we merely cannot know; whether a borderline statement can fail to be either true or false; whether the indeterminacy lives in the world, in language, or in us; whether the boundary is itself vague (higher-order vagueness); and whether "borderline" names one phenomenon or several. I want the leaves to include at least epistemicism and supervaluationism, and also subvaluationism, the contextualist view on which the boundary shifts with the conversational score, a degree-theoretic view, ontic indeterminacy, and the deflationary option holding that the puzzle is an artifact of a false picture of meaning — flagging honestly any two leaves the tree cannot separate, and any combination of answers that no one occupies, with a sentence on whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Two things I want handled with care. First, do not build the cases out of the heap and the bald man; those are so worn that my friends will recognize the shape of the answer before they answer, which defeats the whole purpose. Give me fresh cases drawn from a novelist's world — how many emendations before a manuscript is a different novel; how much of a coauthor's prose before the book is no longer mine; how blurred a portrait's edge must grow before the sitter is unrecognizable — each phrased as a question a person can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Second, I have a distinction of my own that I would like the tree to test, and I want you to tell me whether it tracks the epistemicism/supervaluationism line or cuts across it: between a borderline case in which a latent stipulation already seems to inhere in the term (as with "middle-aged," where a community could simply decide and the decision would feel like discovery) and a borderline case in which any stipulation would feel like a fresh act of invention (as with "art" or "sacred"). Please write the tree in American English, keep every branch question concrete enough that a smart reader answers from the case rather than from theory, and give each leaf a name, a one-line statement of what it is committed to, and its most awkward consequence.
