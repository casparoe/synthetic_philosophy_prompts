A friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about something that began as a joke and turned serious. In cosmology it is entirely routine to say that the universe is about 13.8 billion years old, that the microwave background was released when the universe was roughly 380,000 years old, and that the light from a distant galaxy set out when the universe was half its present age. Every such statement treats the universe as having a single clock, the one belonging to the frame in which the microwave background looks the same in all directions, the frame relative to which our own galaxy is measured to be drifting; and most of us would add, without embarrassment, that there are galaxies beyond our horizon right now that we will never see. Yet the chapter before explains that simultaneity is relative and that there is no fact of the matter about what is happening elsewhere at this moment.

My friend, who reads Gödel for pleasure, insisted that this is not harmless bookkeeping. On her account, the practice presupposes a real, observer-independent division of the universe into simultaneous slices, and Gödel's 1949 rotating-dust solution — a universe that satisfies Einstein's equations, contains no singularities, and yet contains closed paths through time, so that a traveller could in principle come back around to a moment before she left — was meant to show that no such division is forced on us by the physics. If the slices are there at all, they are there because of a contingent fact about how the matter in our universe happens to be arranged, and Gödel took it to be absurd that the reality of time should hang on a contingency of that kind. I replied that cosmic time is only a convenient coordinate, like choosing to work in a rotating frame, and that nothing absolute has been smuggled in. She answered that if that is all it is, then our confident dating of cosmic events is a fiction and ought to be labelled one.

What does the practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the view of time that the dating of cosmic events requires. Does it need an objective present, or only a preferred slicing, and is a preferred slicing by itself enough to make a statement like the age of the universe true of the universe rather than of our choice of coordinates? Which rival views of time does the practice quietly rule out, and is it consistent with its own use of the relativity of simultaneity a few chapters earlier? Distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with. Finally, if the presupposition were replaced, what would the practice have to become — could every age and every date be stated relative to a congruence of observers, the way we relativise velocity, without losing anything we now do with it? And tell me where the practice is underdetermined, so that several views of time would license it, and where it is not.
