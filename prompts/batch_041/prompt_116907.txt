I keep having the same argument with a friend in my metaphysics reading group, and I want to lay it out properly. His picture, which he's very attached to, is this: mental causation is like two people shooting the same guy at the same instant, each bullet by itself enough to kill him. One gun is the brain event, the other is the mental event. Since the brain event alone already fixes what the body does, the mental one is either a second, redundant cause — overdetermination — or it's nothing, just the shadow the brain throws. He thinks this kills non-reductive physicalism, or at least leaves the mental doing no work. He's also fond of pointing out that it wouldn't be a one-off: it'd happen every single time you think or do anything, which he says makes it worse rather than better.

Is the analogy carrying the argument, or is there an argument underneath it? Walk me through it. What maps onto what in his picture, and where does the mapping actually break? I'm especially unsure about two things. First, whether the two guns are independent causes the way two shooters are — and if the mental supervenes on the brain, whether theres even a candidate for a second cause in the first place. Second, whether the mental event and the brain event are two events at all in the views he's attacking, or one event under two descriptions, in which case the two-gun picture is just wrong from the start.

Then the bit I care about most. If I run his analogy on things nobody thinks are causally idle — a statue's weight breaking the scale, boiling water scalding someone, digestion putting on weight — do I get the same excluded-or-overdetermined verdict? If yes, what does that do to his conclusion? If no, what's different about the mental case, and is the difference one that helps him or hurts him?

And finally: is there a better picture here, or does the whole thing come down to which premise you give up — causal closure, the no-overdetermination intuition, or the claim that the mental is genuinely distinct from the physical? Give me the correspondence table, the breaks that matter for what he wants to conclude, the ones that don't, and a straight answer on whether his conclusion survives.
