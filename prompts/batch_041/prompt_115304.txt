I convene a second-year undergraduate seminar in logic and philosophy of mind — eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, most of them comfortable with formal notation but none of them trained in metatheory. I want a session that makes them feel from the inside the difference between working within a formal system and reasoning about it: the point at which the only way forward is to stop applying the rules and start proving things about them — the move sometimes called jumping out of the system. I do not want that point announced in advance, and I do not want them to be handed the answer.

Please design the session as a runnable activity rather than a lesson plan for a lecture. I am putting the system at the centre of it here, so that you need not rely on any recollection of the published version (it is Hofstadter's MIU system). The symbols are M, I and U; strings are built from them; the single starting string is MI; and there are four rules of transformation:

1. any string ending in I may have U appended to it;
2. a string of the form Mx may be rewritten as Mxx, that is, whatever follows the M is doubled;
3. any occurrence of III may be replaced by U;
4. any occurrence of UU may be deleted.

The target is MU. The target cannot be reached: no sequence of applications of those four rules takes MI to MU. Part of what I want the students to discover is how one could possibly know such a thing, rather than merely suspect it after an hour of failure. Do not put the impossibility result in front of them at the start; but the deliverable should contain the argument by which it is established, written out in a form I can put on the board at the end and in a form I can deploy if the group stalls.

The activity itself should include:

- A clear set of rules, roles and materials (worksheets, rule cards, tokens — whatever you judge best) and a scoring scheme that creates a genuine pull to keep generating derivations, with a stake or prize that involves no real money. At least one target other than MU should be reachable, so that the group knows what success looks like and cannot explain its failure by insufficient effort; satisfy yourself that it really is reachable by exhibiting a derivation.
- A social mechanism by which derivations must be certified by someone other than their author, so that the group runs into the difference between producing a string and establishing that a string is producible.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, including a phase in which the group is genuinely stuck and a phase in which the means of escape is made available in such a way that they have to take it themselves.
- A debrief that maps what happened onto the idea, and that ends by asking the students what kind of statement their reason for believing MU is impossible would be — whether it is one of the strings the system can generate or a statement about the system — and what would have to be true of a system for that statement to be generated inside it.
- An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time (the student who finds the invariant within ten minutes; the group that brute-forces until the clock runs out; the prize claimed on a technicality; the collapse into competitive point-scoring) and what I should do about each.
- A frank note on which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, and which part of it I must therefore supply myself. I would rather have that stated plainly than have the session pretend to more than it can do.

They should leave having felt the pull of staying inside the system, the difference between not having found something and having shown that it cannot be found, and the necessity of stepping outside — without my having named any of that at the outset. Write in British English.
