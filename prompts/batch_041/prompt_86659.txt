I retired from a chemistry department a few years back and I've been teaching myself epistomology since — so treat me as a beginner who knows how theories get tested but not who said what. I keep bumping into Nozick's tracking idea (roughly: you know something when your belief is true, you wouldn't have held it if it were false, and you would have held it if it were true) and the complaint that it breaks the closure of knowledge under known entailment, i.e. that you can know A, know that A implies B, and still not know B.

What I want first is the spec, not the verdict. Write the desiderata that any theory of knowledge has to meet: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results it can't contradict — each with the case or consideration behind it. Include the obvious ones (Gettier cases, lottery propositions, the skeptic, closure under entailment, whether knowing entails knowing that you know) but also the things a working scientist would insist on: that the theory say something sensible about knowledge you get by inferring from a theory to a prediction, and about knowledge you get from an instrument whose workings you don't fully understand. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp.

The part I most want is whether some subset of these can't be satisfied together. My hunch is that demanding closure and demanding that knowledge be sensitive to falsity can't both be had, and I'd like to know if that's right, what the price of each is, and which requirement knocks out the most candidates. Then a few sentences on how the tracking view and a reliabilist view each fare against the list. Finish by telling me how confident you actually are in all this, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
