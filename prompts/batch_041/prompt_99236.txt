For several years now I have watched rationalist blogs and forums cycle through the same fight about whether "better than" is transitive, usually set off by some version of a spectrum argument: line up a sequence of alternatives in which each adjacent pair is intuitively a wash, run the sequence out to a case that is intuitively far worse than the first, and conclude that either one of the hairline steps really does make a difference or the all-things-considered betterness relation is not transitive. The two sides talk past each other every time. One camp treats transitivity as a precondition of having values at all -- it is what lets you have a utility function, a representation theorem, a decision procedure immune to money pumps and Dutch books. The other camp treats the spectrum as a reductio of that precondition and is willing to buy intransitivity, or vagueness, or incomparability, as the cheaper cost. I want the document these two camps could actually put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Give me the following. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what each side accepts about the shape of the spectrum argument, about which of its premises are doing the work, about what transitivity is a property of (a relation among items? a constraint on choice? a feature of a representation?), and about which of the familiar objections -- money pumps, the alleged conceptual truth of transitivity, the disanalogy with sorites cases -- are live and which are red herrings. Second, a disagreements table: the precise residual disagreements, each side's position phrased in wording that side itself would endorse, with no formulation so hedged that it says nothing and none so loaded that only one side could sign it. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each crux the case, argument, or thought experiment both sides agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction written down in advance. If part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why.

Two additional demands, because otherwise both sides will simply replay memorized moves. Run the whole disagreement through a concrete spectrum that is not the population-ethics one everybody has seen -- use, say, a sequence of individually negligible risks, or of individually trivial delays, or something of your own choosing -- and report whether the structure of the dispute changes with the example. And address explicitly a third position that neither camp tends to foreground: that a bare claim of the form A is better than B is really elliptical or context-relative, so that transitivity holds within a context but can fail across contexts. Say whether that possibility dissolves the disagreement, relocates it, or merely restates one side's view in friendlier words, and whether the two camps could sign a joint statement that leaves this third option open.

No bibliography, reading list, or citations -- a statement that turns on those is useless to me.
