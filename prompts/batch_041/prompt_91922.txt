I am a novelist at work on a book about a woman who comes to believe a conspiracy theory. The theory itself is not famous; I invented a compact, self-contained story about a manufacturer suppressing an internal safety finding, and its invented particulars are not what I need from you. What I need is to know how to write her mind — whether she is a person who has reasoned badly, or a person who has reasoned about as well as anyone could from where she stands. That is a question about the epistemology of conspiracy belief rather than about her, and I would rather have your honest probability than a verdict on conspiracy theorists in general.

Please give me your credence in the following proposition, which I will call P: that there exists a single criterion, statable without reference to any particular conspiracy theory, that reliably sorts warranted from unwarranted conspiracy beliefs — reliable in the sense that applying it would seldom certify a belief that ought not to be certified and seldom reject one that ought not to be rejected.

Then decompose that number. I expect it to rest on at least the following, and I would like a rough probability for each, with a line of justification, and then the combination:

First, whether conspiracy theory names a natural epistemic kind at all, or only a loose family held together by sociology rather than by structure; a single criterion needs a single subject.

Second, whether the argument from leaks is sound — the thought that large, long-running conspiracies tend to leak, so a theory that posits one and yet reports no leaks is antecedently improbable. I have been calling the quantity this argument trades in a theory's leak budget, and I suspect it is the closest thing to a general test that anyone has; tell me how much of P it could actually carry.

Third, whether warrant in this area is internalist or externalist — whether it turns on the believer's evidence or on how the world in fact is — since a leak-budget criterion looks externalist and would then fail to tell my character what she may believe, which is the very thing I need it to do.

Fourth, whether any general criterion must survive dispersed conspiracies with no central conspirator to leak, and whether those cases break the natural-kind assumption above.

Fifth, the prior probability that conspiracies of the relevant size and duration actually occur, since a criterion that ignores base rates will misfire in one direction or the other.

And sixth, whether the demand for a single criterion is itself the wrong shape of question — whether the epistemology of conspiracy theories might be irreducibly particular, so that P is not merely false but a category mistake.

Then combine the terms and tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to: which sub-question, were it to resolve the other way, would move your credence most, and what argument or finding would move it most.

I will tell you what hangs on the answer. If P is high, I will write my character as applying a procedure and arriving at a defensible result; if P is low, I will write her belief as a matter of temperament and tribe, and the book becomes a different book. A bare it depends is no use to me here; I need the number and the reasoning that yields it, including the place where you think the reasoning is weakest.
