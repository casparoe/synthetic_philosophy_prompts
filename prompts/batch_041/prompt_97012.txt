My colleague in the bio department is finishing a trade book on biodiversity and has asked me to sanity-check the opening chapter before it goes to his editor — the chapter has a few paragraphs on the species problem and he knows I teach philosphy of biology. Here's the relevant passage, more or less as he's written it:

'For most of the history of biology species were assumed to be natural kinds, each defined by an essence — a set of properties that all and only its members share. Darwin destroyed that picture, and with it the idea that species are real divisions in nature. What we call a species is just a convenient slice through a gene pool that is always shifting; the boundaries are ours, not nature's. This is the so-called species problem, and it is not a deep metaphysical puzzle but a practical one: the difficulty of drawing lines through continuous variation. It matters because conservation law, red lists and biodiversity counts all need a stable unit to count. The good news is that modern genetics has largely settled the question — we now define species by reproductive isolation and genetic distance, and what disputes remain are bookkeeping rather than disagreements about what species really are.'
