ok so I've been reading Lara Buchak's Risk and Rationality (Oxford, 2013) and I want a short story that actually runs on risk-weighted expected utility instead of just name-dropping it in one paragraph.

Quick reminder of the machinery, so you don't have to guess: line up a gamble's outcomes from worst to best. A regular expected-utility maximizer weights each outcome by the probability of getting exactly that outcome. A risk-weighted maximizer instead takes an increasing function r from 0 to 1 with r(0)=0 and r(1)=1, and weights each outcome by r(the chance of getting that outcome or better) minus r(the chance of getting something strictly better). If r is the identity function you're back to plain expected utility. If r is convex, the person is risk averse: the worst outcome ends up carrying more weight than its probability deserves and the best outcome less, so she'll turn down gambles a straight expected-value person takes — and on this view that's not a mistake, its a different but perfectly coherent set of rational preferences.

What I want from the story:

- A protagonist with a specific r that isn't the identity, stated explicitly and early in some natural way — she's the sort of person who explains her own decision rule, or an actuary, or a poker player with a homemade rule of thumb. Make it a two-piece function: convex in one region and concave in another, so that she both insures against small chances of disaster and buys tickets for tiny chances at something huge. The story should make those two habits feel like one personality rather than a contradiction.
- A pair of decisions that shows the pattern an expected-utility economist would call incoherent: same three outcomes, same utilities, just the probabilities shifted around, and she takes the safe option in one pair and the gamble in the other. Show that for her it isn't incoherent — walk through her arithmetic once, in her own voice, but keep it short.
- A second character who maximizes ordinary expected utility, and let their disagreement be about what's worth doing with a life rather than about who did the math wrong.
- Somewhere, a scene showing what a convex r costs her: she pays to avoid a small chance of a big loss even when the premium is steep, and we see the price in ordinary terms — a job she doesn't take, a move she doesn't make, something she hedges that maybe should have been risked.
- The ending should turn on the shape of r, not on a coincidence.
