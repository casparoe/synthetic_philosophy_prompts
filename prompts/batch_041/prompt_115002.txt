I am a high school student preparing an application to a summer research programme in philosophy and economics, and I need a short research proposal: roughly two and a half pages, for a ten-week project supervised by a faculty mentor. Please act as a philosopher of economics with a strong background in game theory and Bayesian epistemology. Think carefully before you write. Begin by brainstorming two or three different ways of framing the topic below, with a sentence on what each framing would buy me, and only then commit to one and write the proposal out in full.

My half-formed idea concerns the common prior assumption — the assumption, standard in Bayesian game theory, that rational agents who disagree do so only because they hold different information, never because they start from genuinely different prior probabilities. Under that assumption, agents who announce their posterior beliefs and regard each other as rational cannot go on disagreeing: the disagreement has to evaporate. The same assumption is what underwrites the results showing that purely informational speculation is impossible — if two traders share a prior, a difference in their beliefs about an asset's value can only reflect a difference in information, so whoever takes the other side of the trade must know something you do not. The difficulty is that real people plainly do start from different priors, and the thought that everyone would share a prior if they were suitably rational is a slogan rather than an argument.

What I would like to explore is whether the assumption can be rescued by moving it up one level. Instead of requiring agents to agree on a first-order prior over states of the world, require only that they share a prior over priors: a common distribution over the candidate first-order priors, from which each agent's own prior is the one that fits their private information. The question I want the proposal to be about is roughly this: does a common prior over priors do genuine work — restoring the agreement and no-trade results in some weakened form, or explaining why persistent disagreement is stable — or is it merely a redescription of the original assumption, so that anything proved with it could have been proved without it? I want the proposal to be honest about that possibility, because I suspect it is the real danger. If the hyperprior is common knowledge and each agent's first-order prior is simply what that hyperprior yields given their private information, I worry the construction collapses back into the ordinary common prior assumption on a larger space, and I would rather discover that at the proposal stage than at the end.

I can handle basic probability, and I am comfortable working through two-agent, two-state examples by hand. I am not going to prove general theorems, and I would prefer the project to be a careful conceptual one with small worked examples rather than an attempt at a contribution to decision theory.

Please write the proposal so that it contains: a precise statement of the question, sharp enough that I could tell whether I had answered it; an explanation of why the standard defences of the common prior assumption, and the standard criticisms of it, leave my question open; the method — what I would actually do, week by week over ten weeks, including the kind of example that would be decisive, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternative of simply arguing for or against the assumption on intuitive grounds; what a real result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest note on scope, including whether ten weeks is enough and what I should cut if it is not.

Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list, and do not cite particular papers or attribute results to named authors — I will add the references myself, and I would rather not be handed citations I cannot check. Refer to positions and arguments by description instead.
