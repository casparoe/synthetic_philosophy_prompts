I've been going round in circles on pain asymbolia and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a lit review. Quick reminder of the phenomenon, so we're on the same page: after certain insula/limbic lesions, people still feel pain -- they can localize it and rate how intense it is -- but it doesn't bother them at all; they report pain and are completely untroubled by it, sometimes not even moving away from the noxious thing. One camp reads this as proof that the awfulness of pain is a separate ingredient from the sensation, so the badness must come from something bolted on -- an attitude or a reaction to the feeling rather than the feeling itself. The other camp says a state that nobody minds just isn't pain, or that the patients are misdescribing or confabulating, and that you can't get from what someone says to what they feel. Same evidence on both sides, nobody's budged in decades.

My hunch is that the real sticking point is a modal intuition rather than a fact or a definition: whether an experience that feels exactly like pain but isn't bad for you is even possible. If you think the badness is baked into the felt quality, asymbolia has to be about something else; if you think it isn't, asymbolia is the evidence -- and no clinical finding breaks the tie, because either side can absorb any finding. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden empirical question (report reliability, which circuits do what) doing the work? And what would actually count as progress -- an experiment, a distinction, or admitting the two sides are answering different questions under one word? I dont need citations.
