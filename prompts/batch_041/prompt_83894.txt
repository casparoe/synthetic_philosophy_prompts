For most of my career I was a marine invertebrate zoologist, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy, mainly the literature on animals and speciesism. My old department has asked me to lead a session for biology graduate students, who tend to assume the whole subject reduces to a single question about whether a creature can suffer. I would like a decision tree I can put in front of them, and I would like it built the way a taxonomist builds one: first the characters, then the groups.

Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, and no numbered lists; if you need to enumerate, do it in running sentences. I want it to read like a long letter from a colleague, with the branching carried by the prose itself.

Every branching point should be a question the reader can answer by endorsing or rejecting a claim about a concrete case, phrased so that someone who knows none of the labels can still answer it, and so that the answer sorts them by what they say about cases rather than by which name they like. I want the questions to come apart along at least four axes: what grounds moral status, whether it be actual capacities, capacities normal for the kind, capacities the individual would have had but for an accident, bare sentience, or something relational such as membership in a community of reciprocal claimants; whether status is a threshold or a matter of degree, and if a matter of degree, whether the degrees are commensurable or lexically ordered; whether species membership is ever itself status-conferring or only a rough proxy for other properties; and whether the wrongness of killing is separable from the wrongness of causing suffering.

Certain cases have to appear somewhere in the tree, because they are where my students' intuitions will collide: a pig and a human infant stipulated to have equal cognitive and affective capacities; a human being in a persistent vegetative state; a dog, a chimpanzee, a chicken, an octopus, and a shrimp, ranked; a being that would have had full capacities but for an injury; a member of a cognitively simple species that has been genetically enhanced; and, the one I care most about, a species whose individual members are cognitively simple but which forms in aggregate a rational and self-aware community, so that the reader has to commit on whether status attaches to individuals or to systems.

At the leaves I would like eight to ten positions, each labeled with a short descriptive name, with the conventional name appended in a subordinate clause where one exists; I am not asking for citations or a reading list. The tree should be able to reach at least these: equal consideration of interests, a rights view, a hierarchical view on which status tracks the capacities typical of a kind, modal personhood, a rational-agency-only view, a relational or contractualist view, a welfarist view that permits humane use, and a biocentric or ecocentric-adjacent view. Each leaf should carry one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any of my questions, say so plainly rather than papering over it, and where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, say that too and tell me whether you think the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

I have read enough encyclopedia summaries of this debate to know what they say; what I want is the instrument, not a survey of it. Please write in American English.
