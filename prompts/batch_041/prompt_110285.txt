In the space of a single year I received two apologies, and their shapes were almost exactly opposite. The first came from the insurer of the driver who put me in hospital: a letter expressing sincere regret that the accident had happened, a paragraph expressly reserving the question of fault, and a sentence to the effect that nothing in the letter was to be taken as conceding anything. I am told that in some places this is not merely an insurer's habit but a matter of statute, and that at least one province has a law shielding an apology from being read as an admission of fault. The second came last spring, when my city formally apologized to the descendants of the community it displaced a century ago. The wrong was named without hedging, the city spoke as the very entity that had done it, and in the following breath it made plain that no compensation would follow and that no one now living was to blame. I read the first at my kitchen table and stood at the back of a hall for the second, and what I cannot get past is that the two apologies concede opposite things. The insurer's concedes that something happened but not that anyone did it. The city's concedes that someone did it but not that anything is owed. And the city managed, within one ceremony, to claim continuity with its own past in order to apologize and discontinuity with its own past in order to excuse itself — one entity when an apology is owed, another when money is.

What I want is the implicit theory of apology these practices presuppose. It seems to me that an apology can contain several distinguishable things: the expression of regret; the acknowledgment that a wrong, and not merely a harm, was done; the acceptance of blame; and the undertaking of repair. For each of the two practices, tell me which of these it requires, which it merely permits, and which it forbids. Reconstruct whatever theory would make each practice coherent, and say whether they presuppose one theory or two that cannot both be right. I am especially interested in whether the theory is coherent at all, or whether the practices trade on an equivocation — the insurer's letter perhaps on the gap between acknowledging a harm and acknowledging a wrong, the city's on the gap between an entity that acts and a people who are answerable. Distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with; I would not be surprised to find that several quite different theories of apology would license exactly what I witnessed.

Then tell me what would have to change if we insisted on the thicker view, on which an apology just is the acknowledgment of fault together with an undertaking not to repeat and, where possible, to make good. Could a state apologize for a wrong whose perpetrators are dead and whose victims are also dead? Could an insurer apologize to me at all? And on that view, what was I doing in that hall? I had no standing of my own — I was not wronged, my grandparents were not of that community, and those who were wronged can receive nothing now — yet it seemed to matter that somebody was there to hear it. Say who can accept an apology on behalf of the dead, and whether that question has an answer or only a convention.

Finally, say which rival views of apology these practices quietly rule out. If what happened in the hall was not an apology, what was it — a disavowal, a promise about the future, an act of institutional self-definition? And if the insurer's letter was not an apology, what was it, and does it matter that it was the only thing I received? Argue rather than survey; where the practice is underdetermined, say so plainly instead of inventing a single presupposition to hang it on.
