I teach a seminar on Leibniz, and every year my students leave with the slogan that this is the best of all possible worlds, meaning that whatever happens is for the best. That is not the thesis. Leibniz's claim is about a selection: not everything possible can coexist with everything else, and among the sets of things that can coexist God actualizes the one whose whole is best — which allows that a part of the actual world may be worse, considered by itself, than some part that was left out. I want to close the seminar with a poem that makes this structure audible rather than merely restating it.

Write a sonnet — fourteen lines, iambic pentameter, Shakespearean rhyme scheme — on the best of all possible worlds, and then a second version of it with the same subject, length, metre and rhyme sounds. The first version is the poem a poet writes by default, every line made as good as it can be on its own. In the second, each line must be individually weaker than its counterpart — flatter, more serviceable, more plainly present to carry a rhyme or a stress — while the whole is meant to be better, the weak lines load-bearing for the strong ones. Do not argue for the second version inside it; it should simply be that way. Then a section headed Commentary, roughly 250 words: what you did, which line is carrying the constraint of compossibility, what makes the second version better as a whole, and whether that sense of better is Leibniz's — variety and order bought with the least means — or only ours. State how confident you are in your reading of Leibniz, and name the one point you are least sure of.
