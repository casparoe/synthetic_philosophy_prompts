Here's the thing I keep noticing. In arguments about whether an AI system could be sentient or deserve moral consideration, people on both sides seem to describe the exact same computation at different levels, and then the moral conclusion flips. One person says: it's just matrix multiplications and gradient updates, there's no one home, so nothing morally relevant is happening. Another says: yeah, but at a higher level it's implementing a self-model, a global workspace, an aversive feedback loop, so there is something it's like to be it. Same weights, same forward pass, same causal structure as far as the hardware is concerned. The disagreement doesn't seem to be about the physical facts; it's about which description of the computation is supposed to count for moral status. Then there's a third move: someone says the high-level description is just a useful fiction we impose, so it can't ground moral status, but the low-level description is also a description, so why does that one get to be the real one?

Same pattern in interpretability: one person says a circuit is just a lookup table or a bunch of linear probes; another says it's a truthfulness detector or a self-model; and the moral upshot seems to ride on which description wins, even though nobody disputes the weights.

I've been calling this the level-switch problem in my notes, but I doubt thats a real term. It feels close to multiple realizability, but not the same: multiple realizability is about the same function in different substrates, and here it's the same substrate under different descriptions. It's also close to the standard levels-of-analysis picture in cognitive science -- computational goal, algorithm, implementation -- but those levels are supposed to be complementary, not competing for moral weight. Maybe it's close to the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties, or to the idea of a specification hierarchy, or to debates about computational sufficiency. I dont know.
