I'm a retired field ecologist who has drifted into philosophy of science and social epistemology over the last few years, and I've wrote a short essay for a reading group that I'd like you to tear apart. I don't have the formal econ background, so I'm mostly worried about whether the idea is real or just a relabeling. Here are the notes, not the full essay.

The usual sequential-learning story says that once enough people have acted, later people rationally ignore their own private information and follow the herd. I want to push on a version of this that I think gets missed. Suppose each person's private signal is not about the answer to a fixed question but about which question is worth answering. Early choices can then lock in a question, and later people can be perfectly rational, have independent private signals, and still ignore evidence about the answer, because the cascade is now about relevance rather than truth. I call this a relevance cascade. It's not a cascade about the state of the world; it's a cascade about the allocation of attention. My hunch is that alot of what gets called irrational herding in science and markets is this instead.

Toy version: two questions, A and B. Each agent gets a signal about which is more tractable, not about the correct answer. The first few pick A. A later agent gets a signal that B is more tractable but reasons that the earlier agents must have known something, so picks A anyway. The result is a locked-in research program that no one thinks is true, only relevant.

What I want from you: comments on whether this distinction is genuine or collapses into standard informational cascades once you allow correlated signals or payoff externalities. Is there a clean model where relevance cascades and truth cascades come apart? Also tell me if the essay is interesting or just a fancy way of saying attention is scarce. And give me the strongest objection I should address before I show it to people who actually know this stuff. I'd rather you be blunt than polite.
