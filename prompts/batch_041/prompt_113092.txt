I am a novelist, four years into a book that is narrated in part by a colony organism, and next month I am teaching a three-hour workshop at a writers' conference to twelve published fiction writers, none of whom has any formal training in science or philosophy. The subject is writing minds that are not human, and the manuscripts I have read for it share a single failure: a person in a strange body. An alien who grieves like a suburban widower; a hive intelligence that thinks in the first person singular; a raven that reasons in complete English sentences. My diagnosis is that they do not need more imagination but a working concept of intelligence, and that the only way to give them one is to make them feel the genuine difficulty of the question rather than hand them a definition.

Please act as an expert in comparative cognition and the philosophy of mind who has also spent years teaching writers, and design that session for me. Think the pedagogical problem through before you write anything: begin by setting out your candidate carrying examples and the dependency order of the ideas, and only then commit to a plan. Do not explain intelligence to me; I am asking for the plan by which I will explain it. If you think my framing of the difficulty is wrong, say so briefly before the plan. Then give me, in order: the sequence in which the ideas must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it; the single example or case that should carry the session, what it must have and must lack, and whether the octopus — a majority of its neurons in its arms, an arm that can reach and choose without the brain's leave — is that example or a distraction; the misconceptions the room will arrive with or form, and the exact point in the sequence at which you would head each one off; one question or short exercise to put to them at the end that would show me whether they have got the concept rather than merely enjoyed the conversation; and what you would deliberately leave out at this level, stated as a refusal.

Here is material to work with, so that you are not guessing at my framing. I want them to come away suspicious of: intelligence as a single ladder with us at the top; the reification of the psychometric g-factor, which is a pattern of correlations rather than a substance; the layered reptile-mammal-cortex story; the equation of intelligence with consciousness and of both with sentience; the assumption that more neurons means more mind; and the Turing-test instinct that a mind is proved by conversation — which is, I suspect, the fiction writer's instinct as well, since we reveal minds through speech. I want them to see that a system's intelligence is partly a fact about its body and its environment rather than a quantity stored inside it (the frame problem; Moravec's paradox, on which chess is cheap for a machine and catching a ball is not; the no-free-lunch results, which make a genuinely general learner impossible without built-in assumptions about the problems it will meet); that cognition may be continuous with the doings of slime molds, plants, and bacteria; and that the opposite error is just as dangerous — reading cleverness off behavior, as with Clever Hans, or denying mental continuity on principle. Be candid about where the evidence thins and the analogy takes over, since that boundary is precisely what these writers need to learn to feel.

Keep the plan executable in three hours, in a room without a projector. Write in American English.
