I am in the middle of a novel about a decision theorist — a man of real gifts and one spectacular flaw, which is that he will tell you, without embarrassment, that he prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — and I have reached the scene in which a colleague proposes to make the flaw visible: an afternoon of trades, each of which my man accepts, that carries him round the cycle and sets him down exactly where he began, with less money. My editor has flagged the scene as too abstract to be dramatic, which I take to mean that the argument has to be on the page rather than gestured at, so I want the machinery exactly right. The trouble is that the same colleague could mount a formally parallel attack on a person of an entirely different kind — someone whose degrees of belief are not probabilistically coherent, and who can therefore be sold a package of bets that loses whatever the world does; I believe the second argument is generally called a Dutch book and the first a money pump, and a friend who teaches the subject assures me the comparison is a commonplace, though every time I try to write it down the two arguments slide apart in my hands. What I would like from you is a careful comparison of the two: each stated as precisely as it can be, an account of exactly where the parallel holds and where it breaks, and then a verdict on whether the pump is as good an argument for its conclusion as the book is for its own.

Three things in particular I want thought through. First, is the force of either argument really that the agent is irrational, or only that he is exploitable — and if those come apart, which of the two does my scene actually need? Second, the betting argument appears to require nothing beyond the agent's attitudes as they stand at a single moment, whereas the pump requires the agent to go on choosing in a certain way across an afternoon; does that mean the pump only works on someone too dim or too impulsive to see it coming, and if my man is sophisticated — if he foresees the whole sequence and announces that he will simply refuse the third step, or plays along to the second and walks away — does the pump collapse, and is there any analogous escape open to the incoherent believer? Third, I want the difference marked between a pump that runs on a fixed cycle of preferences and a pump that runs on preferences that change from one moment to the next, since my plot eventually turns on whether the character is inconsistent with himself or merely unstable across time, and I am not at all sure those are the same accusation.

I am not asking for a reading list — I am not going to read the literature, and citations would only tempt you into guessing — but for the two arguments laid out side by side, with the objections a professional in the field would actually take seriously, and your own judgment about which of them is the more compelling and why. If you can also tell me which single sentence, put in a character's mouth, would be the most philosophically respectable version of the objection that the pump is a trick that works only on the unwary, I will give it to the colleague.
