I lost most of last night to an argument with my flatmate that began as a joke and ended with her telling me I don't have a decision theory, I have a mood. The two cases she kept coming back to: (1) Omega flips a fair coin — if it comes up tails she asks me for £100, if it comes up heads she hands me £10,000, but only on the condition that she predicted I'd have paid her had it come up tails. It came up tails, and she's standing there with her hand out. (2) A letter arrives saying: we've buried charges under ten houses, send us £100 or we set them off, and we only post this letter to people we've worked out will refuse. She calls the first one counterfactual mugging and the second one XOR blackmail, and her line is that they're two faces of one committment: in the first case the money I'd be buying was never in my branch of the world anyway, and in the second the harm I'd be buying off was, by construction, already settled by a prediction about me — so anyone who pays in either case is paying for a fiction. I think that's too quick, and I also suspect the two cases come apart somewhere I can't point to without sounding like I'm just announcing my preferred answer. I'm probably mangling the standard versions of both; if I am, use the proper ones and tell me what I got wrong, but don't tidy them up so much that the interesting bit falls out.

What I want back is not an essay and not a tour of the literature. Build me a decision tree I can sit and answer — something that sorts me into a position by what I say about these cases rather than by which name I like the sound of. So work outwards from the questions rather than inwards from a list of theories: first decide which questions genuinely split the space, then which combinations of answers are actually live, then hang the names on the live combinations. Every internal node should be a single question about the cases, phrased so I can answer it without knowing where my answer leads — "when I evaluate paying, am I holding fixed my decision procedure or only the physical act?", "when I'm told the coin came up tails, is that news about the world or news about which agent I am?" — that sort of thing, though you should find the ones that really do the work. Leaves should be named positions: I need causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, the timeless/functional family and the updateless one, plus whatever unnamed positions the space throws up (a view that pays Omega and stiffs the blackmailer; one that refuses both but for two quite different reasons; one that says the cases are only well-posed if you could have pre-committed beforehand). Under each leaf put one line on the commitment I'd least want to have to defend out loud. Keep the whole thing to something I can get through in twenty minutes — six to nine questions, say.

Two things I'd rather you didn't fudge. First, don't let every fork come off a single axis: my flatmate reckons the entire dispute is one question about whether a choice can matter other than by causing things, and I'd bet there's a second fork about whether the probability I'm supposed to use is the one I had before learning which branch I'm in or the one I have after, and a third about whether any of it survives Omega being 90% reliable rather than infallible — I'd like to see which theories that last one actually threatens. Second, be straight about the joins. If two positions can't be seperated by these cases — I have a hunch a couple of them only part company over cases with two copies of me in them, or over a prediction about an act I could have pre-committed to — flag it at the node where they diverge instead of inventing a question that pretends to sort them, and where an answer-combination comes out empty, tell me whether that's because nobody defends it, because it's incoherent, or simply because nobody's bothered.
