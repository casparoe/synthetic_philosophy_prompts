I convene a final-year undergraduate seminar in social epistemology and I am setting the examination paper. I need one essay question on epistemic injustice, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must not be answerable by reciting Miranda Fricker's distinction between testimonial and hermeneutical injustice: a candidate who can do no more than sort examples into the two received categories should not pass. I want the crux of the question to be a genuine judgement call, so that a strong candidate has to argue for a verdict rather than retrieve one.

Please build the question around the following material, which you should refine and present to candidates as part of the question paper.

Case A. A woman, Rosa, is among a small number of people to have undergone a novel surgical procedure and, afterwards, to experience a state she can only call a second attention: a persistent, low-grade awareness of her own body from a viewpoint she cannot locate. No term in her language and no shared framework captures it. When she tries to describe it to doctors, friends and colleagues she meets blank puzzlement, and some conclude that she is anxious or attention-seeking. Her inability to make herself understood leaves her unable to seek help, to compare notes, or to have the experience recognised as something that is genuinely happening to her. Nothing about Rosa's social position, however, explains the gap: the experience is simply new, and no one, powerful or powerless, has a concept for it.

Case B. Suppose the same experience is common among a group that is socially despised, and that the dominant group has over time declined to develop or borrow any vocabulary for it, treating the subject as distasteful and those who raise it as complainers. Here the gap persists because those with the power to shape the shared language have turned away from it.

The question should require candidates to say whether, and in what sense, Rosa is wronged in each case; to state the condition Fricker places on hermeneutical injustice, namely that the interpretive gap be a product of the subject's exclusion from the practices that shape shared meaning, and to assess whether that condition is defensible; to distinguish the wrong of being unable to render one's experience intelligible from the wrong of being disbelieved once one speaks; and to say whether the difference between a gap that is merely present and a gap that is actively maintained by those who could close it is a difference that matters. A strong answer may connect the maintained gap to the thought that hearers can be culpably unwilling to take up interpretive resources they already possess, and may note the phenomenon of speakers who pre-emptively truncate their own testimony when they expect hostile or unreceptive uptake; do not let the answer depend on naming theorists beyond Fricker.

Then supply the following.

First, the essay question exactly as it would appear on the paper, self-contained and requiring no reading beyond the case material you provide.

Second, a model answer of about 700 to 800 words. It should take a definite position rather than survey the field: it should commit to a verdict on each case, give the best argument for that verdict, and then state and meet the strongest objection to it. It should make its points without leaning on named publications, and it must not depend on quotations or on any citation, since candidates will have no access to sources.

Third, a marking scheme worth 20 marks, itemising the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific moves that separate it from an upper-second. Assign a mark to each must-hit point and state, in one line each, how candidates typically get that point wrong. Make the top-band criterion a matter of argumentative grip on the crux rather than of coverage.

Write the question, the model answer and the marking scheme in British English, using British spelling, idiom and punctuation throughout (for example, recognise, analyse, judgement, whilst where it reads naturally).
