Ok so I'm a novelist, I'm 200-odd pages into a book that only works if the reader ends up blaming one particular character, and I've been reading philosophy on the side and now I've gone and confused myself. Here's where I've landed. Tell me if it hangs togeather.

(1) I buy Galen Strawson's argument that nobody is ultimatly responsible for being who they are: to be responsible in that deep sense you'd have to have made yourself, and you can't, because by the time the making gets going the maker is already there.

(2) I also think blame needs the could-have-done-otherwise condition — you're only answerable if you genuinely could have done the other thing.

(3) I don't think blame is really up to us. You can't argue someone out of resenting the man who wrecked their life; the attitude just happens to them. And I don't think that's a bug — I think responsibility is a fact about our practices rather than about the world.

(4) But I think blame is true of my character. Not just licensed by our practices — true. A reader who finishes the book and doesn't hold him answerable has got something wrong, and I'd stake the novel on that.

(5) Here's the bit that's really chewing on me: every cause of him is something I typed. His childhood, his temper, his luck — all mine. So if being your own cause is what it takes, he's the nearest thing to a self-made man in existence, and yet I'd say he's plainly answerable and a real person with the same history isn't. I can't tell if that's a real problem or just me being cute.

(6) And on the craft side, I've built the book so the reader slowly learns why he's like this, and I'm banking on that understanding making the blame curdle. If you understand everything you forgive everything, more or less.

Which of these are actually pulling against each other, and which only look like they are? Rank the real ones by how bad, say what the cheapest fix is for each and what it costs me, and please don't tell me they're all equally tense. Also: where is your reasoning here most likely to be wrong, and what would actually change your verdict? If nothing would, just admit it — I'll notice.
