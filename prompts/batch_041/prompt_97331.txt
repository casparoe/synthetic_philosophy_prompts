Before I take a side on whether the brain is a computer, I want the specification settled: an account of what any adequate theory of computational implementation — of what it takes for a physical system to count as computing a given function or running a given algorithm — has to deliver. Write me the desiderata, with the consideration behind each: the cases the theory must classify correctly, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the facts or results it must stay consistent with.

At a minimum I want it to handle these cases. A system whose states and transitions can be put into a one-to-one correspondence with those of some automaton, where the correspondence is constructed after the fact rather than read off the system. A device that emits the correct outputs from a hard-wired table, with no internal process answering to the intermediate steps of the algorithm. A system whose evolution a physicist can describe and predict with an equation, such as an orbiting body. A system whose states are continuous rather than discrete. A system that computes only relative to a user or interpreter who supplies the interpretation, together with the fact that the same system can be described as computing something else under a different assignment. And it must keep apart computing a function from merely realizing that function's input-output relation, being describable in computational terms from being a computer, and computing from being used to compute. It must also say what fixes the states of a computation, given that a physical system does not arrive pre-carved into state boundaries. Keep the brain in view as the application throughout: the account should make the claim that the brain computes a substantive one, neither trivially true nor trivially false.

Flag any pair of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive accounts. My suspicion, which I want you to test rather than assume, is that the demand that implementation be objective — that a system compute or fail to compute independently of how we describe it — sits in tension with the demand that not every system implement every computation, and that this tension is where the real work lies. A brief note on how the main families of account fare against the list is welcome, but the criteria are what I am after; the scoring I can do myself.
