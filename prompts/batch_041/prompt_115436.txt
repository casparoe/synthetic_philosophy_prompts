I'm on my school's debate team, and I've been reading about a technique that some AI labs use to check the answers of models that may be smarter than the people reviewing them. As I understand it, two copies of a model are given the same hard question and told to argue opposite sides; a human judge, who may know much less about the question than the models do and may have only a few minutes to spend, reads the exchange and decides which side won. The winning side's reasoning is then treated as the better answer and used to train the model. Our coach likes to say that in a real round the better arguer usually wins even when the better position doesn't, and that a judge who doesn't know the topic will reward confidence, speed, and polish. That makes me suspicious of the whole setup, and I'd like you to help me figure out what it is quietly assuming.

The first thing I can't get past is the judge. The entire point of the method is that the judge could not answer the question alone — that is why we need the debate in the first place. Yet the judge's verdict is treated as a good enough signal to train on. Is the practice inconsistent about how much the judge knows, or are those two claims about the judge compatible? I suspect the method needs the judge to be ignorant of the answer but competent at recognizing a good argument, and I want to know whether that combination is coherent.

Second, does the practice assume that truth has an advantage in adversarial argument — that a debater who is right can always find an argument a limited judge can follow and a debater who is wrong cannot answer? Or does it need only the much weaker claim that being right tends to correlate with winning? Third, it seems to assume that any question worth asking has one correct answer that a side can be said to have gotten, which is plainly false of the policy and ethics topics my team actually debates. What does the method commit itself to about the kinds of questions it can be used on?

Please reconstruct the view this practice presupposes: which commitments it strictly requires, which it merely fits comfortably with, and where rival views would do just as well. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced that view with the alternative — that winning a debate is a matter of persuasion rather than of truth.
