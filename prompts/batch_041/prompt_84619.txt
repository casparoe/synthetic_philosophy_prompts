I spent 35 years as a comparative physiologist measuring things people swore couldn't be measured — metabolic rates in animals the size of a grain of rice, that sort of thing. I'm retired now and I've been reading philosophy, badly but enthusiasticly. I keep running into Nozick's utility monster, the being that gets vastly more pleasure out of any given apple than the rest of us get out of ours, so that by the lights of plain utilitarianism everything should just be handed over to it.

Every philosopher I mention this to reaches for the same escape hatch: we can't measure or compare pleasure between individuals, so the monster is a non-starter. I dont buy it. Edgeworth was already sketching an instrument for exactly this back in 1881, and in my experience biologists have never once met a real capacity that turned out to be unmeasurable in principle — bounded, yes, noisy, yes, but not unmeasurable. So here's the twist I want you to take seriously: suppose hedonic capacity is a measurable biological trait like body mass or metabolic scope, and suppose it varies continuously across individuals and species. The monster isn't a freak, then; it's just the far end of a distribution the rest of us are also somewhere on, the way shrews and elephants sit on the same metabolic curve.

Act as an expert in normative ethics with a solid grasp of the science of welfare measurement. Then give me the most important implications — and I mean the ones that actually bear on a decision I have to make. Does any of this change how I should split it, or is the whole exercise irrelevant at that scale? And tell me which of the implications you list are robust and which ones fall apart if I'm wrong about the measurability.
