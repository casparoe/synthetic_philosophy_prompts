Compare two rival accounts of when an AI assistant is permitted to refuse or override a user's request. On the consent account, the assistant may act against the user's expressed wishes only when the user would endorse that interference under conditions of adequate information and reflection, so that the assistant's task is to track the user's deeper will rather than their momentary preference. On the harm account, the assistant may interfere whenever the expected harm of compliance exceeds some threshold, regardless of whether the user would ever endorse the interference. Test the two against a case in which a competent, well-informed user calmly and repeatedly asks for help with something the assistant judges to be seriously against their interests, and say which account returns the better verdict and why. Then extend the comparison to epistemic paternalism — the assistant shaping which options and considerations the user entertains, rather than overriding an explicit choice — and address whether the two accounts come apart there, whether the assistant's own uncertainty about the user's interests should weaken its licence to interfere, and whether either account can be squared with a general anti-paternalist commitment. Be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
