Ok, I keep going back and forth on how to think about famines and I want help comparing two framings of them, because I suspect theyre not really rivals so much as answering different questions.

Framing one is basically Amartya Sen's: famine as an entitlement failure. The point being its usually not that theres literally no food around, its that a chunk of the population loses the ability to command food — through wages, prices, land, jobs — so people starve in the middle of plenty. The Bengal famine is the classic case, food even getting shipped out while people died. On this view a famine is a failure of a system of distribution, and asking "who did this" is kind of the wrong question.

Framing two is famine as a political crime — something done rather than something that happens. You see this with arguments over the Great Leap Forward, or whether the Irish famine was genocide, or Mike Davis's case that colonial ideology plus an El Niño turned a weather event into mass death in late-1800s India. Here the famine is a product of decisions, priorities, indifference, ideology.

Compare these two for me — as explanations and as moral/political framings. What does each let you see that the other hides? And the bit I really care about: if the entitlement story is right, does anyone actually do anything wrong, or does the crime framing have to smuggle in an agent and an intention that the structural story dissolves? And going the other way, does the crime framing just turn every bad outcome into a culprit?

Also, please be upfront about whats actually settled among people who study famines versus whats genuinely contested. I don't want one of these presented like its just the consensus view, since my sense is people disagree a lot — e.g. about whether entitlement really explains the Irish or the Chinese cases.
