I'm a PhD student in philosophy seconded for a year to the secretariat of a national advisory body on museum restitution, and I have been asked to write the triage procedure our case officers will actually run. The panel sits monthly; each case goes to a single officer with roughly ninety minutes, no philosophical training, and a file rather than any prospect of commissioning new provenance research; the board's votes transfer legal title irreversibly and are politically live, so the procedure has to be executable by someone who cannot be trusted to reconstruct the theory from scratch each time. Build it from four commitments I have argued for and do not want quietly dropped in the translation: legal title and moral entitlement can come apart, and the procedure must report both; the governing question is not whether the original acquisition was lawful under the law then in force but whether the object would plausibly be held by the claimant community today but for the wrong; claims of custody must be assessed against the present interests of living people rather than the rights of a defunct polity; and the lapse of time counts only through its effects on those present interests, not as a freestanding limitation period. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a recommendation against restitution, where it must record that the law blocks the outcome it would otherwise recommend and what it should recommend instead, and where it has to stop and hand the file to a human because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts — mark that last category explicitly rather than burying it in a scoring rubric. Then run it on two cases and show your work: a set of Benin objects in a regional museum with three competing claimants, a royal house, the national commission of the source state, and a descendant community that says neither of the other two speaks for it; and a group of Parthenon sculptures removed under an authorization from the imperial authority then governing Athens, which the claimant state wants reunited with the building they came from. Include at least one verdict I will not like. Finish with the cases the procedure will misfire on — I am especially worried about human remains, and about sacred objects whose wrongness does not consist in the loss of a title at all. Stipulate any facts you need; I care about the reasoning, not the history.
