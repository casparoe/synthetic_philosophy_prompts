I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a historical novel, and I keep getting into arguements with a friend who teaches philosophy. She insists I'm a pragmatist about truth and I keep telling her I have no idea what that means. So I want to actually test it. Take the two classic pragmatist accounts of truth -- Peirce's, roughly that truth is the opinion inquiry would end up settling on if you pushed it far enough, and James's, roughly that a true idea is one that works, that pays off in practice and holds up against the rest of your experience -- and restate each of them in the vocabulary I actually think in. Drafts. Revisions. Whether a scene earns its place. Whether a character would really do that. What a reader will believe on the page. How much weight a story can carry before it breaks. Whether an ending feels true even though I made the whole thing up.

Be honest about what survives the trip. For each of the two, tell me what translates cleanly, what I have to fudge to make it fit, and what has no counterpart at all in the way I work. Then the part I actually care about. My friend says the pragmatist question is about truth in the real world, but most of what I call true in a novel is only true inside a world I invented, so it feels like a different animal. Is that my vocabulary missing something the philosophers have, or is it their debate quietly assuming there's a fact of the matter that fiction never hands them? And for what it's worth, the closest I get to a working definition is this: a scene is true if you couldn't cut it without the rest of the book falling apart. I have no idea if that's Peirce, or James, or neither, so tell me where it lands and what it costs me. I can follow an arguement if you keep the jargon down, but I've never read any of these people, so don't assume I know the terms.
