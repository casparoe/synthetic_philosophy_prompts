Suppose we define a condition C as luminous when, necessarily, if C obtains, then one is in a position to know that C obtains. Define the KK principle as the claim that, necessarily, if one knows that p, then one knows that one knows that p. (a) Explain the difference between luminosity and the KK principle. (b) Reconstruct the role of a margin-for-error premise in an anti-luminosity argument. (c) Does the anti-luminosity argument, if sound, show that the KK principle is false? Defend your answer.

Here is my classmate's answer:

The KK principle says that knowledge iterates: if you know something, then you know that you know it. Luminosity is the stronger thesis that some conditions are self-intimating: if you are in them, you cannot fail to know that you are in them. Actually, the KK principle is just the luminosity thesis applied to the condition of knowing, so if anti-luminosity works, it immediately kills KK too.

The margin-for-error premise says that if you know a proposition, then it must be true in all nearby possible worlds; otherwise your belief could easily have been false. This is really just the infallibilist requirement that knowledge requires certainty, and it is why the argument is controversial.

The anti-luminosity argument imagines a slow series of changes, such as a person gradually cooling from warm to cool. If warmth were luminous, then at each stage where the person is warm, she would know it. But because knowledge has a margin for error, she would also have to be warm in nearby stages. So there can be no first stage at which she is cool, which is absurd. Therefore no condition like warmth is luminous.

This shows the KK principle is false, because the person knows she is warm at the earlier stages but cannot know that she knows it once the changes get subtle. So knowledge does not always iterate.
