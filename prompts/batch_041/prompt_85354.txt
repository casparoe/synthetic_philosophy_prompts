Ok so a friend in my lab has an argument I can't climb out of, and I want you to tell me where it goes wrong — or if it doesn't, which premise to drop and what dropping it costs me.

(1) Every action I take has a complete physical cause: some brain state together with the laws.
(2) If the brain state plus the laws is already enough for the action to happen, nothing else is needed for it.
(3) If nothing else is needed for an effect, nothing else causes it.
(4) The intention I acted on isn't the same thing as that brain state — the same intention could have been realised by different neurons, and those neurons could have produced a slightly different intention.
(5) So my intention doesn't cause the action. The action happened because of the brain state; the intention is at best a shadow travelling alongsid it.
(6) But that can't be right — reasons are what make actions intelligible, and if intentions never cause anything then deliberation is theater.

He says (5) is a real result and the escape is to say that causation just is difference-making: the intention causes the action when varying the intention varies the action, and it doesn't matter whether the neurons are doing the producing. That feels cheap to me.

So: which step do I reject? If some step is a equivocation or a slide, tell me what it slides between and why it looked airtight. If the thing is valid and I just have to give something up, price it — I'd most like to keep (1) and (6). And take his difference-making exit seriously for a minute: does it actually dissolve the problem or does it only relocate it, given that I can't vary the intention without varying the brain state? If it works, does it also make every higher-level cause real — is the drought a cause of the famine, is the market a cause of the crash — and is that a feature, or does it show the fix makes causation too cheap to do any work? Don't tell me it depends what you mean by cause; that's the question.
