So I'm on the board of our town's little ranked-choice experiment — we switched the mayoral race over to ranked ballots a couple of years ago: you rank the candidates, and if nobody's over half we knock out the last-place finisher and push those ballots to their next choice, and so on until someone's left. Last cycle it did something that's got the whole board arguing. The candidate that every poll said would beat each of the others one-on-one got knocked out in the second round, and we ended up with somebody who was honestly most people's second or third choice. The ranked-choice people say that's fine and even a feature, that the winner is the one with the broadest support. But the same week, we make every campaign sign a pledge not to tell their supporters how to rank the other candidates tactically, as if there's one correct sincere ballot and a corrupt strategic one. And meanwhile for the neighborhood budget stuff we do the exact opposite — people get a handful of tokens and dump them on the projects they care about most, and we just add up the totals, so my neighbor who loves the new playground ten times more than I do gets to count that. So one process treats my ranking as the only thing that matters and the strength of my feeling as irrelevant, and the other treats the strength of my feeling as the whole point.

I want you to dig into what each of these practices actually presupposes. What picture of preferences is each one running on — are preferences just orderings of things, or do they come with amounts attached, and are two different people's preferences even comparable to each other or not? What picture of a collective will does the ranked-ballot thing need — is there some fact about what the group wants that exists before we ever pick a rule, or does the rule just manufacture a winner and we call it the will? Which rival views does each practice quietly rule out, and is the board actually being inconsistent, or can both be defended under one story? Someone keeps muttering that there's some theorem showing you can't have a voting rule that's fair in all the ways you'd want at once — I don't remember it, but if that's the point, tell me what it does and doesn't actually force on us. And then tell me what would concretely have to change — in the rules, or in what we tell voters to believe about their ballots — if we swapped out whichever picture you think we're running on for the other one.

Be honest with me about where you're least confident. I'm probably describing our own rules imprecisely, so tell me which step of your reasoning is most likely to fall apart and what would change your mind about which view the practice really presupposes — like, what would you need to learn about how people actually fill out these ballots to decide between the readings.
