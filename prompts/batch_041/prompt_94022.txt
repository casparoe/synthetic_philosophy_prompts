I'm a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist does what philosophers call deferring to moral testimony: on the hard calls — whether to report a colleague, whether to keep a promise that has turned ruinous, how much of what she has she owes to strangers — she takes her verdict from a friend she believes to be morally wiser, and acts on it without working the question out for herself. I have a fair sense of why philosophers find this troubling; what I need is not a survey of the debate but the consequences of it for the making of the character. Please describe the most important implications of the problem of moral testimony for a person built this way: what she can and cannot be said to know; what manner of goodness she can and cannot have; whether the only honest arc is the one in which she begins to judge for herself, or whether a life of sustained deference can be written as something other than a deficiency; and which of these implications become visible only in scene — in what she says when pressed for a reason, in what she cannot say, in how she behaves on the day her adviser is wrong. I would also like you to test a distinction I have been working with: that the interesting line is not between moral and non-moral deference but between borrowing someone's verdict and borrowing their attention — my character may not adopt her friend's conclusions so much as her friend's sense of what matters and what is salient. If that distinction survives scrutiny, say what follows from it for the novel; if it collapses, say where. Tell me how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of. I don't need a reading list or citations.
