I would like a short story, of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, built on the three layers of a message that Hofstadter distinguishes in Gödel, Escher, Bach: the frame message (the implicit claim, carried by the gross structural features of the carrier, that there is a message here and it can be decoded), the outer message (whatever tells the receiver how to build the mechanism that extracts the content), and the inner message (the content the sender meant). What interests me is that the frame layer is the one layer that cannot be written into the carrier on its own authority: a receiver who does not already suspect that there is a message will not go looking for a key, so the recognition has to come from something the receiver brings with them. I want the story to press on that point, and I want it to take a position rather than circle it.

Set the story on the sender's side. Your protagonist designs a marker — an artifact, a structure, a signal — meant to be read by strangers across a gap so wide that no one on the sending side will ever meet a receiver or ask a question: ten thousand years, another species, a people who share neither language nor mathematics with the designers. The designer cannot count on any shared code and cannot consult anyone.

Requirements for the story:

1. The carrier must be non-linguistic, durable, and of a kind that nature might plausibly have produced something similar to, so that a receiver could reasonably wonder whether anyone made it at all.

2. By the end, the reader should be able to say which concrete feature of the design carries the frame layer, which carries the outer layer, and which carries the inner layer. Show the designer deciding each, and show at least one design feature being repurposed from one layer to another.

3. Include two failures. In one, a receiver encounters the design and does not recognize that there is a message — a frame failure. In the other, a receiver recognizes a message and extracts the wrong content, or cannot extract anything — an outer or inner failure. The two failures must be different in kind, and the story should make the difference visible.

4. The designer should arrive at the thought that the frame layer cannot be built into the artifact — that it has to be supplied by something the receiver already possesses — as a specific observation, not as a speech.

5. The designer must then commit to a definite hook for the frame layer (a biological form, a symmetry, a regularity, a repeated feature, a departure from what erosion does), and the story must show the price of that choice: what kind of receiver it excludes, or what it makes unreadable for everyone else.

6. End on the decision. I do not want an ending in which it is left open whether there was ever a message.

7. Invent the setting. Do not build the story around any real agency, site, program, or historical marker.

After the story, add a short note in your own voice, three or four paragraphs at most. In it, take a definite position on where the meaning of the designer's message is located — in the designer's intention, in the structure of the artifact, or in the receiver's act of recognition — and say which part of your own story would have to be false if one of the other two answers were correct. If the story leans on a contested reading of the distinction — for instance, if you let the designer smuggle part of the frame layer into the artifact, or if you let a receiver manufacture a frame the designer never intended — say so plainly and defend the choice. Commit to the reading you think is right, with your reasons; do not lay out the alternatives and leave the decision to me.
