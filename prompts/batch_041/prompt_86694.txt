So my housemate is doing a PhD in behavioural econ and I'm a term into an MA in philosophy, and for about three months now we've been having the same fight at the kitchen table, usually late, about whether "weakness of will" is even a thing. Her line is that it's folk psychology dressed up: what's actually going on is that my preferences over my own future consumption aren't time-consistent — the discount I apply to a delay shrinks as the delay gets shorter, so my ranking of one drink now versus two tomorrow flips once tomorrow becomes today. She says "acting against my better judgement" is doing no work in that story; at the moment I order the drink the ordering reflects a preference I actually have, and the only reason I call it a failure is that I'm privileging the earlier self's preferences, who isn't obviously more me than the later one. She also says the declining discount rate falls out of a paper she likes, where you get hyperbolic-ish discounting for free once you assume you're uncertain how long the future is going to last — so it isn't even irrational, it's what a rational agent who doesn't know the hazard rate looks like.

My line is that she's modelling the choice pattern and mistaking it for the phenomenon. Two cases, both from my own January, which is why this got personal: (a) on the 12th I was standing at the fridge and I still thought I shouldn't, I hadn't changed my mind about anything, I just did it anyway; (b) on the 3rd of Feb I'd genuinely come round to thinking the whole dry-January rule was silly and dropped it. She says (a) and (b) are the same event described twice, a preference reversal, and I say if she can't tell those apart she hasn't got a theory of the thing I'm asking about. I also keep bringing up the Huck Finn bit, where he does the thing we think is right while believing it's wicked, which seems to show that acting against your own considered judgement isn't always a failure of anything; and the thing I half-remember reading about weakness of will being specifically about not sticking to a resolution you already formed rather than about desire at all, with the opposite failure — just not being able to be bothered — being a seperate item entirely.

Anyway, we're going in circles and I think we'd actually get somewhere if we did it properly. So: draft the joint statement the two of us could both sign. Start with a short preamble, then a numbered list of claims we both accept — about what the cases are, what the discounting data does and doesn't show, what any theory here has to explain, and where the descriptive question comes apart from the normative one. Then a disagreements table: each row a question we still differ on, with my wording of my side in one column and hers of hers in the other, each written so that the person whose view it is would actually endorse the sentence, plus a column for what turns on the row. Then the cruxes — I'd bet one is whether a preference reversal and a genuine conflict of judgement can come apart at all, and another is whether the earlier self's endorsement has any authority the later self's lacks — and for each crux, something we'd both agree counts as evidence, with each of us writing down in advance what we predict we'd see. If part of what we've been shouting about is just us using "preference" and "judgement" differently, say so and say which part. Don't cite anything, we both hate that; just make the formulations tight enough that neither of us can wriggle out.
