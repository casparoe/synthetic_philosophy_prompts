ok so I'm in a third-year consciousness seminar and my mate Dan has built an argument he's extremely pleased with, and I can't tell if it's a real result or a card trick. It's the paradox-of-phenomenal-judgment thing our lecturer keeps gesturing at, except Dan's version is his own and it's tidier than the lecture one. Can you take it apart for me. Here it is in his numbering:

(1) Epiphenomenalism: phenomenal properties never cause anything physical.
(2) My saying "ow, that stings" is a physical event — air moving, muscles moving.
(3) So if epiphenomenalism is true, the stinging isn't among the causes of my saying it.
(4) Counterfactual test. If the stinging isn't in the causal history of the utterance, then the utterance would have come out the same whether or not the stinging was there, and the same for any stinging at all. The utterance floats free of the thing it seems to report.
(5) Something that floats free of a fact can't be a report of it, and can't be knowledge of it — at best a lucky coincidence.
(6) So on epiphenomenalism, no phenomenal report is knowledge of the state it reports.
(7) But all anyone has to go on when arguing for epiphenomenalism is phenomenal reports — reports about what experience is like, about it not being the same thing as the brain state, and so on.
(8) So epiphenomenalism, if true, is a thesis nobody could ever be in a position to know, and its own evidence is worthless by its own lights.
(9) Any thesis whose truth would make all the evidence for it worthless is self-undermining.
(10) So epiphenomenalism is dead.

Dan says this is the cleanest kill he's seen. Two things bother me. First, (4)–(5) seem to prove alot more than that. A classmate brought up the barometer/storm thing — the reading doesn't cause the storm and the storm doesn't cause the reading (both come off the pressure, I think), yet nobody says the barometer tells you nothing about the weather. And the same shape seems to fit predictions: the event I'm forecasting is in the future, so it can't have caused my forecast, and yet a well-grounded forecast is exactly the sort of thing we'd call knowledge. So either (4)–(5) are too strong or the barometer intuition is doing something I'm not seeing, and I genuinely can't tell which. Even if the barometer reply goes through, Dan's going to say the epiphenomenalist now owes an explanation of why my "ow" lines up with the stinging at all, and I suspect that explanation is where the real bill lands.

Second thing: if the barometer reply does work, does Dan's argument still sink anything? Or does it turn out to kill only epiphenomenalism-by-name while every nearby view strolls away untouched? That feels suspiciously convenient for Dan, who is a property dualist.

So I need a verdict, not a menu. Name the step you'd give up, say why it looked fine in the first place, and price the alternatives properly — including what the epiphenomenalist has to pay once you patch it, and whether Dan's worry survives in some weaker form afterwards. Be definite about it; don't tell me it depends what I mean by knowledge. And write it in British English, my supervisor is picky about that and it's rubbing off on me.

I'd rather not give up (2) or (7), they look like plain facts to me.
