Military institutions and the international deliberations that surround them have converged on a design norm that now functions as a requirement: an autonomous or semi-autonomous weapon system must be built and fielded so that a human being retains meaningful control over the use of force, whether by authorizing each engagement, retaining a veto, or supervising the system closely enough to intervene before it acts. The norm is stated as a constraint on engineers and commanders alike, and it is usually defended in one of two ways. The first is that a machine cannot be responsible for anything, because it cannot understand reasons, answer for its conduct, or be punished. The second is that if no human is in the loop, then when something goes wrong there is no one to blame, and we will have opened a responsibility gap: a class of harms for which no human is responsible and no machine can be.

Consider this norm against two other practices of the same legal and military systems. Command responsibility holds a commander criminally liable for atrocities committed by subordinates that he did not order and may never have learned of, provided he failed in his duty to prevent or punish them; responsibility here is ascribed to a person whose causal and epistemic connection to the harm is thin. And the same systems treat corporations as legal persons capable of liability, guilt, and punishment, distributing responsibility across an organization rather than locating it in any single mind.

What theory of responsibility does the meaningful-control requirement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Does the requirement rest on a control condition on responsibility, such that a person is responsible only for what falls within their control — in which case, say what that implies for command responsibility, which seems to violate it? Or does it rest on a tracing condition, on which responsibility follows a causal chain back to some culpable human origin, and the requirement is simply a way of guaranteeing that the chain has a terminus? Or does it rest on something else: that responsibility must be answerable, that it must be borne by an entity that can be called to account, or that it is essentially individual and cannot be distributed over a socio-technical system?

Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined — where several theories would license the same design requirement. Then say which of the two defenses above the practice actually needs, whether the practice is internally consistent given command responsibility and corporate liability, and what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the implicit theory with a distributed or systemic account of responsibility, or with the view that some harms genuinely have no responsible agent and that this is a cost we should simply accept.
