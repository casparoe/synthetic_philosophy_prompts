I'm the chair of my university's film society, and I'm also currently taking a course in aesthetics. This Friday we're holding our annual "bleak night" screening — the one film each year that leaves the room hollowed out, and that people nevertheless argue about for weeks afterward. I've been asked to give the opening remarks, roughly five minutes, before the lights go down. The audience is mixed: perhaps a third are first-years who have never been to one of these, a few were dragged along by friends and are openly skeptical of the whole exercise, and the rest are regulars who will wince if I get sentimental or pretentious. Please write the remarks.

There's a particular problem I want the speech to take seriously, because it's the only reason I agreed to speak. In class we've been working with a distinction my professor drew between two ways of accounting for a painful experience. On the moment-by-moment accounting, watching something devastating is simply unpleasant; there is no honest way to say you are enjoying it while it is happening. On the retrospective accounting, you are usually glad you did it and would recommend it to others. Most defenses of sad art work only on the retrospective side: they promise that the discomfort is instrumental, a price paid for some later payoff — insight, solidarity, a good cry, the sense of having been through something. But then the film's actually being unpleasant starts to look like a regrettable delivery mechanism, something we would remove if we could, and a version that handed you the payoff without the pain would be strictly better. That seems wrong. The pain looks like part of what we value, not a tax levied on it. I want the speech to hold both of these in view without collapsing them into a tidy answer — to leave the audience with the puzzle rather than a slogan — while still giving them a reason to stay in their seats.

A few constraints. No spoilers, obviously: I cannot describe anything that happens. I cannot promise them a good time, and I cannot tell them the sadness is secretly joy or secretly therapy — that is exactly the move I am trying to avoid. And it has to sound like remarks delivered before a screening, not like a lecture: concrete, spoken aloud, addressed to people sitting in a room waiting for the lights to go down.

Please give it to me in these parts, in this order, keeping each roughly to the length I've indicated:

- "Opening" (~100 words): the first thing I say, acknowledging the oddity of the occasion without being cute about it.
- "The two accountings" (~250 words): introduce the distinction in plain language, with one homely example that is not the film we are about to watch.
- "The trouble with the payoff" (~250 words): the objection that if the payoff could be had without the pain we would prefer it, and why that should unsettle us.
- "Why I'm still asking you to stay" (~200 words): whatever honest reason survives, offered without resolving the puzzle.
- "Lights down" (~80 words): the last words before the film starts.
