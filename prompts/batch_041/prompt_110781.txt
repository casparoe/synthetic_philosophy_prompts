You are a philosopher of law specializing in criminalization and the enforcement of morals. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a brief planning note — a few sentences, not an essay — setting out the strongest version of each of the two positions below and the two or three sharpest objections each can press against the other. Then write the dialogue.

The dialogue is between two equally competent interlocutors, neither of them a strawman. The first defends legal moralism: the criminal law may prohibit conduct because it is morally wrong, independently of any harm that conduct causes, and a society is entitled to protect the shared morality that sustains it. The second defends the harm principle: the prevention of harm to people other than the actor is the only adequate ground for criminalization, and the fact that most people find an act disgusting or degrading is never by itself a reason to punish it. Each must answer the other's actual points rather than talk past them. You may draw on the historical debate over legal moralism, but the dialogue must not be a summary of it, and neither character may settle a point by appeal to what a famous author said.

Their occasion is a parliamentary committee considering a novel bill that would criminalize the private preparation and consumption, by consenting adults, of food cultured from cells donated, with consent, by the diners themselves — a case with no victim, no coercion, no corpse, and no measurable injury to anyone, which nonetheless most people find degrading. Neither character may treat the case as easy.

Make each side press the other on what its central term actually means. The moralist must say whether the morality in question is the majority's positive morality, a moral truth, or a requirement of social cohesion, and what the law should do when the community is divided or its morality is shifting. The harm-principle advocate must say what counts as harm, whether diffuse or collective harms — to a shared moral environment, or to the character of a society — count, and whether the principle can explain the criminalization of conduct that injures no person, such as cruelty to animals.

Close with each character stating, in one or two sentences, where he takes the crux of the disagreement to lie and what, if anything, he has conceded. Do not cite statutes, cases, or scholarly literature; invent examples if you need them and mark them as hypothetical. Aim for roughly 900–1400 words, with no filler.
