I keep encountering the claim that the brain is a computer. Most recently a colleague used it in a seminar to argue that a sufficiently faithful simulation of a human brain would for that very reason be conscious, so that uploading a mind is an engineering problem rather than a conceptual one. I doubt the slogan can bear that weight, but I cannot say precisely where the trouble lies, because I suspect several quite different theses are being bundled together. I have heard people insist that the brain merely processes information, that it runs a program, that it is a particular kind of digital machine, and that almost anything can be described as a computer if one is loose enough about the definition. I have also heard it said that the whole dispute turns on what it takes for a physical system to implement a computation at all, and that on a permissive enough account of implementation every physical system implements every computation. I cannot tell which of these claims is supposed to be doing the work, or whether they are even compatible with one another.

Please set out, as a menu rather than a single favoured reading, the distinct precise claims that the sentence 'the brain is a computer' might be making. Order the readings from the nearly empty to the most radical. For each, give a one-line characterisation in plain terms, note whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a genuinely contentious one, or simply false, and say what it would commit one to. Then say which reading or readings would actually be required to reach my colleague's conclusion about simulated consciousness and mind uploading, and whether any of them is both needed and defensible. I am interested in the logical space of claims rather than in intellectual history, so please avoid references, citations and reading lists.
