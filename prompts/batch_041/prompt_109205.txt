I've been kicking around a draft peice on the hinge of history for months and I want you to go at it hard. Here's roughly where it stands.

Claim: the interesting question isn't whether we're at the hinge, it's what kind of object a hinge is, and the existing back-and-forth (Bostrom defending something like the hinge hypothesis, MacAskill pushing the outside-view longtermist line that we should expect the future to look like the past and be suspicious of the present being special) mostly argues past itself because it runs two different things together.

The cheap version, which I'll call the wide hinge: a period in which an enormous amount of value is at stake in the long-run future, so the marginal unit of effort matters more than it did in 1400. That's basically a claim about the scale of the future plus the fact that we're the first century with the tech to end or remake things — astronomical waste, the urn that might have a black ball in it, all of that. Nearly trivial; I don't think anyone seriously denies it.

The expensive version, the narrow hinge: a moment where the far future is a step function of something we do — a cliff, where you remove one small intervention and the long-run outcome is completely different. That's what would actually license "drop everything, this is the moment".

Most defences I've read slide from the wide claim (cheap, granted) to the narrow claim (expensive). MacAskill's outside-view point strikes me as really an attack on the narrow hinge, and a good one, but the practical conclusion he draws is about buck-passing versus direct work, and that mostly dissolves once you notice the two hinges come appart. If only the wide hinge holds, you still do the direct work, you just stop being so dramatic about it.

The test I want to use: ask what the hinge felt like from inside. 1945 felt like one, 1962 felt like one, lots of centuries have felt like one, and the feeling is cheap. So what would have to be true for the feeling to be tracking anything? My candidate criterion: a hinge is where the future's value is a step function of something we control now and can't control later, and the test for that is irreversibility plus bottleneck. Under that criterion I honestly can't tell whether the step is in front of us or behind us — the grabby aliens argument for our being early cuts one way, the thought that our actions are heavily replaceable cuts the other — which I want to claim is itself an argument for acting as if the step is ahead, since the regret asymmetry is real.

What I want from you: (a) have I got the Bostrom/MacAskill thing roughly right or am I strawmanning one of them? (b) is the narrow/wide distinction a real one, or does it collapse into something already standard — I have a nagging feeling it's just "is influence concentrated or diffuse", which is a different question. (c) what's the strongest objection I'm not engaging with; I suspect the anthropic/Copernican stuff is doing more work than I'm crediting it for. (d) is the 1945/1962 move a fair test or a cheap shot? And tell me what to cut — the last paragraph is trying to do too much. No reading lists please, just tell me where I'm wrong.
