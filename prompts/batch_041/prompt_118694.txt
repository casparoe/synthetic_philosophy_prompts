I'm a fourth-year PhD working on animal ethics, and I've been asked to moderate the Q&A for a visiting speaker at our work-in-progress series next month. His line is that preserving habitat is usually a net welfare loss for wild animals -- convert the forest, lose the arthropods, and you've done the animals a favour. It's a minority position and he knows it, but he's slippery in one particular way: press him on a worry and he says some version of 'that's an empirical question, and the burden is on the conservationist to show the numbers go the other way.' I want a run of questions where that move gets progressively harder to make.

Could you draft eight to ten questions, in the order I should actually ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give? I need at least one each on:

- what the per-animal welfare claim actually rests on -- r-selected life histories, survivorship curves, juvenile mortality -- and what observation would count against the thesis that wild lives are on average net negative. I'd like the follow-up to press him on whether a death that ends a life with no remembered past and no anticipated future is a harm at all, or whether he needs the asymmetry about coming into existence to get the conclusion.
- the sentience filter: if invertebrates come off the table, does the conclusion survive on vertebrates alone? And if he retreats to 'we should act under uncertainty', I want a follow-up on why uncertainty doesn't cut the other way, given that conversion is one-way and no one can compensate the animals that never get to exist.
- my own case, which I've been chewing on for weeks: an old-growth hectare versus the same hectare as cattle pasture. Nobody ever counts the pasture's own invertebrates -- dung fauna, soil nematodes, the grass itself -- or the cattle. I want him to say which hectare is worse and to commit on whether the answer is driven by biology or by his welfare metric. Related: I want him to say whether the right unit is welfare per animal or welfare per hectare-year, because I suspect the whole thing turns on that and he's never had to say.
- the totalist/person-affecting fork and the world-exploder thought experiment: if the ideal outcome is a world with no wild animals in it, what exactly is left of his disagreement with the people who want to abolish predation, and what does he say to the intuition that biodiversity has value that isn't welfare value?

Also worth a question: habitat destruction as actually practised isn't a clean deletion -- it's fires, edge effects, starvation, displacement onto roads and farmland -- so is his thesis about conversion in the real world or about a hypothetical tidy conversion? And one on whether the view worries him politically, since it's awfully convenient for developers.

A couple of things. I don't need citations or a reading list -- I know the field and I can't check anything from here anyway. And please push back on me if I've set this up badly: if the position isn't as clean as I'm making out, or if one of my questions is really a gotcha that presupposes something he'd just deny, say so plainly rather than writing me a smooth list, and tell me which ones you'd cut. I'd rather have eight that land than ten that don't.
