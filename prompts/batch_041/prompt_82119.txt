Three months ago my mother had a cardiac arrest, and she has been in a minimally conscious state ever since. The hospital's research team has invited us to enrol her in a study built around a device that measures what they call the global ignition of her cortical activity, and they have handed the family a glossy information leaflet to explain what the study will and will not tell us. The leaflet promises, in the level tone of a patient-information sheet, to help us learn whether she is "still in there." I trained in philosophy, and I found that I could not read it without laughing and then, a moment later, without wanting to cry: it glides, without ever marking the glide, from a claim about her brain's capacity to broadcast information to a claim about whether anyone is home.

I would like you to write a parody of that leaflet. Keep it in the clinic's own voice — a family-facing FAQ, deadpan and entirely plausible, that quietly collapses the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness. Aim for about 750 words, laid out as a numbered list of questions and answers, with a short glossary and one or two boxed asides.

The humour has to come from the philosophy rather than from the clinic being villainous. A researcher who works on global-workspace-style theories should recognise the leaflet's moves as moves their own field makes, and should laugh because the parody is fair rather than because it is a straw man. So let every overclaim be one a real proponent could in principle make: treat "conscious" and "reportable" as interchangeable; define experience in the glossary as information made globally available for report; treat a positive scan as proof that the patient is conscious and, more revealingly, treat a negative scan as proof that she is not, while conceding in a footnote that the inference does not follow; and answer the family's question "but could she be experiencing things she cannot report?" by explaining that unreportable experience is not the sort of thing the device is designed to detect and therefore, for the purposes of the report, not the sort of thing there is. Let the leaflet acknowledge the hard problem in a sidebar and then carry on as though it had settled it. Include one boxed "objection from a sceptical ethicist," written so that the clinic's reply to it gives the objection away, and include a question about what a family should do with a negative result, answered in a way that leaves the reader feeling the weight of the decision rather than the joke. Never let the leaflet wink.

What I want a reader to take away is not that these people are monsters but that a device can measure the functional profile of access without yet answering the question the family is actually asking.
