I'm in the third year of a philosophy degree and am currently taking a course in applied ethics; the unit on animals has left me with a set of verdicts I cannot get to sit on a single line, and I would rather not be handed a theory to accept. What I want is to know what principle, if any, actually fits the verdicts I already hold, what that principle says about the cases I have not thought through, and which of my verdicts I ought to abandon if nothing fits.

Here is what I think, case by case.

- Eating beef: I am about as confident as I am of anything that this is seriously wrong. A beef meal feels to me like the worst of the ordinary options.
- Eating chicken: also wrong, but noticeably less so than beef. If I had to put a chicken meal and a beef meal on a scale, the beef is the worse one.
- Eating plants: I used to treat this as the clean option, but I have read enough about what harvesting does to field animals — the mice, the rabbits, the birds that are simply in the way of the machinery — that I no longer believe a plant-based diet is free of animal death. Those deaths strike me as mattering as much as any others.
- And yet I still think a plate of beans is clearly better than a plate of beef, and roughly level with, perhaps a little better than, the chicken.
- Pasture-raised beef: people assure me this is the humane option, but my instinct is that it is no better and possibly worse, because it takes so much more land per calorie. I notice I cannot say why more land is bad without appealing to the wild animals that would otherwise have been living on it, which is exactly the kind of thing I said matters in the crop case.
- Insects: I genuinely do not know whether to count them. If I do, the crop case above gets much darker and I begin to wonder whether the plant option is better than the chicken at all. When I catch myself quietly setting insects at zero in order to keep my other verdicts intact, I grow suspicious of my own reasoning.
- Wild animals generally: I think their suffering matters even when no one causes it, but I have no idea how to weigh it against the suffering we cause directly.
- The case I keep going back and forth on: eating a single hamburger once a year, against eating chicken three times a week. Per meal the hamburger is the worse act; per year of eating I cannot convince myself the chicken comes out ahead.

The principle I keep reaching for, though I do not trust it, is this: what matters is the total amount of suffering an option causes per calorie of food delivered, weighted by how confident I am that the creature suffering is sentient. I cannot tell whether that principle is coherent, whether it is what I have actually been using, or whether it forces me to give up something I would rather keep.

So: state the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best fits these verdicts; say precisely what each clause is doing and which verdict motivates it; tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed — a large wild-caught fish, a meal of oysters, the once-a-year hamburger against the thrice-weekly chicken — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I should surrender that verdict rather than the principle. If a simpler principle captures almost all of the verdicts at the cost of one, I would rather see that trade stated plainly than papered over.
