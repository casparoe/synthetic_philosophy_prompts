I've been trying to figure out whether my gut reactions to a bunch of "rationally irrational" cases add up to anything, and I keep going in circles. Here's where I actually land, case by case.

Parfit's hitchiker: I'm stranded in the desert, a guy in a truck says he'll only drive me to town if I promise to mail him a hundred bucks when I get home. No enforcement, no way he'd ever find out, and paying him once I'm home is pure loss to me. My verdicts: promising is clearly the right move, not paying once I'm home is also clearly the smart move, and on top of that I'd rather be the sort of person who does pay, even though it costs me. I dont feel any tension saying all three.

The toxin thing I've seen described a few places: a rich guy will wire me a million tomorrow if tonight I form a genuine intention to swallow a vial tomorrow that'll make me sick as a dog for a day, no lasting harm. Next morning the money's in my account and drinking it is pointless. Verdict: forming the intention tonight is rational, drinking it tomorrow is irrational, and I won't give up either verdict even though I can see they don't sit together. An intention I know I won't act on feels like a defective intention, but it's also the thing that gets me the money.

Deterrence, same shape: it seems plainly right for a country to threaten a retaliation it would be suicidal and pointless to actually carry out. Verdict: build the threat, don't fire.

Newcomb: I take just the one box. Not because I've got causality confused, I just think the predictor's track record is evidence about what's in the boxes and that's what I should go on.

One-shot prisoner's dilemma against a total stranger, no repetition, no reason to think our choices are correlated: I defect, no hand-wringing.

Sunk costs: a friend keeps pouring money into a restaurant that's clearly going to fail because he's already sunk his savings into it. Verdict: he's being irrational, and the fact that it's the *past* spending moving him makes it worse rather than better.

Overconfidence: a tennis player I know is the underdog by every measure and plays better when she believes she'll win. She does in fact believe it. Verdict: her belief is irrational. I don't care that it helps her. I'd tell her to be confident, not to believe something the evidence doesn't support.

So the principle I keep reaching for is something like: what's rational isn't the act, it's the policy or the character you'd pick for yourself in advance, before you knew which of these spots you'd end up in. That seems to get the hitchhiker (pick the promise-keeping character), the toxin (pick the character who can actually form the intention), and it makes Newcomb and the one-shot dilemma come out different because your character is correlated with the boxes and the other player in one case and not the other. But it seems to bless my sunk-cost friend too — if you chose in advance to be someone who honors commitments, doesn't he qualify? And it seems to bless the tennis player's self-deception, which I won't have.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely enough that I could run it on a case I haven't listed, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. If the honest answer is "you need two principles, one for what you do and one for what you're like," say that and tell me where the line between them falls. And if I'm just wrong about one of these, I'd rather hear which one than get a theory that launders all of them. I'm not asking what Parfit or anybody else said about these cases, I want to know what my verdicts add up to.
