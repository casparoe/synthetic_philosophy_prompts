I coordinate advance-care planning for a small community hospital, and the work has become personal: my father's aphasia after a stroke means that his words, the situation that frames them, and what he is trying to tell us frequently come apart, and I have watched staff and family fill the gap with whatever reading is most convenient. I want to give our volunteer visitors and bedside clinicians a reusable procedure for determining what a patient's utterance or advance directive actually means when the literal content, the framing context, and the intended message diverge. The procedure should be grounded in the frame/outer/inner-message distinction: the frame message tells the receiver how to take the outer message, the outer message is the explicit content, and the inner message is what the sender is trying to convey. Please write the procedure as a sequence of questions to ask in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, identify where the procedure must stop with a determination that the meaning cannot be recovered, and mark the points where the decision must be handed to a person -- the patient's surrogate, a clinician, or an ethics consultant -- because it turns on values or on unrecoverable intention rather than on evidence. It has to be usable by someone with no background in semiotics or philosophy of language, in under ten minutes at the bedside, and it must not pretend to more certainty than the situation allows. Our volunteers see about fifteen patients a day, and any interpretation that would change code status must be signed off by the charge nurse and me. Then run it on three cases: a patient who asks to go home while on a ventilator; a family that insists on doing everything although the patient's written directive refuses intubation; and a translated advance directive whose original phrasing may have carried a different force. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the family will not like, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do.
