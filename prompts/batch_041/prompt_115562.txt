OK, oddly specific request, but bear with me. I've been circling the aesthetics literature for a couple of years now — not an academic, I just read too many blogs and then end up in the journals — and the thing that keeps bugging me is aesthetic testimony. You know the drill: the recieved wisdom that you can't just take someone's word for it that a painting is good, the way you can take their word for it that the train leaves at six, plus the various carve-outs and the endless back-and-forth about whether that's an epistemic point or a practical one.

Here's my problem with the field: 90% of the ink goes into one cell of a 2x2 I've been sketching, and I think it's the boring cell. Two axes. First: what the testimony actually hands you — the bare verdict, or the verdict plus the grounds for it. Second: what you do with it — form a belief about the work's merit, or shape your own experience of the work, i.e. actually get the thing to land for you. That gives four cells:

1. Bare verdict, used to form a belief ("everyone says it's great, so it's great").
2. Grounds, used to form a belief ("it's great because of how it handles X").
3. Bare verdict, used to shape your experience ("everyone says it's great, so I'll go in hunting for what's great about it").
4. Grounds, used to shape your experience ("look at how it handles X" — and then, if it works, you see it).

My hunch is that cell 4 is what's actually happening in real life almost all the time — teachers, friends, gallery labels, good criticism — and it's the one the literature barely touches, while cells 1 and 2 get ground into dust. I also suspect the interesting question in cell 4 isn't justification at all. It's more like the testimony is doing the job of directing attention, and the real puzzle is whether attention can be handed over at all, and what the failure modes are (getting the illusion of appreciation, or a sort of parroting that collapses the moment someone pushes you on it).

So: suppose I have a small pot of money and one postdoc for three years, and I want to spend it on aesthetic testimony, but not on the four questions everyone else is already grinding on (whether testimony can justify aesthetic belief, the acquaintance principle thing, the analogy with moral testimony, whether aesthetic disagreements are faultless). Which open questions in this area should actually get the money, in what order, and why? I want the reasoning far more than the list. Which questions are upstream of which — if you settled this one, which others get easier or dissolve? Which are tractable for someone doing pure philosophy with no lab, no collaborators, no data? Which are neglected because they look boring or unfashionable rather than because they're settled? And which of the supposedly central questions should I actively deprioritise because the answer wouldn't change much downstream? Tell me what would move something up or down the list — what would a good argument have to look like to make me abandon cell 4 and crawl back to the well-trodden stuff?

Be honest about whether my 2x2 is even the right cut. If you think a different distinction does the same job better, say so — but then answer the prioritisation question with respect to your version instead. No reading lists or citations please, I'd rather have the argument. Write in British English, I've been in the UK long enough that the other spelling looks wrong now. And finish with one line, starting "Verdict:", naming the single question you'd fund first.
