So I just got out of a two-hour argument with my friend about "real patterns" and I want the full spec before we go at it again. Her whole line is that a pattern is real just in case describing the data in terms of it is shorter than describing the data raw -- that's it, that's the criterion. And she thinks that settles everything downstream: beliefs and desires are real because a brain's behavior compresses nicely when you describe it that way, and physics is the same trick one level down, so the physical stance, the design stance and the intentional stance are all on a par, none of them more "basic" than the others. I don't buy it but I couldn't articulate why in the moment, so I want to lay out what a theory of this actually has to get right before I pick a side.

Give me the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of real patterns / the stances have to deliver -- which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it can't collapse, which objections it has to survive -- with the case or consideration behind each one. In particular I want these handled:

- The trivial-compression objection, which is the one I kept fumbling toward. If I can always just *name* the data ("the output of that RNG with seed 42", one symbol) and thereby "compress" any string, then her criterion makes every random string a real pattern, which can't be right. So the theory has to say what stops the criterion from being vacuous, or replace it with something that isn't.
- The relativity-to-a-language / relativity-to-a-purpose worry. Short compared to what, in what vocabulary, for whom? If the answer is "relative to the description language" then does "real" quietly turn into "useful for our purposes", and if so is she ok with that?
- Whether the three stances really come out equal. She wants the intentional stance to be exactly as real as the physical one. I suspect there's an asymmetry (you can in principle derive the intentional from the physical but not the other way, or the physical stance doesn't need us to take it while the intentional one does) and I want the theory to either license her egalitarianism or block it, not dodge it.
- Real vs. useful. A pattern can be a great predictor and still a fiction, and something can be real without being useful to predict with. If the theory can't pull these apart, say so.
- Degrees. She thinks some patterns are "more real" than others. I think that's incoherent. Someone's right -- tell me which and why.
- Projectibility. A rule that compresses the past perfectly but flips its meaning at some future date -- is that a real pattern or not, and does the theory have to rule on it?
- One-offs vs. repeatables, and lossy/approximate patterns: is a pattern that only holds roughly, or only ever happens once, still real?

Then: mark which of these are genuine requirements and which are just prejudices of one camp, flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied (that's the part I most want), and tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the popular positions.
