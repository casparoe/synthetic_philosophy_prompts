I'm giving a guest lecture next month in a first-year cognitive science course -- sixty minutes, non-philosophy students, no readings assigned, and I have to make Dennett's four kinds of minds land: Darwinian creatures that come wired and whose mistakes are fatal, Skinnerian ones that learn by reinforcement (their mistakes cost them but they survive them), Popperian ones that can run the trial inside themselves and let the hypothesis die in their place, and Gregorian ones whose thinking is done partly with external tools -- words, maps, other people, their own notes. I dont need you to explain the four levels to me; I need the plan. What has to come before what and why -- in particular, do they need the nesting claim (each level sits on top of the ones below rather than replacing them) before or after they've met the levels themselves? What single example should carry the whole hour, and what does it need to have, and to lack, so I can walk it up all four rungs without it turning into a just-so story about progress? Which misconceptions will they form -- my guesses are that they'll hear it as a ladder where Gregorian beats Darwinian, that they'll collapse Skinnerian into "behaviorism," and that they'll think the Popperian label is about Popper on science -- and where exactly in the sequence do I head each one off? Then give me one question to ask at the end that would tell me whether they've got that these are stacked layers of a single mind rather than four kinds of animal, and tell me what I should openly refuse to do in an hour.
