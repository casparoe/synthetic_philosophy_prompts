I'm putting together a reading group on pragmatism and I've got two people in it who've been going at each other for months. One's a Peircean: truth is what inquiry is fated to converge on at the limit, full stop, and James basically changed the subject by turning truth into a psychological and commercial affair. The other's a Jamesian: truth is what it's good for us to believe, ideas become true as they get cashed in, and Peirce's infinitely deferred consensus is a promissory note nobody will ever collect. I want to make them write the joint statement their adversarial collaboration would have produced instead of another semester of them talking past each other.

So draft it for them. Numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table. On the agreed claims, make them load-bearing — things a non-pragmatist would actually deny, not we-both-like-inquiry mush. On the disagreements, state each side's position in wording that side would sign off on, not a strawman version of the other guy.

Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling it would really move at least one of them. For each crux, give the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction about that case before anyone sees the answer. I want them to have to handle three cases that neither canon really does: (a) a claim that's permanently unverifiable and makes no practical difference to anybody — say, what the last thought of some hermit who died alone in 1400 was about; does it have a truth value at all, and on each view what makes it true? (b) probabilistic forecasts, e.g. a 70% rain forecast on a day it rains — is the forecast true, and what does true even attach to there? (c) mathematical truths, where there's no inquiry-terminating experiment to converge on and nobody's cashing anything in, which is usually where both of them start shuffling.

There's also a third chair in the room I want them to answer: a deflationist who says true isn't a property at all, just a device for endorsing and generalizing, and that all this pragmatist machinery is describing the use of truth-talk rather than the nature of truth. Both pragmatists have to reply to him and say whether they can sign a joint answer.

Finally: say which parts of the Peirce/James fight are actually verbal and why, and for each side, what they'd count as a serious argument against their own position. Dont bother with page refs or citations, I don't have the books here and neither do you.
