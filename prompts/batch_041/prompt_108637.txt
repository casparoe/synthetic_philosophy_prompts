I'm a first-year PhD student in a decision-sciences group, currently drafting a short paper on resource-rational models of search, and I keep running into an objection that I cannot make sense of. Last week I presented a toy model of a hiring committee. The committee reads applications in sequence, works from a fixed shortlist of five desiderata, and stops as soon as it meets a candidate who clears a bar settled on before it begins looking — say, a candidate who satisfies at least two of the five. My formalization writes this as a stopping rule: accept the first candidate whose score clears k, where the score simply counts desiderata met and k is set to 2. A professor in the audience objected that what I had modeled was not satisficing at all. When I pressed her, she said only that on Herbert Simon's account the aspiration level is not something one fixes in advance, and that a model in which it is fixed has already assumed the answer. I did not follow this, and I still do not.

As I see it, the moment I write the bar as a constraint — stop when the evaluation clears k — my committee is simply a constrained expected-utility maximizer, and "satisficing" names the special case in which the binding constraint happens to be a threshold on the objective rather than a budget or a deadline. If that is right, then bounded rationality is not a rival to rational-choice theory at all but a proper part of it, and the "paradigm shift" framing one finds in textbooks is sociology of the field rather than substance. The point seems to me to generalize. Any behavior at all can be described as the maximization of something, provided I am allowed to let what is maximized depend on the situation the agent is in; so "this agent is not maximizing" can never be a well-formed empirical claim — at most it reports that we have not yet found a convenient function to write down. The professor's insistence, then, looks to me like a refusal to notice that the constraint set was doing the explanatory work all along, and that once you let me choose the constraints there is nothing left over for a separate theory of bounded rationality to explain.

So my question is this: once we grant that satisficing is a species of constrained optimization, what remains for a bounded-rationality explanation to do that a constrained-maximization model does not already do? Is there a residue of the satisficing idea that survives my being allowed to specify the constraints, or does the very fact that I can always redescribe the agent as maximizing show that the distinction between satisficing and optimizing is empty?
