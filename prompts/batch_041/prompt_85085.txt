I'm putting together a session for a game theory reading group and I want to use the ferry sequence from The Dark Knight as a way into Hofstadter's superrationality stuff. You know the scene: two ferries, each has a detonator for the other, the Joker says one of you blows the other by midnight or I blow you both, and the twist is that the convict on the prisoner ferry just chucks the detonator out the window and the other boat doesnt press either. I want a reading of that scene through the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery -- the two experiments Hofstadter describes in Metamagical Themas. In Platonia, some trillionaire tells 20 people that exactly one of them must send a telegram by noon and that person gets a billion, and the superrational move is for each to send with probability 1/20. In the Luring Lottery, Scientific American ran a contest where you sent a postcard with the number of entries you wanted, the prize was a million divided by total entries, and readers basically blew it up by sending googol-sized numbers. So the question: does the ferry scene dramatize superrational reasoning, or does it cheat by making the convict's act a kind of moral heroism that isn't really superrational? What maps onto what -- the ferries as players, the detonators as telegrams, the Joker as the experimenter -- and where does the mapping break? The ferry payoff matrix isn't the same as Platonia's: in Platonia exactly one sender wins; on the ferries, if exactly one presses, that boat lives and the other dies, but if both press both die, and if neither presses both die (unless Batman saves them). So what would a superrational passenger actually do? Does the film's resolution vindicate Hofstadter or quietly refute him? And does the Luring Lottery side of it -- the way real people sabotaged the game by over-entering -- show up in the film's crowd psychology, or is Nolan too optimistic about the convict? I want a reading that takes both the film and the game theory seriously, doesnt just use the movie as a cute illustration, and says what the scene adds to the philosophical debate that the Platonia and Luring Lottery cases don't. No need for citations or reading lists, just the argument. Also, if the honest answer is that the ferry scene is a bad fit for superrationality and a better fit for something like team reasoning or Kantian universalizability, say so.
