I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy who came to the field after several years as a bedside nurse, and I'm assembling a proposal for a twelve-month fellowship application — the document itself needs to be about 1,200 words, so I need the bones of it, not the whole thing. The question I want to work on grew out of my last clinical rotation before I switched fields. A patient with a documented do-not-resuscitate order and a feeding tube was dying over several days, and the attending kept describing what we were doing as "letting nature take its course" — while we titrated sedatives, withheld antibiotics, and eventually removed the tube. I could not make the act/omission vocabulary fit what was actually happening at the bedside, and I have not been able to stop chewing on it since. So this is personal as well as philosophical, and I would like the proposal to sound like it.

My half-formed idea is this. The doing/allowing debate normally treats the distinction as a fact about the shape of the causal relation between an agent and an outcome — the agent either introduces a causal sequence or merely declines to interrupt one — and then argues about whether that shape can carry moral weight, usually via the familiar claim that when everything else is genuinely held equal, the bare difference between killing and letting die cannot amount to much. I suspect the framing itself is off. What seems to do the moral work in these cases is not the act/omission shape but the relation between the agent's choice and a baseline that the agent is already sustaining: keeping a ventilator running is as much an ongoing positive intervention as switching it off is, so the live contrast may be between choices that hold a causal pathway open and choices that close or reroute one, with the moral weight attaching to which baseline the agent treats as privileged rather than to doing versus allowing as such. I want to test this against a cluster of cases that are awkward for the standard picture precisely because they all count as "letting die" while demanding quite different positive actions — continuous deep sedation, withdrawing a feeding tube, declining to start antibiotics, and a standing DNR order — and ask whether a baseline-relative account sorts them in a way that tracks considered moral judgment better than the act/omission account does, or whether it just relocates the old problem.
