A friend of mine who did a masters in public policy sent me this little explainer he wrote on path dependence, and I'm trying to figure out how much of it to believe. Here it is:

"Path dependence is the idea that once a society or a technology goes down one road, it gets locked in: what you end up with is decided by early, often arbitrary choices rather than by what actually works best. The classic case is the QWERTY keyboard, which beat out the better Dvorak layout purely by accident of history, and the market has been stuck with the inferior standard ever since. The general lesson is that history is destiny — after the first step, the same outcome was bound to arrive anyway, and better alternatives can be closed off permanently. Economists call the mechanism increasing returns, which is a fancy way of saying that the more people adopt something, the more people adopt it. Brian Arthur showed all this with the keyboard, and the upshot is that markets don't reliably pick the best option, so the fact that something survived doesn't tell you it was any good."

I have a nagging feeling that one of those sentences is quietly working against the others, but I cant put my finger on it — and honestly I'm inclined to agree with his bottom line that markets don't reliably select the best option, so I don't want to just wave the whole thing away as cynical econ-blogging. Can you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would nod along to, what would make them wince, and what's flat out wrong? I'd especially like to know which of his claims are the ones doing the real work — i.e. which ones, if they came apart, would take the rest down with them — and whether the QWERTY story is as airtight as he makes it sound, or whether that's one of those cases that's more fought over than the textbook version lets on. And at the end, does his conclusion still stand once you've fixed the parts that need fixing?
