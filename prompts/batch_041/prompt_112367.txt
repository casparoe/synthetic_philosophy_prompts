I am a doctoral candidate working on the philosophy of medicine, and I have been trying to pin down a pattern in how the boundary of disease is actually drawn, without finding a concept that fits it exactly. The pattern is this: a state comes to be classified as a disease, or an existing disease comes to be defined more broadly, at the moment when an intervention against it becomes available, safe, or cheap enough to be worth deploying — and the classification tends to retreat again if the intervention is discredited or its harms come to be thought to outweigh its benefits. The threshold at which raised blood pressure is treated drifts downward after trials show benefit at lower levels; a biochemical or anatomical state long described as a harmless variant is rechristened a disease once a drug is licensed against it; conditions defined chiefly by their power to predict some future harm, rather than by any present malfunction, are carved out as entities in their own right so that they can be studied and treated. In each case the underlying biology has not changed, and often nobody is suffering from anything at the moment of classification; what has changed is the surrounding apparatus of tests, drugs, guidelines and reimbursement.

What I find philosophically interesting is that this makes disease status look like a function of the therapeutic and administrative apparatus rather than of the state itself, and that the harm doing the classificatory work is frequently the harm of being at risk, or of being labelled, rather than any felt dysfunction. I recognise that this is close to medicalisation, to overdiagnosis and to disease mongering, and that it bears on the naturalist–normativist dispute, but none of those seems to be quite it. Medicalisation describes medicine's expansion into fresh territory, whereas what I am after is the category itself being redefined by the availability of the means to act on it. Is there an established concept for this specific dependence? If there is not, would you name one and give it edges — stating precisely what it covers, how it differs from those neighbours, and whether it is best understood as a descriptive claim about how the word disease is actually used or as a normative claim about how it ought to be used? A bare label would not help me; what I need is the carving.
