I translate poetry into English for a small bilingual press, mostly French and Spanish, and about half of what lands on my desk is formally constrained -- acrostics, tight syllable counts, rhyme, lipograms, poems where the constraint is doing real work rather than just decoration. I get through maybe thirty poems a year, each one goes to the poet or the estate for sign-off, and we print a page of translator's notes at the back of the book, so if I break a rule I have to say why in public, and the poet can veto me. My editor's guidance is "just make it sing," which is useless. I've been rereading Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot (the one built around that little Marot poem he calls Ma mignonne) and I want the way of thinking in that book turned into something I can actually run at my desk on a Tuesday afternoon.

So: give me a procedure, not a set of principles. Numbered, in the order I'd actually do it, and at each step the question I'm asking, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, and what I do with each kind of answer. It has to handle the following:

- Sorting constraints by what they're doing, because "preserve the form" can't be one rule. Some constraints are just the container, some carry the sound, some carry content (an acrostic that spells a name that appears nowhere in the poem is content, right?), and some are the entire point of the poem.
- The order in which I sacrifice things when two constraints collide and I can't have both.
- When I'm allowed to swap in a different constraint that does the same job, and how I tell a legitimate swap from me just writing my own poem.
- When to stop translating in verse at all and do a literal line plus a note, and what that note has to contain so the reader isn't cheated.
- The points where I have to stop and ask the poet or the estate, and the exact question to ask them -- not "is this ok" but something they can actually answer.
- Where judgment can't be eliminated, marked as such. I'd rather have three honest holes in the procedure than a tidy flowchart that pretends.

Then run it on three poems from the book I'm on, in detail, and don't hedge about the verdicts:

1. Fourteen lines, seven syllables a line, ABAB, and the first letter of each line spells the name of the poet's dead sister -- a name that never appears in the body of the poem. Seven syllables of her name in English doesn't work.
2. The whole conceit is that it's written without the source language's most common vowel. The poem is about someone who's gone missing, and the missing letter is the joke; one reviewer said the poem is the absence. In English you can keep that vowel out, but only by bending word order until it groans.
3. A poem where the words in every line are in alphabetical order and each line has exactly eleven syllables. The poet says that is the poem. A reader would never feel it.

On number 2 I've basically already decided I'm keeping the vowel out, and I suspect your procedure is going to tell me to drop it. That's fine, I want to know, but say it plainly rather than hedging.

Don't send me off to other books or articles. I can't get to them and I don't want a reading list. And assume I'm not going to learn any linguistics to use this -- my co-translator is a poet with no theory at all and she has to be able to run it too.

Format it like this:

- "The procedure" -- numbered, one page maximum, no prose padding between the steps.
- "Why it's in that order" -- about 200 words, including which distinctions you dropped to make it usable.
- "The three poems" -- one short section each, about 250 words, ending with the call and one line on whether I'll like it.
- "Where it hands off to me or the poet" -- about 200 words, with the exact question to ask.
- "What it can't do" -- about 150 words.
