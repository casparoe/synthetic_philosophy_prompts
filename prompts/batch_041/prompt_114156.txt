I teach a seminar on the debate over how old nations are, and every year the discussion collapses into a shouting match between 'nations are ancient' and 'nations are modern inventions', with nobody able to say which of several distinct claims they are actually defending. Build me a decision tree that sorts the positions. Each branch node should be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf a position, named if a standard name exists and marked 'unnamed' otherwise.

The tree has to pull apart at least these questions: whether what is claimed to be old is national identity among ordinary people, nationalist ideology, or the state; whether the nation is the cause of nationalism or its product; whether pre-modern ethnic ties are a necessary ingredient or merely raw material that any ideology could have used; whether 'nation' names a real collective agent or only a way people talk and act; whether 'invented' is meant to contrast with 'genuine' or only with 'unplanned'; and whether the modernity of nations is a claim about when they appeared or about when the belief in their antiquity appeared.

Then run the leaves against four cases: a stateless diaspora that has carried a national identity for centuries without ever having a state; a state that manufactured a nation within two generations and now celebrates its immemorial traditions; a nation whose founding myths are demonstrably forgeries but whose members are not thereby any less attached to them; and a nation defined by a shared language that most of its members do not speak. Where two leaves return the same verdict on all four cases, say so and explain what would have to change to separate them. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
