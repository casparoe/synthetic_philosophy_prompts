I have spent the better part of a year reading Simone Weil and organizing with a small tenants' group, and I have begun to worry that the political position I have assembled out of the two does not hang together. I would like an audit of it before I present it to a reading group, and I would rather be shown the fault lines than be told flatly that the whole thing is incoherent. So here, stated as bluntly as I can manage, is what I actually hold. (1) Political parties are inherently corrupting. A party's first concern is its own persistence and growth; to keep its members aligned it manufactures a collective opinion they are expected to profess; and this corrodes the individual's capacity for attention, for telling the truth, and for loving particular people. I will not join one, and I have no wish to help build one. (2) The powerless can accomplish nothing alone. Against organized capital and the state, only collective, sustained, organized action has any chance of changing anything, and so politics requires organization that outlives a single campaign or grievance. (3) The goods that matter most — justice, compassion, attention, truthfulness — are realized only in individual souls. A group cannot be just; only a person can. Institutions can at best clear obstacles out of the way; they cannot produce or represent these goods. (4) A decision that binds people is legitimate only if those it binds have a genuine channel through which to voice their needs and to contest the terms; absent such a channel, politics is simply domination dressed up. (5) Weil's contention that obedience and hierarchy are themselves needs of the soul, and not merely impositions to be minimized, seems to me correct: people need to obey a legitimate authority and to command within a legitimate order, and a society that has no hierarchy at all starves something real. I would add that she pairs the needs into apparent opposites — equality beside hierarchy, security beside risk — and insists that both members of each pair are genuine, which strikes me as right. (6) Obligations come before rights: what is owed to a person is prior to, and independent of, any claim she is in a position to make, and the language of rights is a thin modern substitute for that deeper fact.

I suspect this package does not hold together, but I cannot see where the real fault lines are, and I do not want to be told that "all six cannot be held at once" without being shown which pairs actually collide. So go through the commitments and tell me which are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict and why the appearance is misleading, and which are simply independent of one another. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what exactly would have to be surrendered to resolve it, and what that concession would cost someone who wants to keep the rest. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, and close by naming the single revision that would repair the most while costing the least.
