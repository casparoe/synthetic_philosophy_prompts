My reading group built this argument together and I think it proves way too much, but I can't find the loose brick. Here it is.

(1) A thing's having a disposition just is its being such that the right trigger brings about the manifestation -- fragile just is: shatters if struck.
(2) For any disposition there's some possible interfering condition, entirely extrinsic to the thing, that stops the manifestation. The glass is packed in foam; a gadget switches its brittleness off the instant it's struck; or, other direction, a gadget bestows brittleness only at the instant of the strike. (We've been calling the middle two masks and finks -- one blocks the manifestation while leaving the disposition in place, the other takes the disposition away -- and the last one a mimic. The argument just calls all of them "interference".)
(3) So when the manifestation does happen, what's responsible is the absence of interference, not the disposition.
(4) And when it doesn't happen, that tells us nothing about the disposition, since interference could be doing all the work.
(5) So the disposition never makes a difference to what occurs.
(6) Whatever never makes a difference to what occurs is not a real property.
(7) So nothing is fragile, soluble, brave, or magnetic.

I want to keep (2) -- the interference cases are real and they're the whole reason the naive conditional story in (1) falls apart -- and I obviously can't have (7). What really scares me is that the argument doesn't stop where it does: if the disposition is idle because the underlying structure is always there doing the work, then the structure is idle because its parts are, and so on down. My friend says fine, only the fundamental level is real. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine, and what it costs me. And please say whether your fix also stops the generalizing slide or whether I have to just live with that.
