I'm a fourth-year undergraduate taking a seminar in the philosophy of medicine, and our next unit concerns whether "disease" is a descriptive, value-free category or an evaluative one. I've read enough to know that the naturalism/normativism labels get thrown around too loosely and that most of the interesting disagreement lies in the middle. I'd like to build a decision tree that sorts someone into a position by the answers they give to concrete cases rather than by the label they'd choose for themselves.

Please construct it as follows.

Begin every branch node as a question answerable by someone who has never heard of any of these positions — a concrete case or a single yes/no claim about what counts, phrased so that neither answer telegraphs the position it leads to. The nodes should turn on the distinctions that actually do the separating work, which I take to include: whether a condition must involve a biological dysfunction at all; whether "function" is to be read statistically (species-typical frequency) or teleologically (goal contribution); whether harm to the person is necessary, sufficient, or irrelevant; whether the relevant harm is indexed to the organism's own good or to the norms of its society; and whether the category is meant to be a natural kind or a social construction. Use cases like these at the nodes: a harmless anatomical variant that is statistically rare; a genuinely disabling condition that arises from no identifiable malfunction (say, a grief reaction that persists and impairs); a condition that was statistically normal in one population and disabling in another (a thrifty-genotype-style case, or dyslexia in a society without literacy); tooth decay in a population where it is nearly universal; and a condition whose harm is entirely a matter of social stigma rather than of function.

The leaves should be named positions, and should include at least: Boorse-style biostatistical naturalism; Wakefield-style harmful dysfunction; a strong normativism on which "disease" is irreducibly evaluative; social constructionism about at least some disease categories; and an eliminativist line on which the category does no theoretical work and should be dropped. Add any further leaves you think the logical space requires (a function-based account that is value-free but not statistical, a hybrid or "thick concept" view, a phenomenological account centered on the lived experience of illness, and so on), and mark as "unnamed" any combination the space allows that no one occupies. Keep it to roughly ten to twelve branch nodes and no more than about eight leaves.

Where two leaves come apart on no case you can construct, say so explicitly instead of inventing a distinction to separate them; and where an unoccupied combination exists, give one sentence on whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. For each leaf, add one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the case it classifies least comfortably.

I'm more interested in an honest instrument than a tidy one, so please be candid about its limits. Tell me where the sorting is most likely to fail, and which single case or distinction, if it were resolved the other way, would most change the shape of the tree. If you think the naturalism/normativism framing itself distorts the space — for instance, if the most defensible positions refuse to answer the descriptive/evaluative question in those terms — say so and adjust the tree accordingly rather than forcing every leaf into one of the two camps.

Write the response in American English.
