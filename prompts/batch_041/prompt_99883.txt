I am a population ecologist who works with life tables, stage-structured models, and demographic accounting, and I know the wild-animal-suffering debate well enough to follow it but not as a philosopher. Take the position that habitat destruction can reduce the net welfare of ecosystems, and so reduce wild animal suffering, because it reduces the number of individuals, especially r-selected species whose juveniles die at very high rates, and perhaps because the survivors are better off. Restate that position, and the main objections to it, in the vocabulary I actually use: survivorship curves, fecundity schedules, intrinsic rate of increase, density dependence, source-sink dynamics, stable age distribution, life-years lived, and a welfare-weighted life table in which each age-class carries a suffering weight rather than a survival weight. Assume the suffering weights are supplied exogenously; do not turn this into a measurement problem. Do the translation claim by claim, as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts translate only if I smuggle in a moral assumption such as that a given life-year is or is not worth living, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. For the residue, tell me whether the failure is a defect of demographic accounting or evidence that the original claim was less clear than it looked. Do not give me reading lists, citations, or bibliographies. I want a committed answer: take a definite view on whether the welfare-weighted life table can settle the habitat-destruction question, and defend it, rather than laying out what each side might say. Write in British English.
