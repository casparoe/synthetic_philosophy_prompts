I'm preparing a session for a perception seminar on Molyneux's question — whether a person blind from birth who suddenly gained sight could, by vision alone, tell a sphere from a cube, or whether the recognition would require learning to correlate the new modality with touch — and I want to use H. G. Wells's "The Country of the Blind" as the literary case. Read the story through the problem. Nunez's valley is a community that has managed competently for generations without sight, with its own working knowledge of its world; he arrives expecting his sight to make him king among them, finds instead that he is helpless and dependent, that his attempts to describe what he sees are received as ravings, and that the elders eventually propose to cure him by removing his eyes. Does Wells dramatize the empiricist answer to Molyneux — that vision delivers nothing a newly sighted person could use until it has been coordinated with the other senses, so that sight is no great prize — or does the story press the other way, showing Nunez perceiving real features of the world that the valley dwellers cannot reach and that leave their world genuinely impoverished? Be careful to separate the literal cross-modal question from the story's metaphorical use of blindness as ignorance, and say where the story resists the reading altogether: Wells may chiefly be after adaptation, the relativity of standards, and the comedy of the sighted outsider's condescension rather than the epistemology of perception. End by saying what the story contributes that the philosophical literature does not — I have in mind the thought that Molyneux's question is usually posed about a single perceiver, while Wells makes it a question about whose experience a community will count as knowledge. The eighteenth-century Cheselden case and the more recent studies of patients who gained sight after congenital cataract surgery can be brought in as the empirical backdrop, but keep the emphasis on the reading.
