My mate Dave did an ethics module last year and has become unbearable about Kant. He summarised the murderer-at-the-door business for me like this:

"Kant says you have to tell the murderer the truth. It's a duty, full stop — you can't lie, ever, no exceptions, because if you universalise lying then promising and contracting become impossible. It's basically the same argument he gives about the false promise in the Groundwork. So when the murderer asks whether your friend is hiding in your house, you have to say yes. Kant knows this sounds unhinged but he bites the bullet: if you lie and the murderer then kills your friend, you're morally guilty of the death, because you're answerable for the consequences of your lie. Constant, who was a utilitarian, said that was absurd — there's no duty to tell the truth to someone who has no right to it, and no one has a right to a truth that would harm another person. Kant's reply is that truth is a possession, so you can't just grant some people a right to it and deny others, and the duty of truthfulness is unconditional anyway. The only concession Kant makes is that you don't have to volunteer information — but if you're asked a direct question, you can't deceive."

I think most of that is right, but a couple of bits feel off. The guilt-for-the-murder line seems too strong, and I've heard people say the essay is really about law and rights rather than morality, whatever that means. Also I'm suspicious of the Constant-as-utilitarian bit, but I don't know enough to push back. And I've got a nagging feeling Kant's actual point is about truthfulness rather than about having to answer the question at all.

Can you go through Dave's summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a Kant scholar would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's plainly wrong? And then tell me whether the bits that survive actually support his closing verdict, or whether fixing the summary changes the verdict on Kant.
