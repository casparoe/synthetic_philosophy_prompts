I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind and religion, about 14 students, and next week we're on Ayer and life after death. The problem is that the reading makes it sound like a slogan -- no evidence, no meaning -- and they all nod along and nothing actually lands. I want an activity where they run into the problem themselves instead of hearing me state it.

So: design a structured deliberation/game, something like a certification board. Call it The Continuity Board or whatever you want. The setup is that a panel has to certify, case by case, whether the person who comes back is the same person who died. I'll hand out case cards, and I want the cases to escalate so that no single criterion survives contact with all of them:

1. Same body revived after a few minutes, full memories, same personality.
2. Same body, memories gone (dense amnesia), personality noticeably changed.
3. Different body, memories and personality perfectly copied, original body destroyed.
4. Two different bodies, both with full memories and personality, both equally continuous with the original, and identical to each other in every relevant respect (original body destroyed).
5. No body at all -- a stream of experiences with the original's memories carries on, but there is never again any body or physical trace.

Rules I want: each student holds up a verdict card, majority rules, and any student can spend a challenge token to demand that whoever is arguing for a verdict name an observation that would have overturned it. If they can't, the verdict gets recorded as unfalsified rather than correct. I want case 4 to make them flip criteria mid-session, and case 5 to expose that they've been running on a criterion they can't operationalize. No lecture up front.

The debrief then has to do three things. (a) Map their flip-flopping onto Ayer's actual argument that personal identity needs bodily continuity, so that disembodied survival comes out not as a hard case but as an unintelligible one. (b) Map the challenge-token phase onto the verification principle -- show them that in cases 4 and 5 the verdicts they issued were compatible with every possible experience, which is exactly where Ayer says the sentence stops saying anything. (c) Be honest about what the activity can't do: it can't reproduce the first-person pull of an actual near-death experience (Ayer had one late in life and, by most tellings, it didn't budge him), and it can't show that his criterion of personal identity is correct, only what it costs to hold it.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong teh first time -- I'm guessing someone smuggles in the soul as a tiebreaker, or insists case 4 is a trick -- and how I should handle those without rescuing them. And if there's a feature of Ayer's view the activity just can't stage, say so plainly rather than pretending it covers it.
