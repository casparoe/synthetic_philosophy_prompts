why do promises bind? i want an essay that actually takes a side. the two big families of answers are (1) promises bind because of a social practice -- there's an institution of promising, you're a participant in it, and breaking a promise makes you a rule-breaker; and (2) promises bind because of something that isn't mere convention -- the promisor's act of will, or the promisee's interest in being able to rely on you, or the fact that breaking a promise wrongly treats the promisee as a tool for your convenience. weigh them and commit to a verdict.

to keep it from being a rehash, test both against two cases that i think pull in different directions. first: two strangers who share no language, no norms, and no institution of promising at all -- one does something intended to bind herself to the other, and the other comes to rely on it. does she actually bind herself, or is nothing normative happening until a practice exists? second: a promise made to someone who can never understand or take it up -- an infant, someone in late-stage dementia, a dog. the practice view seems untroubled by this, the reliance view is in trouble, and the view that promising is about not treating the other as a mere tool is in a different kind of trouble. use the cases to say whats really doing the work, and dont hedge at the end.
