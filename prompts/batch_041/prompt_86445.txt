I write novels for a living, which mostly means sitting alone and arguing with people who aren't there, but last weekend I got to watch two actual friends argue with each other, and I've been chewing on it since. I want you to adjudicate it. Put on your philosopher hat for this — the kind of philosopher who works on levels of description and has thought hard about what has to be true for a pattern to be real rather than merely convenient. And think carefully before you answer: start by brainstorming, in a paragraph or two, what each move below is actually claiming in your own words, and flag any spot where somebody sounds right but is quietly shifting what "real" is supposed to mean. Then give me the verdict.

Background: I've spent two years on a novel about my father, who died in 2019. The whole book rests on one thing about him: he was stubborn in a specific way — he'd dig in forever about small stuff (which restaurant, which road) and fold almost instantly on anything big. My friend Dev (computational neuroscientist) and my friend Marta (documentary editor, twenty years cutting footage of people) got into it about whether that's even a thing that's true about him. Roughly:

(1) Dev: "There's no fact of the matter about your dad being stubborn. There are three ways to talk about him — as particles, as a machine his childhood built, or as a guy with reasons — and the third one is just the cheapest for animals with our memory. 'Stubborn' is a label you're projecting onto a cloud of behavior. The physical story is the only one that's actually true of him."

(2) Marta: "Then 'he sold the house,' 'he had a stroke,' 'he loved your mother' are all projections too, since they're all labels on clouds. A pattern is real when you can say it in fewer words than the raw data and still predict the next bit. 'Stubborn' does that. A list of his neurons doesn't."

(3) Dev: "Fewer words in which vocabulary? Give me a rich enough physical language and the physical story comes out just as short. Compression is relative to a vocabulary and to what you already expect to see. So all you've shown is that 'stubborn' compresses for us — that's instrumentalism with extra steps, not realism about character."

(4) Marta: "Your objection eats every level, including yours. For any physical description there's a vocabulary where that one is the long one. And you don't have the physical description anyway — nobody does. You've got neurons, or hormones, and you call that 'the physical one' because it sounds serious. Also, compression isn't the only test: a real pattern has to earn its keep on cases nobody's seen yet. His stubbornness predicted how he'd be at my wedding. He'd never been to my wedding."

(5) Dev: "Except the house. He listed it Tuesday, it was gone by Friday. You keep the pattern alive by calling that noise. Exception clauses can be as long as you like, and at some point the exception is longer than the rule — at that point you're not compressing anymore, you're just refusing to lose."

(6) Marta: "Noise isn't the same as error. Every real pattern is noisy, including the ones you like. The question is whether the exception is better explained as noise or as the pattern being wrong, and you haven't shown which."

I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, and I have a practical stake: the book needs me to decide wether to write him as stubborn with one glaring exception, or as a man with no character at all, just a long series of decisions that happened to look like one.

So: go through (1)–(6) one at a time and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per move. Then definately tell me who's closer overall, and what both of them are missing. I don't want "they're both onto something" — I want to know which specific claims survive contact. If any of it bears on how I should write him, say that too. And please, no reading list and no name-dropping — I don't have library access and I don't care who said it first, I want the actual reasoning.
