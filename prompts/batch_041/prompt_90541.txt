I run the student conduct office at a small liberal arts college, and last year we threw out our old disciplinary code and replaced it with something we call a restorative accountability process. In practice that means the front-line people — hall directors, two deans, me — have to decide, in the moment, whether a student who's done something is someone we hold responsible and expect an account from (and yes, express real anger or disappointment toward), or whether we flip into what I can only call management mode: this is a problem to be solved, here's a support plan, a referral, a restriction, but nobody is being blamed. Right now that call is pure vibes and it's wildly inconsistent between staff. One hall director gives a kid the full "I'm disappointed in you, what were you thinking" treatment for the same thing another one writes up as a wellness concern. Some weeks we make this call 15 or 20 times.

I've been reading Peter Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" and it feels like the closest thing to a map I've found, but it's an essay, not a proceedure. Can you turn it into one? I want something a hall director with no philosophy background can run in about ten minutes: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "this is a conduct matter, hold them to it" and where it stops with "this is a support matter, stop blaming," and the points where it has to hand the call to a person because it's really a values question and not a fact question.

Two things I care about. First, keep the distinctions that actually do the work in Strawson — in particular I think the difference between a factor that excuses one particular action and a factor that takes the person off the board entirely as someone you can address yourself to at all, and the difference between your own first-person reaction to being wronged and the cooler third-person reaction on someone else's behalf. Drop anything that doesn't survive contact with a hall director. Second, don't pretend the essay hands you a tidy flowchart when it doesn't — if you're reconstructing something Strawson didn't actually say, say so.

Then run it on three cases. (1) A first-year copied big chunks of an essay from a paid essay mill, and when we sat her down she said her parents will cut her off if her GPA slips and she "just needed it finished." She planned it over a week. (2) A student with a documented psychotic disorder shoved his roommate during a manic episode, and was hospitalized after. The roommate is scared and furious and wants him out of the room and off the floor. (3) A student on full financial aid, who is the primary caregiver for a younger sibling, took about $200 of stuff off a roommate's desk. Our instinct was to route the whole thing to financial aid and counseling and never really confront him. I have a feeling the procedure is going to tell me that's exactly the wrong move, and if it does I want you to say so plainly.

At the end, tell me what the procedure can't do and where you expect it to misfire in our setting. And where people who actually work on Strawson disagree about what he meant, or where you're making a judgment call that others who know this literature would make differently, flag it — I don't want a clean flowchart that presents one contested reading as if it were the settled one.
