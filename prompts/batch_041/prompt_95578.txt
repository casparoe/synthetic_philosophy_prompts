I chair the committee that just pushed through ranked ballots for all our departmental elections, and Thursday's faculty meeting is where I get five minutes to introduce the new system. Half the room are economists who know Gibbard–Satterthwaite cold; the other half are humanists who'll glaze over the second I say 'incentive compatability,' and the dean is in the front row. The trouble is that my natural pitch -- rank honestly and let the system sort it out -- is exactly what the theorem forbids: any rule that can put any one of three or more candidates in the winner's slot, and that never gives a voter a reason to misreport, has to be dictatorial. The one clean escape (everyone's preferences credibly single-peaked along a left-right line, where taking the median is honest by construction) doesn't fit a department whose fights aren't one-dimensional, and don't offer me the random-ballot dodge, I already lost that argument upstairs. So write the remarks: honest about manipulability, no proof, five minutes, and it has to end with people actually bothering to vote. Sound like someone introducing a thing he believes in with reservations, not like a seminar.
