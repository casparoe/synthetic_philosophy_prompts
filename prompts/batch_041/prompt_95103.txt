I am the methods editor at a psychology journal. About three times a month a submission reaches me claiming that a belief, a desire, a mood, or a body of category knowledge changes what people perceive rather than what they report, decide, or remember: that social exclusion makes a room look dimmer, that hunger makes food look closer, that a price on a bottle changes how the wine tastes, that an expert sees a board differently from a novice. I have roughly forty minutes per manuscript, no budget for new data, and I am not the one who decides — I make a recommendation, the action editor signs off, and the authors get a single response window, so a bad verdict costs something in both directions.

What I need is a screening procedure: an ordered list of questions to put to a claim of this kind, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers can come from the manuscript and which require me to ask the authors, where the procedure stops with a verdict, and where it must stop and hand the decision back because the question that remains is theoretical rather than evidential. It should be short enough to run in one sitting — a page for the procedure itself, the worked cases after it.

The procedure must sort every claim into one of five bins and defend the placement: (1) a genuine online effect on perceptual content; (2) a selection effect, where attention or gaze changes the input rather than the processing; (3) a diachronic effect of learning or expertise, which is a different phenomenon wearing the same name; (4) a response-level effect, where the perception is unchanged and the criterion for saying or deciding has moved; (5) an effect on what is encoded, stored, or reconstructed rather than on what is seen. For each bin, give the evidence signature that earns it, the evidence that would move a claim out of it, and the default placement when the manuscript simply does not report what the test requires, which will be most of the time.

Rank the following by evidential weight and say where each one misleads: a comparison that holds the proximal stimulus fixed; a signal-detection analysis separating sensitivity from criterion; a task in which the belief has nothing to do, so that a real perceptual change should still show up; the persistence or collapse of the effect once participants know what is being tested; signatures of attention and selection rather than of altered processing; adaptation and aftereffect behavior, or scaling with low-level parameters such as contrast; and the difference between a transient state manipulated within a session and a long history of training. Note where a test cannot be run at all — for instance where the stimulus is ambiguous and there is no fixed proximal stimulus to hold constant.

Then run it on three cases, giving the verdict and the one-line reason for each: a study reporting that hungry participants see food items as larger and brighter; a study reporting that the same wine is rated as tasting more intense when the label says it is expensive, with the authors claiming that expectation penetrates taste; and a study reporting that expert players literally see a board differently, which the authors frame as belief-driven perception. At least one verdict should be one the authors will hate, and at least one should cut against a prior of my own — I am fairly confident the wine case is a response effect, and I want to know if I am wrong. Finish with what this procedure cannot do, and mark plainly which of its steps are theory-laden judgments I am performing in a fixed order rather than tests. State the content of any position you invoke; do not name a theory and leave it at that. I do not need a reading list.
