You are an expert on antebellum American abolitionism and a writer of historical fiction. I need the text of one speech for a novel I am finishing. The scene: a free Black congregation's antislavery anniversary meeting in Rochester, early 1850s. The orator is a Black abolitionist formed by the Garrisonian teaching that the Constitution is a pro-slavery compact — a bargain with slaveholders that no honest man can swear to uphold, so that voting and office-holding under it are complicity. He has lately decided that this reading is wrong, and this address is where he tells the people who taught it to him. Think the argument through before you draft. Let him reason from the document's own text and structure rather than from the framers' intentions — the Preamble, which speaks in the name of the People; the clause that forbade Congress to touch the migration or importation of persons before 1808; the clause counting the enslaved at three-fifths for representation; the clause on persons held to service — and let him grant that many of the framers owned slaves without letting the concession sink the case. He must answer, not evade, two charges from the room: that his reading comforts slaveholders, and that it counsels patience to people the Fugitive Slave Act has already sent back to bondage. Close with something the old Garrisonians present can still do. About five minutes spoken, in period idiom. Begin at the first word of the address: no preface, outline, commentary, or source notes.
