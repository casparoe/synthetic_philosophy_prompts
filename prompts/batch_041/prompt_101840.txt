I host a small podcast on decision theory, and next week's guest is a theorist who argues that superrationality — the rule of choosing as though every agent who reasons like you will reach the same decision — is the correct principle of choice, and who defends it by appeal to the Platonia dilemma (a trillionaire promises a billion dollars to the one person, out of twenty, who sends a telegram, provided that exactly one telegram arrives) and the Luring Lottery (a prize of one million dollars is divided among all entries, so the fewer the entries the larger the share). Draft eight to ten interview questions that force him to defend the view rather than restate it, and for each question include a follow-up aimed at the answer he is most likely to give. At a minimum, cover: (a) the reference-class problem — how he identifies the agents whose reasoning must mirror his, and what the view says when some of them are known to reason differently; (b) why the Platonia dilemma's recommended action is a mixed strategy (send the telegram with probability one in twenty) when the view's whole appeal is that identical reasoners converge on a single answer; and (c) the Luring Lottery's runaway dynamic, in which the individually best reply is to submit as many entries as everyone else combined — the actual contestants answered with astronomically large numbers — and whether this reveals the view as a coordination norm that presupposes everyone is already on board rather than a decision procedure for actual agents. Also press him on the charge of magical thinking (that his choice is being treated as a cause of others' choices) and on how he extends a rule defined only for symmetric games to asymmetric ones. He has mentioned running a modified Luring Lottery in which entry was free but a lone entrant collected a large bonus on top of the divided prize; include a question on what that variant exposes about the difference between superrationality and evidential decision theory.
