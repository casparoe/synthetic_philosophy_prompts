I've been chewing on the Death in Damascus setup for a while and I want to see it rebuilt from the ground up -- without the death, and without the oracle. So: design me a case in that family.

The skeleton I'm after: one decision, two options. Before you decide, something or someone has already committed to a prediction about which option you'll take, and has acted on it. Your choice is correlated with the prediction but doesn't cause it. And when your choice matches the prediction, it goes badly for you. Everything else is yours to fill in, but here are my constraints, and I'll be annoyed if any of them gets fudged:

- Ordinary stakes, ordinary agents. No Death, no demons, no gods, no time travel, no backwards causation, no "the predictor is 100% accurate". It should be a situation a normal adult could actually be in: money, a job, a place at a school, a bet, an apartment. If your case is realistic enough that it might end up in small claims court, you've got it right.
- The predictor has to be fallible, and you have to give me a number: how often its right, strictly between chance and certainty. You also have to say where the accuracy comes from -- some feature of you that both the prediction and your choice are downstream of. If the accuracy comes from mind-reading, or from your choice causing the prediction, the case is void, and you should say so yourself.
- No stipulating the verdicts. The theories have to do the work.

Then walk me through it in these sections:

**The setup** (~200 words): the case, told plainly, with the accuracy number and the mechanism behind it.

**The two verdicts** (~250 words): what evidential decision theory says to do, and what causal decision theory says to do, each in its own terms. Be explicit about what each one conditions on or holds fixed, and say whether they land on the same option here or not.

**Is the advice stable?** (~250 words): suppose you follow whichever recommendation you take seriously. Re-run both theories from where you now stand. Does either theory change its mind about what you should have done? Is there any option that's self-ratifying -- one the theory still endorses after you've taken it? If no pure option is, say whether a coin flip rescues you, and which mixture (if any) is self-consistent. And one more thing: does the flip only show up because the agent is allowed to watch itself choose? If you ban updating on your own choice, does the case dissolve -- and does that look like a fix or a dodge?

**The bookie** (~150 words): build a side bet on the outcome of your case, offered alongside the main decision, that one of the two theories says is clearly worth taking and the other says is clearly not. If the agent who takes it can then be bought out at a profit, show the buyout. If you can't get a clean pump, say what blocks it.

**What's doing the work** (~150 words): name the single feature of the case that generates all of this, then tell me what happens to each theory's verdict if you (a) drop the predictor's accuracy to chance and (b) add a small cost to switching options.

**Reply for the defence** (~150 words): whichever theory comes out looking worse, give me its best response. What exactly does the defender have to deny, and does denying it cost them anything anywhere else?

**My take** (~50 words): your own call on whether the case is a real problem for that theory or an artifact of how it was set up.

And dont cite anything or gesture at "the literature" -- I want the machinery, not the bibliography.
