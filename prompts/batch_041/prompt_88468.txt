I'm in a grad seminar on collective responsibility right now, and we keep circling back to the inefficacy objection. A friend of mine who works in climate policy wrote a blog post about it that I found pretty convincing, but something about it nags at me and I'd like to know how much of it to trust before I start repeating it in seminar and embarrassing myself.

Here's the gist of what he wrote: "The inefficacy objection is the argument that since one person's individual contribution to a collective harm -- one transatlantic flight, one beef burger, one vote -- makes no detectable difference to whether the harm occurs, individuals have no moral obligation to refrain from these things. Philosophers who've looked at this closely have basically conceded the point: you can't ground individual duties in the consequences of individual acts, so they've retreated to saying the duty is really a duty to join collective action, or to be a good citizen, rather than a duty not to do the thing itself. And the upshot is the familiar one: the harm is caused by the system, so only systemic change matters, and individual consumption choices are at best a distraction and at worst a way of letting the real culprits off the hook. That's why the standard view in the field now is that there's no such thing as an individual duty not to eat meat or fly -- just a political duty to change the system."

Can you go claim by claim through that and tell me what a philosopher who actually works on collective harm would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? I'm especially curious about three things: (a) whether the "no detectable difference" premise really does the work he thinks it does, or whether that premise is itself doing something sneaky, (b) whether "the harm is caused by the system, so only systemic change matters" actually follows from anything in the earlier sentences, and (c) whether "philosophers have basically conceded" is a fair description of where things stand or is more of a rhetorical move. I'm inclined to think individual duties survive somehow, but I can't tell if I believe that because it's true or because I want it to be, so please tell me where I'd be fooling myself too. No need for a reading list or citations, I just want the audit.
