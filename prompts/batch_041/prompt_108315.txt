Three things happened to me this term and I've convinced myself they're all the same thing, and I can't find a name for it. Context so you know who's asking: I'm a third-year PhD in linguistics, not philosophy of language proper, so if this is a known thing with a known name just tell me and I'll go read — but I've poked around and I don't think it is.

1. In a lab meeting I said I could *probably* get someone a draft by the end of the month. It was a hedge, and I think everyone in the room could hear the hedge. Four weeks later it's being referred to as the deadline. I say, I said probably. Doesn't matter. The hedge is just gone from the record. What I did in saying that turned out to be committing, and it turned out to be that *because* of how everyone downstream treated it.

2. I made a joke at a dinner that didn't land and someone was hurt. I said it was a joke. That didn't get taken as a correction, it got taken as me doing something worse — so now the thing on record is that I said something cruel and then made excuses. I'm not saying they were wrong to be upset. I'm saying what my utterance *was* got decided by how it was taken, the taking happened later, and my own account of what I meant counted for nothing.

3. A senior person in my department mused out loud that "we should probably stop admitting people without funding" — said as a musing, in a meeting, to nobody in particular. It got minuted. Now it's a policy. Nobody voted on it. There was no moment at which it became a policy, it just became one, and the becoming happened in other people's subsequent turns.

The common shape, as far as I can get it: the act-side of an utterance gets assigned *after* the fact, by the audience, sometimes by people who weren't even the addressee; the speaker's intention isn't decisive and is often not even relevant; the assignment is normatively loaded, i.e. obligations and permissions fall out of it; and it isn't reversible — you can apologise for it, you can't undo it.

I know this is near things I half-know. Austin, obviously, on what has to be in place for a performative to come off — but that's all about conditions holding *at the moment of utterance*. There's the conversation-analysis line that you can only tell what a turn was doing by seeing what the next turn does with it — but that's a method for the analyst, an epistemic point, not a claim about when the obligation appears. Grice is about what the speaker meant, which is sort of the opposite of what I'm describing. What I can't find anywhere is the temporal bit (it's fixed later) or the third-party bit (bystanders do the fixing).

So: does this have a name? If it does, I want the name *and* the boundaries. If it doesn't, coin one, and then tell me what it excludes. Specifically I'd want to know:

- Is this just ambiguity plus social pressure, or a genuinely different thing? What would have to be true of an utterance for it not to be vulnerable to this at all?
- Does it need a record — minutes, a group chat, a written offer? Does it happen in purely spoken, unrecorded contexts, or is the record doing the work?
- Is the bystander version (people who weren't the addressee settling what an utterance was) the same phenomenon or a different one? I suspect different, but I can't say why.
- And is there a reason promises are the worst case? If the content of a promise is whatever the promisee takes it to be, promising looks like the case where the speaker has the least authority over their own act. Is that a real thing or am I making it up?

I'm not after a label for its own sake — I want to know whether I'm looking at one phenomenon or three wearing the same coat.
