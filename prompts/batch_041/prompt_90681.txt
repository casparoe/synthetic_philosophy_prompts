I teach a seminar on global history and we keep hitting the same wall: every time we try to write the past from everywhere at once, something structural gives way, and it's never just bias or politics or the archives being incomplete. I want to hand my students a short story in week 3 that makes that wall visible instead of just asserting it, and I've tried writing it myself three times and it keeps coming out as an essay with dialogue. So: write me the story.

Here's the thing I want it to carry. I've been calling it the flat index. Somebody, in some port town, in one season, gets the job of compiling a register of everything that happened there, and the rule is that no party's vocabulary is allowed to be the headword while everyone else's terms become cross-references. Every entry has to be reachable from every party's own way of naming what happened, and no term gets to be the topic while the others become context. Make the compiler a clerk, not a philosopher. Make the town have at least three parties who don't share a language.

Then show, concretely, why it can't close, and I don't mean because the governor confiscates it. I mean because a list of events needs a principle of individuation and the parties don't agree what counts as one event or where one begins and ends; because simultaneity needs a clock and every clock is somebody's calendar; because a register that privileges no headword quietly stops being a narrative and turns into something else. Include at least one scene where two people describe the same afternoon and the mere act of folding it into one entry changes what happened. Include a page of the index itself, as a break in the story, so the reader can see the asymmetry with their own eyes. And there's a trick I love where a sentence picks out an event by way of something that hadn't happened yet, so nobody living could have said it; use that if it fits, but dont lecture about it.

No wise elder, no narrator explaining the point, and please no frame where a modern historian finds the document and reflects. Thats the move I'm trying to get away from. For an ending I want the clerk to actually choose between privileging a topic (and so a perspective) and keeping the symmetry (and so producing an archive rather than a history), and I want the story to leave it genuinely open whether that's a defeat or a discovery. 1,500 words, 2,000 max. Dont use real named historical figures and dont cite anything.
