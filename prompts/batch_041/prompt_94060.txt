I did 35 years of patch-clamp work on snail neurons before I retired, and now I've been reading philosophy of mind, so bear with me if I mangle the jargon. I keep getting stuck on epiphenomenalism — the view that my experience of, say, the smell of coffee is a byproduct of the brain doing its thing, a shadow thrown on the wall, and the shadow doesn't push anything around. Fine. But then how do I come to say "that smells like coffee"? My saying it has to be caused by something. If it's caused by the smell-experience itself, then the experience wasn't a shadow after all. So the epiphenomenalist has to say the sentence is caused by whatever brain event also causes the experience — two effects of one common cause, running in parallel, never touching. Which means my reports about my own experience are, at best, reliably correlated with the experience without ever being about it. And I can't shake the feeling that "at best" is doing alot of work in that sentence.

Here's the premise I'd like you to build a story on. Imagine a near-future clinic where they've mapped those two parallel channels — one that makes the experience, one that makes the report — well enough to poke them seperately. Cut the experience-channel in a patient and the report-channel keeps humming along just fine: she sits up and cheerfully tells you the walls are blue, describes the exact shade, argues with you about the lighting, and there is nothing it is like for her to be doing any of this. Flip it and you get someone swimming in experience who can't say a word about it.

Write me a short story set in or around that clinic. I want the philosophical meat dramatized, not lectured — no character sitting everyone down to spell out the paradox. At least one character should be a committed epiphenomenalist and at least one should think the whole picture is incoherent, and neither should be a strawman. Don't cheat at the end by having science suddenly prove dualism, or by revealing it was all a simulation, or by making the twist "it was a dream." I'd rather it end unresolved but earned. 800–1500 words or so is fine.
