Hey — I'm a fourth-year PhD student in HPS and I've somehow ended up as the head TA for our big upper-level 'Models, Idealization, and Evidence' course, which means the final exam is now my problem. I need you to write me one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

Here's what I'm trying to test. Every year the students walk in able to recite the F-twist: the unrealism of a theory's assumptions isn't itself a mark against it, what matters is predictive success, as-if reasoning and all that. Then they fall to pieces the moment you ask when a false assumption actually does matter. That's the crux I want the question to hit — what makes a false assumption load-bearing rather than harmless, and how you tell which one you've got in a given case.

So I've built them a case to chew on. I'll tell them it's a stylized composite so they don't waste time trying to look it up (also it's a closed-book in-hall exam, so they can't). The case: a model of a regulated industry in which firms are assumed to have perfect foresight about future regulations. The headline comparative static is that a stricter regulation has no effect on emissions, because firms anticipate it and adjust before it bites. The model's predictions line up pretty well with the industry's historical data. In reality firms plainly don't have perfect foresight — they have partial, noisy expectations. And the paper's punchline isn't just a prediction; it's a claim about the mechanism: anticipation is what neutralizes the regulation.

I want the question to make them say, for this case, whether the F-twist licenses that punchline, and to give me the general distinction that decides it instead of just saying that it depends. I also definately want it to seperate two things half the class conflates: a model fitting the data it was built against, versus a model being trustworthy for extrapolation or policy. And I'd love it if the really good answers noticed that the assumption here isn't merely false — it's false in a way that's supposed to be doing the explanatory work, which is a different situation from your frictionless-plane idealization.

Deliverables: the exam question exactly as it'd be printed, including the time/length note (it's a 50-minute question on a 3-hour paper); a model answer of around 650 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each answer has to make. For each must-hit point, give the marks and a one-liner on how a B answer typically misses it. Also flag anything that's genuinely bonus rather than required, so I don't penalize it by accident.
