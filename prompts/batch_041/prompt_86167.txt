I have taken the Clifford side of the ethics-of-belief dispute since the old blogosphere days — the position that a belief is permissible only when it is held on sufficient evidence, and that this holds always, everywhere, and for everyone. James I read as a fine stylist making an excuse for wanting things to be true. A friend who reads the same blogs has now handed me the following argument, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuine refutation or a trick with a slip in it, so I am passing it on.

(1) A belief is permissible only if it is held on sufficient evidence.
(2) Take any belief of mine that I regard as well supported. At this moment I am not running the supporting computations; I am relying on my past verdict that the evidence was sufficient.
(3) That reliance is itself a belief — the belief that the evidence was sufficient — and by (1) it too must be held on sufficient evidence. The same question then arises for that belief, and for the next.
(4) So the chain is either infinite or it terminates in something held without sufficient evidence. Either way, by (1), no belief is permissible.
(5) Suppose instead that the chain terminates in something that is not a belief at all — a disposition, an entitlement, a way I happen to be wired. A state that is not held on evidence is not held on sufficient evidence, so by (1) it cannot make anything permissible; at most it explains why I cannot help myself.
(6) The same reasoning covers the belief that my faculties are reliable, which I can support only circularly — memory by remembered successes, reasoning by arguments.
(7) Therefore no belief is permissible. But that verdict is one no one can live by, and no one can even assert it without relying on beliefs he holds. So (1) is false: some beliefs are permissible without sufficient evidence, which is what James said.

He calls this a clean reductio — any theory that forbids self-trust has already used self-trust to state its case — and he says the only honest responses are to join James or to stop talking. I find the conclusion convenient and therefore suspicious, and I would rather keep (1) than (7) if there is a way. But I do not want to keep (1) by mumbling about levels and circularity without saying what I am giving up.

Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked so plausible that no one in the argument noticed it, and what rejecting it costs me. In particular: if I repair the principle so that self-trust survives, does anything remain of it that still condemns the credulous, the wishful, and the shipowner who talks himself into a seaworthy vessel — or does saving the one save all of them?
