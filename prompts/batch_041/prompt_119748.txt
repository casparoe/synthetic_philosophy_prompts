I've worked for about a decade as a social worker on a geriatric and palliative unit, and for the last three years I've sat on our hospital's ethics consult service. There's a pattern in how we handle the wishes a patient expressed while they still had capacity, and I've never been able to find a name for it — a colleague keeps telling me it's "just autonomy," which I'm fairly sure is too quick, since if that were the whole story I wouldn't be able to predict, before a consult even starts, which way it will go.

The core of it is this. When a patient, while still competent, refuses something in advance — no CPR, no ventilator, no feeding tube — and later, demented or delirious, says "no, please, do everything," we hold them to the earlier refusal; the old no wins. When a patient, while competent, asks for something in advance — "if I can't swallow, put the tube in," "keep me going" — and later, now without capacity, says "no tube, no, stop," we follow the later no; the old yes loses. So a competent refusal seems to reach forward and a competent request doesn't. I want to know whether that asymmetry has a name, and whether the explanation is just the ordinary point that autonomy is a right to refuse rather than a right to demand, or whether there's something specific to the fact that it's one person at two times rather than two people.

Then there's a second pattern, and I can't tell whether it's the same thing in different clothes. The weight we give the earlier self seems to track how the later self came to differ from them. When the change looks like a loss — dementia, a head injury, a psychosis — we treat the earlier self as the real one and bind the later self to what the earlier one chose. When the change looks like growth — sober after twenty years of drinking, out of a long depression, newly devout, newly in love — we treat the earlier self as superseded, and we let the later self rewrite commitments the earlier one made just as competently and just as clearly. Decline makes the old self authoritative; growth makes it obsolete. Is there a name for that? Does it belong with the first pattern or is it a different animal? If there's no established term for one or both, please coin one and define it tightly enough that I can sort my cases — say which side of the line a contested case falls on — and say which existing concepts come closest and precisely where each of them stops fitting. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether there's a name, and what it is — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
