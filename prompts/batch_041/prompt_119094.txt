Ok, quick background so you know who's asking: I'm 71, I spent most of my working life as a geologist (stratigraphy, mostly oil and gas), and since I retired I've been reading philosophy in the evenings like it's a detective novel. No formal training, so if I say something dumb, blame the geology.

What I'm stuck on is the doomsday arguement. The version in my head goes like this: I should think of myself as roughly a random human being among all the humans who will ever be born. I happen to be sitting somewhere around number 100 billion. If the total number is N, then the chance of landing in the first 100 billion is about 100 billion divided by N, which is tiny when N is enormous. So finding myself where I am is evidence that N isn't enormous, which is to say, not much of a future. Maybe I've dropped a step, but that's the shape of it.

I gather there's a camp that thinks this reasoning is basically sound and a camp that thinks it's broken. The second lot say things like: it proves too much; or it's the choice of what counts as "a random human" doing all the work; or my existing at all is itself evidence that there are more people rather than fewer, and that cancels the whole thing out.

I don't want a book report on who said what. I want the joint statement these two camps could both put their names to after a long weekend locked in a room, honest enough that neither one feels stitched up. Like this:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides accept -- about the logic, about what the argument does and doesn't show, about what kind of evidence would matter here. Nothing so watered down that it says nothing, and nothing only one side would sign.
2. A table of the genuine disagreements, one row each, each stated in wording that its own side would accept as fair.
3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody -- and for each crux, the case or argument both sides agree is relevant, plus what each side predicts about it, written down in advance so nobody can wriggle later.
4. If part of the fight turns out to be about words rather than substance, keep that seperate and say which part.

Then two things I've made up myself, and I want the statement to rule on both:

(a) The sealed letter. A trustworthy source tells us the total number of humans who will ever be born is either 200 billion or 200 trillion, coin flip, and that's all they can say. Does that change anything for me? By how much, and in which direction? I want both sides to answer with actual numbers, not hand-waving.

(b) My grandson's machines. He's a computer person and he insists that in a few decades there will be trillions of conscious computers running, and that this should make me more pessimistic about humans specifically. I can't tell whether that's sharp or just confused. Does the reference class shift? And is "the reference class" a fact about the world, or a choice we make?

Finish it with what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and whether there's anything at all that both sides agree would move the debate.
