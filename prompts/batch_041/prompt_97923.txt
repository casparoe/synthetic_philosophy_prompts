For our philosophy club's session on anthropic reasoning, a classmate introduced the doomsday argument through an analogy I cannot stop turning over, and I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply restate the argument. The picture he gave was this: an urn holds balls numbered 1 through N; you reach in blind and draw one; if the number on your ball is, say, 60, then you should suspect that N is not enormous, that it is more likely than not no larger than about 120, since a random draw landing that low would be surprising in a very large urn. Your birth rank among all humans who will ever have lived, he said, is exactly like that drawn ball: you are a random one of all the humans there will ever be, so the fact that you are roughly the hundred-billionth human is evidence that the total number of humans will be comparatively small, and therefore that extinction is nearer than we would otherwise have thought. He uses this to argue that the human future is probably short. Our teacher pushed back in a way I did not fully follow, saying the analogy cheats twice over: a ball does not get to notice its own number or wonder which urn it came from, whereas you woke up already inside the urn with a perspective on your position; and you cannot even say cleanly what counts as a ball, whether it is people or person-moments or something else, so the very thing being drawn is undefined.

I would like a careful audit of this picture: what maps onto what (the urn, the balls, the numbering, the draw, and the observer doing the estimating), which parts of that mapping are actually load-bearing for the conclusion and which are decorative, and where the analogy breaks in ways that genuinely matter for the intended use rather than in ways that are merely picturesque. In particular, I want to know whether the two objections above are fatal or just complications to be patched, whether the analogy can survive once we try to fix the reference class, and whether the conclusion — that the human future is probably short — is something the analogy can really carry or whether it was doing all the work on its own and the urn is just decoration. If the urn is a poor analogy for this purpose, tell me what a better one would be, or whether the argument resists analogies of this kind altogether. Finally, please be explicit about how confident you are in your verdict and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, so that I know where to dig in.
