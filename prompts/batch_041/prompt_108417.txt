I am writing a novel in which a philosopher-character defends an account of propositional unity by analogy: a proposition is like a brick wall. The words, or the things they stand for, are the bricks; the relation that binds them—predication, or the copula—is the mortar; and the wall is the unified proposition, something over and above a mere heap of bricks. On this picture, once you have supplied the right mortar, the unity is fully explained and no further mystery remains; the conclusion he draws is that unity is external and unproblematic, a matter of one more ingredient applied from outside.

I would like the analogy audited. Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of the bricks, the mortar, the mason, the wall—and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in whether the mortar picture can survive the old regress objection that any bond relating two items must itself be related to each of them, so that a further bond is needed, and so on without end; and in whether the features that matter for the intended conclusion—the specific, asymmetric order of a proposition, its truth-aptness, its dependence on sense rather than mere co-presence—are ones the wall can actually supply. Finally, say whether the conclusion that unity is external and unproblematic survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy would be if there is one.
