I'm in a graduate seminar on early modern science and religion, and for the final paper I've drafted a short essay on Kepler's astrology that I'd like you to comment on. The draft runs roughly as follows.

Kepler is remembered as the astronomer who abolished the crystalline spheres and gave us elliptical orbits, and his astrology is usually filed away as an embarrassing residue — a superstition he was too pious, or too dependent on his patrons, to renounce. I want to argue the reverse: that his astrology is a serious and internally coherent piece of natural philosophy, and that the very features modern readers find most embarrassing are precisely what make it coherent. On my reading, Kepler did not suppose that the planets push or pull on our bodies; he supposed that the soul is a proportion-sensitive faculty, and that it is stirred when it registers a configuration whose ratios are harmonious — the same ratios that govern music and the architecture of the cosmos. Astrology, so understood, is applied harmonics: a claim about which proportions the soul finds consonant, not a claim about rays, effluvia, or occult virtues. That, I think, explains his otherwise puzzling double attitude — mocking the credulity of the ordinary star-casters and the triviality of their predictions, yet defending astrology in principle and casting horoscopes himself. What he defended was a thesis about resonance with harmony; what he mocked was a traffic in particulars that had no bearing on that thesis. If I am right, the standard story gets the emphasis backwards.

What I'd like from you is three things. First, an accuracy check: is this a defensible reading of what Kepler actually held, or am I quietly importing a modern, aestheticizing picture of the soul and projecting it back onto him? Please flag any place where I attribute to him a view he didn't hold, or where I flatten a real tension into a tidy thesis. Second, a verdict on interest: is this a genuinely illuminating reinterpretation, or just a redescription of the same familiar facts in flattering language? And third, if the thesis is worth keeping, how would you shore up its weakest link — I suspect it is the move from the claim that the soul responds to harmony to anything recognizable as astrology.
