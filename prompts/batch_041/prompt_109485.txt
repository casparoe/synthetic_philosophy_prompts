For the past few months a friend and I have been arguing about the hole argument — she is finishing a master's in philosophy of physics, I am an amateur who has spent far too long in the rationalist blogs and forums — and we have finally got our disagreement small enough to attach a number to it. We agree about the mechanics: take a solution of the Einstein field equations, choose a diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside some bounded open region and non-identity inside it, and you obtain a second solution that agrees with the first on everything outside that region while assigning different metrical properties to the very same spacetime points inside it. If the points of the manifold are real individuals whose identity is fixed independently of the fields, then those two solutions describe two distinct physical situations, and the theory fails to determine what happens inside the region from what happens outside it. What I want from you is a credence that the hole argument is a sound argument against substantivalism — that it genuinely leaves no version of the view standing, including the sophisticated version that keeps spacetime points as real entities while denying them any identity over and above their place in the metrical field — as opposed to an argument that merely shows a careless way of stating substantivalism to be incoherent, or one that quietly assumes isomorphic models must be counted as distinct.

Give me the number, and then the decomposition rather than a tour of the literature. The terms I can see are these: the probability that the two solutions related by a hole diffeomorphism do represent distinct physical situations if points are individuals with primitive identity; the probability that the sophisticated substantivalist response is a stable position rather than a concession that gives the game away; the probability that the naive version of substantivalism was ever the live version, so that refuting it amounts to refuting the view; the probability that determinism is a requirement we should hold fixed rather than something the argument reveals to be ill-defined for any theory with symmetries; and whatever further terms you think belong. Give each a number and a line of justification, show the combination, and then tell me which single term our disagreement most likely lives in and what would move it.

One further diagnostic, because it is where I think my friend is wrong. She holds that the construction depends on the smooth structure of the manifold and on the continuity of the diffeomorphism group, so that a discrete ontology — say a finite set of cells carrying a field, with only relabellings as symmetries — would be immune to it. I doubt this, since a permutation can be the identity outside a chosen set of cells and non-identity inside it just as a diffeomorphism can. I would like to know what the construction looks like in that discrete setting, whether anything of substance survives the loss of the manifold, and whether your answer there ought to shift the credence you gave above.
