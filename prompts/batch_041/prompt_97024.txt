i'm running a small reading group on analogy-making this term and we've spent way too long inside hofstadter's copycat — you know the kind of puzzle, abc goes to abd so what does ijk go to. what got us is that the program has no rule in it. it has a pile of half-lit concepts and a randomness dial, so it comes back with ijl on one run and ijd on another (sometimes even abd), and none of those is a bug, because the last letter goes to its successor and the last letter becomes d are the same instruction on the first pair and two different instructions on the second. the concept slides from a relation to a thing. thats what i want the last session to end on, but i dont want a poem that talks about it — i want one that does it.

so: a poem, somewhere in the 20-30 line range, no fixed form, set in one tiny unglamorous microdomain that stays put the whole way (a table setting, a shelf of tins, a drawer of batteries — five or six objects, all visible at once, the way three letters are). one line has to come back three or four times, word for word identical, and each time it has to be doing different work, until on the last pass you can read it two ways at once and the poem refuses to say which. the slip has to happen in the line and in what the lines around it have quietly set up — no explaining, no footnote. and if you can swing it, make the final stanza a re-run of the first with one thing moved, so i have to decide whether its still the same poem.

keep the vocabulary out of it: no concept, no slippage, no analogy, no fluid, no hofstadter, no letters. i want people to feel the slip without the jargon. it gets read aloud at the last session so it has to work in the ear, and give it a title that doesnt give the game away.

after the poem, a short note, not an essay, separating what people who actually work on this agree on from what they genuinely fight about — especially whether a deeper slip is really better or only prettier, and whether this is its own mechanism or just context-sensitivity and metaphor wearing a lab coat. if the poem has to lean on one side of a live disagreement to work, tell me which side, and dont dress it up as settled.
