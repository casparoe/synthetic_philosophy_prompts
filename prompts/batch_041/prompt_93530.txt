I'm a philosophy prof who keeps getting dragged into arguments with the statisitics people in my department, and I'm trying to write a lecture on the frequentist/Bayesian split and the replication crisis that doesn't just recycle the usual slogans. Here's the framing I've been using, and I want you to run with it. Call it the inference-audit gap. Frequentist methods are calibrated by construction — their guarantee is about long-run error rates — but that calibration doesn't tell you what to believe about any particular study. Bayesian methods do tell you what to believe about a particular study, conditional on a model and a prior, but their calibration is only as good as the model and prior, which are exactly the things that replication failures call into question. So the gap is this: the property that makes an inference auditable, namely frequentist calibration, isn't the property that makes it actionable, namely a posterior, and the replication crisis lives in the space between them.

Please describe the most important implications of taking this gap seriously for four things: (1) how journals should evaluate replication attempts, (2) whether preregistration and multiverse analysis are frequentist fixes, Bayesian fixes, or neither, (3) how a working scientist should read a single p-value and a single Bayes factor, and (4) what a graduate methods course should actually teach. I don't want a bibliography or a reading list — that's not the point — and I don't want the lazy answer that it all depends on the prior. I want the conceptual implications, including where the distinction collapses and where the two frameworks are forced to make the same practical concessions. Also, if you think my framing is confused, say so and fix it rather than humoring me.
