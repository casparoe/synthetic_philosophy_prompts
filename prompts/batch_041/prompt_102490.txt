I am preparing the question-period strategy for a paper I will present to an analytic epistemology reading group, and I would like you to draft my Q&A prep sheet.

The thesis I will defend is that verbal testimony (śabda) is a basic source of knowledge — a pramāṇa — on the same footing as perception and inference, rather than something derivable from them. Three planks carry the argument. (1) Testimonial uptake is a distinct cognitive route: a hearer who understands an utterance thereby comes to know the fact asserted, with no perceptual or inferential step interposed between the utterance and the fact. (2) For the resulting cognition to count as knowledge, the hearer must possess a standing, usually unreflective awareness of the speaker's competence and sincerity (the speaker must be an āpta); I maintain that this is a condition on a distinctive route, not a reduction of testimony to inference, because the hearer does not infer the asserted fact from premises about the speaker's reliability. (3) I accept a domain-separation principle, on which each pramāṇa has its own proper object-domain, so the very cognition produced by testimony is not also produced by inference; I use this to argue that analogical knowledge (upamāna — knowing what a thing is through its resemblance to something already familiar, as one might learn what a gavaya is from its likeness to a cow) is a source in its own right and not disguised inference.

The audience is a dozen people. Most are epistemologists who work on testimony and treat the reductionist/non-reductionist divide as the natural frame for any view in the area. Two work on epistemic injustice and testimonial silencing. One philosopher of language will want to know why this is not simply a Gricean, intention-based account of communication. One historian of philosophy reads Sanskrit but works on a different tradition. No one present works on Indian philosophy.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense: the objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by likelihood, each phrased the way a member of this audience would phrase it and tagged with who is most likely to raise it, each with a short reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede at the outset before defending anything, and close with the one question I am hoping nobody asks.

Format: an ordered list, most damaging first. Open with a single sentence stating what I should concede up front; then, for each objection, one sentence stating it as my questioner would, followed by a reply of no more than two sentences and a one-line note on its soft spot; end with the question I hope nobody asks. The whole response must be under 900 words.
