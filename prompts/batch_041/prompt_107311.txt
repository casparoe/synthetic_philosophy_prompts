Lying vs. misleading, an attempt to settle it

A lie, on the standard account, is a statement you believe to be false that you make with the intention of getting someone to believe it. Misleading is when you get someone to believe something false without ever saying anything false — by implying it, by leaving it out, by burying it, whatever. So the lie attacks the truth head on and the misleading sidesteps it.

Most people, my friend included, think the lie is worse. The reasoning goes: with a lie I've done the damage myself, I've put a false belief into your head; with a misleading I've only left you to draw your own conclusion, so some of the responsibility is yours.

I think that's mostly wrong, at least for the implicature cases. When I answer your question with something literally true but obviously meant to point you the wrong way, I'm not sitting back and letting you infer — I'm relying on you to infer, and I've picked the words precisely because I know what you'll infer from them. That's engineering, not omission. And it's worse than a straight lie in one respect: you've not only made me believe something false, you've made me believe it while I think you're being straightforward with me. The deception is doubled — one layer about the facts, another about what you're up to with your words. A plain lie doesn't do that second layer, it just lies. So the "you did it to yourself" defense is hollow, and in some cases the misleading is the bigger insult.

Where I'll give my friend something: there's a real asymmetry in the pure omission case. If you just don't mention something and I assume wrongly, you haven't put anything in my head, you've just let a false belief sit there. And letting a false belief sit there is often fine — nobody owes everyone the full truth all the time, otherwise you'd never get off the phone with your aunt.

So the line that actually matters isn't lie/mislead, it's whether the person had a right to the truth in that context. Grice's maxims are basically rules you're required to follow once you've started talking, and they're what make misleading work in the first place — I can only exploit your expectation that I'm being relevant and complete because you're entitled to that expectation. The law draws its bright line at literal falsity because it needs something it can prove, but morality can just look at the relationship.

So lying and misleading aren't morally different in kind. Both are breaches of the same thing, which is the honesty you owe someone who's taking you at your word.
