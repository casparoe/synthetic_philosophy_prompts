I recently read a primer on the philosophy of mind written for readers with a neuroscience background, and its chapter on the causal exclusion argument ends with a summary that I keep turning over. I am not a philosopher, and I do not want to dismiss the chapter out of hand: the exclusion problem strikes me as a genuine difficulty, and I am prepared to grant that non-reductive physicalism cannot have everything it wants. But the conclusion that my desires cause nothing at all seems to me far more than the argument can carry, and I cannot locate the step that gets there. The passage runs as follows:

"Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument establishes that mental causation is an illusion. The reasoning is compact. Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause; this is the causal closure of the physical, and it is the settled verdict of the physical sciences. Mental properties supervene on physical properties: fix the brain and you have fixed the mind. But if a mental property is genuinely distinct from the physical property it supervenes on, then the neural event that produces my arm's rising is already fully caused by its physical predecessor, and the mental cause is at best a redundant addition. Nothing is caused twice over in that way, so the mental cause is excluded and the mental property is left with no work to do. Kim therefore concludes to epiphenomenalism: our thoughts and desires cause nothing at all. He concedes that a mental property may inherit whatever causal powers its physical base has, but on his picture this makes the mental a dependent, second-hand cause, no more a genuine cause than a shadow is a genuine shaper of the tree that casts it. The only remaining escapes are eliminativism, which denies that the mental exists, or dualism, which assigns the mental a realm of its own. Kim also notes that anyone persuaded by the argument should stop speaking of the causal powers of beliefs, economies, and genes, and he treats this generalization as a dividend of the view rather than a cost."

Please audit this claim by claim. Which of its claims would a philosopher working on mental causation accept as fair? Which are the ordinary simplifications a specialist would wince at but let pass? Which are wrong, and wrong in a way that matters for the conclusion? Where the passage reports what Kim holds or concludes, check the reporting against the position he actually defends, and say what he and his defenders would object to. Where it states a premise as though the premise needed no defense — the closure of physics, the ban on being caused twice over — say whether it does need one, and what work that premise is doing in the argument.

Then tell me what the argument, stated carefully, actually establishes. If it does not establish the conclusion the passage attributes to it, say what it does establish and for whom. I would also like to know whether the dilemma at the end — dualism, eliminativism, or nothing — is a real consequence of the argument or an artifact of this particular gloss, and whether the generalizing of the argument to economies and genes is Kim's own position or his critics' chief complaint. I am not looking for a reading list; I want the diagnosis and the positions.
