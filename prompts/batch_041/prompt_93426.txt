I sit on the medical-necessity appeals panel for our provincial health plan -- not the glamorous end of ethics, the kind where you spend a Tuesday evening reading forty pages about somebody's BMI. We've been fairly consistent over the years, and lately I've started to wonder whether we're consistent for the reason I think we are. Here's the track record as best I can summarize it. Growth hormone: covered for a nine-year-old with a confirmed deficiency, denied for a nine-year-old who is perfectly healthy but in the bottom percentile for height. Testosterone: covered for a man with two confirmed low morning levels and symptoms, denied for a 48-year-old sitting at the bottom of the normal range who reports fatigue and no libido. Bariatric surgery: covered at BMI 44 with type 2 diabetes, denied at BMI 33 with nothing else wrong and a goal of losing thirty pounds. Ritalin: covered with a diagnosis, denied for a healthy undergrad who wants to study longer. Cochlear implant for a profoundly deaf infant: covered, no controversy. But we also covered a double mastectomy with reconstruction for a 34-year-old BRCA1 carrier who has never had cancer, and we denied a very expensive oncology drug that buys about six weeks of life. Everyone on the panel feels these hang together, and I suspect we'd all be embarrassed if you asked us to state the rule in one sentence.

Next month's case: a 74-year-old woman with no diagnosis of anything, who has lost muscle and strength over the past decade and now sits just inside the normal range for her age on the plan's own grip-strength and lean-mass cutoff -- a measure we only started using last year for a different purpose. She has fallen once in the past year, no fracture, no other conditions. The drug is a monthly injection, around $9,000 a year; in the trial it added about a kilo and a half of lean mass and cut falls by roughly a third over two years, which the pharmacy people translate as an NNT of about 40. The same drug is being sold in private clinics to healthy 35-year-olds who want to look more muscular, and it works on them too, which a couple of my collegues think is the entire ballgame and a couple of others think is beside the point.

So: predict what we'll do with her, and tell me which rule we're actually running -- not just "medical necessity," I mean the actual criterion. Then give me the other rules that fit everything above just as well, what each of them predicts here, and the one case that would tell them apart. If we genuinely haven't decided and our precedents underdetermine it, I'd much rather hear that than a confident answer, and in that case tell me what principle we'd be adopting if we cover her and what we'd be adopting if we don't.
