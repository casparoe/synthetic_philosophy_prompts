I teach philosophy at a mid-sized university, and for two years now I have met monthly with a colleague from the law faculty to argue about precommitment — Ulysses at the mast, commitment contracts, entrenchment clauses, and the rest of it — and her verdicts have become the fixed points of our conversation. She always gives reasons, but not always the same reasons, which is part of my difficulty. She has now been asked to comment on a live case, our seminar takes it up the week after next, and I would like to know her answer before I have to say anything about it myself. Her record is as follows. On Ulysses bound to the mast she holds that the binding is conclusive, and that the crew must not release him however he pleads. On a commitment contract in which an alcoholic deposits a large sum with a friend, forfeit to a charity on any drink, she holds that the arrangement is legitimate and should be enforced against his later pleading. On a competent patient's advance directive refusing artificial nutrition and hydration in the event of advanced dementia, she holds that it must be honored even when the later patient appears content or objects. On a constitutional entrenchment of expression, habeas corpus, and equal protection, amendable only by two-thirds majorities in two successive legislatures, she holds the entrenchment legitimate. On a competent adult's irrevocable covenant never to leave his religious order, never to marry outside it, and to forfeit his whole estate if he does, she holds that it is not a precommitment at all and should not be enforced. On a city charter provision entrenching a particular zoning schedule against ordinary amendment, she holds the entrenchment illegitimate.

The new case is this. A state adopted by referendum thirty years ago a bill of rights together with a provision requiring that any amendment to the bill of rights be preceded by a two-year period of public deliberation and a randomly selected citizens' assembly, and then carried by three-fifths of those voting in a referendum. The provision entrenches itself: it can be changed only by that same route. The assembly has now, after four years of unusually thorough public argument, voted by a wide margin to recommend repealing the requirement as an obstacle to ordinary self-government, and the referendum on repeal passes with fifty-eight per cent — short of three-fifths. The question put to my colleague is whether the requirement is binding, so that the fifty-eight per cent majority is simply defeated, or whether a majority of that size, having deliberated as thoroughly as the requirement was meant to make it deliberate, may treat the entrenchment as no longer authoritative.

Tell me what she will say about the new case, and — what I actually care about — the rule she is running, the rival rules that fit every verdict above just as well, and which case, hers or mine, would best separate those rules from one another. Lead with your prediction in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. I am not asking you to survey a literature; I want the most defensible reconstruction of her pattern plus a verdict on the new case, and I want to know what would change your mind — which consideration, if it turned out to be so, would move you to the opposite prediction, and where your reconstruction is likeliest to break down.
