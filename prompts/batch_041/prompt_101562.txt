I've been trying to work out what I actually think about standing to blame by going case by case on gut reactions, and I've got a pile of them now. What I want is the principle, if there is one, that fits the pile — and an honest note on which of my gut reactions it forces me to give up. Here's the pile:

- My brother has smoked twenty a day for years and isn't stopping. He tells me I ought to quit because it'll kill me. That seems completely fine, no whiff of a problem.
- Same brother, same chat, except he says my smoking makes me a bad dad. That lands wrong — I don't think he gets to say that, true or not.
- A colleague who got caught fiddling his expenses five years ago, paid it back and apologised at the time, now sits on the panel pulling up someone else for fiddling expenses. Feels fine to me.
- A colleague caught fiddling expenses last month, still at it, on the same panel. Not fine.
- My mate — the one who introduced me to the bloke who stiffed us both — tells me not to go and stiff him back. That feels out of order from him, even though he's right about the stiffing.
- A total stranger on the internet tells me my diet is immoral because of the animals. Out of order.
- My partner, who eats meat every day, tells me my meat-eating is immoral. Also out of order, though a different flavour of out-of-order.
- My partner, who eats meat every day, tells me my meat-eating is wrecking my cholesterol. Fine.
- Someone who's never met me and has never done the thing I'm doing tells me, privately and unprompted, that what I'm doing is wrong. Grating.
- Same person, same view, but said in public to a room, as a general point rather than aimed at me. Seems fine.

And the one I keep going back and forth on: a man who beat his wife for years, now reformed, tells a younger man in the pub he shouldn't hit his girlfriend. I want to say that's fine — someone who's been there might be exactly who should say it — but I also want to say he forfeited that particular card for good.

So: is there one account of standing here that gets most of these, and what does it have to say about cases I haven't listed — someone blaming their dead father, say, or a whole generation blaming the one before it? Tell me which of my verdicts the tidiest principle makes me drop. If the best you can do is two rival principles, say so and tell me what turns on the choice. I'd definately like the answer in British English.
