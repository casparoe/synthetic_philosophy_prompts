You are a philosopher of science whose research concerns the epistemology of thought experiments — the question of whether, and how, an experiment conducted only in the imagination can deliver knowledge of the world — and you are also competent in the history of physics, in theoretical biology, and in moral philosophy. Please work carefully and in stages, showing your reasoning at each stage rather than jumping to a verdict.

I am assembling teaching materials on thought experiments in science, and I want a defensible ordering of a small set of cases by how much independent epistemic yield each one has. By that phrase I mean the following, and here I need you to be exact: some thought experiments appear to do no more than unpack the consequences of assumptions we already accept, so that they are arguments in vivid costume; others are credited with delivering something we did not previously have — a new possibility, a new constraint, a new sense of what stands in need of explanation. Rank the cases by how much they deliver in the second sense, not by how famous or how pedagogically useful they are.

The cases are these, described in my own words. Where a description departs from any canonical version you may know, treat my description as the object of assessment, and say so if you think the departure matters.

1. The ship and the shore. An observer shut below decks, with no view of the outside, cannot tell by any experiment performed inside whether the ship is at rest or moving uniformly. Only the shore, seen from the deck, reveals the motion.
2. The demon at the door. A very small, very clever agent sorts fast molecules from slow ones as they pass through a hole between two chambers, apparently letting heat flow from cold to hot without any work being done.
3. The falling elevator. An experimenter in a windowless box in free fall finds every local experiment behaving exactly as it would if the box were at rest far from any massive body; and every experiment in a box accelerating upward in empty space behaves exactly as it would in a gravitational field.
4. The cat in the box. A radioactive atom, a detector, and a poison release are arranged so that a cat's death or survival is correlated with a quantum event that has not been resolved; before anyone looks, is the cat alive, dead, or neither?
5. The violinist. You wake to find yourself, without your consent, surgically attached to a violinist whose life depends on the use of your body for nine months; you may detach at any time, at the cost of his life.
6. The vanishing pollinator. An ecologist reasons about a meadow from which a single pollinating insect species is removed: because several of the meadow's plants have no other pollinator, the reasoning concludes that the plant community collapses within a few seasons. The removal is never performed, and for practical and ethical reasons never will be.
7. The cold start. A researcher on the origin of life asks whether the same prebiotic chemistry that plausibly occurred on the early Earth would have assembled the same kind of self-replicating system in a solvent with different properties. No such planet is observable, so the question is pursued entirely by reasoning about the chemistry as described.

Now proceed as follows.

Stage 1 — Brainstorm before you rank anything. Set out in a short paragraph what would have to be true for a thought experiment to count as yielding independent knowledge, and list the candidate criteria you considered (for instance: that the experiment be impossible or inadmissible to run; that it draw on empirical knowledge rather than stipulation; that it change what we take to be possible rather than what follows from what is possible; that its outcome could coherently have gone the other way). State which criterion you adopt and why.

Stage 2 — Rank the seven cases into tiers, three or four tiers, from highest to lowest epistemic yield. Give each case a justification of no more than two or three sentences, tied explicitly to the criterion you adopted.

Stage 3 — Re-sort the cases under a second criterion: yield in conceptual clarification of an already accepted theory, rather than in knowledge of the world. Note where the order changes, and say what the change reveals about the first ordering.

Stage 4 — Identify the placements you regard as genuinely arguable, and for each of the two lowest-rated cases, describe the single modification to the case that would move it up a tier.

Stage 5 — Two of the seven cases are of my own devising rather than drawn from the literature. Say which two you believe they are, and why; then say whether you rated the invented cases differently from the inherited ones, and whether that difference is defensible or an artifact of familiarity.

Present each tier list as an ordered list with tier labels, and keep the whole response under about 1,200 words.
