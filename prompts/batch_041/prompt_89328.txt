I'm retired now — I spent thirty-odd years on invertebrate behaviour (ants mostly, plus a long and slightly embarrassing detour into cuttlefish) and since I stopped running experiments I've been reading philosophy, badly but enthusiastically. I've got a comparison question I keep failing to answer for myself.

Thing one is Descartes on animals, the beast-machine business. Animals are just very well-built machines: no thought, no language, they don't act from knowledge, they just go through the motions. What I've never been able to settle is wether he thought they feel anything at all, or whether the yelping and the writhing is meant to be pure mechanism with nobody home to mind it.

Thing two is an idea a friend of mine who does computational neuroscience keeps pushing, and I don't think it has a name. Roughly: there is no single mind in a brain. There's a pile of semi-independent systems — hunger, fear, pain-avoidance, mating drive, whatever — each with it's own feel-good/feel-bad setting and its own little agenda, and what we call a mind is just whatever coordination happens to occur between them. No boss, no one unified subject of experience, just a parliament that mostly doesn't manage to vote.

What I'd like is a careful comparison of these two. Are they actually rivals, or is the second just a fancier, more respectable way of saying the first? If I say "there are six little feelers and no big one", have I made the animal's inner life richer or made it vanish — is a bundle of valences something that can be harmed, or is it closer to Descartes than to anything we'd normally call a mind? Which of the two is more deflationary about animal experience overall, and does that flip depending on whether we're talking about a cuttlefish, a dog, or a person? Each view should have something to say about why the organism nonetheless behaves as one thing at the dinner table — I'd like to know whether either can explain that integration without smuggling a subject back in through the side door. And what do the two imply for the animal-welfare questions I actually care about: if the pain is real but unowned, is that still suffering in the sense that matters, and does the answer differ between the two views?

Also tell me where I'm cheating. I suspect I keep sliding between "no mind" and "no unified mind" as though they were the same claim, and I'm fairly sure they aren't.
