My seminar leader has been handing down rulings all term on which sayings in the gospels go back to Jesus, and I still can't work out what method she's running — she's never stated one and gets testy when asked. Her rulings so far:

- a short line that turns up in two of the strands we treat as independent, in which the disciples come off as clueless, and which would have been awkward for the movement a generation later: she says it's his.
- a line that survives in only one place, in a source everyone agrees is late, where he says something the later church would much rather he hadnt: she says it's his.
- a line in two independent strands that embarrasses nobody but is like nothing in the Jewish material or the early Christian material we have: she says it's his.
- a line in one late source that fits neatly into a second-century argument the church was having about its own internal order: she says it isn't his.
- a line in one late source that any pious author of the period could have composed to fill a gap in the narrative: she says it isn't his.

Next week's is a line that survives in a single late source, that nobody would find awkward or embarrassing, but that is genuinely unlike anything in the surrounding Jewish or early Christian worlds — it doesnt sound like the synagogue and it doesnt sound like the church. Will she call it his? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her five rulings just as well, and the one further case that would separate them. Then step back for me: if the method really comes down to whatever this is, does any of it get us closer to who he was, or is it just a machine for putting our own priors in his mouth?
