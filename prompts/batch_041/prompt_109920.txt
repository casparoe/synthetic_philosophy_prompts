Okay, I want you to take on a role here: you're a philosopher of probability who has also spent a couple of years sitting in on actuarial rate filings, so you can talk about credences and reference classes but you also know how a car insurance premium actually gets built. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm on paper: list four or five different positions someone might hold about what a "risk class" is and what it's for, and say which of them a pricing practice could plausibly be running on. Then give me your actual answer. No reading list please, I can't look anything up anyway -- just argue it out.

Here's the practice. When a US insurer prices your auto policy, it sorts you into a bunch of classes and charges you the going rate for whatever cell you land in. Age, sex (in most states), marital status, whether you own or rent, your occupation, your credit-based insurance score, your prior claims, the car, how many miles you say you drive, and -- a big one -- where you live, sometimes down to the census block. Race is illegal to use anywhere. But zip code is fine in most states even though it's a decent proxy for race, and a few states have banned credit scoring outright while most let it through. Health insurance in the US runs on the opposite regime: insurers basically can't charge you more for being sick or for your family's medical history, and can only move the price on age, smoking, geography and family size. GINA, the 2008 genetic nondiscrimination law, is why health insurers can't touch your genetic tests or your family medical history -- but life insurers aren't covered by it and still ask about both. In the EU insurers have been barred since the end of 2012 from charging men and women different premiums, while over here life insurers still do it.

Two more wrinkles. Insurance is supposed to be about pooling risk, but the more finely you classify people the less pooling there is; push it to the limit and everybody pays their own expected loss and there's no insurance left, just a bet. And the industry gets squeezed from both directions -- regulators say "classify too finely and you're discriminating," actuaries say "classify too coarsely and the good risks leave and the market unravels."

What I want to know:

(1) What does this whole practice presuppose about reference classes? Does it assume there's a fact of the matter about your "true" risk -- some propensity sitting there waiting for the right class to reveal it -- or does it treat your risk as nothing over and above the rate in whatever class somebody decided to put you in? If it's the latter, what's supposed to make one class choice better than another?

(2) What theory of unfair discrimination does the practice presuppose? Look at the actual line it draws: race no, zip code yes; genes and family history off-limits in health but fair game in life; sex no in the EU, age fine everywhere. Is that line principled, or is it a political settlement wearing a statistical costume? Try to say which single criterion, if any, explains the pattern -- immutability, lack of control, "morally protected" status, causal versus merely correlational, or something about which factors it's decent to make a person's price turn on.

(3) Seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Where is it underdetermined, i.e. which rival theories of risk and of fairness would license alot of the same rules?

(4) Finally, suppose you swap the presupposed view for a hard-line rival: the reference class is a policy choice all the way down and "actuarial fairness" has no content of its own. What concretely has to change in how policies get priced?

Be concrete, and don't hedge everything -- tell me which position you actually think the practice is running on.
