I teach a cross-listed graduate seminar on the ethics of algorithmic decision-making — half philosophers, half students from the computational social science program — and I am trying to write down my own position on statistical fairness in one place for the first time, as a handout for the seminar and, if it survives, as a short position piece. A colleague's parting shot last Thursday was that my view is coherent only because it has never been written down in one place, and I suspect she may be right. So before I commit anything to paper I want an audit, not a survey of the literature and not a defence of any particular criterion. Here is the position, as compactly as I can state it. (a) *Score calibration within groups*: for each protected group, among those assigned the same risk score, the same fraction should actually have the outcome — the number should mean the same thing for everyone it is assigned to. (b) *Predictive parity for verdicts*: among those the policy approves, the same fraction should genuinely be eligible in each group — a yes should mean the same thing whoever receives it. I have been treating (a) and (b) as two faces of a single commitment, which I have been calling *verdict integrity*. (c) *Equalized odds*: the probability of a false alarm and the probability of a miss should be the same across groups; nobody's group should alter their chance of being wrongly decided. (d) I accept the familiar impossibility — when the groups' base rates differ, no nontrivial rule can have both (b) and (c) — and I have ranked them: a common meaning for verdicts first, matching error rates second. (e) *No fairness without a use*: a fairness claim is a claim about a decision rule together with a loss function, not about a score in the abstract; a score with no specified use is neither fair nor unfair, only accurate or inaccurate. (f) In the domains I work in — policing, credit, allocation of scarce benefits — the outcome variable is usually a proxy for an unjust social arrangement (arrests standing in for offending, past spending standing in for need), so the principled response to an unpalatable trade-off is to change the target rather than to optimize parity criteria against a target that should not be trusted. (g) The metric is not mine to set: where similarity-based or individual notions of fairness are at stake, the similarity metric has to come from the affected community, and my job is to satisfy whatever metric they hand me, not to choose it on their behalf.

What I want is a consistency audit of this set: which commitments pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and for each genuine conflict what the least costly repair is, with an explicit statement of what that repair costs. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and do not stop at the pair I have already conceded — I want to know whether the way I have handled it is itself consistent with everything else I have said. I am not asking you to adjudicate which criterion is correct in the abstract, and I am not asking for the state of the debate; I am asking what my own package commits me to and where it cannot be made to hold together.

Answer with exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no preamble, no closing summary, no reassurance. Each item should name the pair or cluster of commitments by letter, say in one or two sentences what the conflict is and what would have to give, and the items should run from the most serious conflict to the least; if a pair that looks like a conflict turns out not to be one, put it where it belongs in the order and use the item to say why it dissolves rather than contradicting the rest.
