I work as a software engineer — compilers, mostly, with a bit of proof-assistant tooling on the side — and I have no formal training in philosophy, so I apologise in advance if I misuse any terminology. For the better part of a year a colleague of mine, who read mathematics at university and is a convinced adherent of the Lucas–Penrose line, and I, who lean much more towards the mechanist side without being able to defend it properly, have been going round in circles over Thursday-evening coffee about whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the human mind cannot be a machine. We have finally agreed to do it properly, and I would like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two camps — the anti-mechanist who holds that the theorems, together with something about our mathematical insight, refute mechanism, and the mechanist who holds that they do nothing of the sort. What I want is the document we could both put our names to without either of us feeling that our own position had been caricatured. I should say at the outset that I do not want anything resting on who published what and when: no reading list, no references, no appeals to authority, since neither of us can check them and they would only paper over the argument. Argue from the arguments themselves.

Concretely, I would like the document to open with a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign — about what the incompleteness theorems actually say and what they do not say, about the gap between provability in a formal system and cognition, about what would count as exhibiting a machine that does what a mathematician does, and about whether either side's position is empirical, unfalsifiable, or partly a matter of words — and I would ask that each agreed claim be substantive rather than a truism, and that where the agreement turns out to be merely verbal you say so instead of recording it as agreement. After that, give the residual disagreements as a table: each row a crux, stated first in a neutral formulation, then each side's own preferred formulation of its view in wording that side would accept, then the case, argument, or thought experiment both sides agree would bear on that crux, and finally each side's prediction about how that case would come out. Some candidate cruxes, though I would like you to add, merge, or drop rows as the arguments require: whether the anti-mechanist is entitled to assume that the mathematician is consistent, or sound, given that we make mistakes; whether the argument has to idealise the human mathematician, and whether the idealisation is doing illegitimate work; whether theorems about provability can be transferred to claims about knowledge or computation without an extra premise, and what that premise is; whether the anti-mechanist's conclusion is really about all machines at once or only one machine at a time; and how the anti-mechanist proposes to handle the disjunctive conclusion Gödel himself appears to have drawn — either the mind outruns every machine, or there are questions that are absolutely undecidable for it — which horn does the anti-mechanist need, and can the other be ruled out? Please also state, for each side, what evidence or argument would actually change its mind, as opposed to merely embarrassing it.

There is one thing in particular I would like the document to do that our own arguments never managed, namely to take a concrete case and write both predictions down in advance. Here is the set-up I have in mind, and you may adjust it if it is ill-formed, since I am a programmer rather than a logician. Take a machine that proves arithmetic. Build a second machine by adding to the first the sentence saying that the first is consistent; build a third by adding to the second the sentence saying the second is consistent; and so on indefinitely, so that every machine in the sequence can prove the consistency of the one before it. The anti-mechanist's claim, as I understand it, is that a human mathematician shown this whole sequence can see something true that no machine in the sequence proves — a sentence sitting at the limit, so to speak — whereas the mechanist's reply is that the limit of the sequence is itself just one more formal theory, that the human's apparent insight amounts to assuming the consistency of that theory, and that the same construction then begins again one level up, so that nothing at all follows about human minds. I want both sides to record in the document what they predict about this case and what would show them wrong; if the mechanist's reply is sound, I want the anti-mechanist to say what is left of the original argument, and if the anti-mechanist is right, I want the mechanist to say which premise would have to go. Finally, say something brief about the second, quite separate Penrose argument, the one concerning physics and the brain — whether it stands or falls independently of the first — but do not let it swallow the rest of the document.
