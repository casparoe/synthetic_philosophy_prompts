For the past six years I have sat on the board of a small housing co-operative — thirty-eight households in a converted warehouse, the sort of place where the same dozen people turn up to every meeting and where the rules are actually enforced, because we are all one another's landlords. Two weeks ago our treasurer told the board that over the past two years she had moved a little over forty thousand dollars of the maintenance fund into a personal account. The money is gone, and she has no way of repaying it. Our rules are unambiguous: any member who misappropriates the co-operative's funds is expelled, and expulsion means losing both her right to live here and the buy-in she paid on joining, which for our long-standing members is a considerable sum. The board votes on Tuesday and I have not made up my mind.

What makes this hard is an offer from another member. She has lived here for twenty years and is, by common consent, the most decent person in the building. She has asked us to expel her in the treasurer's place and to record the theft as hers: she would sign the statement, forfeit her own buy-in — which is larger than the sum taken, so the fund would actually be made whole — and go to live with her sister in another city. Her reason is not closeness to the treasurer; it is that the treasurer has two children at the school on our street and nowhere else to go. The treasurer, to my surprise, wants to accept. She insists she is not asking to escape the shame, only to keep her children in their school. Two of my fellow board members have already decided. One says that since everyone involved agrees and the co-operative ends up no worse off, refusing the offer is mere sentimentality, and that the point of the rule is to answer the wrong, which this does. The other says that an expulsion is not a sum of money that can be settled by whoever happens to pay it, that we would be entering a falsehood in the minutes and announcing it to the membership, and that it would wrong everyone who has ever paid into the fund. There is also a practical worry I cannot shake: if we accept this once, the rule becomes something a member can buy her way out of, provided she has a generous friend.

I have to vote, and I would rather vote for a reason than out of sympathy for either woman. Can a penalty imposed on one person be borne, justly, by another who volunteers for it — and if it can, what exactly has to be true for the transfer to be legitimate? Is there something about this particular arrangement — the deception of the other members, the pressure the treasurer is under on her children's account, the fact that the volunteer is a good person while the person who did the wrong goes entirely unpunished — that makes it different in kind from simply letting someone pay a friend's fine? And how much should it matter that the people we would be deceiving are precisely the people the rule exists to serve? Tell me what you think the right vote is, and defend it.
