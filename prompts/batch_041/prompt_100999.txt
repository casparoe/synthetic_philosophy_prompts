You are an expert in classical Indian epistemology with a specialist's command of Nyāya and its theory of the pramāṇas; please reason carefully, and before you commit to an answer, lay out in your own words the candidate principles that could be doing the work here, because I care more about the principles than about the verdict. A friend of mine, who wrote her dissertation on Navya-Nyāya and reads the texts in Sanskrit, has been giving me verdicts on a series of cases I have been putting to her. She says: (1) seeing smoke on the hill and concluding there is fire is knowledge, and she calls it inference; (2) someone who is in a position to know and is known to be trustworthy tells me it is raining in the next village, and this is knowledge, which she calls knowledge from testimony; (3) having been told in advance that a certain forest animal resembles a cow, I later see such an animal and know that this is what the word I was taught applies to, and she calls this knowledge, obtained by comparison; (4) I look at the table and see that there is no pot on it, and she calls this knowledge as well, insisting it is simply perception and that no fifth instrument is needed for absences; (5) I recall yesterday's perception of the pot, and she says flatly that this is not knowledge, since memory is not a source of knowledge; (6) I cannot tell whether the thing in the distance is a post or a man, and she says I do not know; (7) I take a rope for a snake, and she says I do not know. She gives a reason in each case, and the reasons do not always look like the same reason.

The case I want to put to her next is this: by long observation I have become certain that a particular colleague never eats during the day and is nevertheless stout, and I conclude that he must eat at night. There is no general principle I could cite for this conclusion, since the only evidence for the claim that whoever is stout and eats nothing by day eats at night is the very case in front of me; I cannot rest the conclusion on a recognized pervasion the way I can rest the fire on the smoke. Please tell me (a) what verdict she will give and what she will say the source of the cognition is, and whether she will treat it as knowledge that simply is not a distinct source or as something that is not knowledge at all; (b) the rule she is implicitly running, stated as precisely as you can; (c) at least one other rule that fits every one of her earlier verdicts just as well, and exactly where the two rules part company; (d) what each rule predicts about my colleague, with your best guess about which rule she is really using and your reasons for preferring it; and (e) the further case — something other than the ones I have already given her — that would most cleanly separate the rules, and what each rule would say about it. Where her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so rather than manufacturing a definite answer.
