I host a small monthly reading group for people who like arguing about formal epistemology and AI, and last session we spent two hours on an argument I have not been able to stop thinking about. The argument, as best I can reconstruct it, is this. Solomonoff induction, the ideal predictor that puts a prior over all computable hypotheses with weight falling off as two to the minus the length of the hypothesis's shortest program, is usually praised as a formalization of Occam's razor. But the weight is assigned to programs, not to what the programs do, and very short programs can do astronomically much: a few lines of code can enumerate every program and run each one, or specify a machine that runs for an unfathomable number of steps, and so can generate an unfathomable number of minds, most of them embedded in worlds with nothing to do with ours. So a weighting that looks like a preference for small, boring hypotheses ends up concentrating the prior's measure on hypotheses of maximal computational fecundity, worlds stuffed with observers, and stuffed in particular with observers in states we would call bad, since nothing in the weighting penalizes a program for producing suffering rather than flourishing. The consequence, if you take it seriously, is that a consequentialist reasoning under this prior finds the expected value of everything dominated by populations she cannot reach or affect, and finds herself one computable process among infinitely many copies of herself that the prior already contains; the prior is malign in this sense rather than neutral. We argued about whether this is a real problem, an artifact of measuring by observer-moments, or a confusion between measure over programs and measure over worlds, and we did not settle it.

For next month I would like to open the session with a joke. Please write one joke about this argument: either about the prior being malign, or about what it is like to be a consequentialist who has noticed that the universal prior already contains infinitely many copies of her and that the expected value she is trying to maximize is swamped by simulations she has never heard of. It has to be funny to people who have actually worked through the setup, which means the punchline must turn on the substance of the argument, on the simplicity weighting versus the computational fecundity of short programs, or on the arithmetic of being outnumbered by your own copies, or on the fact that no short program is penalized for the suffering it generates, rather than being a generic joke about nerds, robots, or computers doing math. Keep it short enough to be told aloud to a group of eight. One further constraint, because the group is pedantic and will heckle: the joke must not personify the prior as a malicious agent choosing our fate, and must not imply that the prior assigns probability to a hypothesis because that hypothesis is bad; the malignity has to come out as a consequence of the weighting, not as intent.

After the joke, add two or three sentences explaining which part of the argument the punchline depends on and why the joke would fail for someone who had misread that part, so that I can tell whether it was built on the real mechanism or on a plausible-sounding misreading. Do not worry about citing any literature, and do not name authors or papers; everything the joke needs is in the setup above, and if some detail of the setup seems wrong or underdescribed to you, say so briefly and write the joke against the version you think is defensible.
