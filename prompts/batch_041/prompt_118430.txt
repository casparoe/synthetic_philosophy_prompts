I've spent the last three days on a jury and we're supposed to vote tomorrow morning, and I still don't know what I actually think, so I'm asking you instead of the other eleven. It's a fraud case, nothing violent, and the prosecution's case is a pile of small things rather than one big thing: a signature on a document, a phone call made from a mast nowhere near where the defendent says he was, a receptionist who's pretty sure she remembers him coming in, an ID from a photo lineup (our foreman says he read somewhere that those are the least reliable evidence there is, which I now can't stop thinking about), a bank transfer with an odd reference number, an email with a turn of phrase that matches his other emails. In closing the prosecutor said the case was a rope and not a chain: no single strand would take your weight, but the rope would. And honestly, that landed. If you asked me about any one of those items on its own, I'd say yes, I'd bet on it, I wouldn't feel reckless.

The problem is what happens when I try to say what I believe out loud. I'm confident the signature is his. I'm confident about the phone records. I'm confident about the receptionist. And I'm also -- separately, in the same breath -- confident that at least one of these is wrong, because a case with this many moving parts always has one thing in it that's off. Those two sentences sit fine next to each other and then turn into a mess the moment I try to hold them at the same time. If I believe each of them, don't I have to believe all of them together? And if I don't believe all of them together, what exactly am I saying when I say I believe each one? One juror says this is just being sensible about the fact that people make mistakes. Another said he never buys a lottery ticket because he knows it won't win, and then admitted that he also knows that somebody's ticket does win, and we all laughed and moved on, which I now think was the wrong reaction.

So: guilty or not guilty, and what does "sure" mean when I say it? I'd genuinely like to know if there's a mistake in my reasoning somewhere, or whether "weigh it as a whole" is just the standard way of papering over the thing I'm stuck on. For what it's worth, the judge's direction was only that I have to be sure the prosecution's account is right, and that I'm allowed to rely on circumstantial evidence; he didn't say anything at all about how sure I have to be about each seperate piece, and I didn't think to ask. Everyone keeps telling me to go with my gut, and my gut is currently filing an objection.
