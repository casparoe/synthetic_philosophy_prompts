My reading group is finishing a term on transitional justice next week — we've done the South African commission that traded amnesty for full disclosure, applicant by applicant, then the blanket amnesties in Argentina that got undone later, then the gacaca courts in Rwanda (that's the shape of it as our group has been telling it, anyway) — and I've been volenteered to bring something to close the last session with. I want it to be a poem, and I want it in the voice of my friend's mother, who spent the better part of a year interpreting at the amnesty hearings. She told me the one thing I can't get out of my head: that her job was to say 'I' for people she'd never met, to say the worst things a person can say in the first person, out loud, in her own voice, and then to do the same for the next applicant, and the one after that. She says the pronouns never entirely came back.

So: a pantoum, and strictly, not a poem with a bit of repetition in it — the second and fourth lines of each quatrain coming back as the first and third of the next, and the final stanza closing the circuit back onto the opening one. Five quatrains if that's what the loop needs. The two lines that keep returning should be the two halves of the bargain: the applicant's admission, which she has to render as 'I' and which therefore arrives in her mouth as her own confession, and the condition hung on it, the full disclosure that buys the amnesty. Each time a line crosses into a new stanza it should change hands — what the applicant says about himself gets said again by the woman who took it down, and by the end the two of them are using the same words and it isn't clear whose they are. Let a line shift its meaning if that's what it takes to survive the repetition; that's the point of choosing the form. Plain spoken register, nothing about never again, and please don't let it resolve into reconciliation, because our session didn't.

Before the poem, tell me straight whether the pantoum is really doing the work I'm claiming for it — whether the passing of lines from mouth to mouth actually maps onto what an interpreter does and what a commission's record is, or whether I'm forcing a shape onto material that doesn't want it; and if I'm forcing it, say what form would do the job better and why. Then the poem. Then, after it, a short note: where it takes liberties with how the hearings actually worked or leans on a contested reading of what amnesty-for-truth was meant to achieve, how confident you are in each of those claims, and which single one you're least sure of. British English throughout, please.
