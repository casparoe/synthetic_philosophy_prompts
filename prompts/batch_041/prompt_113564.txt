I grew up in a household where karma was discussed the way other families discuss the weather, and I have been arguing about it for years with a close friend who teaches yoga and describes herself as spiritual rather than religious. Last month a mutual acquaintance of ours had a genuinely brutal run: a divorce, then a diagnosis, then the collapse of his business. Over dinner my friend said, quietly and without any malice, that you reap what you sow, and when I pressed her she clarified that she did not mean he had done anything to deserve it, only that the universe has a way of returning to you what you put into it. A couple of weeks later I heard the same sentence in a completely different register at a family gathering, where an uncle used it to mean that a cousin's drinking had finally caught up with him, that he had it coming, and that we should stop bailing him out. Same words, and I cannot tell whether the two of them are agreeing, disagreeing, or simply talking past each other. I want a menu of readings rather than a verdict, and I would like you to lay it out as a numbered list I can bring to the next argument.

Take the slogan, in its karmic register, and separate the precise claims it could be making, ordered from the nearly empty to the very strong. Consider at least these axes, and add any I have missed. First, is the world being described as administered — a judge, a ledger, someone keeping score — or as lawful in the way a physical system is lawful, with no one at the controls? Second, is the claim causal or normative: is it just that actions have downstream consequences, including consequences for the agent's habits and reputation (which is nearly a platitude), or is it the much stronger claim that what a person suffers is what they deserve? Third, what does the slogan need to be true about persons? If the reaping happens in a later life, the one who suffers is not, in any ordinary sense, the one who sowed, so either the desert claim is incoherent or it attaches to a stream of experience rather than to a person — tell me which of those is the better move and why. Fourth, is any of this falsifiable? A reading that reinterprets every apparent counterexample as itself a fruit of some earlier sowing seems to make no empirical predictions at all, and I want to know which readings have that shape and which do not. Fifth, does the slogan have to be about intention or about the outward act? Buddhist accounts typically put the moral weight on the intention behind an act rather than on its external results, which sits oddly with an agricultural metaphor about seed and harvest, and I would like to know whether that tension is real or only apparent. As a final complication: I have a half-remembered claim from some reading or lecture that the word began as a term for ritual action — the thing one performs at a sacrifice — and only later came to name a general law of moral cause and effect. I have no idea whether that is right, but if it is, it may matter, because the older sense would make the reaping a matter of ritual efficacy rather than of moral desert.

For each reading, give me the claim stated precisely enough to have a truth value, what it commits you to (a cosmic judge, a causal mechanism, a counterfactual guarantee, a claim about well-being, a claim about moral worth), and a verdict: trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false. Then tell me which reading my friend most likely meant and which my uncle most likely meant, whether any single reading can do both the consoling work and the condemning work, and which reading would actually have to be true for my uncle to be entitled to decline to help. I would also like to know where the popular reading fits — the one on wellness podcasts, where the slogan means that your attitudes and beliefs shape your circumstances — since it sounds like the same claim but seems to be about psychology rather than justice. Finally, if I want the argument at dinner to be about something real rather than about two people's incompatible intuitions, which single reading should I insist we are both talking about, and what would count as evidence for or against it?
