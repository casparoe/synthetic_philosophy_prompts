I'm a science-and-ideas journalist at work on a feature about meritocracy, and I've spent the past few weeks interviewing labor economists and philosophers about whether top earners can meaningfully be said to deserve their market incomes. One economist I spoke with floated an account he calls the "two-baseline" view, and I've been turning it over ever since. On his account, what a person deserves is neither the difference between what the world contains with them in it and what it would contain without them (the counterfactual most people reach for first), nor simply whatever the market happens to pay, but the difference between what they actually produced and what they would have produced in the nearest counterfactual world in which they exerted no effort at all, holding their talents and opportunities fixed. He paired this with a thought experiment he called talent reassignment: imagine that at conception a lottery permutes the productive traits of everyone in a birth cohort while leaving each person's disposition to exert effort untouched. His claim is that, under the two-baseline account, most people who reach the top decile of earners through chosen effort would still deserve at least half of their market income even after such a reshuffle, and he suspects the number is closer to all of it.

I'd like your actual credence that his claim is true, and I want the number decomposed rather than hedged. Please give me a single figure for the proposition that, on the two-baseline account, a top-decile earner who got there through chosen effort deserves at least half of their market income in the pre-institutional sense, and then break that figure into the sub-questions it rests on: the probability that the two-baseline account is the correct analysis of desert at all, as against the competing accounts you think are live (an opportunity- or lifetime-advantage account, a marginal-product entitlement account, a luck-egalitarian view on which desert drops out entirely); the probability that the empirical premise holds, namely that for real top-decile earners chosen effort rather than fixed talent and opportunity drives a large share of the counterfactual gap; the probability that market income actually tracks the two-baseline quantity rather than being set by bargaining power, regulation, network effects, or plain monopsony; and the probability that "desert" is determinate enough to carry a truth value here at all. Assign each a rough number and one line of justification, then show me how they combine.

Finally, tell me which of those terms your final figure is most sensitive to, what evidence or argument would move it most, and whether your answer changes if I drop the "half" and ask only whether the deserved share is greater than zero.
