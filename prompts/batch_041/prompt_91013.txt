I'm a novelist, about two thirds of the way through a big historical thing set in the 1840s, and my protagonist is a doctor who is brilliant and wrong — he has a whole system for why people get sick and it just isn't it. The trouble is I keep catching myself writing him as a man straining toward us, like the real plot is how he almost got there, and a historian friend keeps saying I'm doing Whig history, which she says like it's obviously a sin. Fine, but I want the actual standard, not the scolding. So give me the desiderata: what does any adequate account of how we're supposed to understand and narrate past thought have to deliver? What I need it for, concretely, is a rule that lets me both live inside my characters' world and still say plainly that some of them were hurting people and that some things really did get better.

So: the cases it has to get right — the belief that was reasonable on the evidence of the time and false anyway; the man whose reasoning was lousy but whose conclusion we now accept; the crank his own contemporaries ignored who turned out right; the past critic who was right about us and we're still not listening. The distinctions it can't collapse — false versus unreasonable, explaining someone versus justifying them, a belief being a step toward something versus being aimed at it, the actors' own vocabulary versus whatever vocabulary I the writer need to use. What it has to stay consistent with — that most change isn't experienced as progress by the people living through it, that the losers and the roads not taken were real options and not just scenery, that people's self-descriptions are evidence but not the last word, that sometimes what happened definately didn't have to happen. Give me the case or consideration behind each requirement, and say which ones are genuine requirements and which are just one camp's taste.

Then tell me which pairs on that list can't both be had at once, because I'm fairly sure there's at least one, and I'd bet it's the one about story: a novel needs a shape and an ending and some sense that the thing was going somewhere, and I don't see how that doesn't smuggle back in exactly what your list forbids. If that's a real conflict and not just me being bad at my job, say so, and say which side a writer should give up — and whether a novelist is even bound by the same requirements as a historian, or whether fiction gets a telos on loan. Also: which single requirement on your list does the most damage to how most people actually write about the past?
