I keep running into the idea that the self is really just a story — that what I call me is a center of narrative gravity, the way a center of gravity is a real thing you can point to and do sums with even though there's nothing there but a body. I think I get the analogy and I half believe it, but before I read any more about it I want to know what a theory like that is actually on the hook for. So write me the requirements list.

I mean the full spec: what any theory that says the self is a narrative has to be able to do before I should take it seriously. Which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it can't just flatten, which facts about how people actually work it can't contradict — and behind each one, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than just something I'd like to be true. I'm sure some of these are real requirements and some are me smuggling in what I want the world to be like, so tell me which is which.

Here's the pile of stuff I keep tripping over. Use it, or tell me some of it is irrelevant.

- the center of gravity analogy itself. A center of gravity isn't a thing, it's a useful abstraction, and nobody's surprised you can't find it under a microscope. But is the self like that, or is it more like a company — no body anywhere, but it can own things, be blamed, do stuff in the world? Those two pictures seem to want different things from the theory.
- people who make up a reason for what they just did a second after doing it, and are dead sure it's the real reason. If the story can be invented afterwards to cover a gap, in what sense is it constituting anything? (there's the split brain stuff, and ordinary confabulation, and honestly most of what I say about my own motives)
- dementia. Someone who can't tell you their life story any more, doesn't recognise their kids, and the family still says they're in there and it matters morally that they are. Either the story was the person and it's gone, or there's something that isn't the story. Which?
- babies, animals, people who never had language. No stories. Selves or not?
- people who say they don't experience their life as a story at all, just stuff happening. Are they wrong about themselves, or does the theory have to make room for them?
- the felt side. A story is a thing you tell about someone, from the outside. But being me has an inside, a what it's like, and something that feels like it's doing the looking. The story view has to say something about that or it's answering a different question than the one I care about.
- which story? I'm a different narrator with my mother than with my boss than with myself at 3am. If the self is the narrative, which one is it, and what holds them together as one person?
- some self stories are clearly worse than others: self serving, self deceiving, or written for you by someone else — a partner who quietly rewrites your history, or a label that sticks and then you turn into it. A theory that just says narrative is constitutive has nothing to say about a bad narrative. Does it need to?
- other people are doing alot of the narrating. My birth, my childhood, the family story about who I am. How much of me can be authored by other people before it stops being mine?
- memory is reconstructive and gets things wrong, and if the story lives in memory then the self is built out of confabulation too. Bug or feature?
- and maybe the whole thing is a local cultural habit, life as a story, and people elsewhere or elsewhen had selves without it.

Then two things I actually want from you. First: which of these can't all hold at once. My own suspicion is that the story making the self and me being the one who tells it can't both be held at full strength, since the narrator would have to exist before the thing that makes him exist — but I want your list of the conflicts, not just mine. Second: if you could keep only one requirement, which one throws out the most of the popular views?

And if at the end you want to say how the main versions of this idea do against the list — the useful fiction one, the brain building a model of itself for control rather than for storytelling, the no-self thing, narrative as an ongoing process rather than a representation — fine, but keep that part short. Skip the reading list.
