I teach a second-year epistemology module and I've got one 75-minute session with about 20 students to make them feel the knowing-how vs. knowing-that distinction rather than just hear it described. Here's the thing I want to try, tell me if its a dud and what to swap in: a three-stage relay around one task. Group A is shown how to do the task (you pick it, but it needs to fit a minute of table space and be fiddly with the hands -- a knot, a coin trick, a rhythm, whatever's cheap). Group B only watches A and then has to write down a complete rule set for it, with no demonstrating allowed, words only. Group C has to perform it from B's written rules alone, no demo, nobody watching. Then we compare all three. What I want them to run into on their own is that C's failures aren't just B being a bad writer, and A's success isn't just memory -- something goes missing in both directions. Then a debrief that maps the moments back onto the real debate (verbal report, the regress of rules, the cases where you can state it perfectly and still can't do it, and the ones where you can do it and are hopeless at saying how), plus what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it. And be straight with me about what the activity can't deliver: it won't settle whether knowing how is a species of knowing that, and it can't reproduce the uncomfortable case of someone who's expert and gives a confident verbal account of their own skill that's just wrong. Keep the labels off it until the debrief -- no name-dropping up front. If the task I pick turns out to be too easy to put into words, say so and give me a better one.
