I am taking an upper-level seminar that sits between moral philosophy and economics, and I have been keeping a list of cases that I cannot attach to any term I know. I would like you to tell me whether the thing I am describing is a recognized phenomenon, and if it is not, to name it and define it carefully.

Here is the pattern. Two people sit on a body that decides things jointly — a committee, a board, a faculty senate. Their moral outlooks do not overlap, and their concerns lie on different questions: one holds that a certain kind of lie is never permitted, whatever the consequences; the other holds that a particular institution must be kept uncontaminated, whatever the cost. On any single question they disagree, and neither converts the other. Precisely because their concerns lie on different questions, they can always strike a bargain: each supports the other's proposal on some other matter, and each goes home having advanced more of what her own outlook says matters. Neither has altered her view. Under the ordinary description this is a success — a mutually advantageous exchange, gains from trade in values rather than in goods.

What I keep circling is that the two descriptions are one description. The bargain is available to them only because each is using the other's conviction as an instrument: the first is using the second's attachment to purity to get her own prohibition enacted, and she knows that this is what she is doing. And it appears to worsen as it succeeds. The more questions they trade over, the more each is deliberately and repeatedly doing what her own outlook forbids. At the limit, a perfectly successful compromise between two value systems is a state in which each party has made a practice of complicity in what she considers wrong. No one can point to the injury either, because there is no outcome that either outlook can evaluate; the outcome exists only in the space between them.

A second case, the same shape without the bargaining: two organizations with incompatible missions co-author a single report. Each signs off on sentences its own members would have written otherwise, because the report as a whole advances each cause. By its own lights, each organization is telling a half-truth. Each is better off.

I do not think this is dirty hands: that is one agent doing wrong for a public good, whereas here there is no single wrongdoer and no single good. It is not logrolling, which needs only intensities of preference and is indifferent to the fact that each trader is violating her own standards. It is not the bare claim that values are incommensurable; I grant that, and what I want to name is what happens when incommensurable values are nevertheless traded. Nor is it moral remainder, since there is no single act to regret and no one who can state what has been lost.

So: is there a term? If there is, I want to know which nearby concept it actually is, and why my cases are instances of it rather than of something adjacent. If there is not, coin one, define it, and mark its edges. Is the phenomenon the trading, or the complicity, or the fact that the complicity is invisible from inside either outlook? Does it survive when only one side regards the other as a means? What happens when the two outlooks overlap slightly — does it vanish, or does it only become harder to see? I do not need a reading list or citations; the name matters less than the carving. And please take a position rather than surveying the options: if you judge that an existing concept covers the case, say so plainly and defend the identification; if you judge that none does, commit to a coinage and defend the boundaries you draw, including the cases you are excluding.
