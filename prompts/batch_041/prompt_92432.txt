My two best friends both ended up in philosophy departments and I write novels, so mostly I get to referee. Last week it was Avicenna's floating man — the guy created at a stroke, fully formed, eyes shrouded, hanging in the air, limbs seperated so nothing touches anything and he can't feel them; he'd still affirm that he exists, but not that any of his limbs or organs do. It came up because the book I'm writing opens with a character who wakes up bodiless, and I wanted a line of dialogue for her.

I'll number the rounds.

(1) Nadia: the whole thing is a swindle. The man in the story has a body — it's right there in the scenario, floating, with limbs, just out of contact. So all Avicenna shows is that you can be unaware of your body, which is also true of my kidneys. Unawareness isn't non-essentiality. And you can't get from 'I can imagine it' to 'it's possible,' never mind to 'the soul is immaterial.'

(2) Rob: that's a reading, but it's the wrong one. The point isn't whether a body is present, it's that the self shows up to itself with zero bodily content. So whatever the 'I' is that the man affirms, it isn't given as a body, and that's a claim about the essence of the self.

(3) Nadia: fine, but then Avicenna's own psychology turns on him. The man is supposed to run the test by imagining, and for Avicenna the imagination is a bodily faculty. So a body-dependent act is doing the work of proving the self doesn't need a body. Rob: no — the imagining only builds the scene, what gets affirmed in it isn't itself imagined.

(4) Rob again, who reads too much science fiction: then a model trained on nothing but text is the floating man made real. Nadia said if that's the test, her laptop has a soul.

I honestly can't tell who's ahead, and I need it settled for the book. Go round by round and tell me who's right at each step — including whether Rob's essence move in (2) and his answer in (3) hold up — then say what both of them are missing, and who's more right overall.
