I got into a stupidly long argument with my friend Dev last night — we're both in a philosophy of medicine seminar that's currently on the naturalism vs. normativism thing — about whether "disease" is a descriptive or an evaluative term, and it's still bugging me this morning. His line was that the usual framing is a false dilemma: either you're a naturalist, where a disease is just statistically subnormal biological functioning and any values come in later when we decide what to do about it, or you're a normativist, where "disease" is a thick evaluative concept through and through. He says that framing smashes together two questions that should be kept apart — whether the *concept* disease carries evaluative content, and whether *classifying some particular condition* as a disease requires value judgments — and that you can answer no to the first and yes to the second. The concept, on his view, is a purely functional/biological one, but the second you apply it you have to pick a reference class and a cutoff, and neither the biology nor the concept tells you which class to use or where the line goes. His example was adult lactose intolerance: perfectly normal if your reference class is "mammals" or "humans before dairying," a deficit if it's "adults in this population eating this diet," and he says there's just no biological fact of the matter about which class is the right one. He calls this the split view and he is extremely pleased with himself about it.

He also threw in, as if it were obviously the same point, that health isn't simply the absence of disease — that you can have a disease and still be healthy (well-controlled, functioning fine, feels fine) and be unhealthy with no disease at all (sedentary, sleeping four hours, miserable) — and I honestly couldn't tell whether that was a seperate point or just the same point wearing a different hat, or whether it blows up the whole naturalism/normativism debate by showing that health and disease aren't two ends of one scale to begin with.

So: can you explain, carefully, and without just restating the two textbook positions, whether the split view is actually coherent or whether it quietly collapses? I want to know (a) whether the concept/application distinction does any real work here, or whether "purely biological concept, value-laden application" is just normativism with extra steps; (b) whether the same move survives when the contested bit isn't the reference class or the threshold but the *function* itself — i.e. cases where we can't agree on what a part or process is even for, so that the "subnormal functioning" half of the naturalist story is up for grabs too; and (c) what the strongest reply available to a naturalist is, given that Dev is perfectly happy to concede that no actual classification is value-free. Use a concrete example or two of your own rather than the usual textbook ones, and if you think the split view only holds up by quietly redefining "concept" halfway through, say so and show me where the sleight of hand happens.
