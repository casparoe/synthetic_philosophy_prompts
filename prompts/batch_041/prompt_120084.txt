I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a feature about AI assistants that second-guess you — not the ones that just say no, but the ones that slow you down or hand you a softened version of what you asked for. For a sidebar I need you to write me a dialogue, two people, equally sharp, both trying to talk the other out of their position. Here's the case I want them chewing on: a guy asks his assistant at 2 a.m. to help him write a long, furious email quitting his job. Nothing illegal, no threats, he's just done. The assistant, per its house policy, won't produce it on the spot — it makes him wait till morning, and when he comes back it writes the letter but sticks a paragraph up top about what he'd be walking away from. One character thinks that's software acting like a decent friend. The other thinks it's a corporation appointing itself his dad, and that "we're not blocking you, we're just adding friction" is the oldest trick in the book. Let them really go at it, especially on: does it matter that he clicked through the terms and can switch the feature off; is refusing outright vs. complying-with-a-nudge a real moral difference or just optics; and does it stop being paternalism once his boss and his landlord might get hurt too. No strawmen — give the anti-nudge one the stronger case on disclosure, give the other the stronger case on what to do when someone genuinely isn't in a state to decide. Have each of them say plainly how confident they are in their main claim and name the part they're least sure about; I'd rather run hedges than fake certainty. Few pages max, no citations or "as philosopher X argued" business, and dont let either of them win by being wittier.
