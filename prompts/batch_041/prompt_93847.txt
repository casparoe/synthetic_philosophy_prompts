I'm the best man at my oldest friend's wedding next month and I have to give the toast, and I'm a bit stuck on how to pitch it. Here's the situation: the groom (my friend) is a constitutional historian — his whole career is on how the crown actually functions in a parliamentary democracy, the stuff nobody outside the field knows, like the reserve powers that exist on paper but are governed only by convention, the question of who gets first chance to form a government when an election returns no majority, whether the monarch can refuse a dissolution, the caretaker conventions, all that. He's a monarchist but the sophisticated, slightly melancholy kind: he thinks the whole thing runs on conventions that no court would enforce and that it survives only as long as everyone agrees to keep pretending. The bride is a republican — not militant, but she genuinely thinks hereditary public office is absurd, and she's a political theorist who'll hear every word as a claim. Her side of the room is mostly academics and a few Scandinavian and Japanese colleagues who know their own constitutional monarchies inside out and will absolutely notice if I say something glib or wrong about, say, how the emperor's role differs from the queen's, or why the Scandinavian monarchies look so different from the British one. His side is his family, who are lovely and completely apolitical and will not know what a reserve power is, plus a couple of his old supervisors who'll be grading my accuracy in real time.

What I want: a five-minute toast that somehow uses the actual machinery of constitutional monarchy — reign but not rule, power that's real but never exercised, authority that depends on the powerful party voluntarily not using what it legally has, the weird dignity of a head of state who has no opinions — as the thing the whole toast is built on, so that it's really about the marriage and not a lecture. It has to land for both halves: the republicans shouldn't feel I've smuggled in a defense of the monarchy, the monarchists shouldn't feel I've made their position a joke, and nobody should feel they're at a seminar. It should be warm and a little funny and it should have one genuinely non-obvious idea in it, not just the reign-but-don't-rule cliché — something like the point that the crown's power is a power it must never spend, so its whole strength is in restraint, which is either a beautiful model for a marriage or a terrible one and I want the toast to leave that slightly open. Don't quote anyone and don't name any statutes or dates — I don't want to get anything wrong, and anyway half the room would fact-check me. Just write the toast.
