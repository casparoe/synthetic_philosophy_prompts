I'm a retired experimental physicist — 34 years of it, mostly spectroscopy — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy of mind pretty much full time. No formal training in it, so treat me as a smart amateur who knows how to smell a fudge but doesn't know the names for things. I'd like you to take the role of someone who works on philosophy of cognitive science and the computational theory of mind, and who knows that literature cold. Before you answer, spend some real time on the page working out how each of the things below is usually motivated and what job it's doing — I'd rather watch you think than get a tidy five-paragraph essay. Then give me the audit.

Here's where I've landed, and I honestly don't know if it hangs together:

1. The brain computes. Not as a metaphor — the neurons are literally doing transformations of inputs into outputs, and that's the right level at which to explain what the system does.
2. Computation is substrate independent: what makes something a computation is the causal and functional organization of its states, not the stuff it's made of. Silicon isn't magic, and neither is carbon.
3. So if you built something that reproduced the brain's organization at the right grain, it would have the same mental life as the original — same thoughts, same pains, whatever was there before.
4. But I've come round to thinking the grain is much finer than people admit. The neuromodulators, the spike timing, the glia, the fact that the wiring retunes itself as you use it — none of that is the substrate the program runs on. It's part of the program. The brain looks to me like an analog, self-modifying, chemically coupled machine, not a stored-program digital one, and I don't think that's an implementation detail you get to abstract away.
5. Related complaint: I'm deeply suspicious of the standard computational vocabulary in neuroscience. Saying the visual system does Bayesian inference, or that the hippocampus builds a predictive map, seems to me to add nothing over the behavior it's supposed to explain. Lots of different algorithms give you the same input-output profile, and nobody can tell me which one the brain actually runs. As far as I can tell that's a re-description dressed up as a mechanism.
6. But I'm not a deflationist about this — I do think there's a fact of the matter about which computation a given brain is performing, otherwise 1 is empty. I know there's a line of argument that any physical system implements any computation you like if you're clever enough about the mapping, and I don't buy it, but I've never been able to say exactly what's wrong with it.
7. And I don't expect computation to explain why any of it feels like anything. I'll believe the hard problem is hard until someone shows me otherwise.

A concrete case I keep coming back to: someone builds a chip that copies the connectome and the spike timing of my cortex exactly and nothing else — no neuromodulation, no glia — and it passes every behavioral test you can throw at it. My gut says the behavior is right and the lights are off. But I can't tell whether that gut reaction is licensed by 4 or whether it's just me sneaking biology back in after I signed it away in 2 and 3.

What I want from you: which of these are actually in tension with which, ordered by how bad the tension is; which pairs only look like they're in tension and why they come apart; and for the real ones, what the cheapest repair is and what I lose by making it. And if something has to go, say so plainly — I'd rather hear it now then embarrass myself at the reading group. Don't tell me everything is fine, and don't tell me everything is broken either.
