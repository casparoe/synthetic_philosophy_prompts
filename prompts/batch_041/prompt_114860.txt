I'm in a reading group where a colleague regularly reaches for the clockwork image to describe Newton's God: the universe as a great machine, wound up at the Creation by a divine clockmaker and then left to run on its own. He leans on the image to license a further claim — that Newton's theology is ornamental rather than load-bearing, a private eccentricity bolted onto a physics that would work identically without it, so that the Newtonian picture of the world is already, in substance, a secular one in which God does no explanatory work.

I'd like the analogy audited rather than dismissed, and two things make me doubt it. The first is that Newton's own camp, in the Leibniz–Clarke exchange, seems to have rejected almost exactly this picture. Samuel Clarke writes: "The Notion of the World's being a great Machine, going on without the Interposition of God, as a Clock continues to go without the Assistance of a Clockmaker; is the Notion of Materialism and Fate, and tends, (under pretence of making God a Supra-mundane Intelligence,) to exclude Providence and God's Government in reality out of the World." The second is that the God of the General Scholium is not a retired artificer but an active ruler: "This Being Governs all things, not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all: And on account of his dominion he is wont to be called Lord God Παντοκράτωρ, or Universal Ruler."

So how far does the clockwork analogy actually hold as a reading of Newton? Give me the correspondence spelled out — what plays the part of the clock, the clockmaker, the winding, the escapement, the machine's running on its own — then the points at which the mapping breaks, sorting the breaks that matter for my colleague's conclusion from those that don't. I especially want to know whether Newton's God does any work inside the physics: what Newton says about space, about the regularities or stability of the planetary system, and about divine governance, and whether those commitments are separable from the mechanics or entangled with it. Was the analogy ever a description of Newton, or is it a description of the deism he is usually set against? If the image is the wrong one, tell me what image Newton himself would have recognized, and whether the substitute can still carry any version of my colleague's conclusion — or whether that conclusion was riding on the analogy the whole time.
