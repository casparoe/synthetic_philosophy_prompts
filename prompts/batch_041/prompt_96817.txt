So I've been reporting for a while on a story that started small and has gotten kind of out of hand: a hospital network here rolled out self-check-in kiosks across its outpatient clinics, retired most of the front-desk staff, and within about six months the local GPs were complaining that their older and unhoused patients were just... not turning up for appointments anymore. The kiosk wants either a smartphone or a chip card before it will even start, the screen resets after 30 seconds, and the help button rings a call center with a four-minute wait. The vendor keeps saying the kiosk is just neutral infrastucture, a tool, and that any problems are "change management." I've got two sources I keep going back to and they've started to genuinely annoy each other, which is great for me and bad for my word count. One is a design researcher who insists the politics is in the thing itself — the defaults, the timeout, the fact that you have to already be legible to a system before you're allowed to be a patient; she talks about designers writing a script for their user, and about machines quietly absorbing work that used to be done by a person with discretion. The other is a sociologist of technology who thinks that's a category error dressed up as insight. He says the kiosk didn't decide anything, a budget decided, and that if every design choice counts as political you lose the ability to say which ones actually matter; his favorite move is to point out that the identical box in a country with universal ID cards would be a boring convenience, and that the clinic could bolt a staffed desk back onto it tomorrow.

I want to write them up as a dialogue — a real one, not a debate-club setup where one of them is obviously the sensible one. They're both sharp, I don't want either of them sandbagged, and I want each of them to actually answer the other's strongest move, including the one my design researcher keeps dodging, which is that a design can be politically loaded and still not be the thing that caused anything. There's a distinction another source handed me that I'd like the dialogue to sort out, if it's any good: between the politics an artifact has — its effects on who can do what, the way it keeps quietly working after everyone has forgotten the argument that produced it — and the politics merely around it, the conditions of its making and the meanings people read into it. My hunch is my two sources are each defending one of those and half the fight is about which one they think they're having. And at the end, give me for each of them the strongest thing the other said, plus what it would take — what argument, evidence, or case — to actually move them off their line, because I'd rather the piece be honest about where the argument is still live than pretend it resolves.
