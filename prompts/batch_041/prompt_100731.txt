Okay so I'm in a free will seminar right now and last week we did Galen Strawson's basic argument against moral responsibility — the regress one: nothing can be the cause of itself, so to be genuinely responsible for how you act you'd have to be responsible for how you are, and to be responsible for how you are you'd have to be responsible for how you got to be that way, and it just keeps kicking the can back, so basic-desert responsibility is impossible. I half buy it and half don't, and every time I try to say where it goes wrong my intuitions fall apart. I want to know what principle is actually behind my verdicts. Please commit to one — don't give me a survey of positions and leave me to pick, tell me which principle you'd actually defend and what it costs me.

Here's what I actually think, case by case.

Mira spends her whole twenties and thirties deliberately trying to become less selfish — therapy, forcing herself to do stuff she hates, etc. By forty she's genuinely generous. I think she's responsible for that generosity, praiseworthy in the real basic-desert sense. Not just "nice outcome," responsible.

Sci-fi case: surgeons wire a guy up so he compulsively wants to help strangers. He helps them, non-stop. I don't think he's responsible for any of it. That's not him helping, that's the wiring.

The lucky saint: someone who's just naturally kind, never had to work at it a day in his life. I don't feel he's responsible for the kindness either — it's just his luck. But I also don't think Mira is *more* responsible than him just because she struggled, since the struggling was luck too. This pair is where I get stuck.

Cult case: a man raised inside a cult, believes genuinely awful things, never once questions them. I don't hold him responsible for the beliefs. But when he acts on them and hurts people, I do hold him responsible for the acts. Which seems incoherent because the acts just come out of the beliefs.

Exhaustion case: a friend snaps and says something cruel after being up for 40 hours, stressed out of his mind. I still blame him for it. It was still him, he owns it. But I let the cult guy off for beliefs that are alot more central to who he is than one nasty remark. That's definately incoherent too.

Tentative principle, and I'm sure it's broken: you're responsible for a trait or an action to the extent you've at some point endorsed it, or could have, from a standpoint that's genuinely yours — like you own it, even if you didn't build it. That gets Mira (owns it, responsible) and the wired guy (doesn't own the urge, not responsible). But the cult guy would say he owns his beliefs, they're the most his things in the world, and I still don't blame him for them. So either the principle is dead or I'm mis-describing the cult case.
