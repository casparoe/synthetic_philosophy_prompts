I'm commissioning a short story for the ideas section of a general-interest magazine, and I want it to do something that most fiction about mathematicians does not: take a philosophical argument for an afterlife seriously enough to dramatize it from the inside, without either debunking it or turning it into a sermon.

The story should center on a fictional logician — not Gödel, but someone recognizably in his intellectual lineage — who holds a position I'll call eschatological rationalism. The position has three planks, and I want your story to make them vivid rather than merely report them. First, the human mind is not a finite machine: the character accepts the disjunctive conclusion that either minds infinitely surpass any finite machine or there are absolutely undecidable arithmetical propositions, and he takes the first horn. Second, time is not fundamental: he treats the passage of time as a feature of how a certain kind of observer is embedded in the world rather than as a basic ingredient of reality, and he is impressed by cosmological models containing closed timelike curves as evidence that temporal order is not the deep order. Third, personal identity is not essentially temporal: what persists is not a stream of experiences but something closer to a Leibnizian monad, a center of perspective individuated by its point of view rather than by its history and possessing no causal windows onto other such centers. From these three claims he draws the conclusion that death cannot be the annihilation of the person; it is, at most, the loss of one mode of access to a reality the person already partly inhabits.

I want the story to be a scene of intellectual pressure, not a lecture. Put the logician in a room with someone who has a real stake in the question — a dying friend, a skeptical neuroscientist, a religious believer who finds his argument cold — and let the argument be tested in conversation. The story should make the reader feel why someone might find this package compelling, and also why it might be a house of cards. The leap from not a finite machine to not mortal is exactly the kind of move a careful reader will want to see defended, and the character should have to defend it. Let him distinguish survival as continuation in time from survival as inclusion in a timeless order, and let him say what difference that distinction makes to grief, to memory, and to the fear of death. Let the skeptical interlocutor press the obvious objection: that a monad without windows is indistinguishable from nothing at all, and that an afterlife without time is not an afterlife but a metaphysical consolation prize.

To keep the story from becoming a seminar, give the logician a private notebook containing a fragment he has never published — a fixed-point argument for the better world. The idea, which the story can reveal in pieces, is this: if the actual world is rationally ordered and contains minds capable of grasping their own limits, then a world that contains such a mind must also contain the conditions of that mind's persistence, on pain of containing a truth it cannot accommodate. Let the notebook fragment be cryptic and slightly embarrassing to its author, and let the interlocutor's reaction to it — whether scorn, fascination, or grief — carry part of the scene's emotional weight. The story should not resolve the question in favor of either side. It should end with the argument in the reader's hands.

One further constraint: I don't want the story to trade on biography. The character should not be a stand-in for Gödel, and the story should not require the reader to know anything about Gödel's life, his illness, or his personal beliefs. The philosophical materials — the disjunctive argument about minds and machines, the ideality of time, the monad, the distinction between temporal and atemporal persistence, the fixed-point idea — are the raw material, and I'd like them recombined into a fictional position that stands or falls on its own. Write the story in close third person, set it within a single scene of no more than a few hours, and let the philosophical content emerge through what the characters notice, concede, and refuse to concede. The prose should be plain and concrete; the ideas should be allowed to be difficult.
