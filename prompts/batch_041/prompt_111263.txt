I am a high school senior, and next month I will present a short paper at a regional philosophy conference for high school students, where the papers are commented on by graduate students and faculty from the host university. I would like you to prepare me for the question period. The audience will consist mainly of graduate students in cognitive science and philosophy of mind; two faculty members will also be present, one broadly sympathetic to Dennett's functionalism and one who studies mechanistic explanation in neuroscience and treats the intentional stance as a useful fiction rather than a claim about what exists; a machine-learning doctoral student has also signed up to comment. My paper makes the following argument. Dennett's three stances — physical, design, and intentional — are incomplete, and a fourth is required, which I call the training stance: to take the training stance toward a system is to predict and explain its behaviour by reasoning about the objective it was optimised against and the distribution of cases it encountered, rather than about its physical constitution or its intended function. I claim that for a reinforcement-learning agent the training stance often compresses the agent's behaviour better than any of the other three, and that by Dennett's own criterion — that a pattern is real when describing it lets you predict and compress better than the alternatives — this makes the agent's apparent goals genuine real patterns, even though the agent has no inner life. I argue further that the training stance comes apart from the design stance precisely in cases such as reward hacking, where the behaviour selected for by the optimisation pressure is not the function the designers intended.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I want the objections I will actually receive from this particular room — not the ones a Dennett specialist would raise — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each would do. Phrase each objection the way the person who would raise it would phrase it, and give me a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, followed by an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede at the very start, what I must not concede, and end with the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
