I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I've been asked to run the ninety-minute session on the symbol grounding problem for the other fourteen students. I'd rather they did something than listened to me. My plan: split the group into Senders and Receivers on opposite sides of a screen; the Sender sees a card bearing a novel abstract shape and may transmit only strings drawn from a dozen meaningless tokens; the Receiver must pick the matching shape from a display of six candidates. Let them build a code over several rounds, and let it feel as though it is working. Then I want a third role — an Archivist who sees only the token strings and never a shape — and a late-arriving Receiver who must succeed using the archive alone, followed by one final variant in which the latecomer is allowed to watch a single Sender act. What I want them to run into before I name anything is that their code is perfectly transmissible and yet carries nothing: the archive is a dictionary that goes round in circles, whereas the newcomer who can watch someone act is hooked onto the world by participation rather than by more symbols. Please give me the rules, materials, round-by-round timeline, what I as facilitator must and must not say, and a debrief mapping each phase onto the philosophical options — causal or informational coupling versus social and use-based grounding — including an honest note on which part of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I shall have to state outright. Tell me too what will predictably go wrong the first time, given the group size, and how to handle it.
