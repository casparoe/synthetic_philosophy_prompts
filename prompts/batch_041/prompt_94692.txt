I spent most of a long dinner last week listening to two friends argue, and I've been turning it over ever since; they pulled me in a few times, but the disagreement was really between the two of them, and I never managed to come down on either side. One of them is a type designer, the other a cognitive scientist who works on categorization, and they got onto the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a'. It came up because I'd described an old idea for a program: show it a handful of 'a's in different typefaces, and see whether it can extrapolate — drawing the same letter in a new style, or a new letter in one of the styles it has already seen. I'd like you to act as an expert in the philosophy of cognitive science with a working feel for typography and for theories of categorization, and adjudicate their exchange point by point. Think it through carefully before committing to anything, and begin by setting out, in your own words and in the strongest form you can give them, the two positions as they actually emerged; then rate each numbered move below on a scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating. I'm not asking you to vindicate anything I said, and I don't know which of them I think is right.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) The designer opened: there is no essence of the letter. Every typeface invents its own 'a'; what makes them all 'a's is a mixture of inheritance — our 'a' descends from a particular way of writing it — the requirement that it stay distinguishable from its neighbours at reading speed, and the fact that readers have been trained on it. Look at the two shapes we both call 'a': the one that is essentially a circle with a straight stem on the right, and the one that is a curved hood over a bowl with a stem. They share no geometry worth naming, so whatever they share is not a shape. (2) The scientist: that cannot be the whole story, because on that account 'this is an a' would be pure fiat. If 'a'-ness were nothing but our convention, we could announce tomorrow that the shape we call 'a' is really the letter 'e', and nothing about the shapes would have changed and nothing would be wrong — which seems plainly false. Some shapes push back. There is a skeleton under the style: a body at x-height with a vertical element on the right and an enclosed or nearly enclosed counter attached to it; every 'a' satisfies that, and style is only its modulation. (3) Designer: the skeleton does not survive the evidence. The two-storey 'a' has two counters, the one-storey has one; that is a difference in topology, not a modulation. And in several italic and blackletter hands the 'a' is a bowl with a hook and little else. Thin the skeleton enough to cover all of those and it becomes either a long disjunction of shapes — family resemblance in a lab coat — or something so abstract that letters which are not 'a' satisfy it too. Notice as well that the Cyrillic 'а' is the same shape as ours and is a different letter: same geometry, different letter, which is what you would expect if letters were roles in a system rather than shapes. (4) Scientist: the Cyrillic case shows that shape is not sufficient for letter identity; it does not show that it is not necessary. And genealogy cannot be what makes something an 'a' either, since someone who had never encountered our alphabet and who converged on that form would have drawn an 'a', not a new letter. Besides, even a reader who cannot read our script finds the bowl-with-a-right-stem closer to 'a' than to 'z' — there is a perceptual gradient there, and a convention does not explain it. (5) Designer: 'closer to a than to z' for a non-reader is a claim about our visual system, not about the letter. You are sliding between two quite different claims — that there is something about the letter itself that makes it the letter it is, and that our perceptual machinery has biases that make some shapes easier to read as 'a'. The second is true and uninteresting; the first is the one you need, and everything you have said is compatible with the letter being nothing but a node in a network of distinctions. (6) Scientist: if that is all it is, then 'is this an a?' has no answer beyond what people will tolerate, and then your own training story cannot explain why some novelties are read immediately as 'a' while others are rejected as illegible. You keep appealing to the tradition as though it were a fact about the world, but the tradition is just a long sequence of choices, and at each one somebody had to judge that the new shape was still the same letter. What were they judging? (7) Designer: they were judging whether it would work — whether readers could still tell it from 'o' and 'e' and 'd' at speed, and whether it fitted the style of the rest of the face. That is a functional judgement, not a glimpse of an essence. And we do get these things wrong, and we do change our minds: shapes once read as one letter come to be read as another once a typeface becomes familiar, and some shapes sit ambiguously between two letters. So your 'we couldn't be wrong' argument is carrying more weight than it can bear. (8) Scientist: I will grant that we can be wrong about particular shapes. But that only makes sense if there is something to be wrong about, and I do not see how a convention supplies it on its own, since a convention can be changed by fiat and this cannot.

Now please adjudicate. Specifically: (a) who is right about whether the one-storey/two-storey variation refutes a shared skeleton, and whether a properly thinned skeleton still earns its keep or collapses into a disjunction; (b) whether the Cyrillic point in (3) and the convergence point in (4) are doing what the parties think they are doing; (c) whether the 'could have been otherwise' argument in (2) and the 'we can be wrong' replies in (7) and (8) establish anything about essence at all, or only about the epistemology of reading; (d) whether the two of them are even answering the same question, and if not, name the questions each is really answering; and (e) what both of them are missing — I have a nagging suspicion that they are treating the letter as one kind of thing when there is more than one thing to explain, but I would rather you tell me than confirm my guess. Close with an overall verdict: which of them has the better position, and whether there is a defensible middle position between them that neither was willing to state. Please reason from the cases they actually used rather than from a catalogue of famous theories; if a well-known position genuinely does work here, name it and say what it buys you, but do not let the answer turn into a survey.
