I am a novelist at work on a scene in which a character has to decide, with no means of checking, whether a particular very large number is prime — the decryption of a message, and a good deal besides, turns on her being right. I have no head for this sort of thing myself, so I put the problem to two friends over dinner, and they fell out badly over it.

Douglas, who teaches history, said her conclusion was nonsense on its face. Primality is not a matter of chance, he said: the number is either prime or it is not, and that was settled long before she was born. A probability can attach only to something that might genuinely go either way; she has dressed up a guess in the costume of a measurement, which is worse than an honest guess.

Iris, who spent thirty years as an actuary, said he was confusing chance with ignorance. Everything she ever priced was, in his sense, already settled — whether this particular man of fifty-four would see the year out, whether this particular house would burn. Her probabilities measured the weight of her evidence, not any dice in the world. And the character, Iris said, has done rather better than quoting a base rate: she has eliminated the small prime factors one by one, and she is entitled to suppose that the remaining properties of the number are independent of one another, that being the default position. You need a reason to think two things are connected; the absence of such a reason is not itself a reason to fear one.

Douglas said that 'default' was precisely the word he objected to. To treat the number's digits as independent is to assume that nobody chose it to defeat exactly this reasoning — a substantive assumption, not a neutral one, and in this case a poor one, since the number is a key and keys are chosen by people who want them to resist guessing. More generally, he said, 'I have found no reason to think otherwise' licenses the assumption only when your search would probably have turned up a reason had one existed. She checked for small factors; a composite built from two large primes passes that check unscathed. Her test cannot distinguish 'prime' from 'craftily composite', so its silence tells her nothing.

Iris said that if he would grant her the base rate and what she knows of the chooser, she would take it. But she would not concede that the checks were worthless: each eliminated factor genuinely raises the odds, and the eliminations are independent in the only sense that matters, since no single fact about a number makes it divisible by seven and by eleven; multiplying them is therefore legitimate. It is the same assumption that lets anyone multiply two pieces of evidence together, and if you refuse it, evidence can never accumulate at all — one witness, one document, one carbon date, each worthless alone and all worthless together.

Douglas said that 'independent in the only sense that matters' was where he would stop her. The eliminations are not independent pieces of evidence at all, he said; they are one assumption — that the number was drawn at random from numbers of its size — applied over and over. Repeating an assumption twenty times does not yield twenty pieces of evidence, and if the assumption fails, all twenty checks fail together. Evidence that fails together was never independent.

I have no stake in this beyond wanting the scene to be right, and I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it. It seems to me the quarrel is an old one about how far one may reason with probabilities about questions that have a definite answer, and about when it is legitimate to assume that two things are unconnected. Please go through the exchange move by move, rating each move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and giving a short justification for each rating. Then say what each of them is missing, and who has the better of it overall — not merely that both have a point somewhere. I should add that I don't want either of them to come out of the scene looking a fool, but neither do I want to write a character who is quietly fooling herself. Please write your answer in British English.
