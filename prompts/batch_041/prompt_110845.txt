Thirty-five years ago I would have described myself without hesitation as an experimental physicist, and for most of that time my working life consisted of deciding which of several competing models a noisy dataset actually supported. I have now retired, and for the past three years I have been working my way through graduate philosophy of science with considerably more enthusiasm than background. I would like to apply for a one-year residential fellowship of the kind intended to help established researchers move into a new field, and I need a proposal of roughly two thousand words. I would be grateful if you would help me turn the following half-formed idea into a proper one.

The idea is this. For decades I chose among models by a rule I could state but never justify: prefer the simpler hypothesis, and count the parameters. When I finally read the algorithmic account of simplicity — Solomonoff's universal prior, Kolmogorov complexity, and the invariance theorem to the effect that complexity measures defined relative to different reference machines agree only up to an additive constant — I expected it to vindicate the rule. Instead I noticed that every reassurance it offers is asymptotic. The constant in that theorem is negligible only for very long descriptions, whereas the hypotheses I actually compared were tiny: a linear trend against a quadratic one, a genuine signal against a fluctuation, two rival laws each containing a handful of parameters. In that regime the constant is not negligible, and if it is not negligible, then which of two short hypotheses counts as the simpler can depend on a convention that no one is compelled to accept. I am therefore tempted to conclude that the algorithmic story grounds Occam's razor only where it is not needed, and falls silent precisely where working scientists invoke it.

My hunch — and I hold it loosely — is that the difficulty arises from asking a single principle to do two quite different jobs: ranking hypotheses within a fixed space of candidates, and selecting that space in the first place. I suspect the reference-machine arbitrariness is damaging for the first job but comparatively harmless for the second, or conceivably the reverse, and that the treatments I have encountered tend to blur the distinction.

Please write the proposal. It should state the question so precisely that it admits a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions leave it open; set out the approach I would take, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives; say what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of what can realistically be accomplished in one year by someone with my training. Assume a mixed panel of philosophers and scientists, so keep the technical apparatus to what that audience will tolerate. No bibliography is needed — I will supply the citations myself.
