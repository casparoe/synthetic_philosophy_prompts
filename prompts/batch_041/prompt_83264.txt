I am in a graduate seminar on the foundations of hedonism, and I have been asked to write a short paper on the heterogeneity problem, but I keep going in circles because the two people I usually argue with defend opposite fixes. The problem, as I understand it, is that the experiences we call pleasures — the warmth of a bath, the taste of something rich, the click of a proof coming together, the relief when a headache finally lifts, the satisfaction a cruel person takes in someone's humiliation, the pleasure a masochist reports in pain itself — do not appear to share any single phenomenal feature. If there is no common property, then pleasure looks like a merely nominal kind, and the hedonist's claim that pleasure and only pleasure is intrinsically good loses its determinacy. My friend A defends the qualitative line: all pleasures share a distinctive hedonic quality, a felt pleasantness intrinsic to the experience and detectable by introspection even when the experiences are otherwise utterly unlike. My friend B defends the attitudinal line: what unifies pleasures is not anything intrinsic to the experience but the subject's relation to it — liking it, wanting it to continue, taking it as desirable — so that two experiences with nothing phenomenally in common can both count as pleasures in virtue of the same attitude. A third friend, largely to needle us, floated a functional line: pleasure is whatever plays the role of reward in the systems that shape an agent's subsequent behaviour, so phenomenal heterogeneity is exactly what one should expect and not an objection at all.

Please compare the qualitative and attitudinal accounts, keeping the functional proposal in view as a foil, and let the comparison turn on cases rather than on labels. Work through at least the following: the masochist who genuinely enjoys pain; the person who takes pleasure in another's misfortune; the relief of a headache ending, where the pleasure seems to be the removal of something rather than the presence of any positive sensation; experimental dissociations in which an animal will work persistently for a stimulus to which it shows none of the usual hedonic reactions, so that wanting and liking come apart; and the pleasures of abstract thought, where there is no obvious sensory vehicle for the liking to attach to. For each case, say which account handles it naturally, which has to bite a bullet, and whether the accounts threaten to collapse into one another — in particular, is liking itself a phenomenal quality, in which case the attitudinal account is a version of the qualitative one rather than a rival? Does the functional line rescue hedonism or merely empty it, since a reward role can be filled by states nobody would call pleasant?

Close with a verdict: which of the two main proposals is the better answer to the heterogeneity problem, and does accepting that answer change what hedonism as a normative thesis actually asserts, or does it leave the thesis intact while relocating the difficulty elsewhere? Argue rather than survey; I do not need a bibliography or citations, and you need not attribute the views to their historical proponents.
