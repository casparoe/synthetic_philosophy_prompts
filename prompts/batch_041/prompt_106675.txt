Act as a philosopher of decision theory and game theory with a specialist's command of superrationality — the idea that when the people in a symmetric predicament are reasoning alike, your choice should be the one you would want everyone in your position to make, because their choices are correlated with yours rather than independent. Before you write anything else, think on paper for a few lines about what that idea genuinely licenses and where it is known to be fragile.

I have just been elected coordinator of a small cooperative of independent fishing crews, and the job keeps putting me in the middle of choices whose payoff depends on how the other, similarly-placed members choose. I need a decision procedure I can actually run, in about ten minutes, whenever a member — or I myself — faces such a choice. Set it out as questions to ask in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the point at which it returns a firm "cooperate" or "don't", and mark the point at which it hands the decision back to the person as a matter of values rather than reasoning. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a procedure.

Then run the procedure on two cases: (i) a crew that can make one unusually profitable haul which would take the cooperative over its voluntary seasonal limit; and (ii) me, deciding whether to keep honoring the cooperative's costly and unenforced ban on a piece of gear while a neighboring fleet that never agreed to anything uses it to undercut us. I expect at least one of these to come out against cooperating, so that I can see the procedure actually bite.

Close with what the procedure cannot do — in particular, how it degrades when the cooperative is large, when members differ in how they reason, and when the real question is not "what would everyone in my position do" but "what can I survive doing". The final call stays with the member, but I need to know where the reasoning stops being able to help.
