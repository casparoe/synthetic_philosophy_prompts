I teach the unit on self-deception in an upper-level epistemology seminar, and I have to return graded work on the midterm tomorrow. I would like you to grade one student's answer and give me the comments I will hand back with it.

The exam question, worth 20 points, was:

"(a) [6 pts] State the so-called static paradox of self-deception and explain why it is thought to threaten the very possibility of the phenomenon. (b) [8 pts] Explain two strategies for dissolving the paradox: one that divides the mind, and one that abandons the requirement that the self-deceived hold contradictory beliefs. For each, say what it must give up in order to work. (c) [6 pts] A classmate proposes the following: 'Sartre's account of bad faith is really just a version of the division strategy — the bad-faith subject keeps the unwelcome truth in a compartment of the mind that the rest of her cannot see.' Assess this proposal."

The student's answer:

"The static paradox begins from the traditional model, in which self-deception is modeled on deceiving another person. If A deceives B into believing that p, then A knows or truly believes that not-p while causing B to believe that p. When A and B are the same person, it seems to follow that A must simultaneously believe that not-p (as the deceiver) and believe that p (as the deceived). But believing a proposition and its negation at the same time is not a possible state of mind. So on the traditional model, self-deception turns out to be impossible.

The first strategy accepts both beliefs but denies that one mind holds them at once. Davidson partitions the mind, so that the belief that not-p is walled off in one subsystem and the belief that p lives in another; no single subsystem is ever in the contradictory state, and the wall is what lets the deception succeed, since the deceiving part need not be transparent to the deceived part. What this strategy gives up is the unity of the self: it buys the possibility of self-deception at the cost of positing a mind divided into parts that do not share their contents.

The second strategy, the deflationary one, denies the dual-belief requirement outright. Mele's account says the self-deceived person holds just one false belief, one acquired and maintained because she wants it to be true. No intention to deceive herself is needed, and no second belief registering the truth is needed; 'deception' becomes a metaphor for the way desire distorts the formation and retention of belief. This strategy gives up the analogy with interpersonal deception, and with it the easy claim that the self-deceived are responsible for their condition. It also gives up something Davidson keeps: on Mele's view the unwelcome truth is still walled off in a partition of the mind, but the partition is now described as motivated rather than structural, so the contradiction is avoided in just the way Davidson avoids it.

For (c), I think the classmate is right. Bad faith is exactly the divided-mind phenomenon in its everyday form: the waiter who plays at being a waiter and the woman who leaves her hand in her date's are each keeping a truth they cannot face in a part of consciousness that the rest of the mind cannot reach, which is why the maneuver works on them. Sartre's whole point is that we can hide things from ourselves only because the mind is not a single transparent whole, and bad faith is what that hiding looks like from the inside. So the division strategy, the deflationary strategy, and bad faith are three descriptions of one architecture, and self-deception is possible after all."
