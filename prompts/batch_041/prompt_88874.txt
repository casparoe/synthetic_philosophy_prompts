I'm a novelist — five books in, literary fiction, mostly families doing slow damage to each other — and I've somehow ended up with a friend, Miriam, who teaches philosophy and has become the unofficial referee for every manuscript. It started when I asked her whether a character of mine was actually responsible for something awful he did, and she gave me a straight answer instead of a hedge, and now I can't finish a draft without sending it to her and getting back a paragraph on which of my people are "answerable" and which are just weather. (She insists on "answerable," not "responsible," and gets visibly annoyed when I use them as synonyms.) She's a compatibilist, aggressively so — determinism is a red herring, she'll say, free will survives it fine — but she's much stricter about manipulation than the other philosophers I've met through her. I once watched her loose an argument at a dinner party over exactly this and not care at all.

Her rulings so far, roughly: Marta, the pickpocket in book two, raised by a family of thieves in a town where everybody stole, who has never once wanted another life and says so — responsible. Clara, who grew up in a closed religious community, left at twenty, spent a decade away, came back at forty and chose the whole thing over again with her eyes open, having read every critic — responsible. Grace, who at thirty fell in with a movement and went through two years of what I'd call brainwashing, and who now genuinely cannot hold the thought that the leader is a fraud, not for a second, and who also completely and cheerfully endorses everything she does — not responsible. Vera, whose husband ground anti-anxiety pills into her dinner for a year, during which she agreed to sell the house and move across the country, and who was so fogged the whole time that she couldn't hold two thoughts together — not responsible. And Tomás, who in book four sells out a friend for money, deliberately, with total clarity, and despises himself for it — doesn't endorse his own greed at all, hates that he's the kind of man who does this — responsible. When I asked her about him she said the fact that he wishes he were otherwise doesn't get him off the hook, it just makes him unhappy.

Book five is two brothers. Saul, at twenty-six, decides to remake his younger brother Emil: two years of it, isolation, scripts, small engineered conversions of taste and value, and Emil was twenty-three and a mess and went along with it. Saul's been dead for a decade. Emil is forty-five now, a hospice nurse, and it isn't a costume — it's his actual life. He'd tell you he can't imagine wanting anything else, and he isn't frozen about it either; he's genuinely open to argument, he's changed his mind about smaller things in the years since, he'd listen. The last act has Emil make a decision I need the reader to judge. So what's Miriam going to say about him? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rule she's actually running — and in the other rules that would fit everything she's said just as well, because I suspect she's got a tidy principle in her head that her five rulings don't actually pin down. If two readings of her survive, say which one you'd bet on and why, tell me what case I should put in book six to break the tie, and be honest about how confident you are and what would change your mind — if there's a detail about Emil I should add or take out that would flip your prediction, I want to know that too.
