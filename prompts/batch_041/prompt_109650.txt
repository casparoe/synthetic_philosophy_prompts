My old teacher died two weeks ago — 74, taught Buddhist philosophy at a small liberal arts college for basically his whole adult life — and I've somehow ended up as the person giving the eulogy at the memorial this Saturday. The room is going to be a mess of audiences: his sister and her kids, who are Catholic and mostly just want to hear that he was loved; a couple dozen former students plus two of his old colleagues from the philosophy department, who will definately be listening to see whether I get the Madhyamaka right; and a handful of people from the zendo he sat with for thirty years. He spent his career on Nagarjuna, emptiness, dependent origination, the two truths — and he was insistent, the way he was insistent about very few things, that the tetralemma (the four-corners thing: X, not-X, both, neither) was not a menu of four answers but a way of showing that a question had gone wrong in the asking. In the notes he left for the service he wrote that whatever anyone says about him now shouldn't land in any of the four corners: not that he's still going somehow, not that he's simply stopped, not both, not neither. That's the box I'm stuck inside of.

And then last night I got into a long, stupid argument with one of his old colleagues, who said, gently, that since everything is empty anyway my grief is 'just a convention,' the eulogy should be short and dry, and I shouldn't make a scene of it. I know he's wrong, or I half know it. I couldn't say why conventional truth isn't just a polite fiction we tell ourselves to feel better, and I couldn't say why his line isn't exactly the nihilism charge Nagarjuna's opponents kept throwing at him — the one about emptiness eating the Four Noble Truths, karma, the whole practical business of caring about anything. I've been chewing on it for hours and I'm no closer.

So please write me the eulogy. About five minutes spoken, maybe 700–900 words. It has to comfort a room of non-philosophers without asserting anything he would have considered false, and it has to give the students something real without turning into a lecture on the two truths — and it has to answer his colleague's point, not by rebutting it in a footnote way but by being a speech that simply couldn't have been given by someone who believed it. Work in one concrete memory-shaped detail I can swap for a real one (a beat-up car, a tomato garden, a bad habit of correcting people's Sanskrit under his breath) but don't lean on anything I'd have had to actually witness. Then, after the speech, put one final line starting 'Summary:' that states in one sentence how the eulogy kept grief on the conventional side without letting emptiness dissolve it.
