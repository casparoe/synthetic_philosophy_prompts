I've spent the last few months reporting on lobbying and money in politics, and I keep tripping over the same puzzle, so I'd like you to help me think it through properly instead of handing me the usual 'money corrupts everything' line. Where I've been reporting from — and I suspect this holds in most democracies, not just the one I'm sitting in — bribery is a crime only if you can show a deal: a quid pro quo, something given in exchange for a specific official act, with corrupt intent attached. Give a minister a case of good wine, fly them to a conference in the sun, put their nephew on the payroll, hire them the week they leave office, and none of that is bribery; it's 'access', or 'networking', or 'the revolving door'. Meanwhile it's flatly illegal to hand a voter cash for their vote, and yet it's perfectly legal for a billionaire to spend an unlimited sum on adverts designed to make thousands of voters vote a particular way — a distinction that every lobbyist I've ever bought a drink for privately admits is a fiction. Same story with the line between a 'contribution' and an 'independent expenditure': one is capped and disclosed, the other sometimes isn't, and the only real difference is a wink about who's coordinating with whom.

What I want from you is the implicit political morality of that arrangement — not what a reformer says about it, but what the rules themselves presuppose. What theory of corruption does a system need in order to draw the line exactly there, at the explicit exchange? Does it presuppose that influence is a commodity that may legitimately be bought and sold, with only the formal contract policed? Does it presuppose a view about coercion versus persuasion — that a gift, a job, a favour or a well-placed word is merely persuasion, while cash-for-vote is coercion? And does it presuppose something about representation: that a representative is a free agent whose judgement is only corrupted once it has been formally purchased, rather than someone whose attention and priorities can be quietly rented by the hour? Then tell me which bits are strictly presupposed and which merely sit comfortably with the practice. I suspect the whole thing is compatible with several quite different theories of corruption, and I'd rather know where it's underdetermined than be sold a tidy answer.

It would also help to see what a rival theory would force us to change. Take a view on which the wrong isn't the deal but the systematic direction of policy by wealth, or a view on which the evil is domination rather than exchange — what would each of those have to do to the bribery statute, the disclosure regime and the lobbying register? To make it concrete, test the theories against three proposals I keep hearing floated: (a) cap donations hard but leave independent spending alone; (b) keep donations unlimited but route them through a blind trust, so the politician never learns who gave while the public finds out after polling day; (c) ban the revolving door outright, no former official may lobby for five years. Say which theory of corruption each proposal implies, and whether any of them is actually coherent on its own terms. Please write it in British English, and don't give me a reading list or a pile of case citations — I can't look anything up from where I'm filing this, and what I need is the argument, not the footnotes.
