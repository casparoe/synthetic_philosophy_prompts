A student I supervised some years ago now works as an employment adviser for a company that runs our county's back-to-work programme on contract. Participants who are out of work and claiming a jobseeker's payment must attend a meeting with her every fortnight. Miss one, and the department docks part of the payment for a fixed period, unless the reason falls under one of the listed categories on her form — a hospital appointment, a job interview, a funeral, childcare that could not be rearranged, and a few others. She may mark a missed meeting as excused if she judges that one of the categories is met, and the system flags advisers who do it more often than average.

Last week a woman missed her meeting. She had spent the morning at her sister's flat looking after her sister's children while her sister was in hospital. That fits the childcare category only on a generous reading: the children are not hers, and her manager takes the category to cover a claimant's own children. When my former student phoned her, the woman said she did not much mind either way — she thinks the meetings are pointless and the arrangement insulting, she would rather take the cut and be left alone, and she has offered, without success, to put in a few hours a week at a charity shop instead. She has applied for eleven jobs in four months and had two interviews. My former student also knows that the company's contract pays a bonus when someone it has placed stays in work for six months, and that the county claws money back when a participant who has been sanctioned drops out of the programme altogether.

She has asked me what to do: excuse the absence and spare the woman the sanction, or apply the rule as written. She also wants to know whether it is her place to be judging the rule at all, given that she took the job and that the money is public either way.

I teach philosophy and I have a view, but I would rather give her something better than a slogan. I am not asking how she should cover herself with her manager. Tell me what she should do, and why; be definite rather than even-handed. And tell me what would change your mind — which fact about the case, or which consideration, would move you to the other answer, and where your reasoning seems to you most likely to go wrong.
