I'm a PhD student in philosophy and I've somehow ended up doing a placement with a community home-care org (it's an "engaged philosophy" thing my department makes us do, don't ask). They've asked me to do something I'm honestly not sure is possible: turn care ethics into an actual decision procedure their care coordinators can run.

Here's the setup so you can calibrate. There are four care coordinators, each managing about forty older-adult clients in their homes. Once a week they sit down for a 90-minute case conference and decide things like: who gets the scarce home-care hours out of a fixed municipal pot, whether to push a client to accept a service they've been declining, whether to loop in adult protective services, whether to keep funding a carer who's burning out. So maybe fifteen real decisions a week, per coordinator. The stakes are real but mostly not life-or-death — falls, skin breakdown, isolation, a daughter who's hit her limit, dignity stuff. The coordinators have social-work backgrounds, not philosophy ones. They get about twenty minutes per case and they have to write a short justification that their supervisor signs off on, because the funder audits us for consistency and we can't get away with shrugging and saying every case is unique.

And that last bit is the whole problem, right? Care ethics basically distrusts rules. The whole point (as I understand it, and as Gilligan was getting at back in the 80s) is that what matters is the particular relationship and the response to *this* person, not applying a generalizable standard that flattens them into a case type. So a procedure is almost a contradiction in terms. If I just write a rubric with weights and boxes, I've smuggled in exactly the impartial, generalizing thing care ethics was reacting against, and the coordinators will tick boxes and stop thinking about the person. But if I write "use your judgment, attend to the relationship," I've given them nothing to do and nothing to write down, and the funder will eat us alive.

So what I want from you is a procedure that keeps faith with care ethics but is still something a coordinator can actually run in twenty minutes. Concretely: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a question of judgment rather than something the procedure can settle. Two things I want baked in and I think care ethics requires them. First, a step that captures the cared-for person's own account of *receiving* the care — not just whether the service got delivered, but how it landed for them, because a client can be getting lots of care and still be experiencing it as control. Second, a step that lets the carer's own limits count, including the coordinator's — care ethics shouldn't require the carer to quietly dissolve. I also want an explicit "this case is not procedurally decidable, here's what to do instead" flag, so the procedure is honest about its own edges instead of pretending to cover everything. And I want you to be blunt about which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where it's likely to misfire.

Then run it on three cases and tell me what it spits out:

1. Mrs. Adeyemi, 82, keeps declining the bath aide. Her skin's starting to break down and the nurse is worried. She says she's washed herself for eighty years and doesn't want a stranger doing it. The coordinators are itching to override her and I'm not sure they're wrong.
2. Mr. Kaur's daughter visits every day, manages his meds, does his shopping, keeps his house spotless. On paper a model care arrangement. But he's told his coordinator twice that he feels managed, that it's her life not his. Nobody's neglecting him and yet something's off.
3. Two clients both need the same two hours a week and there's only two hours to give. One is warm and articulate and easy to like; the other is terse and hard to read. I already know how the room will vote and it worries me.

For each case I want the verdict, which step is doing the work, and whether the procedure gives an answer the coordinator won't like. I'd honestly be suspicious of a procedure that always agrees with them, so if it lets them do the comfortable thing in all three, tell me and tell me why.

Then, after all that, give me a brief structured summary I can paste into my notes, as a JSON object with exactly these fields: `procedure_name` (string); `steps` (array of objects, each with `order` int, `step` string, `question` string, `sufficient_answer` string, `if_answer_is_no` string, and `is_judgment_call` boolean); `handoff_points` (array of strings, the places where it has to go to a person); `cases` (array of objects, each with `case` string, `verdict` string, `step_doing_the_work` string, `coordinator_likely_dislikes` boolean, `procedure_misfires` boolean, and `note` string); and `known_limits` (array of strings, what the procedure can't do).
