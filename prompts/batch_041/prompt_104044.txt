A friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about whether the world we have is the world we had to have, and we got nowhere, largely because we were running two questions together: whether the same outcome would have arrived anyway, and whether it would have mattered if it hadn't. He takes the deflationary line — every outcome is inevitable under a coarse enough description and none is under a fine enough one, so there is nothing left to argue about. I don't buy it, because the cases still don't feel alike to me after I fix the grain. What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that actually fits the verdicts I hold. Here they are.

The standard keyboard arrangement. The choice among workable layouts was close to arbitrary at the outset, and the winner's advantage compounded — typists, manufacturers, schools, each following the others. I think we were locked in early and are still locked in: changing now costs more than it buys. But I don't think the lock-in cost us anything. A different layout would have served about as well, and nobody should regret the counterfactual keyboard.

Electrical supply. AC beating DC does not look path dependent to me at all: at the scale the grid reached, AC's advantage in moving power over distance was decisive, so that outcome was coming either way. The specific frequency is another matter. Fifty cycles against sixty looks arbitrary, is now effectively irreversible, and has cost real money for a century, since equipment built for one will not run on the other. One case, two verdicts, and the difference between them is the grain of description.

Rail gauge. Arbitrary at the start, compounding afterward — and this time the arbitrariness is load-bearing. Systems that chose differently were stranded, and the costs of the break are still being paid. If anything is a genuine lock-in that mattered, this is it.

The zero meridian. Nobody should care that Greenwich won. Some single zero had to be adopted, one candidate had to win, and had it been Paris we would all be living with Paris, and it would feel equally natural. Arbitrary, universal, durable, and inert.

Calculus notation. The content was coming anyway; two people reached it independently and the theory belongs to neither. But the notation that won did real work. One national tradition kept working in the losing notation for generations, and the students who switched had to relearn how to see. So here a purely conventional part of the outcome had consequences about as large as the gauge's — which sits badly with my wanting to say the keyboard's conventions had none.

The early automobile. This is the one that breaks the pattern. The internal combustion engine did not win by coin flip; by the time it won it had advantages that look functional — cheap fuel, a fueling network, a starter that made the thing usable — but those advantages were themselves built up out of the early lead, and the lead came out of a few decisions. So I want to say the electric car's loss was contingent and consequential, that the shape of the twentieth century was fixed by it. But if I say that, I have given up the idea that lock-in requires an arbitrary fork. If I don't, I have given up my strongest intuition about the case.

My tentative principle, offered mainly to be broken: an outcome is locked in when the fork was near-arbitrary, the winner's advantage grew with the number of people who had already chosen it, and the choice left a durable constraint on what could come later. I would keep a separate claim about mattering: the lock-in matters to the extent that the road not taken would have been better for the people who had to live on the road taken.

Please tell me which principle fits these verdicts, and name the clause each case is pulling on. If no single principle fits, tell me which of my verdicts has to go, and be specific. Then say what your repaired principle implies for a case I haven't mentioned: the language of science. That some single language fills the role looks functional to me; that this one filled it, and that its position is now self-reinforcing enough to make the next language expensive to adopt, looks to me like the gauge. I would like to know whether the principle agrees, and where it stops being able to tell me anything I did not already believe.
