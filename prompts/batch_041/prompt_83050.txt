I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on computation and the limits of rationality, and I keep meeting the same argumentative shortcut, in published work and in student essays alike: a problem is shown to be NP-hard, or the unresolved status of P versus NP is invoked, and a philosophical conclusion is drawn from it — that the ideal of perfect rationality is a fiction, that no mind could be computing the optimum, that an agent cannot be required to do what no tractable procedure could deliver. In discussion these conclusions look interchangeable, but I suspect they are reached by quite different routes, and I want an instrument that makes the routes visible and separable.

Please write me a decision tree — a flowchart in prose — that starts a reader from a single commitment and carries them to one of these conclusions, with every branch node phrased as a question the reader can answer without knowing which conclusion it leads to. Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of authors: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space.

Start the reader at one commitment: that at least one problem central to a theory of reasoning, planning, or aggregation is hard for any procedure we could run. Let the tree do the rest, and make sure it turns on at least these distinctions, adding whatever else the space demands:

- In what sense is the problem hard — undecidable, so that no algorithm exists at all, or intractable, so that algorithms exist but none is fast enough? These license very different conclusions and are routinely conflated.
- Is the difficulty intrinsic to the problem, or relative to a machine model and a stipulation about what counts as feasible? In particular, is the identification of feasibility with polynomial time a fact about the world or a convention about our devices, and does the robustness of that class across reasonable machine models settle the question?
- Is the relevant measure worst-case (some instances are hard) or typical-case (most instances are easy)? This is the difference between a claim about all instances and a claim about the instances that actually arise.
- Is the normative target even well-posed as a computational problem? A theory charged with intractability may never have been given a definite input-output specification, in which case the hardness result is being applied to something that was never a computation to begin with.
- Is the conclusion descriptive (agents cannot do this) or normative (agents cannot be required to do this)?
- If normative, is an ought-implies-can principle in play, and which notion of can does it use — the agent's actual resources, or the resources of some idealized agent?
- Is the ideal of perfect rationality a coherent but unreachable limit, on the model of a frictionless plane, or an incoherent target to be abandoned?

Let the leaves be the positions. Include at least: a defense of ideal theory that accepts the hardness result and denies it has any normative force; a requirement-abolitionism holding that nothing is required of an agent which no tractable procedure could deliver; a bounded-rationality reconstruction that redefines rationality relative to available resources; a conventionalism or architecture-relativism denying that feasibility is a fact about the world at all; a typical-case optimism treating worst-case bounds as irrelevant to practice; an ill-posedness deflationism denying that the complexity result applies; a confusion diagnosis treating the argument as trading on the undecidability-intractability conflation; a computationalist constraint reading the result as a bound on theories of mind; and a physical optimism making feasibility a matter of the physics available to the agent. Add any leaf the space demands, and mark a leaf unnamed where the combination is occupied by no one.

Each leaf should carry its most awkward commitment in a single sentence. Note the combinations of answers that no one occupies, and say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. Set it at seven or eight branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves, and keep every node a question about a concrete case or claim rather than a request for a label — no node should read simply as a request to say whether one thinks rationality is bounded.

I would rather you commit than survey. Tell me which leaves you take to be coherent and which not, where my opening characterization of the shortcut actually lands once the tree is applied to it, and which of the leaves you would defend yourself, with your reasons.
