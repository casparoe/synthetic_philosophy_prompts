I have been reading longtermist and effective-altruism material for the better part of a decade — the blogs, the forum threads, the occasional technical report — and after a good deal of back-and-forth I have more or less landed on what is usually called the hinge-of-history hypothesis: the claim that we are living through an unusually pivotal period for the long-run trajectory of civilization, such that the expected value of what we do now is enormous relative to what people could have achieved at almost any earlier time. I hold the modest, defeasible version of this: not that we are certainly at the hinge, but that the probability is high enough, and the downside risk large enough, that we should act as if we were. I am not especially interested in the familiar moral objections (that longtermism is too demanding, that it is the ideology of the comfortable, that it discounts present suffering). What I want is a serious audit of the empirical and structural core of the view.

So please play it straight and tell me what would have to be true for the hypothesis to be false. Which observations, arguments, or formal results would count against it; which of these are merely awkward and which would be genuinely fatal; and what does the position predict that its rivals do not, so that there is something for the world to disconfirm? I would also like to know which of these defeaters a committed hinge-theorist would themselves concede, as opposed to dismissing as question-begging or as a misunderstanding of the claim. In particular, I keep bumping into a symmetry or self-selection objection that I cannot shake: that every generation has contained people convinced they stood at the decisive moment of history, and that a claim which has seemed compelling to so many past observers cannot by itself carry much evidential weight about where we sit. I want you to say whether that symmetry argument is actually decisive or merely a caution, and whether the hinge-theorist has a principled reply — for instance, an argument from a reference class, or an appeal to features of the present that are supposed to break the symmetry with every earlier generation.

And please be honest about whether the hypothesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether, once we allow that the hinge might be a multi-century window, that its value could be realized far in the future, and that our uncertainty is itself part of the picture, there is anything left for evidence to push against. If the honest answer is that the view is well insulated, I would rather hear that plainly, with an assessment of whether the insulation is a defect of the view or simply the price of any claim about the far future.
