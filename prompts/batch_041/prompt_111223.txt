I am a high school student working on a presentation for my school's philosophy club, and I have been reading Hume's chapter on miracles in the first Enquiry. I think I follow the basic shape of the argument: he begins from the nature of the evidence, reasoning that a miracle would be a violation of a law of nature and that the evidence establishing such a law is as firm as evidence of that kind can be, so that testimony for a miracle could only prevail if the falsity of the testimony were itself more extraordinary than the event it reports; and he then adds a second, more empirical argument, that the testimony we actually possess for miracles is untrustworthy, partly because the miracle stories of different religions undercut one another. What I would like you to do is imagine Hume brought into the present and asked to rewrite that chapter, because I suspect our situation cuts both ways and I want the analysis done carefully rather than glibly. Please take the role of an expert on Hume's epistemology and on the philosophy of testimony, and begin by brainstorming the features of the modern landscape that have no real analogue in his century.

Among the things I would like you to think through are these: recordings, whether audio, photographic, or video, that seem to make testimony about an event independent of any witness's honesty; hospital and laboratory records that document recoveries no physician can explain; the medical panels that some religious bodies still convene to certify cures they cannot account for; and above all the fact that testimony now circulates through networks that make it massively correlated rather than independent, since a single claim can reach millions of people who are not, for that reason, a million independent witnesses. For each of these, tell me as precisely as you can what Hume would say, which portions of his case survive untouched, which would have to be revised or given up, and whether he would regard the new material as strengthening or weakening his conclusion. I am especially curious about the second, empirical half of his argument, and about two questions in particular: whether the cancelling move still works when the competing miracle claims are all circulating in the same media environment rather than in separate traditions that never met, and whether a single well-documented, medically inexplicable cure gives him anything he did not already have in ordinary testimony. I also notice that readers disagree about whether his first argument is a claim about the logic of evidence or merely a prudent rule of thumb, and I would like you to say which reading makes his position strongest and what follows for the modern cases. Where his framework simply has nothing to say, please say so honestly rather than forcing it to answer everything.

Finally, consider seriously the possibility that he would refuse the exercise altogether, on the ground that whether any particular body of evidence clears his bar is a question of fact to be settled by weighing probabilities case by case, and that the lasting point of the chapter was never a blanket prohibition but a warning about how easily credulity and the pleasure of the marvellous distort judgment. If that is the right way to read him, I would like to know what he would then say about the modern cases, since the warning seems to apply to us at least as much as to his contemporaries. To close, please state your overall judgment in a fixed, machine-readable form: on the last line, write exactly \answer{...} with the braces containing one of the labels no-revision, minor-revision, major-revision, or abandonment, whichever best describes how much of Hume's case would have to change if he took the modern evidence seriously.
