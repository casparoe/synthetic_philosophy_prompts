I am writing a novel, and I need a joke to put in the mouth of one of its characters. One good joke is worth more to me than five passable ones, so please write a single joke and make it count.

The novel is set in a small forecasting firm: six analysts in a converted warehouse, selling probability estimates to insurers, to traders, and once, memorably, to a divorce lawyer. The founder came into the business from philosophy, and he pays the analysts according to a strictly proper scoring rule, meaning a rule under which reporting any credence other than the one you actually hold can only lower your expected score. The analysts complain about it constantly, it has never been revised, and they understand precisely why it cannot be: under a strictly proper rule, honesty is not a virtue but a dominant strategy, and the founder likes to say that the compensation formula is the only honest employee he has. They also understand the difference between being calibrated and being accurate. A forecaster can report 0.7 on the days that turn out to be 70% days, year after year, without a single one of those 0.7s being close to the truth about the particular day it was attached to. The score registers the difference; the clients never do.

The joke is told after hours, in a bar, by one analyst to the others, with the founder safely at home. It should be a real joke, with a setup and a turn, and one or two sentences will do if they are the right ones. It must be funny to someone who has genuinely thought about accuracy-first epistemology — the view that the sole fundamental epistemic good is accuracy, closeness to the truth, and that everything else a credence is supposed to do (obey the axioms of probability, update by conditionalization as the evidence arrives, defer to known chances) is supposed to fall out of that single commitment. The laugh should come from the content of the view, from a consequence of it that a listener recognizes as correct and had not quite noticed. I do not want a joke about how hard it is to predict the future, or about forecasters being wrong, or a generic joke about nerds. I want one that lands only on someone who knows what a strictly proper score does and why anyone would care.

The analysts' humor runs to the bitter end of the theory: that a rule which makes lying unprofitable makes telling the truth compulsory, and therefore no credit to anyone; that a credence can be beyond reproach by one measure and useless by another; that a view promising to derive all of epistemology from a single good has no way of saying which measure of that good to use.

Give me the joke, and then a sentence telling me what it turns on, so I can tell whether the laugh is where I believe it is. If a second joke turns on a different feature of the theory rather than restating the first, include it; otherwise stop at one.
