Last night I got into a three hour arguement with a friend who does philosophy, about money pumps, and I want to turn it into something I can actually use at work.

Context: I'm a decision analyst at a small consultancy. We help public bodies choose between big irreversible things — a reservoir siting, closing a ward, which of three sites gets the depot. We elicit preferences from a client committee in a day-long workshop, then build a scoring model that they sign and I sign and that ends up in a published report. Two or three of these a year, and they're expensive to get wrong.

My friend's line: if a committee's preferences are cyclic or incomplete, a money pump proves they're irrational, full stop, and our job is to keep nudging the preferences until they're consistent. My line: sometimes the cycle is real, the values are genuinely plural, and the pump needs an infinite series of trades, so there's no step at which anyone is actually worse off — calling that exploitation is a cheat. We definately didn't settle it. Also our committees aren't the same people from one meeting to the next, and I don't even know who the agent getting pumped is supposed to be there.

So: take the role of a decision theorist who also has to sit in the room with people who've never heard the phrase "diachronic rationality." Think carefully, and brainstorm the kinds of vulnerabilities that actually matter here before you commit to a structure.

Give me a procedure I can run in a workshop: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with "this structure is exploitable, don't paper over it," where it stops with "leave it alone," and where it hands the decision back to the committee because it's a values question and not mine to fix. It has to be usable by someone with no philosophy training — keep the distinctions that matter, drop the ones that don't, and tell me what you dropped and what that costs. It should also flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. The cases it has to handle: a committee with a strict cycle over three sites where no money appears in any of the comparisons; a committee that says two of the options simply aren't comparable; and a committee whose discounting of near versus far years flips depending on when in the process you ask them. Then run it on all three, including the one where it tells me something I won't want to hear.

Tell me how confident you are in the procedure as a whole, and single out the one part of it you're least sure about.
