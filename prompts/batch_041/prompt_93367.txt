Here's my thesis, roughed out. Everyone agrees the old story — famine as natural disaster, Malthus, act of God — is dead, and the Sen-flavored replacement is now the orthodoxy: famines are about entitlements, about who can no longer command food, and so they're political. I want to argue the orthodoxy is half right and half misleading. Calling a famine a "political crime" smuggles in intent and a perpetrator, and most famines have neither — nobody sets out to starve a province. What they have instead is a state committed to some *other* priority (winning a war, hitting an output target, not distorting a market) and a group of people the state's information system simply never registers as a cost it has to pay. So my frame is that famines are better read as failures of *political standing*: the starving fell outside the set of people whose hunger was legible to power as something that had to be answered. I try it out on the Bengal famine, the Irish one, and the Great Leap Forward — not as a general law, just three cases where the same shape shows up under completely different politics.
