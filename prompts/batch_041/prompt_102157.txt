I'm a journalist who covers AI and cognitive science, and next month I'm guest-running a session of a science-and-ideas reading group in my city — about 16 people, mixed backgrounds: a few grad students, two software engineers, a retired judge, one guy who's genuinely good at puzzles, everyone else just curious. I get 90 minutes and I want them doing something, not listening to me. The idea I want them to feel the force of is the idealization that AI-safety folks call HCH, and the broader factored-cognition program around it: the picture where a hard question gets answered by a tree of small, individually-trustworthy sub-answers, and no single node in the tree ever sees the whole problem — a kind of cleaned-up ideal of deliberation where a mind (or a firm, or a model) reasons by farming out bounded little questions to copies of itself. The thing I want them to run into on their own, before I name anything, is that from inside that setup you can't tell a well-factored, honest chain of small steps from a chain that has quietly laundered one plausible mistake all the way to the top. Right now they'd all just nod at that sentence; I want them to have felt it in their hands first.

So design me the session. Setting and constraints are above; change the details if you have something better, but keep it runnable by one facilitator with no tech beyond index cards, envelopes, and a timer.

What I'm picturing, roughly: split the room into two teams of eight on two matched logic-grid puzzles of the same difficulty, each with a unique solution (I'll write the puzzles and hold the answer keys). Team Flat deliberates normally — everyone sees everything, whiteboard, talk out loud, 25 minutes. Team Tree runs the HCH protocol: only the Root Agent gets the actual puzzle. Everyone else is a consultant who receives exactly one card with one question and nothing else — no context, no seeing the other cards, no talking. Consultants can sub-contract by writing a new question on a fresh card and mailing it back through a courier (me), so the tree can go a couple of levels deep. The Root assembles a final answer plus a confidence number. I want a rule that a card may not refer to anything not defined on that card — watch what happens when people try to say "the third suspect" or "the number from the earlier card," because I suspect that friction is where the idealization really bites. Then a second tree round where I slip one subtle but wrong sub-answer into a leaf and see if it surfaces or rides all the way up looking perfectly respectable.

I need: the rules written out cleanly enough that I can hand them to strangers, the roles, the courier mechanics, a payoff scheme that creates a little real temptation — I'm thinking points rather than money, maybe paying people for *answering* cards rather than for answering them *right*, plus a penalty for a confident wrong answer, and something that converts to who picks the snacks next month. A timeline for the 90 minutes. A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the pieces of the idea — decomposition, bounded context per node, the aggregation step, the verification gap, the assumptions the idealization quietly makes about honest consultants and unambiguous language — and that saves all the vocabulary for the debrief. Be blunt about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I already suspect the Flat team will just win on time and everyone will conclude the tree is a handicap, and that one loud person will hijack the Root role) and how to handle each. And tell me honestly which feature of the idealization this game *can't* reproduce, so I can say so out loud instead of pretending the activity covered it.

Oh and one practical thing — how do I pick puzzle pairs so the tree version is big enough that eight people can't just hold it in working memory for 25 minutes, but small enough that they actually finish? I keep getting that wrong when I design stuff.
