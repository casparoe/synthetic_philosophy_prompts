My roommate and I have been going back and forth about the modal version of the ontological argument and I've written out a version that seems to prove its own opposite. I can't find the bad step. Here it is:

(1) A maximally great being, if it exists at all, exists necessarily. Something that could have failed to exist isn't maximally great.
(2) Whatever is possibly necessary is necessary -- if there's a world where P holds in every world, then P holds in every world.
(3) A maximally great being is at least possible. There's no hidden contradiction in the idea, it isn't like a square circle.
(4) So a maximally great being exists.

Now I run the same machinery:

(5) A maximally great being is at least possibly absent. Again, no contradiction in that; nobody thinks atheism is incoherent.
(6) So a maximally great being does not exist.

(3) and (5) can't both be true, and each looks just as modest as the other, so the arguemnt seems to establish exactly as much as its mirror image, which is to say nothing. My roommate says that's the end of it: the whole thing is a wash, pick whichever premise you find more intuitive and stop. But I don't think it's a wash. I think I've smuggled something into a step and I want to know which. So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can -- (1) because it seems like it's just what maximal greatness means, and (2) because I lean on S5 constantly and don't want to give that up -- but price it for me honestly. Also tell me whether the two halves are really as seperate and symmetric as I've made them look.
