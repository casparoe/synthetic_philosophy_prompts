i'm giving a talk on letterforms next month and want to pressure-test the view i've landed on before i commit to it. here it is: (1) whether a shape is an 'a' is a real fact about the shape, not something we decide — there are borderline cases, but borderline doesn't mean anything goes. (2) no feature is shared by all 'a's: drop the closed bowl, the tail, the single-storey form, the x-height, and a thing can still be an 'a', so it's family resemblance the whole way down. (3) family resemblance by itself can't separate an 'a' from an 'e', or from a doodle that happens to trip enough of the cluster, so being an 'a' has to be relative to a style — a shape is an 'a' in font F partly because of how it sits against F's 'b' and 'd'. (4) styles are holistic that way: you can't say what F's 'a' is without dragging in the rest of F. (5) still, the 'a' in Garamond and the 'a' in Futura are the same letter, not two letters that happen to share a name, and you can recognise a lone 'a' with no font context at all. (6) a shape that's a perfectly good 'a' rotated 180 degrees isnt an 'a' — an upside-down 'n' is a 'u', not an 'n', and letters seem to work like that.

so: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest revision that saves the most? rank the tensions by how much damage they do, and tell me whether a single change cleans up the worst of them.
