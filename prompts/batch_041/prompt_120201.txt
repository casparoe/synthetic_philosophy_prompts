I teach a third-year elective in the philosophy of rational choice, taken by a mixed cohort of about twenty undergraduates and MSc students, and I have a single seventy-five-minute session for the topic of sunk costs. I want the session to run on a game that the participants play in teams rather than on a lecture, and I want them to leave unsure which of two verdicts to give on a case they have just lived through.

The verdict I want them to feel the pull of, and then to lose confidence in, is the standard one: that honouring a sunk cost is always irrational, because only marginal costs and benefits can bear on what to do next. Arrange the game so that this verdict is plainly right in one arm of play and defensible in another, so that the tension is produced by what the participants do rather than by anything I say afterwards.

In the first arm, teams invest in a project over several rounds and the expenditure already sunk is pure noise with respect to the project's remaining prospects, so that persistence can only be explained by attachment to what has been spent. In the second arm, hold the payoff structure fixed but make the accumulated expenditure informative: the per-round cost of the project is itself fixed by the project's hidden type, so that a project which has absorbed a great deal of funding is, on the evidence available to the team, more likely to be one of the good types. In a third arm, keep the expenditure uninformative but make the teams' continuation decisions observable to a player who controls the funding of subsequent rounds — a banker — so that persistence purchases credibility and quitting is penalised. Sequence the arms so that the group plays the first, commits to a verdict, and only then meets the second and third, and build the payoff scheme (chips, points, a public scoreboard, no real money) so that the three arms are exactly comparable, so that no team can identify which arm is the trick by reading the numbers.

What they should come away seeing is that the charge of irrationality is a claim about the status of a consideration — a reason, a signal, or an incentive — and not a claim about which choices happen to pay.

Then write the debrief, at whatever length the timing allows, mapping particular moments in the session onto particular claims: the difference between treating an irrecoverable cost as a reason and treating it as evidence; the difference between the rationality of a choice given the state of the world and the rationality of the policy or rule that produced the choice, which is where the precommitment, reputation, and elimination-tournament arguments live; and the further question whether the evidential reading dissolves the charge of irrationality or merely relocates it, including the case in which the agent's other information screens the expenditure off, so that its evidential force is contingent on what else is known. I would like the debrief to close on the residual figure: the agent who sincerely takes the sunk cost itself to be a consideration in favour of continuing, and whether the activity can make that agent intelligible without making that agent correct.

Give me the rules, the materials, the roles, and a timeline minute by minute. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect the arithmetic and the team dynamics to be the main sources of noise — and how to handle each failure without abandoning the design. And be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply myself. No bibliography and no suggested readings; state positions and arguments in your own words.
