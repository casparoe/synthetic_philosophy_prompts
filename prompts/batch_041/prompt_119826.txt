I am setting the final paper for a master's module on rational choice and decision theory, and I want one long question on the absent-minded driver problem — the case, standard in the literature on imperfect recall, of a driver who cannot tell the two intersections on his route apart and cannot remember whether he has already passed one. The difficulty is that my students can all reproduce the usual computation from the lecture notes, and last year's scripts were dispiritingly uniform: almost everyone wrote down the expected value, differentiated it, found the same fraction, and concluded that the puzzle dissolves because the plan is optimal. I want a question that forces them to say precisely why the local reasoning and the global reasoning agree, and then to confront what is left over once they have said it. Please write the question itself, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme.

The question should be pitched at 90 minutes and 25 marks, with roughly the following shape, though you may improve the phrasing and the balance. First, the students should be given the full payoff structure in the question — exiting at the first intersection yields 0, exiting at the second yields 4, and continuing past both yields 1 — and asked to say what a strategy can even be in this problem, to compute the optimal plan and its value, and to compare that value with what the two pure plans deliver. Second, they should work out what the driver ought to believe about his location at the moment he finds himself at an intersection, and show that if he is following the optimal plan he is exactly indifferent at that moment between continuing and exiting; they should then show that, in general, the node-level comparison between continuing and exiting is proportional to the derivative of the ex-ante value with respect to the continuation probability, and say what this implies about whether the case is a genuine dynamic inconsistency. Third, they should suppose that the driver can randomise only at departure, over whole plans — committing in advance either to continue at every intersection or to exit at every one — and compare the best value available that way with the value from the first part, saying what the comparison shows about the equivalence of mixed and behavioural strategies when recall is imperfect, and noting what the driver would obtain if he could distinguish the intersections. Fourth, and this is the part I care about most, they should test whether the puzzle is an artefact of my particular numbers: taking the payoffs to be 0 for exiting at the first intersection, a for exiting at the second, and b for overshooting, they should find the payoffs for which the optimal plan is to continue with probability one half, verify that the node-level indifference survives there, and then offer a defended reading of what the real philosophical issue is — whether the case shows that plan-optimality and act-optimality come apart without perfect recall, whether it requires treating the driver at an information set as a locus of genuine indeterminism, or something else altogether — engaging at least one rival reading and saying what it gets wrong. In the marking scheme, give the must-hit points with the marks attached (total 25), say which specific errors should cost marks and what misconception each of those errors reveals, and state the difference between an A answer and a B answer in terms of the moves each contains.
