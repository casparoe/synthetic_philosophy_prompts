Six months ago I took over the accounts at a small manufacturing firm that my family has operated for two generations, and in the course of an ongoing argument with a relative who believes that a depressed region can be revived simply by putting more money into circulation, I was sent back to Hume's essay Of Money, which I was assured settles the question. It has not settled anything for me. I keep returning to the opening paragraph and finding that I cannot state in my own words what Hume is claiming about money, because the claims seem to pull against one another. The paragraph, as I have it, reads:

MONEY is not, properly speaking, one of the subjects of commerce; but only the instrument which men have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another. It is none of the wheels of trade: It is the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy. If we consider any one kingdom by itself, it is evident, that the greater or less plenty of money is of no consequence; since the prices of commodities are always proportioned to the plenty of money, and a crown in HARRY VII.'s time served the same purpose as a pound does at present.

A few paragraphs later, in the same essay, I find this:

The greater number of people and their greater industry are serviceable in all cases; at home and abroad, in private, and in public. But the greater plenty of money, is very limited in its use, and may even sometimes be a loss to a nation in its commerce with foreigners.

What I want is a reading of these two passages taken together, not a summary of the essay and not a textbook label. Specifically:

1. The figure of oil and wheels. Oil is not a wheel; but a machine that lacks oil does not run, and a machine that has more oil than it needs runs no better for the surplus. Which of these does Hume mean? Does the figure commit him to money being causally necessary but causally contentless, and is that a coherent position to hold?

2. The qualifier "if we consider any one kingdom by itself." What work is it doing? Does the claim about money's inconsequence hold only for a closed economy, and if so, does that make the claim trivial, or does it make it a substantive thesis about what money does within a system whose boundaries are stipulated?

3. The phrase "of no consequence." Of no consequence for what — for real output, for welfare, for the distribution of goods, for the level of prices? It cannot be for prices, since the very same sentence says prices are proportioned to the plenty of money. So the claim must be about something else; I want to know what, and whether Hume has the vocabulary to draw that distinction as sharply as the question requires.

4. The relation between the two passages. If money is only oil, why should a nation's plenty of money ever be "a loss"? And what is lost — wealth, trade, industry, something else?

5. Finally: is there a single consistent doctrine here, or is Hume of two minds? If the passages can be reconciled, give me the reconciliation and tell me what it costs. If they cannot, tell me which reading of the essay the rest of it supports, and which sentence I should stop treating as load-bearing.

You may take the paragraph sentence by sentence if that is the clearest route, but the deliverable I care about is the last question. Do not tell me that this is the quantity theory of money and leave it there, and do not cite secondary literature; I have no library and no way to check references, and the labels I can find on my own are precisely what has failed to help.
