Bit of an odd request so bear with me. I'm a science journalist — evolution, mind, that beat — and I moonlight running a weekly seminar for upper-year students in our science-and-society program. Twelve weeks of Dennett and his critics, the cranes and skyhooks thing, greedy reductionism, universal acid, the lot. Final's in three weeks and I'm writing it now.

Every year I set an essay question on this material and every year I get the same paper back. Cranes good, skyhooks bad, greedy reductionists skip the cranes, Darwin 1859, Dennett 1995, the end. They can recite the labels and they can't use them. I want a question where reciting the distinction gets you a C at best.

So here's what I want it to force. I'm going to put four mini-cases on the exam sheet, and I'll paste them here so your model answer can actually chew on them:

1. A researcher says depression is "just" a wiring problem and that the psychological level should be retired once the circuits are mapped.
2. Someone says consciousness is a fundamental feature of the world, like charge or spin.
3. A critic says evolution can't generate real moral oughts, so the evolutionary story has a hole in it that no amount of biology will fill.
4. Someone says a high-level pattern — a developmental constraint, or market regularities, pick your poison — actually constrains what the molecules underneath are allowed to do.

For each one the students have to say whether the explanation on offer is a crane or a skyhook, and whether the objection to it is a legit demand for a crane or an instance of greedy reductionism. The bit they always fluff is that greedy reductionism isn't the opposite of reductionism, it's a vice inside it, and the same move can be a crane or a hook depending on whether the lower-level machinery is actually specified or just waved at. And I want them to be able to say when the demand for a lower-level story is itself unreasonable. I think case 3 is the interesting one there — the complaint isn't obviously a skyhook even though it's asking for something no crane seems to deliver. I want them to take a stand on whether that's a real gap or a category error about what an ought even is.

So: please write the essay question itself (45 minutes, they should be writing around 800 words), a model answer of 600-700 words that actually does the sorting and commits on case 3, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with the marks for each. For each point add a one-line note on how students usually get it wrong. Somewhere in the scheme make explicit what seperates an A from a B — I don't want a rubric that just rewards name-dropping the terms. Also a small thing: they can't look anything up during the exam and I don't want the scheme keyed to page numbers, so no citations from you either. If you need to name a source once to fix where the terms come from, fine, but the answer has to stand on its own. I've been burned before by rubrics that only work if everyone's read the same edition, and honestly alot of my students read the secondary stuff anyway.
