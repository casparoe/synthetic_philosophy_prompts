For a seminar on population ethics I have to write a short reflective-equilibrium piece: I lay out my considered verdicts on a range of population cases and then work out what axiology, if any, they add up to. Before I commit to it, I want the set stress-tested.

Throughout, I'm imagining myself as a benevolent designer choosing which of two worlds to make actual. Welfare is measured on a scale with 0 as the neutral point: a life at 0 is neither worth living nor worth not living, positive lives are worth living, negative lives are worth not living. Unless I say otherwise, nothing varies between the worlds except people's welfare levels and how many people there are.

1. Small addition. World A: ten million people at 100. World B: those same ten million at 100, plus another ten million at 5. No one in B is worse off than in A, and the newcomers' lives are worth living. Verdict: B is better than A. Bringing worthwhile lives into existence is a good thing.

2. The large trade-off. World C: ten million people at 100. World D: ten trillion people at 5. Verdict: C is better than D, and not by a little. I do not accept that an enormous population of barely-worth-living lives beats a small flourishing one.

3. Neutral additions. World E: ten million at 100. World F: ten million at 100 plus ten million at exactly 0. Verdict: E and F are equally good; adding neutral lives changes nothing.

4. Wretched additions. World G: ten million at 100. World H: ten million at 100 plus ten million at -20. Verdict: H is worse than G.

5. Authenticity. World I: ten million at 100 plus ten million at 5, where the newcomers are exactly as well off as their level says, but only because they are systematically deceived about their situation. World J: the same, except the newcomers' equal welfare rests on true beliefs. Verdict: J is better than I. So even with welfare held fixed, how that welfare is realized seems to matter to me.

6. Identity. World K: ten million people at 100. World L: ten million entirely different people at 100, with the original ten million never existing. Verdict: K and L are equally good; swapping in a wholly different population at the same welfare changes nothing.

I can already feel that these cannot all survive contact with one principle, and I'd rather find that out now than in front of the seminar. So don't hand me the standard menu of positions. Take a definite stand. Tell me which principle, or small set of principles, actually fits the verdicts, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and why that is the trade-off worth making rather than some other one. Then tell me what the surviving principle implies for two cases I haven't given a verdict on: (a) ten million at 100 versus ten million at 60 together with ten billion at 6; and (b) a world in which the added lives are worth living but their welfare depends on resources taken from the people who already exist. Please write in American English.
