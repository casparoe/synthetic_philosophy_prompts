I'm running a 90-minute session on Dutch book arguments for a mixed group — about a dozen people, mostly first- and second-year philosophy grad students, plus one economist friend of mine who keeps rolling his eyes at the way philosophers talk about probability. They'll have had some basic probability and a little expected utility theory and nothing else. Skip explaining the argument to me, I know it; I need you to plan the teaching of it. You're a philosopher of probability and decision theory who's taught this a hundred times, so think it through carefully — maybe start by brainstorming two or three candidate orderings and then commit to one. What I want first is the dependency spine: what has to be in place before what, so that it lands as an argument rather than as a parlor trick. My hunch is that the chain is longer than people admit — something like: credences come in degrees and are the sort of thing that can be measured at all; the measuring device is a bet at the agent's own rates of exchange; 'coherent' means no combination of bets she'd individually accept leaves her down in every outcome; the theorem says coherent iff finitely additive probability; and then a separate and much shakier interpretive step converts 'a hypothetical bookie could exploit you' into 'you ought to have probabilities'. If that's not the right spine, fix it and tell me what I've got out of order.

The carrying example is where I keep getting stuck. Everyone reaches for the three-horse race or the four-ticket lottery, and my students say 'well I just wouldn't take those bets' or 'the bookie is out to get me' or 'the loss is fifty cents, who cares'. So tell me what the example has to have — I assume a small partition where exactly one thing happens, each bet fair by the agent's own lights, the package bad in every outcome — and what it must not have (nowhere for risk aversion or a hedging instinct to rescue her, and I suspect it can't be denominated in money at all unless we've already done the money-versus-utility move, which is exactly the move I want them to notice). If the three-horse race can be repaired rather than replaced, say how; if not, give me the replacement and tell me what it costs. Then the misconception map, with timestamps: 'Dutch book means you'd really get caught by a real bookie', 'coherence is defined as what you'd actually bet', 'the theorem shows incoherent credences are impossible to have', 'the bookie has to know my credences', 'the whole thing begs the question because it assumes expected utility maximization', 'risk aversion saves me', 'probability is just betting rate, so it's circular', and the one my economist will raise, 'this is all about money and money isn't utility'. For each, the exact point in the sequence where I head it off, and which ones I should let stand for a while on purpose because answering them is the interesting part of the session. Finish with a check for understanding I can run in the last ten minutes that would separate 'can recite the three-horse race' from 'sees that the theorem is a claim about the geometry of a set of bets and that the philosophy is a further question'.

Also give me an honest cut list — I'm guessing the general proof, the diachronic version with the bet that gets called off if the conditioning event doesn't happen, and anything about countable additivity, since I take it the construction only ever forces finite additivity.
