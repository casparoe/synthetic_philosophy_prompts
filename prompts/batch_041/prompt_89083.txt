So for my history class we're doing a whole unit on the Holocaust and my teacher has set up this debate about the Browning vs Goldhagen thing — you know, were the guys doing the actual shooting just ordinary middle-aged family men from Hamburg who got caught up in it, or were they willing killers who did it because of a centuries-old antisemitic culture that made mass murder of Jews seem like the obvious thing to do? She says we have to actually commit to a position and defend it instead of saying "well it's probably a bit of both," which honestly is what everyone does, so I want to come at it from a different angle. I'm supposed to lead a session next week and I thought the cleanest thing would be a decision tree / flowchart that starts from questions people can actually answer about the case and ends at a named position, so that people can see which one they're already committed to without realizing it.

Here's the stuff we've been given so you can build it on real details: Reserve Police Battalion 101, middle-aged working-class men from Hamburg, drafted but not fit for regular army service, sent to occupied Poland in 1942, and at Józefów their commander told them that anyone who didn't feel up to the job could step out — fewer than twelve out of around five hundred did, and one man who did get himself released from the killing was apparently promoted once he was back in Germany. Browning's read is that this shows the men killed voluntarily but for pretty ordinary reasons — obedience to authority, not wanting to look like a coward in front of their buddies, careerism, the brutalizing effect of the war in the east. Goldhagen's read is that the opt-out offer proves nothing except how thoroughly antisemitism had soaked into German society, and that these were willing executioners, not reluctant ones. Our teacher also keeps bringing in Arendt's "banality of evil" idea and the sociologist Zygmunt Bauman's argument that this was a product of modern bureaucratic rationality, so those should probably be in there somewhere too.

What I want from you: a decision tree with about eight to ten leaves. Every branch node has to be a question a normal person could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and no branch can just be labeled with a position name (so not "are you a situationist? yes/no" — that defeats the whole point). Start from something like what level the explanation lives at — inside the individual, in the immediate situation, in the wider culture, or in institutions and bureaucracy — and then split further on the things that actually separate the views: whether the killers' beliefs matter or whether only the pressures on them do, whether antisemitism was a necessary cause or just background noise, whether the men really could have refused, and whether one explanation has to cover every unit or it's allowed to vary case by case. Leaves should include: a character/disposition view (sadists and authoritarian personalities), a Milgram-style obedience or "agentic state" view, a peer-conformity view, a careerism and material-interest view, a war-brutalization view, Goldhagen's eliminationist-antisemitism view, Arendt's thoughtlessness view, Bauman's bureaucratic-modernity view, and a "no single story, it depends on the unit" view — and if there's a slot nobody actually defends, tell me which one it is and why (is it incoherent, just unattractive, or has nobody bothered?). Also flag any two leaves your tree can't really pull apart, don't just paper over it. Keep the whole thing under 900 words, and if you have to cut, cut my scene-setting not the branches.
