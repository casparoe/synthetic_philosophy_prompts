I'm in a reading group working through Mill's Considerations on Representative Government and I've hit the chapter where he goes after what he calls false democracy — the idea that whichever side gets the majority of votes in each district should take the seat and the losers get nothing. I can follow him up to a point, but I think I'm smuggling in modern assumptions.

The gist of the bit I'm stuck on (paraphrasing here, not quoting): he says that in a genuinely equal democracy every section of opinion would be represented in proportion, not just the larger one; that a system where the majority in each locality wins everything and the minority is simply outvoted isn't really democracy but a kind of rule by the numerical majority as a class; and that the test of a good system is whether it represents all, not whether it hands power to the most numerous. He also seems to think that under winner-takes-all districts a minority spread out evenly gets erased entirely, while a minority that's geographically concentrated gets seats — so the system secretly rewards one kind of minority over another for reasons that have nothing to do with how many people actually hold the view.

Here's what I need help with. First: is he making a proportional representation argument in the sense we'd recognize now, or is he doing something else? He seems to want each person's vote to end up attached to a representative who actually shares their views, which could be PR or could be something stranger. Second: what's the actual logical step from "the minority is outvoted" to "the minority is unrepresented / oppressed"? Because a defender of first-past-the-post would say: you lost, that's what losing means, you're still a citizen with rights and the next election is in four years. Does Mill have a real reply to that or does he just assert it? Third: what work is the word "section" doing for him — is it a party, an opinion, a class, a region? The argument seems to shift depending on which one he means, and if he means class then the whole thing looks different than if he means a minority political opinion. Fourth, and this is the one really bugging me: does his argument depend on being represented as itself a good, apart from getting the policies you want? Because if representation is only instrumentally valuable, then a minority that consistently loses but still gets decent outcomes has nothing to complain about, and I can't tell whether he'd accept that or whether that's exactly the view he's attacking.

Don't give me a history of PR or a list of who said what — I want the structure of the argument and where the load-bearing premises are, and if you think I'm misreading him, tell me what the misreading is.
