I want a sestina about the idea that the brain is a computer, but the annoying version of it. Here's the thing that nags at me: if "implements the computation" just means "you can describe what it does in computational terms," then it looks like almost anything implements almost any computation -- a rock, a puddle, the weather -- and "the brain is a computer" stops saying anything at all. But tighten the definition enough to rule out the rock and you risk ruling out real brains too, or you have to name the extra ingredient that actually matters (causation? the right causal structure? being the thing the system really uses?). I want the poem to sit in that tension rather than pick a side.

Use the sestina's six end-words as the load-bearing vocabulary of the debate -- pick six words that do real work (something like state, rule, map, step, matter, same) so that each stanza re-describes the mind in the same six terms in a new order, and the permutation itself feels like the point: same computation, different substrate, over and over. Save the turn for the envoi, where all six have to cram into three lines -- thats where I want the squeeze: whether the pattern can ever hand you the felt thing, or whether re-running it just gives you more pattern. Dont name any philosophers. It should read as a real poem first and a philosophy argument second.
