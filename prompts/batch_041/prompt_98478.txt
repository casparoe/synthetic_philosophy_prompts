Please describe the most important implications of Keynes's account of probability and uncertainty for the practical reasoning of a small decision-making body — for instance, the risk committee of a mid-sized charitable foundation. Keynes treats probability as a logical relation between propositions rather than a mere degree of belief, insists that a proposition's probability be distinguished from the weight of the argument in its favour, and holds that there are future events about which no calculable probability can be formed at all. I would like the implications drawn out concretely: whether the committee's unanimous agreement, when every member reasons from the same body of evidence, should be read as strong confirmation or rather as a sign that the weight of the argument is thin; how the committee should proceed when it can assign no single number to a risk and cannot even rank two risks by magnitude; and what follows for the value of delay, of hedging, and of falling back on convention when the situation is uncertain in Keynes's sense. Please attend throughout to where these implications diverge from what a standard expected-utility treatment would recommend, and give me the consequences for practice rather than a general summary of the theory.
