Ok so here's a thing that keeps happening in design reviews and I want to know if it has a name.

We're arguing about whether some rare race condition will actually bite us in prod. One guy says there are roughly a million possible interleavings of these three threads and exactly one of them is bad, so it's one in a million. Another says look, either it happens or it doesn't, and we've all seen this class of bug before, so call it 50/50. A third goes through three years of incident reports and says the base rate for this kind of thing is about 1 in 40 releases. Nobody is being dumb, everyone is using real evidence, and the answers come out five orders of magnitude apart. And after a while its clear the arguement was never really about the evidence — it's about what counts as one case. Is a case an interleaving, a release, a production day? Whichever you pick, that's your probability, and each pick looks completely natural to the person making it. Some of them are reasoning from the mechanism, some from the frequency, and from the inside neither feels like a choice at all.

The part that bothers me is that whoever gets to set the granularity basically gets to set the answer, and they never have to say out loud that that's what they're doing. So you end up defending a number when what you're actually defending is how to carve up the possibilities, and that never gets argued about directly.

I'm pretty sure this is somewhere near Bertrand's paradox (the chord in the circle one), and I've heard "principle of indifference" and "reference class problem" thrown around. What I can't tell is whether what I'm describing is just one of those, or whether it's a narrower thing: the carving getting picked, maybe without anyone realizing, because of the number it spits out. Does that have a name? If not, coin one, and tell me exactly where it sits next to Bertrand's paradox and the reference class problem — what it shares with each and the point where it stops being them. A name on its own won't help me. I want to know what the thing is and isn't, and where the edges are: does it stop being this if more data settles the argument? If two people independently pick the same granularity? What if everyone agrees on the granularity, but for reasons that are really about the answer they wanted?
