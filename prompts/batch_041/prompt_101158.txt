Had a proper row with my mate Dan last night. He's got a kid on the way, I made some offhand remark about that old asymmetry business — that we tend to think it'd be bad to bring a miserable person into the world but don't think it's any loss when a happy person never gets born, since if nobody exists there's nobody to have lost anything — and he went off on one about it for about an hour. Anyway, lying awake afterwards I kept trying to put it into my own language, because I do the books for a couple of small firms and I think in debits and credits, not philosophy.

So here's what I want. Take that asymmetry and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: double-entry, every credit needs a debit, accruals, provisions, contingent assets versus contingent liabilities, the prudence principle where you book a probable loss the moment you can see it coming but never book a hoped-for gain until it's basically certain, counterparties, the fact that nothing gets into the ledger at all without a transaction, and the going concern assumption.

The four bits I want translated one by one: (1) suffering is bad when it's there, (2) pleasure is good when it's there, (3) the absence of suffering is good even though there's no one around who's having that good, (4) the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there's somebody who's been deprived of it.

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually care about. Which of those four books cleanly in my language? Which can only be got in by fudging — booking to a counterparty who doesn't exist, or dressing an empty case up as a nil entry? And which one can't be said at all — and is that because double-entry is missing something, or because the original claim was up to something dodgy, like quietly reinstating a subject in the very case where it says there isn't one? My hunch is that the prudence principle is doing most of the work and the rest is hand-waving, but I'm definately not sure, so push back on me if I've got that wrong.
