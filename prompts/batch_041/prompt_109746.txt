I've talked myself into a relational view of personhood — the one where being a person isn't a biological fact you're issued at birth but a standing you earn and keep by being recognized by, and participating in, a community. I'm taking it seriously as a metaphysical and ethical thesis, not just a nice slogan: so personhood comes in degrees (you can be more or less of a person, and you can lose it), and the relevant community isn't just the currently living adults — it includes the dead and the not-yet-born.

What would count against it? Give me the defeaters, ranked from would-sink-it down to merely awkward. I want the cases it can't describe or has to describe badly (the hermit, the infant, someone with severe cognitive disability, someone whose community refuses to recognize them at all), the consequences it's committed to that I might not actually want, and the places where it threatens to collapse into a rival — either a flatly sociological claim that whoever a community happens to recognize as a person just is one, or a dressed-up individualism that never really left the atomistic picture behind. Tell me which of these a committed relationalist would themselves concede count against them, not just the ones critics like to raise, and whether the view has been quietly qualified into something that can't lose — and if it has, whether that's a real problem here or just the price of any view about persons.

Skip the generic communitarian-vs-liberal stuff, I know that debate. I want the specific pressure points. For what its worth, the case that would move me most is one where the view makes me say someone isn't really a person and that verdict just seems plainly wrong.
