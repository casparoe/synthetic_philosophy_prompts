I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory, and our professor has asked us to evaluate a family of decision rules for cases in which an agent's credence is represented not by a single probability function but by a convex set P of them. The five rules on the table are: maximality, on which an act is permissible just in case no alternative has at least as high an expected utility for every member of P; E-admissibility, on which an act is permissible just in case it maximizes expected utility for at least one member of P; interval dominance, on which an act is permissible just in case its expected-utility interval is not wholly below that of some alternative; maximin, on which one chooses the act with the highest minimum expected utility across P; and a proposal from a classmate that I'll call the shape-sensitive rule, on which one first assigns a second-order credence to each member of P in proportion to how central it is to the set, then maximizes the resulting expected expected utility. Please rank these five rules from most to least defensible as a general account of rational choice under imprecise credences, and give each a score out of ten. I'm especially interested in whether the shape-sensitive rule can avoid the charge that it illicitly turns the mere fact of imprecision into a reason for caution, and in whether maximin and interval dominance should be demoted for being insensitive to how the imprecision is distributed. Please write continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
