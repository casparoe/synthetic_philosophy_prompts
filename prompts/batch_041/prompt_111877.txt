I help run a fortnightly reading group on animal ethics and we've been stuck for about six months on a question I assumed would take one session, so I'd like you to help me work out what kind of disagreement we're actually having rather than who's right.

Here's the tangle. Take seriously the idea that wild animals matter and that an awful lot of them live short, nasty lives. The question then is what someone who cares about that should eat — and in particular whether beef or chicken is the worse choice. Camp one says the chicken is the atrocity, because chickens are fed on crops and crop cultivation is a bloodbath: combines, ploughs, pesticides, mowing through mice, voles, birds and insects in enormous numbers per calorie harvested. On that accounting pasture-reared beef, which is mostly grass and a cow standing on it, comes out ahead. Camp two says that's a mirage, because beef eats vastly more land per calorie, land is habitat, and habitat is animals — so the beef is carrying a much larger wild-animal footprint once you count the land and not just the machinery.

We've read everything either side points to and nobodys moved. My hunch — and I'd like you to test it rather than just nod along — is that it isn't really an empirical dispute at all, or not only one, because it seems to turn on a prior question neither side states out loud: whether the lives of the wild animals in question are, on balance, worth living. If wild lives are net positive then you want more of them, and crop farming (which suppresses them) looks worse. If wild lives are net negative — and some of the literature seems to lean that way — then anything that thins out wild populations, including the land-hungry beef, starts to look like a mercy, and the whole per-calorie-deaths calculation flips. I can't tell whether I've found the real hinge here or just added a third thing for us to argue about.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? A hidden empirical question (we simply don't know the field-death densities or the land-use multipliers), a normative one (do insect deaths count, does a mouse's death weigh the same as a chicken's, is a life taken by a harvester worse than one taken by a fox), or two sides quietly answering different questions — one asking 'deaths per calorie', the other 'suffering per calorie'? And where does my net-sign-of-wild-lives hunch fit: is it the hidden pivot, a red herring, or just a re-labelling of the empirical dispute? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a study, a conceptual distinction, a change of question? Or should we expect this one to run forever, and if so, why? I'd rather have the diagnosis with reasons than a verdict on beef versus chicken.
