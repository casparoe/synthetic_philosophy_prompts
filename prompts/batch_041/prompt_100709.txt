I have been reading a short popular book on the foundations of probability, and before I let it settle my view I would like an audit of its central summary. The book presents the three leading interpretations as three answers to the question of what a probability really is, and its own recap runs roughly as follows.

On the frequentist view, a probability is the relative frequency of an outcome over a long run of repetitions, understood as the limiting value that the frequency approaches as the number of trials grows without bound. On the subjectivist, or Bayesian, view, a probability is a degree of belief — an individual's private level of confidence that a proposition is true. On the propensity view, a probability is a real disposition or tendency of a physical setup, such as this coin's tendency to land heads, which the setup possesses whether or not it is ever actually tried. The book then observes that frequentists and propensity theorists are both objectivists, holding that probabilities are features of the world that we discover, while the subjectivist is the odd one out: on that view there is no fact of the matter about what a probability is, only facts about what people happen to believe, so that two people who assign different probabilities to one and the same event are merely reporting different opinions and neither of them is mistaken. Its verdict is that the propensity view is the strongest of the three, since it preserves the objectivity of frequentism while escaping frequentism's fatal flaw, the reference class problem — the difficulty that any event belongs to many classes with different frequencies, so that the frequency of the event itself is undefined — by attaching the probability to the particular setup rather than to a class. The book adds that the propensity view enjoys a second advantage over subjectivism: it is testable in a way that a private degree of belief is not.

I find myself inclined to accept the verdict, chiefly because I have never been comfortable with the thought that probabilities are just opinions, but the way the three views are lined up and dispatched feels too tidy to be trusted. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me, for each, what a competent defender of the relevant interpretation would accept as fair, what they would wince at as an oversimplification or a distortion, and what is straightforwardly wrong — flagging especially any place where the book is quietly running together two things that the view keeps apart. Then tell me whether the book's conclusion survives: is the propensity view best because it is both objective and testable, and does subjectivism really leave two disagreeing agents with no grounds on which either could be criticized, or does that verdict rest on the misreadings?
