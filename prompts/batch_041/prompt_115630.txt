You are a philosopher of mathematics and computer science who has taught computational complexity for years to students who arrive assuming complexity theory is just engineering. I run a single 90-minute session for sixteen upper-level undergraduates in a philosophy of computation course, and I want them to feel the force of three things at once: that verifying a proposed solution can be easy while finding one is not, that hardness travels through reductions so that a single efficient method for one complete problem would collapse an entire class, and that nothing in the mathematics itself tells us which side of that line we are on -- which is why the encryption we all rely on is a bet about the shape of the complexity landscape rather than a theorem. Design a runnable activity for that session. The point has to land in what happens, not in what I say afterward, so arrange roles, rules, and payoffs (no real money; points, tokens, or bragging rights are fine) so that the students walk into the asymmetry themselves before anyone names it. Build in a moment where one group's problem looks just like the others but turns out to be tractable, so that the room has to confront structure rather than size as the source of difficulty, and a moment where the group's shared failure has to be interpreted correctly -- as giving no evidence at all about whether the hard cases are inherently hard. Give me the materials I need to prepare, the sequence of the session with rough timings, the rules, what each team is trying to do, and how the handoffs between teams work. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the formal ideas, and a frank account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, including which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Size the instances so that naive search is hopeless within the time available, and say in your design how I should generate them so that the problems do not fall to a clever student in five minutes. Before you commit, consider two or three candidate activities and quietly discard the weaker ones; I want the one you would actually run. Write the whole thing in continuous prose -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables -- the way you would explain it in a long email to a colleague.
