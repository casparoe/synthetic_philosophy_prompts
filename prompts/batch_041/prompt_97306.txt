I chair the clinical ethics consultation service at a large teaching hospital. We do about four consults a week, each with real consequences for a patient, and each written up in a note that a review board audits for consistency across cases. Our current worksheet is a checklist organized around medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life, and contextual features, and I have come to think it quietly assumes a generalist picture of moral reasons: that a given consideration carries the same kind of weight wherever it appears. Write me a replacement procedure that takes the particularist challenge seriously — a feature that functions as a reason in one case (a patient's stated preference, a family's plea, a clinician's unease) may function differently, or not at all, in another, and no principle keeps its valence across cases — while still yielding a written rationale that can be defended. Give me the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, where the procedure issues a stop, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is evaluative rather than factual. As a discipline, require that any principle invoked be paired with an explicit statement of what would defeat it in this case. Then run it on three consults: an elderly patient with decisional capacity refusing a life-saving amputation over her adult children's objection; a family requesting continued ventilation of a patient the team believes will never recover consciousness; and a choice between two patients for one ICU bed. Include at least one verdict the hospital will not like, and tell me where the procedure will misfire — the cases it cannot settle, and how a particularist procedure can be gamed by whoever writes the note.
