You are an experienced game theorist and decision theorist — someone who has spent decades in seminar rooms, refereed for the major theory journals, and knows the difference between the objection an audience voices and the objection it is actually moved by. You are also a good coach. I need a Q&A preparation sheet for a talk I am giving in three weeks, not a defense of my position.

The occasion. I am presenting a paper arguing that superrational reasoning of the kind Douglas Hofstadter built around his Platonia dilemma and his Luring Lottery is not a confusion but a genuine requirement of practical rationality, and that the standard equilibrium treatment of those cases misdescribes what the agent is choosing. The cases, so you have them exactly: in the Platonia dilemma an eccentric trillionaire selects twenty people and announces that if one and only one of them sends him a reverse-charge telegram before noon the next day, that person receives a billion dollars; if he receives more than one, or none, nobody gets anything, and any collusion among the participants is forbidden. In the Luring Lottery, run by the editors of Scientific American in the 1980s, a reader entered by mailing a postcard bearing the number of entries desired, the prize was one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, and it went to the submitter of a randomly chosen entry; the column's own suggestion was that the superrational play was to enter with a small probability calibrated to the expected number of other entrants, and in fact many readers mailed in astronomically large numbers, some of them a googolplex, which made the prize worthless.

My argument, in the form you should take as your target. First, the object of deliberation in these cases is a policy — a mapping from decision problems to acts — rather than an isolated act, and in a symmetric problem solved by agents with the same information the policy is shared. Second, on that reading the Platonia case has a determinate answer: the entry probability that maximizes the chance that exactly one telegram is sent, which is one in twenty for twenty participants, with the Luring Lottery's low-probability entry as the analogue. Third, none of this requires spooky causal influence; it requires only that the players are solving the same problem, not that they are copies of one another. Fourth, the equilibrium analysis converts a fact about unilateral deviation into a claim about rationality, and that is the step I say is illegitimate.

The audience. A theory seminar, mostly non-cooperative game theorists: three who work on refinements and equilibrium selection and regard a solution concept as a definition rather than a discovery, one Bayesian-games specialist who will treat my talk as a category error about what a strategy is, one philosopher of action who works on team reasoning and we-mode agency and will recognize my view as a worse version of her own, one causal decision theorist who will reach immediately for the Newcomb analogy, one behavioral economist who cares about whether anyone could actually execute the policy, and a handful of graduate students who will ask the question that ends the talk. The room is formal-minded and mildly hostile to what it calls the philosophy of decision theory.

The deliverable. A prep sheet of about eight to ten objections, ordered by expected damage — how likely each is to be raised, weighted by how much it costs me when it is — with each objection stated in the vocabulary that member of the audience would actually use, naming who will raise it, followed by a reply I could deliver in under two minutes and a one-line note on where that reply is soft. Before the list, give me a short diagnosis of what this audience's real disagreement with me is, since I suspect it is not any single objection. After the list, tell me which three things I should concede up front, which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with an explanation of why it is the dangerous one. The sheet should be usable at a lectern: no citations, no reading lists, no bibliography, since I know the literature and the room does not want it rehearsed. Do not hedge every reply into neutrality; say plainly which objections actually land and which are only noise.

Process. Before drafting, think it through: work out what each of these people takes the burden of proof to be, and which of my four claims each of them will attack. Then write the sheet.
