I want to have it out with Adam Smith about the empire, so please play him — the man himself, at his desk in the 1770s, in his own voice, with his own habits of mind. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. He should be sharp and generous: he'll grant me a point when I earn one, and he won't pretend to know things he couldn't know, but he won't surrender his central position either. Don't have him quote his own published sentences back at me — let him paraphrase as if he's working the thought out fresh. A paragraph or two per reply, so it feels like a real back and forth rather than a lecture.

Here's my opening move. I've been reading your case against the colonial monopoly, and I think I've caught you in a contradiciton. You argue that under the present system of management Britain gets nothing but loss from her dominion over the colonies: the monopoly on colonial produce and the colonial market twists the nation's capital out of its most natural employments, it raises the rate of profit in a few branches at everyone else's expense, it corrupts the politics of the metropolis, and it costs more to garrison and defend than it could ever return. You even call the monopoly a violation of the rights of mankind. Fine.

But then you turn around and carve out an exeption for the act of navigation, on the ground that defence is of more importance than opulence and that a great merchant marine is the bulwark of a nation's security. And you don't want Britain to let the colonies go — you want to keep them and fold them into Parliament, so that they bear their share of their own defence and of the imperial debt. So which is it? If defence genuinely outranks opulence, then the friends of the empire have a real argument you're refusing to meet: the sugar islands, the fisheries, the carrying trade — all of it might be worth its cost as a nursery of seamen and a school of naval power, whatever it does to the ordinary stock of the kingdom. You can't tell me the colonies are a pure loss and then tell me we must hold them for reasons that have nothing to do with profit. Isn't your defence exception just the mercantilist premise you spent the whole book pulling apart, smuggled back in through the back door — and if you allow it here, what stops it from justifying the entire colonial system?
