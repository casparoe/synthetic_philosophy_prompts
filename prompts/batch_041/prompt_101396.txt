I am working through the section of al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers that deals with causation (commonly counted as the seventeenth discussion), and I would like help understanding one strand of the argument. I do not have a translation in front of me, so the following is my own paraphrase rather than a quotation; please treat it as a summary to be interpreted, not as a faithful rendering of any particular translation.

Al-Ghazālī sets out to deny that the connection between what we ordinarily call a cause and what we ordinarily call its effect is a necessary connection. On his account, the two are conjoined only because God has decreed a habitual order, not because one produces or acts upon the other. He takes the standard examples — drinking water and the quenching of thirst, eating bread and the arrival of satiety — and argues that the one is not the agent of the other. He then presses the case of fire and cotton: the philosophers hold that fire is a body with a nature, and that burning is its distinctive action; but if fire is inanimate, then burning cannot be an action that belongs to it in the way that, say, motion belongs to a moving body. So the fact that burning regularly accompanies contact with fire does not show that fire does the burning. Al-Ghazālī says the burning could instead be created directly by God, or created through some intermediary, and he insists that the philosophers have no demonstration that would rule this out. He also offers a thought experiment: a man who had never seen the sun, but who received light through a window whenever the sun was present, would naturally take the sun to be the cause of the light; yet God could produce the light without the sun, or the sun without the light. At the same time, he grants that God has in fact established a settled course of events, so that we may ordinarily expect fire to be followed by burning; what he denies is that this regularity is a necessity of the kind the philosophers claim. He then says that there are two ways of answering the philosophers: one may deny that fire is the agent of burning at all, or one may concede that fire is the agent but hold that God can prevent it from acting, as when the rod becomes a serpent or Abraham is unharmed in the fire.

What I cannot settle for myself is what exactly is being claimed and what is being denied. Is the thesis metaphysical — that created things have no causal efficacy whatever — or is it merely epistemic — that we cannot demonstrate a necessary connection, so that we have no right to rule out a miracle a priori? When he says the connection is not necessary, does he mean that there is no causal power in fire at all, or only that the power, if it exists, is not sufficient on its own and can be suspended? And how should the two answers he gives be weighed against each other: does the second answer (fire is an agent but can be prevented) commit him to a weaker position than the first, and if so, why does he not simply rest with the weaker one? I would also like to know whether his talk of a habitual order is meant to replace causation with something like constant conjunction plus a decree, or whether it is meant to leave the ordinary notion of causation intact for practical purposes while denying it any metaphysical necessity.
