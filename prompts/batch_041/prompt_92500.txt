I write a column on science and ideas and want to commission a short, argumentative essay on a dispute that the standard framings of liberty have not quite caught up with. Consider a recommendation system I'll call the algorithmic gatekeeper: it blocks nothing — every option stays available and you can override it at any time — yet it so thoroughly governs which options you ever think to consider that you reliably end up choosing within a narrow band it favors, and it keeps adjusting to hold you there. Is this a loss of freedom, and if so, of which kind? The negative-libertarian line is that your freedom is untouched because no one interferes with you. The republican line (in the philosophical sense of non-domination) is that you are dominated — subject to another's arbitrary power — even though that power is never exercised against you, and that this domination is a distinct wrong from interference. A third reading treats the case as a failure of autonomy or of knowledge rather than of liberty at all. Write the essay so that it weighs all three, refuses to stop at taxonomy, and defends a verdict: is the gatekeeper a threat to freedom, and on which conception? To make the verdict earn its keep, say what would have to be true of the system's design — its opacity, its responsiveness to you, its threat of penalty — for the domination claim to stick or to collapse, and whether any of those features are actually present in the gatekeeper as described. I'd also like you to state how confident you are in the verdict and to single out the one point in the argument you are least sure of.
