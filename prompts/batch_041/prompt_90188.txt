I'm a novelist, and my new book turns on a lottery of the Platonia kind — the one where the pot gets split between everyone who enters, so the clever-looking answer only works if everybody else has already reasoned their way to the same place you have, and where the whole thing collapses the moment a few people pile in for reasons that have nothing to do with reasoning (they misread the form, they're drunk, they enjoy spoiling it for everyone). I've been asked to be the visiting speaker at a decision theory reading group next month, and the thing I actually want to argue is this: the puzzle isn't a paradox of rationality at all, it's a coordination problem in a paradox's clothing, and "superrationality" is a norm that quietly assumes a population already persuaded of the argument — so its clever answer stops being an answer to anything once real people are in the room. And I think there's a nastier second limb: the puzzle only goes through if you're allowed to treat your own reasoning as a copy of everyone else's, and nobody has ever told me why I get to do that. My novel uses its own version of the rules, so if you need the mechanics pinned down to make the objections bite, pin them down yourself and say that's what you're doing.

Act as a decision theorist who's sat through far too many of these interdepartmental seminars and knows exactly how the room works. The audience: mostly microeconomists, one game theorist who runs lab experiments and is allergic to anything that smells of philosophy, a philosopher of action who'll want to talk about intentions and what it means to "decide for everyone", and two literary critics who came for the fiction and will ask why my characters can't just talk to each other. Think carefully, and brainstorm a bit first, about who in that room cares about what. Then give me my prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get — not the ones I'd get from a room full of rationalists — ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first minute so I don't look like a crank, and finish with the question I'm dreading, the one I'm hoping nobody asks. No reading lists, no references, I can't look anything up from the podium, and don't be polite about the weak bits — I'd rather find them here than up there. British spelling, obviously.
