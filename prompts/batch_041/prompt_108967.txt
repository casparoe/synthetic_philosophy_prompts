I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, splitting my time between the philosophy of mind and the history of early analytic philosophy, and I'm drafting a chapter on why the question of life's meaning so often gets treated as confused rather than as simply hard. Ramsey's Epilogue — the short piece printed at the end of the posthumous collection of his logical essays — keeps surfacing in that literature, usually cited as an early statement of the view that the question is ill-formed rather than open. I'm at a summer institute right now and my books are in storage, so I've been reconstructing the argument from my notes. What follows is my own paraphrase, not a quotation, and I'd ask you not to treat the wording as authoritative or to lean on it as textual evidence: Ramsey begins by observing that the question of life's meaning is typically asked in a mood of anxiety, and that people expect the answer to be a fact about the universe — that the cosmos is vast and our lives are momentary, or that our lives slot into some larger purpose. He then suggests that this expectation involves a shift of perspective: comparing the size of the universe to the span of a life treats the significance of a life as a quantity on the same scale as the extent of things, whereas the significance of a life is not that kind of quantity at all. His illustration is a picture drawn in perspective, where foreground figures are larger than distant ones on the canvas without any consequence for the actual sizes of what is depicted. From there he moves to the thought that the question is not one to be settled by discovering a fact but one that dissolves once you see the point — and he adds that where he appears to be arguing for a conclusion, he is really trying to get the reader to see something.

What I can't get straight, and what I'd like help with, is whether the paraphrase above can be made internally consistent, and if so how. Three things trouble me. First, if the question genuinely dissolves, the piece should end in silence, yet it reads as though it is telling us something positive about meaning — that meaning is conferred by our cares and activities rather than found in the world's furniture. Second, if meaning is conferred, the obvious objection is arbitrariness: why is the meaning I confer on my life binding on me rather than something I could revise at will, and does Ramsey have any resources for answering this that don't simply reintroduce the very fact about the universe he has dismissed? Third, and this is the one that really blocks me, the move from the perspective analogy to the dissolution looks less like an argument than like a redescription: it tells me that my anxiety was a category mistake, but it doesn't show me that it was, and I can't tell whether that is a defect in my reconstruction or a real feature of the position — that is, whether Ramsey is offering a proof that the question is confused or an invitation to stop asking it, with the persuasion doing work that an argument was supposed to do. Please work through all three, distinguishing where my paraphrase has probably distorted the position from where the position itself is under pressure, and say plainly what the strongest version of the view is and what it still owes us.
