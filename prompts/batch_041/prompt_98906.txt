I teach a graduate seminar on perception and the a priori, and this term we have spent an unreasonable amount of time on the question Molyneux put to Locke: whether a man blind from birth, on suddenly gaining sight, could tell the cube from the sphere before touching them. My difficulty is that the original case has become too well-worn to be useful — the students arrive already knowing the empiricist answer and the nativist answer and can recite both without thinking, so the exercise degenerates into a recital of positions rather than an argument. I have therefore been pressing a variant on them, and I would like you to work it out as a dialogue between two philosophers of equal competence: one an associationist who holds that each sense delivers its own proprietary ideas and that any recognition across the senses must be learned by correlation, the other an amodalist who holds that shape is a common sensible, given to sight and touch alike without need of translation. Neither should be permitted to win by caricature, and each should be made to answer the other's strongest point rather than his weakest.

The variant is this. The subject, blind from birth, receives sight, and within the first hours — before any opportunity to handle the objects — can sort the sphere from the cube by sight alone; so far the amodalist is vindicated. But she cannot say which of the two lies nearer to her, and she cannot tell whether a visible bracket opens to the left or to the right, though she can do both at once with her hands. I want the associationist to argue that these failures disclose what has really crossed over: not shape in any genuinely spatial sense, but at most a modality-neutral signature or label that gives her no purchase on the layout the eye presents, so that the apparent success is an artifact of a coarse classification rather than evidence of shared content. And I want the amodalist to argue that the failures locate the trouble in the calibration of an egocentric spatial frame — a matter of learning to use sight — while leaving intact the amodal content of shape itself, to which the successes attest. Let them pursue whether shape means the same thing on the two sides of the argument, and whether the associationist's signature can do any explanatory work at all.

Please write the dialogue in a few pages of natural prose, with the speakers trading specific objections and concessions rather than delivering set speeches, and with neither of them reduced to a position no thoughtful person would hold. You may let them converge at the end on what would settle the matter empirically, but they should not resolve the disagreement itself.
