A regulatory proposal now under discussion would divide AI oversight into two tiers. Systems certified as tools — systems with no objectives of their own, whose outputs are confined to text, predictions, or recommendations returned to a human operator — would be exempt from the heavy tier of obligations: pre-deployment capability evaluations, adversarial testing, incident reporting, and continuing monitoring. Systems certified as agents — systems that pursue objectives across extended horizons, act through external interfaces, and can revise their own plans or code — would bear all of those obligations. The drafters' rationale is that a tool has no ends of its own and therefore cannot exhibit the failure modes that make agency dangerous, so oversight can be concentrated where agency is present. Assume that the criteria are applied in good faith and can be assessed reliably at the moment of certification. I have been asked to write the philosophical commentary on the proposal. Describe the most important implications of the tool/agent distinction, as the drafters draw it, for whether the two-tier regime can deliver the safety it promises, and for how the notions of a tool, an agent, and responsibility ought to be understood once AI systems are embedded in larger sociotechnical pipelines rather than used in isolation. Rank the implications by importance, and be explicit about which of them are conceptual claims and which are empirical predictions.
