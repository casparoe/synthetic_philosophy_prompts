Question 3. Indicative and counterfactual conditionals.

Part 1 (4 marks). Classify each of the following as indicative or counterfactual, and say what in the sentence you went by.
(1) If Marta is in Lisbon, she's missing the meeting.
(2) If Marta were in Lisbon, she'd be missing the meeting.
(3) If Marta was in Lisbon last week, she missed the meeting.
(4) If Marta had been in Lisbon last week, she would have missed the meeting.
Then: in (2), nothing in the sentence is about the past, yet the if-clause carries past-tense marking. What is that marking doing, if it isn't locating anything in past time?

Part 2 (3 marks). A detective is already convinced that the gardener took the silver. She says: "If the gardener had taken the silver, he'd have had to come through the kitchen." Is that sentence a counterfactual? Does calling it one tell us anything about whether the antecedent is true? Explain.

Part 3 (3 marks). A power cut has blacked out the village. The line runs along a ridge, and the only thing up there that could bring the line down is the old oak at the bend — no other trees, no ice, nothing else. As it happens, the oak did come down on the line. Engineer A, who knows the line is down but hasn't yet found out why, says: "If the oak didn't come down on the line, then something else brought it down." Engineer B, who has walked the ridge and seen that the oak was the only candidate, says: "If the oak hadn't come down on the line, something else would have brought it down." Does it follow, from the fact that these two sentences can be used about the same situation, that they express the same proposition? Explain.

Part 1. All four have the same 'if', so the difference has to be in the morphology, and I think the morphology is a reliable guide. (1) is indicative: present tense, no 'would'. (2) is counterfactual: 'were' in the if-clause, 'would' in the consequent. (3) is indicative: 'was' refers to last week, so it's a genuine past reference. (4) is counterfactual: past perfect plus 'would have'. The pattern is that indicatives are tensed to the time of the situation being talked about, while counterfactuals carry an extra layer of past marking that doesn't put anything in past time. (2) makes this clear, since Marta's being in Lisbon would be a present situation — nothing there is past — so the 'were' can't be doing past-time work. What it does instead is mark the situation as remote from the actual one, i.e. as one that doesn't obtain. That's why we call these counterfactuals.

Part 2. I'd say yes, it's a counterfactual. It has the counterfactual form — past perfect in the if-clause, 'would have' in the consequent — and the detective is using it to work out what must have happened, i.e. what route the thief had to have taken. She isn't supposing the antecedent false; she's certain it's true, and the conditional is her way of drawing out a consequence of something she already accepts. So the label is about the form, not about the antecedent's truth: the past marking here signals that she's holding the antecedent out as a supposition rather than asserting it, and that's independent of whether it's true.

Part 3. I'd say they come to the same thing. Both sentences are about the same oak and the same line, and both say that the oak coming down is what stood between the village and a dark night. The difference is one of presupposition: engineer A is treating the cause as an open question, engineer B is treating it as settled. The oak case also shows why the two can come apart in truth value — A's sentence is true (the line is down, so something must have brought it down, and if it wasn't the oak it was something else), while B's is false (there was nothing else up there to do it). So they don't have the same truth conditions, but I don't think that's because they say different things: the difference is which worlds we're being asked to look at, and looking at different worlds is a matter of what we're taking for granted, not of what's being claimed. (Strictly, indicatives like A's aren't truth-functional anyway — the material conditional reading is wrong — so A's contribution isn't really a truth condition.)
