I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and I want it to be a decision tree instead of the usual list of names. Start from the bare slogan that you can't just add up everyone's good and bad because persons are separate, and make every branch node a question that pulls the readings of that slogan apart: is separateness a claim about what exists (there's no super-person whose good is at stake), a claim about value (each person's good is a separate locus of value that can't be traded against another's), or a claim about the structure of practical reason (each agent may weight her own projects and may not treat others merely as means)? Push each branch down a couple more levels with concrete cases rather than labels — the transplant surgeon, the utility monster, two people each losing an eye versus one going blind, and so on. Then include the node I think is the sharpest one, because I dont see it on most handouts: if boundaries between persons block aggregation, what about boundaries between times inside one person — is discounting your own future good over the years the same mistake as sacrificing one person for another, or a different one? Which leaves does each answer land you in, and does the tree split the same way for both? Name the leaves where there's a real defender (the Rawls/Nozick side-constraint line, Nagel-style agent-relative reasons, a Parfit-ish view where separateness comes in degrees, the plain anti-aggregation reading of the repugnant conclusion, and whatever else belongs) and mark them unnamed where nobody's actually there. Flag any leaves the tree can't tell apart, and say for each empty combination of answers whether it's empty because the view is incoherent or just unexplored. Keep it to 8-10 nodes, and no node should just ask "are you a utilitarian?" — someone should be able to answer every one without knowing which label it leads to. Answer in American English.
