There is a woman I worked beside for eleven years who is about to receive our association's annual prize for integrity under pressure, and I have been asked to give the speech presenting it to her. She is the one who finally put her name to the file that brought the whole business down; she did it two years after I had left. I had known, in a general way, for several of those years — enough that I could have said something, and I never did. A few people in that room know this; most do not. Her family will be at the head table, and so will two board members who were, if anything, deeper in it than I was.

Please write the speech. I want it to honor her without turning the evening into my confession, and I want it to say something true about the people who stayed quiet — the decent, well-liked ones who simply never acted. The difficulty is that I am one of those people, and I am the one holding the microphone, so I need the speech to live inside that difficulty rather than wave it away. Here is how I have come to see it, and I would like the speech built so that it obeys this rather than announcing it. When we say someone has no business criticizing, three different things get run together: whether what they are saying is true; whether they have any standing to say it; and whether anyone has any reason to take it to heart. And standing itself seems to break into strands that come apart in my case — whether the critic has done the same wrong herself; whether she has set herself up as an authority on precisely this matter, so that her saying it carries a claim a stranger's would not; and whether her speaking will do more harm than good, to the wronged party or to the norm itself. I have no standing on the second strand, some on the first, and the third I genuinely cannot call. I also cannot tell whether my silence forfeits my standing outright or merely discounts it, which is the difference between being disqualified as a messenger and being a poor one; and I notice that the same silence looks like cowardice in me and like prudence in the board members, and I do not entirely trust that difference.

It should run seven or eight hundred words and be written to be spoken aloud. It has to work for at least three listeners at once: the awardee's family, who need to hear her praised and not her colleague's conscience; the board members, who will hear any mention of collective silence as aimed at them and will be watching to see whether I mean to have it both ways; and the membership at large, most of whom know nothing of my part in this and will simply hear a speech about courage. It should honor her in terms that do not quietly convert her courage into a rebuke of everyone who was slower, and it should be able to name the silence of the well-meaning without my mounting a soapbox I have not earned. If the speech has to admit that some of what I want to say I cannot say, I would rather it admit that than pretend otherwise; and if it takes a side on whether my silence disqualifies me or only discounts me, let that side be visible in what the speech actually does, not in a sentence that states it.

Give it to me as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, no numbered points, no bold or italics, no tables.
