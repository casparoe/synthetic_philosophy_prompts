I am reading through Aristotle's discussion of slavery in Book I of the Politics (Rackham's translation), and I have become stuck on the following passage:

"Again, as between the sexes, the male is by nature superior and the female inferior, the male ruler and the female subject. And the same must also necessarily apply in the case of mankind as a whole; therefore all men that differ as widely as the soul does from the body and the human being from the lower animal (and this is the condition of those whose function is the use of the body and from whom this is the best that is forthcoming) these are by nature slaves, for whom to be governed by this kind of authority is advantageous, inasmuch as it is advantageous to the subject things already mentioned. For he is by nature a slave who is capable of belonging to another (and that is why he does so belong), and who participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it; for the animals other than man are subservient not to reason, by apprehending it, but to feelings."

What I find hard is not the general thrust of the passage but the internal logic of the definition it offers. Two features in particular trouble me. First, the definition appears to run in a circle: the natural slave is identified as the person "capable of belonging to another," and then the parenthetical adds that this capacity is precisely why he does belong to another. Second, the slave is said to "participate in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it," a formula that seems to lift him above the other animals while still withholding something essential from him.

In trying to get clearer, I have been separating two possible readings of what the passage is claiming, and I would like you to help me decide between them. Call them the capacity reading and the service reading. On the capacity reading, a natural slave is someone whose rational capacities are intrinsically deficient in a specific respect, so that the deficiency itself is the ground of his subjection. On the service reading, a natural slave is someone whose characteristic function is bodily service to another, so that the defining mark is what he is for rather than what he can do; on this reading the deficiency, if any, is downstream of the function rather than its basis.

Please help me understand the passage, addressing the following:

1. Which of the two readings does the quoted text actually support, and is the text committed to one of them or deliberately indifferent between them?
2. How should the clause about being "capable of belonging to another" be construed so that the definition is not viciously circular, and does the parenthetical rescue it or merely restate the problem?
3. What work is done by the distinction between apprehending reason and possessing it, and is that distinction one of kind or only of degree?
4. The passage grounds natural slavery in a series of comparisons — soul over body, human over animal, male over female. Do these analogies favor the capacity reading or the service reading, and does the analogy to the body's relation to the soul import the service reading by way of the notion of a function?
5. Aristotle elsewhere denies that slaves have a deliberative faculty at all, while allowing that women have one that lacks authority and children one that is still immature. Is that later claim consistent with what the quoted passage says about the slave's apprehending reason, or does it commit him to the capacity reading?

I would like an interpretation that stays close to the quoted words, and that distinguishes carefully between what the passage states and what would have to be supplied from elsewhere in the argument.
