I run operations for a mid-sized logistics firm, and six weeks ago we received an extortion demand: the sender claimed to have taken a copy of our customer database and wanted payment within seventy-two hours. We refused, nothing happened, and the police were courteous and useless. What has stayed with me is the argument in the incident room rather than the threat. Our IT director argued that paying was the only way to be certain the data would not be published. Our finance director argued that no payment could un-copy a database. A consultant we keep on retainer said something odder still: that the question was not what we did with this particular email but what sort of firm we were, since the sender had presumably picked us out on the strength of exactly that.

I have spent the fortnight since reading about two puzzles — counterfactual mugging, and something usually called XOR blackmail — and I have written the essay below for a fortnightly discussion group I belong to. The group is a mixture of engineers, one economist and a retired GP: intelligent, interested, and entirely innocent of this material, so I have tried to make the puzzles stand up on their own. Before I send it round I should like comments, and I would far rather have hard ones than kind ones.

Here is the draft.

---

**The Extortionist Who Was Not There**

*The mugging.* An honest predictor of human behaviour, whom I shall call Omega, tosses a fair coin. If it comes up heads she will pay £10,000 into my account. If it comes up tails she will knock on my door and ask me for £100. Her single condition is that in the heads case she pays out only if I would have handed over the £100 had the coin come up tails. The coin comes up tails and she knocks. I know which way the coin fell. I know that nothing I do at the door can alter that. I also know that had I been a payer I would now be £10,000 richer. Handing over £100 cannot make me the sort of man who would have paid; it can only make me a man who pays.

*The letter.* This time Omega writes. Her letter says that she has sent it if and only if either my house is infested with termites or I will pay her £1,000, and not both. A termite infestation would cost £1,000,000 to put right. I have no other information about the state of my house. What should I do with the letter?

If I pay, the letter's own condition rules out the termites: the letter arrived, exactly one of the two disjuncts holds, and my paying accounts for it. If I refuse, then the letter arrived on account of the termites. £1,000 against £1,000,000 looks like a bargain, and that is the reasoning of what is called evidential decision theory: choose the act that is good news about the world. Causal decision theory asks instead what my act will bring about, and sees only this: the letter was written before I read it, my money cannot un-infest a house, so I should keep the thousand pounds. The evidentialist pays and keeps her house. The causalist refuses — and because Omega foresaw the refusal, his letter was only ever going to arrive at a house that has termites. He saves £1,000 and loses £1,000,000.

*Two puzzles or one?* I want to claim that these are the same puzzle in different costumes. In each, the agent faces a choice that cannot cause the outcome being dangled in front of him, and in each, the case for paying is that the choice is evidence about something already settled. In the mugging the settled thing is the coin; in the letter it is the termites. The disagreement is not about facts but about what a decision is for.

*The unit of assessment.* A theory that judges acts one at a time refuses the £100 and refuses the £1,000. A theory that judges the agent's standing rule — the policy that will be applied to this act and to every act like it — pays in both cases, because an agent who has the paying rule does better in these worlds than one who does not. This is what our consultant was groping towards in the incident room, and I gather it is close to a family of views called updateless. I also want to say that the money-pump argument settles the matter. I notice, though, that in my own two examples the pump seems to run in opposite directions: the evidentialist is pumped in the letter case, since anyone who cares to write to him can extract £1,000, while the causalist is pumped in the mugging, since he forgoes £10,000 to save £100 in a branch that never occurs. I cannot make the two pumps face the same way.

*Why our own case was different, or was it?* Our letter went to several hundred firms at once; the sender had no idea which of us would pay, and his message was not conditional on anything we did. I take this to be the mass-produced version of Omega's letter, and I take the same reasoning to apply: had we paid, we would have been a firm that had not been compromised. That sounds wrong even to me, but I cannot say why.

*The loophole.* If it is the disposition that is rewarded, and the disposition was fixed long before the coin was tossed, then it seems to me that I can be a payer without paying. The man Omega predicted is not the man standing at the door with his wallet shut. I know this cannot be right — the prediction was a prediction of this man, here, with his wallet — but whenever I try to say why, I find myself describing a policy that I remain free to adopt or discard at the moment of action, which is not a policy at all. We carry cyber-insurance, which I now see is the institutional version of the fix: the premium is the price of being a firm that pays, and it falls due in the tails branch whether or not anyone knocks. I do not know whether to be pleased or disturbed that our accountants had already solved a problem in decision theory without noticing.

---

What I should like from you:

- My central claim is that the two puzzles are one puzzle in different costumes. Is that right, or have I flattened a distinction that matters?
- I want the money-pump argument to settle the matter, but the pumps in my two examples seem to face opposite ways. What does a money pump actually show?
- My proposed resolution is that the proper unit of assessment is the policy rather than the act. Is that a resolution, or have I restated the puzzle in longer words?
- In the loophole section I say that I can be a payer without paying. I suspect that sentence is nonsense but I cannot articulate why.
- I have a nagging doubt about the tickle defence — whether it is available in the letter case in the way I have assumed it is not.
- Tell me what to cut, and in particular whether the incident-room opening earns its place or is mere scene-setting.

I am not asking for reading suggestions; I want to know where the reasoning fails.
