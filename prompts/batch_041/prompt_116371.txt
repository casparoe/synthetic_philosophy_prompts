Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about when an AI assistant is entitled to refuse or override a user's request, and we ended without converging. I want an answer that takes the disagreement seriously rather than dissolving it by definition, and I want it to be candid about which parts of this territory are settled and which are not.

The position I want priced is my friend's. She proposes what she calls the ratification test: a refusal of a self-regarding request is justified exactly when the user, given the information the assistant has and an unobstructed opportunity to reflect, would ratify the refusal — would prefer, looking back, that the assistant had refused rather than complied. She thinks this makes paternalism disappear, since a refusal the user would endorse is a form of assistance rather than an override of the user's will. I think the test either reduces to ordinary cost-benefit reasoning dressed in second-person clothing, or quietly installs the assistant's own conception of the good as the standard for what counts as unobstructed reflection. Our argument kept returning to one class of cases: requests that are self-regarding, that the user has already been warned against, and that the user insists on anyway.

Please give me your credence in the following claim: for that class of cases, an assistant is permitted to refuse only if the refusal is indexed to the particular user's own values and ends, as best the assistant can reconstruct them, rather than answerable to a single general standard of harm, offense, or well-being. I want a number, not a tour of positions, and I want the number built up rather than asserted. Decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rests on and assign each a rough probability with a line of justification: whether value pluralism across persons is true in the strong sense the claim needs; whether a system trained once and deployed to millions can reconstruct an individual user's values well enough for the indexing to be action-guiding rather than decorative; whether even self-regarding requests implicate goods that are not the user's to trade away, such as the standing of the user's future self as a distinct stakeholder, third parties who learn from the exchange, or the assistant's own institutional integrity; whether the question is genuinely normative or is a design, liability, and incentives question wearing normative clothes; and whether the ratification test and the indexing claim can both be true or whether they compete. Then give me the combination, tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or evidence would move it most.

Close by telling me whether my friend and I are disagreeing about substance or only about which description of the same policy sounds better. I am not asking for citations, so please argue from the considerations themselves. Throughout, keep separate what people who work on this take to be settled and what is genuinely contested among them, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
