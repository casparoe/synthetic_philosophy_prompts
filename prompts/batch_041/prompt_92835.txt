The frame: the students are a government Bureau of Weights and Measures that has to publish an official count of how many objects are in a room, and the count has to be (a) derived from a stated rule about when a bunch of things compose a further thing, and (b) defensible to ordinary people who will complain about it. I'll seed the room with stuff that makes the cases bite -- a chair, a bowl of marbles, a block of wood, a pile of sand, a stack of books. Each group writes down its composition rule, computes a count from it, and then we hit them with the hard cases: the marbles in the bowl, the block, the pile, the cloud out the window, and a case where their own rule spits out an answer they hate. They get points for matching a common-sense answer key but lose points wherever their stated rule and their answer disagree, so no rule survives both. Later I want two groups to merge inventories and have to reconcile their rules by vote.

The sting I'm after: universalism and nihilism each look clean until you actually run them, and the middle-ground rules (only some collections compose) keep having to gerrymander. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the three standard answers and onto the thought that ordinary counting already presupposes a controversial answer.

Give it back to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up, since I'm pasting this straight into my session notes.
