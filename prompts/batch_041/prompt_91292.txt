I'm not a philosopher by training — I read it in the evenings and I keep a dream journal — but I've spent a few months stuck on the old dispute about whether dreams are experiences, the one usually framed as Malcolm against Dennett, and I've finally written up my own answer. I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher of mind who knows this literature well, read what follows closely, and give me detailed comments. Please think it through properly before you write: work out the strongest version of each claim I make, and consider the strongest reading of my position rather than the silliest one. I'd rather have two sharp objections than ten vague ones.

My essay:

Almost everyone who writes about dreams asks whether a dream is an experience, and I've come to think that question is really three questions tangled together. First, does anything with a felt quality happen while you are asleep? Second, is anyone there for whom it is happening — a subject who is present to it as it occurs? Third, is the story you tell on waking a report of that first thing, or is it something your mind assembles at the moment of waking, so that the order of events in the story need not match the order in which anything actually happened? My own answer is a combination I haven't seen stated in quite this way. On the first question I say yes: something phenomenal really does occur between falling asleep and waking, and it is not merely a memory. On the second I say no: nobody is there. The 'I' who is chased down the corridor, who decides to fly, who is embarrassed to be naked at a lecture, is a character in the content and not the person whose brain is doing the work. On the third I say the waking report is a reconstruction — but a reconstruction of something that genuinely occurred, in the way an eyewitness account is a reconstruction of an event rather than an invention. So dreams are experiences without an experiencer: felt, but not felt by anyone. What pushed me here was three years of keeping a dream journal and noticing that the dream-self makes choices I would never make, knows things I don't know, and is frightened by things that leave me cold, which seems to me to tell against the idea that I am the one undergoing it. What I can't decide is whether 'an experience that nobody undergoes' is a genuine possibility or just a contradiction wearing good clothes; whether I have quietly restated one of the two positions I was trying to get between; and whether, if I am right, the whole dispute turns out to be verbal, with each side answering a different question and neither noticing.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing summary. Between them, the five should cover, roughly in order of priority: where the argument actually breaks down or equivocates; whether my position is genuinely distinct from the two I say I am getting between or is a restatement of one of them; what I am committed to if I keep it; whether it is interesting enough to be worth developing; and anything I have said that is simply mistaken about the debate. Don't pad the list to fill space.
