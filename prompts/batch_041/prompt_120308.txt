I am to give the dedication remarks at the opening of a new cancer-treatment wing at the hospital where my father was treated; my family paid for it. The room will be split — oncologists and research staff on one side, my religious community on the other. I am an occasionalist in al-Ghazālī's line: I hold that God alone is the agent, that fire does not burn the cotton, and that what we take for natural causes are only the occasions on which God creates what follows, the order of the world being His custom rather than a necessity in things, with our own action acquired rather than produced by us. Both halves of the room will be listening for something. The clinicians will hear any denial of their causal power as contempt for their work; my community will hear any softening of the doctrine as betrayal. Write the speech, five or six minutes. It must thank the doctors and mean it, hold out a real hope for what the wing will do without assigning the outcome to anyone present, say what my family's money has done given that on my view it causes nothing either, and explain in ordinary language, without a lecture on causation, why someone who believes this still builds hospitals. The obvious exit — that God works through doctors as secondary causes — is precisely what I deny, so it is closed to you. I would also like one honest sentence about what the doctrine leaves of a doctor's responsibility when the outcome is bad; I find that the hardest part.
