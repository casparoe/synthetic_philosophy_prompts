My sisters getting married in three weeks and I'm giving the best-man speech — she insists on the title even though I'm her brother, which should tell you a lot about her. The complication: she met her fiancé at an anthropics workshop and they are, no exaggeration, the two most annoying people at any dinner party. The one thing they've never resolved, and have agreed never to resolve, is the self-sampling vs. self-indication thing. It's definately load-bearing at this point. He's self-indication, which is why she calls him the presumptuous philosopher — under his view he should figure he's almost certainly one of the many rather than one of the few, which is exactly the kind of thing he would think. She's self-sampling, which is why, when a flight gets delayed, she instantly assumes she's in the majority of delayed flights.

Half the reception will be workshop people who will clock it in ten seconds if I say anything that only works under one of the two assumptions. The other half is family, who think anthropics is a kind of antibiotic. So: about four minutes, genuinely warm, no resolving their disagreement, no taking a side by accident — including in throwaway lines about luck and odds and out of all the people in the world. He gets teased as the presumptuous philosopher, but it has to read as fond. My aunt should not feel taught at.

Here's a bit I made up, and I'd like the middle of the speech to hang on it. Two weddings, same night, same hotel: a ballroom with 400 guests, a little side room with 20. You're a guest and you can't see which room you're in — you just know you're at one of the two. Self-sampling says: you're a random guest, there are twenty times as many guest-slots in the ballroom, so you're probably in the ballroom. Self-indication also dumps you in the ballroom, for a different reason: the bigger wedding was the one more likely to be happening at all. Then the twist I like — it's the same couple in both rooms, so it isn't about which couple you got, it's about which version of the night you're in. I genuinely don't know what that does to either assumption, and I'd rather the speech not pretend to know.

Write the speech, roughly 600 words. If I've mangled either assumption, say so in one line first.
