For most of my working life I was a particle physicist, and I have spent the years since retirement reading philosophy of science. I keep returning to an argument I heard and made for decades, and I would like to know what it committed us to. The argument ran roughly as follows. The mass of the Higgs boson, left to itself, ought to be dragged up to the highest scale at which the theory is still valid, because quantum corrections to it grow with that scale; yet it sits far below. Something must be suppressing those corrections, and the most economical something was a new symmetry pairing bosons with fermions, which would cancel them. This reasoning — that a dimensionless parameter is acceptable only if it is of order unity, and that a parameter which is not must be explained — governed which theories were invented, which were funded, which were published in the best journals, and, I suspect, which many of us half-believed. Then the collider turned on and found nothing new where we had expected it, and a number of colleagues announced that this criterion had never been a scientific argument in the first place.

Meanwhile in cosmology the same community, often the same people, accepted an account of the cosmological constant that strikes me as an inversion of the same reasoning. Its value is absurdly small, they say, but we should not demand a dynamical mechanism for it: we should say instead that the universe is one of an enormous ensemble, most of whose members permit no galaxies, and that we necessarily observe one of the rare members whose value allows structure. There, fine-tuning is defused by selection among many universes; here, fine-tuning was treated as a demand on the theory.

I would like you to treat this contrast as a case study in what a practice presupposes. Reconstruct the view of theory assessment that the first argument requires. Does it presuppose that there is a well-defined prior distribution over parameters, so that improbability is measurable? That the parameters of a fundamental theory are the kind of thing that can be typical or atypical at all? That a theory requiring fine-tuning is for that reason less credible, independently of any observation? Then tell me whether the anthropic treatment of the cosmological constant is consistent with that view or whether it presupposes a rival and incompatible one, on which improbability is not to be explained by dynamics but dissolved by selection. And locate the tension precisely: are the two practices strictly inconsistent, or could a single underlying conception of confirmation license both, and if so, what would that conception be, and what would it cost its defender to state it plainly?

What interests me is not whether supersymmetry or the multiverse is true, but what the conduct of the field — what it rewarded, published, and quietly abandoned — reveals about its implicit philosophy of science. Please distinguish what the practice genuinely requires from what it merely happened to be accompanied by, and tell me where it is silent, so that several incompatible philosophies would account for what we did equally well.
