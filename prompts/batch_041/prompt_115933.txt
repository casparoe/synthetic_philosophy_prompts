I'm preparing to interview, for a political-theory podcast, an anthropologically minded theorist who argues that corruption is best understood as a violation of a role-ethic of office rather than as the diversion of public resources to private ends. On her account, every political exchange runs on two ledgers: a ledger of office, on which an official's entitlements and obligations are fixed by the impersonal terms of the post, and a ledger of relationship, on which they are fixed by a history of mutual obligation between particular people. What we call corruption, she says, is a misfiling — an entry belonging on the relationship ledger is booked on the office ledger, or the reverse — so that a gift to a kinsman who would in any case have been owed support is not corruption, while a salary drawn for work never done is, even when no one is harmed. She further insists that the term's critical force is internal to a practice, and that there is no culture-free standpoint from which to call another society's arrangements corrupt.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press this position rather than invite her to restate it. I want at least one question each on: (1) whether she can distinguish a bribe from a gift without covertly appealing to the very impersonality she wants to relativize; (2) whether misfiling makes corruption a bookkeeping error rather than a wrong, and what exactly is wrong when the two ledgers conflict — say, an official who steers a contract to an underqualified cousin who is nonetheless the only person likely to show up; (3) whether the internal-standard claim survives contact with a society whose own members denounce as corruption the very clientelist practices she redescribes as gift-exchange; (4) the regress question, whether the obligations on the relationship ledger can themselves be traced to earlier transactions, and if not, what grounds them; and (5) the efficiency objection, that patronage works as informal insurance where formal insurance is absent, and whether that argument can be run without treating those outside the network as acceptable collateral. For each question, name the answer she is most likely to give and add a follow-up that makes that answer harder to use, so that the sequence gets progressively harder to deflect.
