I am trying to establish whether a pattern I keep running into has an established name, and if it does not, I want a name and a careful definition rather than a loose label.

The pattern is this: a claim is put forward in a form whose verification costs far more than the claim itself requires, and the choice of form lies entirely with the person making the claim. I have met it in several guises.

In software work, a colleague submits a change that I believe to be correct, but the only way to be sure is to redo the derivation that produced it. When I ask for the reasoning, I am given one further step, and that step itself needs a step, and so on; each explanation is individually plausible and always one increment beyond what I can check in the time I have. I end up either approving on trust or rejecting on suspicion, and neither of those is verification.

In auditing, the same thing happens with arrangements that are lawful but whose lawfulness can be established only by searching a space of counterexamples with no obvious bound. The firm can always name a structure that would have been unlawful but is not the one it used, and the auditor cannot tell which of the two situations obtains.

The sharpest case is the proposal for keeping powerful AI systems in check by having two of them argue a case before a human judge. Nothing false need be asserted. One side chooses a formulation of a true statement whose checking exceeds the judge's budget; the judge, unable to check, must go by presentation; and the other side, to be believed at all, would have to perform the computation the judge cannot. Honesty becomes a handicap.

Three features seem to me to belong to the pattern, and they are why I do not think it is merely a name for difficulty. First, the cost of verification is under the claimant's control and can be raised at will without the claim becoming any less true. Second, the verifier's budget is fixed, known in advance, and not negotiable. Third, the effect is asymmetric: it penalises whoever states things plainly, so that parties who begin by being clear acquire an incentive to become obscure, and the standard of proof degrades for everyone. A pattern with those three features can arise with no liar present at all, which is why I resist calling it deception or rhetoric.

Is there a term for it? The neighbours I keep reaching for all seem to be missing something. Information asymmetry and the principal–agent problem name the imbalance but not the fact that the imbalance is manufactured; adversarial examples are about perturbing an input rather than about choosing a representation; computational hardness is a property of problems, not a move in an exchange; and the assumption that checking is easier than producing is precisely what the pattern defeats. I would be glad to be told that the literature on machine debate has a term for this and that I have simply not encountered it — but if the term there is narrower than what I have described, if it presupposes a particular game, or a liar, or a judge of a particular kind, then I want to know exactly where the two come apart.

So: the name if there is one, and if there is not, a coined one with a definition and its edges. Is a document that is merely long and dull an instance, or does the pattern require that the obscurity be chosen? Is a claim that is hard for a person but trivial for a machine an instance, or does it require that the verifier's limits be the binding ones? What of a genuine ambiguity that nobody controls — the same phenomenon, or the neighbouring one? And is there any account of when the honest party's best response is to make its own case equally hard to check, so that the whole channel settles at a worse equilibrium?
