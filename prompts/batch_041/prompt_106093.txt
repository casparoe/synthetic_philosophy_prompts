I don't have any philosophy or math background, but a friend of mine who does keeps bringing up this paradox and it's been rattling around in my head for weeks, so I want to write a short story about it. Here's the gist as I understand it, and honestly please have the story itself correct me if I've got it wrong: take any set of rules you can write down in the language of set theory. If those rules can describe some world with infinitely many things in it, then they can also describe a world you could in principle count — a world where every single object gets a whole-number label, first, second, third, and so on, with nothing left over. Set theory is one of those sets of rules. So there's a countable world that satisfies set theory, one where you could in theory list every set it contains. And yet that same world proves, all on it's own, that some of its sets are bigger than the counting numbers — genuinely uncountable, more than you could ever finish listing. The resolution (I think?) is that "uncountable" is something the world says about itself from the inside, and the list that would show one of those sets is actually countable just isn't an object that exists in that world — so from out here we can see the set is countable while from in there nobody can ever point to the list. Like a map too small to hold the proof that it's too small.

So: write me a short story, maybe 1000-1400 words, set entirely inside that countable world, from the point of view of a person who has been told — by someone who seems to be standing outside it — that her whole universe, every person and every object, can be numbered, and that a complete list of all of them exists somewhere. I want the drama to be that she can never find or make that list, no matter how clever she is, and that this isn't because she's dumb or because the list is hidden, but because her language and her world simply can't express it; meanwhile there are collections in her world she's dead certain are unlistable, and she's right by her own lights, and the story should let both of those be true at once without ever resolving the tension. Please don't have her crack it and finally produce the enumeration — that would be cheating — and don't make the outsider smug about it either, more matter-of-fact, or a little sad. A concrete setting would help: I keep picturing a big old census office or records hall where clerks are trying to catalog everything in the city, and one clerk keeps running into collections that resist the catalog in a way she can't explain to her supervisor, and there's a stranger who comes in one afternoon and says something offhand that undoes her. But use whatever setting you like, just keep the inside/outside distinction straight and make me actually feel why the whole thing is dizzying rather than just explaining it at me.
