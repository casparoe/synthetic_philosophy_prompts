I'm putting together the final for my 300-level philosophy of social science class and I need one essay question for the unit on national character. The concept has this whole pedigree — Herder and the romantics, then the culture-and-personality folks in anthropology, then everybody declares it dead — and every single year I get the same paper: essentialism is bad, nations are imagined, the idea is unfalsifiable, the end. I want a question they can't answer with the obituary.

Here's the angle I want it to force. In lecture I gave them a distinction I'd like the question to make them actually use. Take a claim like 'the Dutch are frugal.' You can read it aggregatively — somewhere there's a distribution of frugality and the Dutch mean sits higher than the comparison group's. Or you can read it dispositionally — there's something shared and internal and character-like in Dutch people that produces the frugality. So question one: does the dispositional reading have any content left over once the aggregative reading has been stated in full? If yes, what on earth would count as evidence for it? If no, is 'national character' anything more than a colorful way of restating a histogram? I also want them to run into the case where a claim is true of a group and false of nearly every individual in it, or true of each subgroup but false of the whole — don't name that second one in the question itself, but an answer that can't see why its possible is a B answer at best.

Question two is the normative half. Suppose some such distributional claim is actually true. What, if anything, makes it acceptable to state it or act on it? I want them to pull 'true' apart from 'usable,' and to say whether it matters that you're a hiring manager, a doctor, or a novelist writing a character — two students got into a shouting match about the novelist case after section and I'd like the exam to make them argue it out on paper instead.

So please write me: the question itself (it's one of three on a two-hour final, so roughly 40 minutes of work — don't make it ten separate parts), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each one has to make. And list the three or four mistakes that should cost marks — I see the same ones every year and I want the TAs calibrated.
