I retired from an applied entomology department about four years ago — thirty years on insecticide resistance and integrated pest management, mostly cotton and vegetables — and since then I've gotten obssessed with philosophy. Two friends from the local meetup and I run a small podcast, and next month we're recording a long interview with a philosopher who has somehow become the go-to person for the idea that pest control is a serious moral problem. She's a philosopher by training but reads a fair bit of insect neurobiology, so I can't bluff her on the physiology. I'd like questions that dig in instead of letting her give her book talk.

Here's her position as I understand it, so you know where to push. She thinks the odds that insects have experiences that can go well or badly for them are high enough that we have to take them seriously, and she says IPM is built around the wrong kind of threshold: the economic injury level — the pest density at which the value of the crop you're losing equals what it costs you to spray — and the lower action threshold at which you actually pull the trigger. She wants those replaced by what she calls a moral injury level, set by how much suffering the spray causes rather than by money. Fine. But then she also argues that pest insects in farm fields probably live net-negative lives — short, parasitized, starved, eaten from the inside — so that killing them is often no worse than leaving them alone, and can even be a benefit. Which means the upshot of her view is that spraying is mostly morally fine and the real scandal is all the suffering out in the wild. That pleases the industry people (they like the conclusion) and annoys the animal people (they think she's selling out), and I suspect both reactions are too quick.

Could you draft 9 or 10 questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? I want them in the weeds, not a tour of whether insects matter. Specifically I'd like at least one question each on: (1) what she thinks is actually happening in an insect's nervous system when it suffers — I spent a career recording from insect neurons and a nociceptive reflex never struck me as evidence of much, so make her be concrete; (2) the net-negative-lives claim and the comparison problem I can't get past — net negative compared to what, given that if nobody had planted the field these particular insects would never have existed at all; (3) how arbitrary the pest category is, since the same species is a pest as a larva and something people put on their tea towels as an adult, and since biocontrol releases parasitoid wasps that kill pests by the billions — and the wasps are insects too, so is that better or worse than spraying; (4) whether her view is consistent with wanting to wipe out a mosquito species with a gene drive; (5) whether the welfare math flips once you count the pollinators and the natural enemies you kill along with the pest, plus the sublethal effects; (6) whether her view pushes toward more spraying or toward land sparing versus land sharing, i.e. which land use actually produces more insect lives, and lives of what quality; (7) how we could ever know any of this, and what would change her mind; and (8) what she actually wants a grower to do on Monday morning. A ninth or tenth question of your choosing is welcome if there's a bigger inconsistency I'm missing — one that makes her squirm a little would be ideal.

Two things to keep in mind. Don't send me off to a reading list or tell me who to cite; I can't keep up with that literature and the questions need to stand on their own. And no warm-up questions about how she got interested in this or what her book argues — assume the audience knows the setup. I'd definately rather have one hard question about the action threshold than five soft ones about whether insects matter.
