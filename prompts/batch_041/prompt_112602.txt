I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and for a seminar on the philosophy of logic I have to present on a puzzle that I find slipperier than the syllabus admits. It is the dialogue Lewis Carroll published in Mind in 1895, in which Achilles and the Tortoise consider a simple inference — from a conditional and its antecedent to the conclusion that follows — and the Tortoise, while cheerfully accepting both premises, refuses to draw the conclusion until Achilles supplies a further premise, namely the conditional that if the two original premises are true then the conclusion is true. Achilles supplies it, the Tortoise accepts it, and then demands yet another premise licensing the application of that one, and so on without end. The standard reactions are by now familiar: that inference is a matter of following a rule rather than believing a proposition, that the justification of deduction cannot itself be propositional, or that the puzzle is merely a confusion about the difference between a conditional and the act of inferring. What I want to explore instead is a historical question, and I would like you to answer it in the form of a short, argued essay rather than a set of bullet points.

The figure I want to put to the test is Leibniz, who strikes me as the patron saint of precisely what the Tortoise is attacking. Leibniz spent much of his life on a universal characteristic and a calculus of reasoning — a symbolic language in which disputants would settle their disagreements not by arguing but by computing — and he held that in every true proposition the predicate is contained in the subject, that the truths of reasoning rest ultimately on the principle of contradiction, and that reasoning is in principle a mechanical operation on symbols which a suitably designed calculus could carry out. If reasoning really is calculation, then the Tortoise looks like a fatal objection: a calculus cannot tell you how to apply itself, and every rule you hand it becomes one more item that itself stands in need of a rule. So I want you to reconstruct, as faithfully and as charitably as you can, what Leibniz would have said about the Tortoise had the puzzle been put to him. Would he have treated the regress as a genuine threat to the mechanization of reasoning, or as a confusion to be dissolved — and if the latter, exactly where does the confusion lie, and what does the dissolving? I am especially interested in what work his own doctrines would have done in his reply: the containment of the predicate in the subject, the grounding of the truths of reasoning in the principle of contradiction, the distinction between truths of reasoning and truths of fact, and his conception of a characteristic in which thought is replaced by the manipulation of characters. And I want you to be explicit about what, if anything, he would have had to concede to the Tortoise — whether the regress exposes a gap his system cannot close, or whether he would have said that the Tortoise is demanding something no account of reasoning was ever obliged to provide.

A few constraints, since this is going in front of a seminar. I do not want a survey of what commentators have said about the paradox; I want your reconstruction of Leibniz's likely position, argued from what he actually held, and I want the argument to be one I could defend in discussion rather than a hedge that says he might have thought several things. Please mark clearly which parts of the reconstruction are genuinely Leibnizian and which are your own extrapolation, because I do not want to attribute to him a view that is really ours. Finally, a classmate insists that the puzzle is a mere curiosity about how we talk about inference and has no bearing on anything real; say whether Leibniz's reply would have anything to say to the modern ambition to mechanize reasoning — in a formal calculus, in a computer, or in a system that is trained to produce inferences without ever being given the rules — and if so, what.
