I have been reading a popular book on thermodynamics and information, and I want to know how much of its treatment of Maxwell's demon to trust. The author has a physics background and the book is generally careful, but the following passage is the one I keep coming back to:

"Maxwell's demon is the most famous counterexample to the second law of thermodynamics. Picture a gas in a box divided by a wall with a small door. The demon watches the molecules approach and opens the door for fast molecules heading one way and slow molecules heading the other, and after a while one side of the box is hot and the other cold. The temperature difference can drive an engine, so the demon appears to have created work out of nothing but his own cleverness. The standard resolution is that the demon is himself a physical system, and the second law claims him too. To see which molecule is which he must shine a light on it, and the light he scatters carries entropy away into the surroundings; the books balance as soon as we count the demon's measurements. The decisive step was Landauer's principle, which identified the real cost: because the demon's memory must be cleared so that he can go on making measurements, and because erasing a single bit of information always costs at least kT ln 2 of heat, the demon returns everything he has borrowed. This is what people mean when they say that information is physical: information is a quantity that can be traded against heat and work, which is why information and entropy are ultimately the same thing measured two ways, and why Maxwell's demon, once the embarrassment of thermodynamics, is now its best introduction to the physics of information."

I am not a physicist, though I know enough to suspect that the verdict is right and the reasoning is where the trouble lies. Please audit the passage sentence by sentence. For each substantive claim, say whether a specialist in the thermodynamics of information would accept it as stated, accept it only with a stated qualification, or reject it; where a qualification is needed, give it precisely; and where the passage is doing something subtler than being simply wrong — using a technical term in its everyday sense, conflating two claims that a careful treatment keeps apart, treating one side of a live dispute as settled consensus, or presenting a superseded resolution as the current one — say which and why it matters.

Then three larger questions. First, does the passage's concluding claim, that the demon is now the best introduction to the physics of information, follow from the parts of the account that survive the audit? Second, the passage explains why the demon loses, but does its accounting capture what the demon's information has to do with the work he is supposed to extract — and would the same books still balance if the demon never erased anything at all? Third, is there serious dissent among specialists about whether the second law is saved in the way the passage describes, and if so, what does the disagreement turn on? I do not need references or a reading list; I want to know what to believe and where this passage would embarrass a specialist who recommended it.
