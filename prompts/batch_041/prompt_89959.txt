I have agreed to deliver the commemorative address at our city's annual observance of the abolition of the slave trade: eight to ten minutes, spoken outdoors, to a genuinely mixed crowd. A handful of my colleagues from the history and philosophy departments will be there, and they will catch every allusion to Eric Williams's Capitalism and Slavery and to Kwame Anthony Appiah's The Honor Code; everyone else — neighbors, high-school students, local officials, descendants of the enslaved — is there to be moved, not lectured. The committee's invitation already contains the sentence I have been dreading, to the effect that abolition proves the power of moral argument to defeat entrenched interest, and I want to write something that honors the occasion without repeating that flattering story.

Please write the address. I want it organized around a distinction I have been working out, and I want the speech's shape — not its thesis statement — to carry the distinction. The distinction is between two ways a discredited practice can die: it can become dishonorable in the eyes of one's peers, so that continuing it costs one standing; or it can become inconsistent with something the agent already claims to be, so that continuing it costs one self-possession. My working hunch is that dueling ended by the first route and the slave trade by the second — that what moved British slaveholders was less the fear of contempt than the discovery that they could no longer be the people they had been describing themselves as. You need not defend that hunch as settled scholarship; you need to make a listener feel the difference between the two routes and to leave open which one did the heavier lifting.

The difficulty is the audience. My colleagues will wince if I sand off Williams's point that the decline of the plantation economy was quietly doing work alongside any change of heart, and they will wince just as hard if I treat Appiah's mechanism of honor as a mere footnote to rational argument. Everyone else will drift if the address turns into a literature review. So let the three explanations — argument, interest, honor — stand together in the room without my adjudicating between them, and let whatever consolation the speech offers be earned rather than asserted. Write it to be spoken aloud, in plain sentences, and end on something a person can carry home without having been told a comfortable lie.
