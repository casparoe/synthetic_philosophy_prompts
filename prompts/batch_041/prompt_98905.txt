I have been trying to pin down a pattern I keep meeting in arguments about what we must never do, and I cannot decide whether it already has a name or whether I am lumping two different things together.

Here is the pattern. Someone asserts that a certain act — killing, lying under oath, torturing a prisoner, breaking a promise — is forbidden without exception. Pressed for the reason, they give one that is plainly about the damage the act does: it destroys a life, corrupts the one who lies, degrades both the torturer and his victim. Yet when the act is the only way to prevent a far greater quantity of that very same damage, they hold the line anyway, and they say the greater damage is not theirs to weigh: they will not do the forbidden thing, and what happens otherwise is not on their hands. So the rule appears to be grounded in the harm it prevents and then, at the decisive moment, to be treated as though the harm it prevents were beside the point. The rule seems to be doing the opposite of its stated job — the value it exists to protect is what gets sacrificed to it — and the person is not obviously confused or insincere; they seem to think this is precisely what taking the rule seriously amounts to.

What I want to know is whether there is a received name for this configuration, and if so, exactly which part of it the name picks out. I suspect I am somewhere in the vicinity of the literature on absolute moral constraints, but that phrase names the subject matter, not the phenomenon, and I can already see at least two things I may be conflating: (i) a genuine structural problem — that a constraint justified by the badness of the acts it forbids appears, in some cases, to demand more of those acts than its own rationale can tolerate; and (ii) an argumentative tic, where someone treats the constraint as defeasible when they want to act and as absolute when they want to refuse. I care much more about (i), but I do not know whether the name I am after covers it alone, covers both, or covers neither and leaves me describing something the literature has not isolated.

Please do not hand me a label and a paragraph. I want the carving: the nearest established concepts, what each gets right about the case above, and the precise point at which each stops fitting. If no term captures exactly this, say so and coin one, with a definition sharp enough that I can sort new cases by it. I am especially interested in the edges — whether the pattern requires the agent to be the one who would otherwise have to act, whether it survives when the agent could prevent the harm without acting at all, and whether it dissolves when the constraint is grounded not in the badness of the act but in something else, such as the status of the person or the wrongness of using someone merely as a means. I would also like it distinguished from the adjacent ideas I keep reaching for: the difference between doing and allowing, the thought that a harmful result may be foreseen without being intended, and the suggestion that a person may have a right to do what is morally wrong.
