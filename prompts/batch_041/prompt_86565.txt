I'm assembling cases for a unit on the persistence of artifacts, and the standard Ship of Theseus has become useless — every answer produces the same version, with the planks removed one at a time, stored, and later reassembled. Construct a fresh case that reproduces a specified three-way split by a different mechanism. The split must fall out of the views rather than be stipulated: a continuity view, on which a thing persists just in case its stages form an unbroken spatiotemporal-causal chain, must count the continuously maintained candidate as the original; a matter view, on which identity is carried by the material, so that the original is wherever its planks now are, must count the other candidate as the original; and mereological essentialism, on which a thing has its parts essentially, must say the original no longer exists at all.

Constraints: one realistic scenario, no time travel, teleportation, or branching; the second candidate must not be produced by anyone deliberately reassembling the original's parts — it has to arise incidentally, from the discarded material being reused in the ordinary course of business; the split must come out of the physical history alone, not from a renaming, a change of registry, or anyone declaring which is which; and apart from the matter-versus-continuity difference itself, the two candidates should be as alike as the scenario allows, so that no further asymmetry (a name, a title, a record) is doing the work.

Then, in order: the verdict of each view and the step that yields it; the single feature of the history that produces the split; what each verdict becomes if that feature is removed; and where a defender of the continuity view will say the matter view has changed the subject.

Give the scenario in one paragraph of at most 150 words and everything after it in at most 300 words. Flag which of the three verdicts is genuinely contested among people who work on persistence and which, if any, is settled; do not present a contested view as the consensus.
