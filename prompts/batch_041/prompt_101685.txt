My friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about acausal trade, and we ended up so far apart that I suspect we were not even disagreeing about the same proposition. I put roughly 0.65 on it being a real thing; she put roughly 0.15. I want your credence, but mostly I want the argument laid open.

Take the proposition to be this: there can exist two agents who are, and always will be, causally isolated from one another -- no signal passes between them, neither can alter the other's physical state -- such that each is rationally required, or at least rationally permitted and made better off by its own lights, to choose an action whose payoff depends on the other independently choosing a corresponding action, where the reason is not any causal influence between them but the fact that both agents' choices are fixed by the same decision procedure applied to a shared description of their situation.

Give me a number for that, then decompose it. I would suggest at least these terms, though add or replace as you judge right: (a) whether identity of decision procedure can create the kind of dependence the trade needs, as opposed to a mere correlation inside some reasoner's model; (b) whether such agents are physically or computationally realizable, rather than artifacts of an idealized reasoner with unlimited self-knowledge; (c) whether the payoff structure can keep the trade non-vacuous given that neither agent can verify the other's compliance; and (d) whether, even granting the mechanics, this grounds anything properly called a trade or an obligation rather than a self-interested bet on a coincidence. For each term give a number and a line of justification, then the combination, and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to.

Her sharpest move, which I could not answer on the spot, was this: any two agents who will reliably implement the same decision procedure are in fact causally connected -- through a shared origin, a shared designer, a shared training process, or a shared evolutionary history -- so the trade is never acausal at all; it is ordinary trade at a distance along the causal graph of their common cause. I could not tell whether that was a refutation of acausal trade or merely a redescription of it.

So, after your decomposition: tell me where she and I most likely actually disagree -- whether it is one of those terms, or whether one of us is using acausal trade for something the other is not -- and identify the single consideration that, if it came out one way rather than the other, would move your number the most.
