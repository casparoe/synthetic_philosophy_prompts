In 2000 the French Cour de cassation, sitting in plenary session, allowed Nicolas Perruche — born in the early 1980s with severe disabilities after a laboratory misread his mother's rubella tests and told her she was immune, when she had said she would terminate an affected pregnancy — to recover in his own name for the harm of the handicap he was born with, on the reasoning that the negligence had deprived his mother of a choice she was entitled to make. The ruling was widely read as declaring that some lives are not worth living, and in 2002 the legislature intervened to bar any claim founded on the bare fact of having been born; German constitutional doctrine had already rejected such claims as denying the equal worth of disabled life.

Read this episode through the procreation asymmetry rather than through tort doctrine. Take the asymmetry as: pain is bad and pleasure good; the absence of pain is good even where no one enjoys it, whereas the absence of pleasure is bad only where someone is deprived of it; so coming into existence can be worse for the one who exists, and never better. Then map the case onto it. What exactly was the court compensating — a handicap, or a life? Which comparison does the asymmetry license, this child disabled against this child healthy, or this child against no child, and does the second comparison have a subject at all, given that no one exists in the counterfactual branch to be better off? Does the asymmetry vindicate the court, or does it dissolve the court's disability-specific reasoning by implying that every child born has the same complaint, so that the rubella does no work? And what does the case do to the theory in turn: is the use made of the asymmetry here — a eugenic argument that one particular life was not worth starting — the same use its antinatalist proponents make of it, or does the theory require a threshold between a life worth not starting and a life worth compensating that it has never supplied? Say where the theory is silent rather than stretch it to speak.
