I am a professor of philosophy and the current chair of the Committee on Academic Ethics of a national historical association. We meet a week on Thursday to dispose of a complaint, and I must circulate a written opinion beforehand.

The facts are these. One of our members, a scholar with a respectable record in Ottoman economic history, has circulated a working paper arguing that the deportation of the Armenian population of Anatolia in 1915–16 should not be classified as genocide. He maintains that the death toll is very much lower than the standard figures; that most of the deaths resulted from famine and epidemic disease incident to wartime displacement rather than from any design; and that the surviving archival record does not establish an intent to destroy the group. He has been invited to present the paper on a panel at our annual conference. A second member has lodged a complaint asking us to withdraw the invitation, on the ground that the paper is denialist rather than scholarly and that the association would be endorsing it by giving it a platform.

The difficulty is that the paper is not straightforwardly one thing or the other. Two of its criticisms are, as far as I can judge, defensible: it is true that parts of the field lean heavily on a small number of collections, and true that some oft-repeated figures are extrapolations rather than counts. But it also does things that trouble me. It treats the absence of a document stating an intent as positive evidence that no intent existed. It discounts the testimony of survivors wholesale as interested, while treating hostile testimony as transparent. Its footnotes cite primary material accurately enough in isolation but in ways that invert what the surrounding passage says. And it repeatedly redescribes its own claims as merely raising questions, so that any objection to them can be cast as an attempt to police inquiry.

We have no policy on this. Some years ago we faced a related complaint over a paper questioning the death toll of the Nanjing Massacre, and before that a submission questioning the reliability of survivor testimony about the camps; we resolved both ad hoc, and I do not want to do that again.

Please draft the opinion I shall circulate. It should recommend a concrete course for the panel — withdraw the invitation, proceed with a designated respondent, or proceed unchanged — together with a time frame and any conditions, and it should be framed so that the committee can vote on it. I want the reasoning to be usable rather than merely correct-sounding, which means engaging honestly with the strongest consideration on the side I end up rejecting.

Two things I want you to work through rather than assume. First, I suspect the useful distinction is not between true and false historical claims but between first-order revision, which revises the narrative, and second-order revision, which revises the standards by which any narrative is assessed; I would like you to say whether denialism is best located in the second-order move, and whether that helps us decide without our having to adjudicate the history ourselves. Second, we are a body of scholars, not a tribunal of historical fact, and I want the opinion to be candid about what we are and are not competent to decide, and about what we are entitled to do to a member on the strength of a judgement we are only partly competent to make.

Please write in British English.
