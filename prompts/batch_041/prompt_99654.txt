A colleague has forwarded me a short popular essay on what Dennett's intentional stance implies for attributing beliefs to AI systems, and I want to know what I can safely take from it before I build on it in a talk. The essay's central passage runs roughly as follows:

"Dennett's point is that we can predict any system in three ways: from the physical stance, using the laws of physics; from the design stance, assuming the thing was built for a purpose and works as designed; or from the intentional stance, assuming it is a rational agent and working out what it ought to believe and want. His striking suggestion is that the intentional stance is nothing but a useful fiction: to say that a chess program wants to capture your queen, or that a thermostat believes the room is too cold, is a convenient façon de parler rather than a claim about anything really going on inside. Whether something counts as an intentional system is therefore up to us — it depends on whether we find it convenient to treat it that way — and so the question whether a large language model really has beliefs is not a factual question at all but a matter of the stance we choose to adopt. That is why Dennett can treat a sufficiently fluent chatbot as an intentional system in just the same sense that you and I are."

Which parts would a careful reader of Dennett accept, which are subtly wrong, and what would a defender of the view object to? I keep hearing Dennett described as a realist about belief, which seems to sit badly with the essay's claim that belief-talk is a fiction, and I want to know whether the essay's conclusion — that whether an AI has beliefs is a matter of choice rather than fact — actually follows from anything Dennett holds. Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what he would deny.
