I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about a case we both found harder than we expected. A wealthy stranger offers to clear your debts if you sincerely admire his collection of amateur watercolors. You cannot simply decide to admire them, but a reliable pill is available that would make you admire them sincerely. Is the stranger's offer a reason to take the pill, a reason to admire the watercolors, both, or neither? My friend kept saying, 'Wrong kind of reason, so it doesn't count,' and treated that one-liner as decisive: because the offer is the wrong kind of reason, it cannot be a reason to admire the watercolors at all, and the only rational response is to take the pill if you want the money and can tolerate the side effects. I am no longer sure whether the phrase is doing any real work or is just a label for the conclusion.

What I want from you is a menu of the precise claims that the one-liner could be making in this vicinity. Please distinguish, for example: the claim that reasons for attitudes are simply not reasons for actions; the claim that there are reasons for attitudes but only object-given ones, not state-given ones; the claim that state-given reasons are reasons to cause yourself to have an attitude rather than reasons for the attitude; the claim that such reasons are genuine reasons but cannot be the reasons for which one holds the attitude; the claim that they are genuine reasons that are silenced or overridden by object-given reasons; the claim that they are not normative reasons at all, only explanatory or motivating ones; and any other reading you think is distinct and worth separating. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial restatement, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something that is actually false or unstable, and say which reading would actually support my friend's conclusion that the offer cannot be a reason to admire the watercolors. I would also like to know which readings make the pill case come out differently from the case in which no pill is available, and which readings collapse the two cases. Finally, tell me which reading you think my friend most likely had in mind, given that he used the one-liner to end the argument rather than to begin an analysis of it. Lay this out as a numbered menu I can take back to him, with a short verdict on each item rather than a single overall answer.
