Last autumn I joined my high school's philosophy club, which is run by a psychology teacher who also covers our unit on religion and morality, and since then I have kept a notebook of her verdicts on whether one or another practice, belief, or institution makes people more moral. Her view is that the question of whether religion makes people better is nearly unanswerable as it is usually put, so she answers narrower questions instead, and her narrower answers have been remarkably consistent. She holds that the fact that religious people in many countries give away a larger share of their income than the nonreligious tells us almost nothing, because the difference is concentrated in giving to one's own congregation and co-religionists, and she counts generosity as a moral improvement only when it reaches people who are not already one's own. She holds that the Nordic countries are at once highly secular and high-trust, and takes this to show that what sustains trust is institutions and shared expectations rather than belief, so that the erosion of religion need not erode the thing people are most afraid of losing. She holds that a man who tithes mainly because he is terrified of damnation has done a great deal of good but has not become more moral, whereas a woman who anonymously gives a kidney to a stranger after a religious retreat has become more moral even though she describes what she did as obedience to God. And she insists that being more moral cannot simply mean producing more good outcomes, since someone who does the right thing by luck, or only because a crowd is watching, has not improved at all. A friend of mine thinks this whole way of talking is a category mistake, which is how we ended up arguing about it at lunch.

Next week the club takes up a case I brought in, and I would like you to predict her verdict on it and to tell me what her earlier verdicts commit her to. My older cousin, who is entirely nonreligious, belongs to a secular giving circle that asks its members to pledge five percent of their income to overseas charities; the members meet monthly, circulate a list of how much each person has given, and apply gentle public pressure to anyone who falls behind. My cousin is candid that the pledge and the group are the only reason she gives, and that she would stop the day the group dissolved, though she adds that she has lately found it hard to walk past a fundraiser without reaching for her wallet. Has belonging to this circle made her more moral? Please predict what our advisor will say, set out the two or three rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, say what each of those rules implies about my cousin and which one you would bet on and why, and name the further case that would best show which rule she is really running. I would also like to know what her answer implies about the larger question she keeps dodging, namely whether a secular society can sustain the trust that religious communities are often said to provide.
