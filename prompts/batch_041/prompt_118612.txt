Okay, I need a hand with a piece I'm writing. I cover science and ideas for a Sunday paper, so my week is mostly reading stuff like this and then trying to explain it to normal people. Right now I'm on conditionals — the if-this-then-that business — and specifically on David Lewis's triviality results, which everyone tells me are the reason you can't say the probability of 'if A then C' is just your credence in C given A. Fine, I believe them. But I tried to write the argument out for readers and my reconstruction ends up proving something so big that I dont believe it. Here's the version I've got, numbered the way I'd put it in a sidebar:

(1) Take a credence function P and a sentence 'if A then C'. Assume that P(if A then C) = P(C given A), whenever P(A) is above zero.
(2) Assume conditionals are the sort of thing that gets a probability in the first place, and that they can be nested, so 'if B then, if A then C' is a sentence with a probability like any other.
(3) Assume 'if B then, if A then C' says the same thing as 'if A and B then C'.
(4) Then by (1) and (3): P(if A then C, given B) = P(C given A and B).
(5) By (1), P(if A then C) = P(C given A). And by the ordinary law of total probability, P(if A then C) = P(if A then C, given B) times P(B), plus P(if A then C, given not-B) times P(not-B).
(6) Combine (4) and (5), do the same thing with the probabilities of B and not-B given A as the weights instead, and you can show that for any A, B and C at all, either P(C given A and B) equals P(C given A and not-B), or else B is independent of A.
(7) So as long as there is some B that's relevant to A, learning B can never tell you anything new about C once you know A — for every A, B and C whatsoever.
(8) Which is obviously false. So (1) is false, and the probability of a conditional is a mistake.

Step (7) is the one I can't stomach — I use conditional credences all day and they work fine, and if they didn't we'd have alot more trouble getting through Tuesday. It's possible I've botched the reconstruction rather than that Lewis did, so tell me if that's what's happened. Assuming it isn't: which step is the cheat, and what does each of the standard exits cost the people who take it? I'm thinking of the crowd who say conditionals don't have truth values at all, so there's nothing to hang a probability on; the crowd who keep truth conditions but restrict where the equation is allowed to apply; and the crowd who say the probability is fine but it's a non-classical sort of probability that the usual axioms don't fit. I'd rather keep (1) than (8) if I have to choose, but I want to know what I'd be buying and what I'd be giving up. And does the headline change — did Lewis kill the probability of conditionals, or only one version of it? If part of this fight is really two sides meaning different things by 'probability', say so, because I would hate to write 1,200 words about a verbal dispute.
