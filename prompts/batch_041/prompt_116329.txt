Here's the question and what the student wrote.

Question 3. Kant, "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy" (1797). Kant denies that it is permissible to lie to the would-be murderer who asks whether your friend is hiding in your house. (a) Set out the position Kant is responding to, and the argument he gives for his own conclusion. (b) Does his conclusion follow from his own principles? Argue for your answer.

Student's answer:

Kant is answering Benjamin Constant, who argued that we owe the truth only to those who have a right to it, and that a man who lies to a murderer about where his friend is hiding therefore does nothing wrong. Kant's reply is that veracity is a perfect duty and perfect duties admit no exceptions for the sake of good consequences: let one exception in, on the grounds that alot is at stake, and the duty becomes a rule of thumb that anyone can bend whenever the stakes look high enough. He doesn't put it Constant's way, but he keeps the substance -- the murderer has a claim on our truthfulness simply because he is a person asking us a question, and that claim doesn't evaporate because he means to do something terrible with the answer. Kant's own example of why the honest answer is also the safer one is the case where you lie and your friend, slipping out the back, runs straight into the murderer in the street and is killed; then you are answerable for the death, whereas if you had told the truth and the murderer had gone off to search the house, the death would not be your doing. So the utilitarian calculation people usually run here actually cuts the other way, since you can't control what the murderer does with your answer and lying only adds your own agency to the causal chain. On that basis I think the conclusion does follow from Kant's principles. Veracity is a duty to others, and to make an exception here would be to treat the murderer merely as a means to a good outcome. There is a weaker reading on which Kant is only saying you mustn't lie, not that you must answer -- you could simply refuse to tell him anything -- but that is a minor qualification and doesn't affect the main point.
