ok so I've been trying to write down my actual views on precommitment and self-binding for a reading group next week, and I keep going in circles, so I want an outside audit before I present. Some context: I spent my formative years on the rationalist blogs and I've absorbed the Ulysses-contract worldview pretty thoroughly — I think of myself as a temporally extended collection of agents who can and should negotiate with each other, and I think constitutions are basically the same trick at the scale of a polity. Here's where I've landed. (1) Reasons are internal: nothing binds you no matter what you happen to care about, and all the "you ought to" talk is relative to ends you already have. (2) Ulysses contracts really do bind. If I sign something or install something to stop me drinking, then future-me who disarms it is acting irrationally, and "I changed my mind" is not by itself a good reason. (3) What does the work in (2) is that the tempted self's preferences are distorted — he isn't really endorsing the drink, his ends are being hijacked, so his preference at that moment doesn't count the way his calm preferences do. (4) Personal identity isn't all-or-nothing; what matters in survival is psychological connectedness and it comes in degrees, so my relation to my 70-year-old self is much thinner than common sense pretends. (5) Entrenched constitutional rights — the ones that take supermajorities to touch — are legitimate and valuable, and I'd want them even in a pretty decent democracy; my line is that the rights are constitutive of the democratic game, so entrenching them isn't a past majority bossing a present one, it's just fixing the rules that make a majority legitimate. (6) The dead hand objection is basically correct: a past democratic majority has no authority over a present one, I'm against balanced-budget amendments and anything else that locks policy in, and the right response to "but the framers intended X" is "so what". (7) A case verdict I feel strongly about: if I sign an advance directive refusing treatment once I have dementia, and the demented me is cheerful and wants the treatment, the directive should win — earlier me gets to decide, and the cheerful guy isn't the author of my life.

I can feel that something in there is doing more work than it's licensed to do, but evey time I try to say which one has to go I end up defending all of them. Please audit the set. Don't tell me it's all a matter of degree and everyone is inconsistent, and don't just list every pair that could be read as clashing — I want to know which commitments actually pull against which, which clashes are only apparent, and what the cheapest way out is.

Format I'd like, in this order:

- Bottom line: two or three sentences naming the single commitment most likely to be the odd one out, plus the runner-up.
- The real tensions, ranked: one numbered subsection per tension, worst first, 150–250 words each, saying which commitments collide, why it isn't a surface clash, and exactly what would have to be given up to fix it.
- False alarms: the pairs that look tense but aren't, a couple sentences each on why the appearance dissolves. Around 150 words.
- The cheapest repair: the single revision that fixes the most with the least collateral damage, what it costs me, and what I'd be stuck with that I probably won't like. 200–300 words.
