I have been arguing for weeks with a friend who is a keen amateur of collapse narratives, and he deploys the Easter Island story as a proof rather than an illustration: a small, isolated, finite island whose inhabitants stripped their own forests and toppled their own society, which he says is simply our planet in miniature, a dress rehearsal at a smaller scale. His contention is that the analogy is scale-invariant — substitute eight billion of us for a few thousand islanders, fossil carbon and topsoil for the palm forest, growth economics for the statue-building, and the same dynamics run — because both systems are closed, bounded, and deplete a single limiting resource with delayed feedback. From this he concludes that cases of adaptation or partial recovery teach us nothing, since collapse is simply what closed systems do, and the only remaining question is whether we choose our descent or have it forced upon us. A third party in the argument insists that collapse is a historian's artefact and that societies never really fail, they merely reorganise — she offers the Maya, whose cities emptied while the people carried on — and says the island is therefore the wrong model altogether.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than the history. Begin with a correspondence table: island against planet, islanders against us, palm forest against whatever you think genuinely plays that role, the statues against whatever, any incidental actors the story contains — stowaways, pests, pathogens — against their supposed counterparts, and the surrounding ocean against whatever it is meant to stand for. Be explicit wherever the target has no counterpart at all. Then separate the disanalogies that actually do work for his intended conclusion from the ones that are merely differences and change no inference; I do not want an inventory of every way an island is unlike a planet, only the ones that alter what can be concluded. Two features strike me as load-bearing. The first is whether closure and isolation transfer at all from a mid-Pacific island to a planet that has had global trade, migration, technological substitution and access to energy sources with no island analogue. The second is whether the premise that the islanders knowingly destroyed themselves is secure enough to bear the weight he places on it; if the archaeology is genuinely contested, say so plainly and tell me what follows for the analogy. You cannot look anything up, so I would far rather you flag uncertainty than invent detail.

Finally, does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? Does my third friend's objection bite on the analogy itself, or is it orthogonal to the question of whether the island models the planet? And if you were to replace the analogy with a better one for our situation, what would it be, and what would that replacement quietly smuggle in that the island does not?
