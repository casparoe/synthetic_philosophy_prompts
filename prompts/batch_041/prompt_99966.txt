I'm giving a paper at our department's philosphy of religion workshop in a few weeks and I want to be ready for the question period, so I'm asking you to build me a prep sheet. Here's what I'm defending: middle knowledge is real, but it is not a third kind of knowledge sitting between God's knowledge of necessities and his knowledge of his own decree. It's just God's natural knowledge of a special class of truths -- namely, truths about what each possible free creature would do if placed in various circumstances -- and the mistake everyone makes is treating those as contingent truths that need a truthmaker outside God. On my view they're true in virtue of the creature's individual essence, and I'm treating the essence as a dispositional profile rather than a bare haecceity, so God knows them the same way he knows any other essential truth. No grounding problem, no extra "moment" in the divine mind. Call it Molinism about the content but minus the third category. I know that sounds like it can't work; I have about 25 minutes for the paper so the whole thing rests on one move, that these counterfactuals of freedom are essential rather than contigent.

The room is about ten people and I know them well. Two are Reformed compatibilists who think middle knowledge is an incoherent attempt to give God foreknowledge of free acts without taking responsibility for them; they'll also say my move makes the counterfactual true no matter what God does, so I've just relocated the problem instead of solving it. Two are open theists who think libertarian freedom is incompatible with God knowing the outcome at all, and they'll say I've made things worse by making it necessary. One is a classical Thomist who'll insist God knows creatures by causing them, so any counterfactual that's true before God acts is a fiction. One or two grad students who mainly want to know if this is just Ockhamism with extra steps. And my commentator works on grounding, so he'll probably go straight for the truthmaker principle I'm leaning on.

Give me the prep sheet. I want the objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how likely each one is and how much damage it does, each phrased in the vocabulary of whoever would raise it (the Thomist's objection shouldn't sound like the open theist's), with a two-minute reply for each and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which one or two objections have to be defused or the paper is dead, what I should just concede up front to buy credibility, and finish with the question I'm dreading -- the one I'm hoping nobody in the room thinks to ask. Write it in American English, and skip the reading lists and citations, I only want the questions and the replies.
