Me and a friend have been going round in circles on the paradox of deontology — the worry that if a constraint against killing one person to save five is supposed to protect people from being killed, it's odd that the constraint forbids you from killing one precisely in order to stop five killings; the constraint seems to care about the act itself rather than the harm it exists to prevent, which makes it look self-undermining. I've ended up defending a line I've half made up myself and I want to know how exposed it is. The line is this: constraints are absolute — you may never violate one in order to prevent more violations of the same constraint — but they only bind you in your dealings with agents who are themselves bound by the same constraint, so a reciprocity or accountability condition fixes the scope. I suppose it probably has a proper name and I just don't know it, but I'm calling it reciprocity-restricted absolutism. I like it because it seems to explain why we owe the full constraint to fellow moral agents while keeping it absolute inside that circle.

What would count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and don't just give me the critics' list — tell me what someone who actually holds this would themselves concede counts against them. I'm most interested in three things. First, whether it collapses into something else once you spell out the reciprocity condition — contractualism, some rule-consequentialism, or a threshold view that isn't really absolutist at all. Second, the cases it can't describe, above all the non-reciprocators: infants, people with severe cognitive disabilities, animals, future people. If the constraint doesn't bind with respect to them, does the view license anything at all done to them, and is that a consequence I have to swallow or a reductio? Third, whether the paradox simply reappears inside the circle of reciprocators, so that the absolutism is doing no real work. Also be honest about whether I've quietly made the thing unfalsifiable by defining the circle of reciprocators so as to exclude exactly the hard cases, and whether that would even be a problem for a view like this. If you think one particular case or result decides the whole business, say which and why. I'd be most moved by a case involving someone who genuinely can't reciprocate.

British English, please.
