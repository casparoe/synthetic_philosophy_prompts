Alright, wierd ask. I've been roped into giving the commencement address at my old college this spring — small liberal arts school, a few hundred grads, families in folding chairs, the whole thing. The twist is the dean asked me to "touch on" how our country shot up the World Happiness Report rankings last year, and I'm pretty sure she wants a line about us being one of the happiest places on earth. I'd rather not, and not just because of nerves.

Here's the thing I can't stop chewing on, and it's what the speech needs to be built around. My understanding is the ranking is basically one survey item: you ask people where they'd put themselves on a 0-to-10 ladder for their life as a whole, you average it, you rank countries. That's a life-evaluation number, not a happiness number. It doesn't tell you whether anyone is happy as they actually live, and it doesn't tell you whether their lives are going well by any thicker standard — whether they get what they want, or live well in some objective sense; pick your theory, the ladder doesn't care. A friend in the psych department swears we only "rose" because the survey switched from face-to-face to phone interviews, which changes who answers and how people use the top of the scale — no idea if that's true and I can't check. And then the obvious thing: the second topping the ranking becomes the goal, you get every incentive to move the number without moving anything it was supposed to stand for. (I've spent way too many evenings on rationalist blogs, so "the measure becomes the target" is basically my personality now.)

So what I want from you is a commencement speech, 8-10 minutes, that's genuinely warm — the parents and grandparents in the back need to leave feeling good about their kid, and I do want to hand these students something real to aim at, not a cynical "all metrics lie" shrug. But the whole thing has to be shaped by my actual view: the ranking measures something thin and maybe partly manufactured, and a country — or a person — trying to top it is the surest way not to. And it has to survive the econ and psych faculty, who know exactly what's in that survey and will be watching me. Don't have me cite anyone, don't have me say we're the happiest place on earth, and if you can land one non-obvious point — say, why a place can climb the ranking without a single person getting happier — even better.
