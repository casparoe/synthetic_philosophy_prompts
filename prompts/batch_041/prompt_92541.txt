I would like to know whether the following pattern has an established name, and if it does not, I would like a careful account of what the pattern is and where its edges lie — a bare label would not help me.

The pattern shows up whenever a decision to abandon something is justified by the sheer magnitude of what has already been spent on it. A laboratory has worked a line of research for six years and is at last getting results; the next experiment is cheap and the expected payoff is large. The director shuts the line down on the grounds that too much has already been put into it. A board cancels a product that is nine months from launch, on schedule and on budget, because the cumulative expenditure looks bad and the directors do not want the figure to grow. In each case nothing about the future argues for stopping; what argues for stopping is the size of the past. And in each case the people involved take themselves to be clear-eyed about waste rather than sentimental about it.

What interests me is that the standard vocabulary for this area appears to be built for the opposite direction. The sunk cost fallacy is ordinarily described as the tendency to continue because of past expenditure — throwing good money after bad, escalating commitment. What I am describing is the tendency to stop because of past expenditure. The two look like the same error: in both, the decision turns on a quantity with no forward-looking bearing, and they differ only in the direction in which that quantity pushes. That makes me suspect that the sunk cost fallacy does not name a bias so much as one special case of one — the case in which the irrelevant quantity happens to favour continuing — and that the underlying phenomenon is better described as deciding on the cumulative ledger rather than on the margin. A further wrinkle: the past expenditure is sometimes not even the decider's own. An insurer that has already paid out heavily on a patient may decline to fund the treatment that would now help most, and the fact that the money is gone from the insurer's accounts rather than the patient's does not seem to change the shape of the reasoning.

So I would like to know three things. First, whether the stopping direction has a term of its own; the label I have seen for the continuing direction, escalation of commitment, plainly does not fit a cancelled project. Second, whether there is a term for the general pattern, if the two directions are in fact one phenomenon. Third, and most importantly, where the boundaries fall: how the pattern I have described differs from ordinary loss aversion, from a decision-maker's defensible concern for reputation, from an honest preference for finishing what one has begun, from the tendency to sell winners early and hold losers, and from the case in which a large prior investment genuinely is evidence that the project is unsound — this last being the case in which the past really does bear on the future.

Please commit to a position rather than surveying the field. Tell me whether you take these to be one phenomenon or two, where exactly you would draw the line between them, whether the stopping version is a mistake in the same sense as the continuing version or a different and perhaps less culpable one, and why you draw the lines where you do.
