I teach history at an international school where most of my students are Japanese or Korean, and several of them carry their home country's history textbook in their bag; the two books do not agree. Last month my unit on the first half of the twentieth century ended in a shouting match — one student told another she was repeating a lie, a third walked out — and I spent the hour afterwards understanding that I had taught them the events and not the argument. I teach the unit again next term, and this time I want to teach the shape of the disagreement rather than referee it. Please plan the unit; do not explain the history to me.

My working sketch, which I would like you to correct if it is wrong or badly ordered, is that a historical quarrel can sit on four levels. The factual: how many, when, whether coercion was involved. The compositional: where the story begins, what counts as its cause, which facts are load-bearing and which are dropped. The lexical: what the event and the people in it are called, and what each name quietly asserts. The purposive: what the story is for now — pride, warning, atonement, reconciliation — and who has standing to tell it. My diagnosis of the shouting match is that most quarrels get prosecuted on one level as though they were on another: someone is called a liar when the real dispute is about salience, or a plain factual question is waved away as mere narrative.

For three fifty-minute periods with fifteen- and sixteen-year-olds, I need: the order the ideas must come in, and what has to be in place before the four levels can even be distinguished; the one case that should carry the unit, what it must have (all sides agreeing on the bare facts, the differences lying only in selection, naming and purpose) and what it must lack (no outright falsehood on either side, no cartoon villain), and why that case rather than the obvious ones; the misconceptions the students will arrive with and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off — I am already expecting them to say that the truth lies in the middle, that any shaped account is a distortion, and that a family's account is simply what happened; the single exercise that would show me whether they can place a disagreement on the right level rather than merely being polite to one another; and what I should openly refuse to resolve in front of them, and say so. No reading list — whatever carries this has to be something I can put in the room.
