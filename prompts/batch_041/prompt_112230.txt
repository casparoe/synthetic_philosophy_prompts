A doctoral student in my proseminar has put the following argument to me, and I have not yet found a diagnosis of it that satisfies me. Its conclusion is one I reject with more confidence than I attach to any of its premises, so something in it must give way. I would like you to find the failure and price the repairs.

(1) The world may contain infinitely many people: space may be infinite, or the future endless and populated, and I cannot assign that zero credence. (2) Every person's welfare counts equally, and the value of a state of affairs is the sum of the welfare of the people in it. (3) The sum of infinitely many finite positive quantities is itself infinite. (4) Whatever I do, the world that results contains infinitely many people, and adding or subtracting the finite difference my action makes leaves that sum infinite. (5) So the world's value is the same infinite quantity whatever I do. (6) If two options have the same value, I have no reason to prefer one to the other. (7) Therefore nothing I do makes the world better or worse, and I have no reason to act at all: the difference between saving a child from a life of agony and rearranging the pens on my desk is not a difference that counts.

I am confident that (7) is false, but I am attached to (2), and reluctant to surrender (1), since I have no way of ruling out an infinite world a priori. Tell me which step fails, and why it looked innocent. Then tell me what repairing it would cost. Can I keep additive aggregation and revise only my decision theory for infinite cases, or does the trouble reach (2) itself? And if the argument is instead valid and one of (1) through (6) must simply be given up, say which, and say what that surrender costs elsewhere — for instance, what becomes of our ordinary judgements about the far future once it is made.
