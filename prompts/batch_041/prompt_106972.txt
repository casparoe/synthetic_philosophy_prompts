I spent forty years sorting specimens into species and signing my name under the resulting names, and I never once asked what I was doing. I have now retired, and have spent the past year reading philosophy, which has had the unfortunate effect of making me ask. I should like you to build me a case that I can use in a short essay on whether species names track kinds or lineages, and I should like it built to a specification.

Set out two views of what a species is. On the first, a species is a kind: a class of organisms sharing a sufficient cluster of diagnostic characters, with no requirement that its members share a common ancestor. On the second, a species is a particular: one spatiotemporally continuous lineage, an individual with parts rather than a class with members, whose identity is fixed by ancestry and continuity rather than by any list of characters. Your case must make these two views disagree about whether two given populations are one species or two, and it must do so in the direction that is hard to arrange: the kind view must return one species, the lineage view two.

The constraints are these. The scenario must be one a working systematist could actually encounter: no science fiction, no instantaneous speciation, nothing requiring new physics, and no more than a single paragraph — I would rather have a plain case than a baroque one. The two populations must be as alike as you can make them, differing in nothing but ancestry, and their likeness must be explained rather than stipulated: give me a plausible reason why two separate ancestries should have arrived at the same appearance. There must be no gene flow and no hybridisation between them. The disagreement must be about the criterion and not about the evidence: assume the evidence is as complete as you please, including whatever would expose their separate ancestries, and the two verdicts must still stand. Hold everything else fixed — appearance, anatomy, behaviour, ecology — so that the single variable left free is ancestry. And the verdicts must be generated by the two criteria, not smuggled in by the way you describe the case.

Then do the accounting. For each view, state its verdict and the step in its own criterion that produces it. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens when I remove it: in particular, say whether the disagreement collapses if the two populations turn out to descend from a single ancestral population, and why. Say how a defender of the kind view would attack the case, and what would have to be true for that attack to succeed. Tell me what a view that defines a species by reproductive community would say, and whether that is a verdict or a refusal to give one. And tell me which verdict a systematist would in practice reach, and why that is not decisive. If two parts of the specification cannot be met together, tell me which, rather than quietly dropping one.

Please write your answer in British English.
