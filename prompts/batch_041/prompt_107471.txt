You are advising me, the newly appointed chair of the speech-review panel at a mid-sized social platform. The panel meets twice a week, sits three people — me, a lawyer, and a community representative — and disposes of roughly a hundred appealed moderation decisions a month under a two-week clock. The backlog is real, and our decisions are published in a transparency report, so every ruling has to carry reasons a stranger could read.

Our users are concentrated in the United States, Germany, France, and the Netherlands, and we have promised, in a public charter, two things at once: that we will never remove a post merely because it is offensive, since we do not want to be the arbiter of what counts as true or decent; and that we will meet every legal obligation we are subject to in each country where we operate. The legal team discharges the second promise by handing us a list of posts that are unlawful somewhere. The panel's problem is everything else — the posts no court has ruled on that a hundred users have reported.

I want a decision procedure the panel can run on any reported post: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a decision to leave a post up or take it down, and where it must stop and hand the question to a person because it has become a question of value rather than of fact. I want it grounded in the actual difference between the American approach to hate speech and the European one, not in a bland compromise between them. Take the American materials seriously: the content-neutrality and viewpoint-neutrality presumptions, the insistence that the remedy for bad speech is more speech, and the deliberately narrow categorical exceptions — the incitement standard requiring both an intent to produce imminent lawless action and a real likelihood of it, the true-threat standard, and the fighting-words standard with its near-demise in modern doctrine. Take the European materials seriously too: the dignity-based reasoning that treats some speech as an assault on the equal standing of a group rather than as a contribution to a debate, the group-defamation and incitement-to-hatred provisions, the memory laws that criminalise denial of atrocities, and the underlying premise that a state committed to equal respect may legitimately restrict speech that would erode it — the thought that neutrality is not a live option, and that a state which refuses to choose has simply chosen the status quo. The interesting question for me is not which tradition is right; it is what a procedure looks like when it must answer to both, and what it has to give up to do so.

The procedure therefore has to be honest about three specific pressures, each named and handled rather than smoothed over. First, the same post can be plainly lawful in one of our markets and plainly criminal in another, so we must decide whether to run one global rule or several, and what each costs. Second, the American exception-categories are narrow precisely to keep the state — or us — from deciding which viewpoints are hateful, while the European approach requires exactly that judgment; any combined procedure must say who makes the judgment and by what standard. Third, our charter's first promise may be a promise we cannot keep, because some of what we remove for the sake of the second is offensive speech we claimed never to touch.

Then run the procedure on three cases, in this order, showing the questions answered and where the procedure stops: a post denying a historical atrocity, written in the register of academic revisionism, by a user in a country where such denial is a criminal offence; a slur used by members of the group it targets, in an in-group context, reported by outsiders who saw it out of context; and a widely circulating cartoon that mocks a religion, defended by its author as satire and condemned by the group it depicts as an attack.

At least one of your three outcomes must be one I will not like — either a post left up that I find repellent, or a post taken down that I regard as valuable speech — and you should say plainly which it is and why the procedure forces it. End by telling me what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will get wrong, the judgments it performs mechanically only in appearance, and the point at which the panel should stop pretending it is applying a rule and admit that it is choosing a policy.
