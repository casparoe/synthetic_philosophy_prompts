For the past eight months I have been trying to get a straight answer about how my father died. He was in a residential care home on a schedule of checks every two hours through the night; the log shows that two of those checks were skipped, and he was found dead at dawn from a condition the checks were specifically designed to catch. I am a philosopher by training and have spent years working on the metaphysics of causation, but until this year I treated the question of whether an omission can cause anything as a tidy seminar puzzle. The log has made it personal, and I have arrived at a position I now want to defend in public: that the skipped checks were not merely a breach of protocol or a background circumstance but a genuine cause of his death — that an omission can occupy the causal relation itself, and not only the moral one.

Next month the regional clinical ethics committee has agreed to hear me out. It is a mixed body of about a dozen people: two intensivists, a nurse consultant, a hospital solicitor, a clinical psychologist, a chaplain, and two lay members. None of them are philosophers. They are fluent in duties, consent, standards of care, and what makes a practice defensible; they are not fluent in the metaphysics of causation, and I expect several of them to walk in already convinced that the death was from natural causes and that the missed checks were a failure of care rather than its cause.

Please draft a question-and-answer preparation sheet for that meeting. I want the objections I will actually face from this room — not the ones a metaphysics seminar would raise — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, and phrased in the vocabulary of whoever would raise it, since the solicitor's version of the but-for question is not the chaplain's. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is weakest, and what I should simply concede rather than fight. Where a reply turns on a distinction — between a cause and a mere background condition, say, or between an absence and an event — state it in one plain sentence the whole room will follow; I do not want to lecture them, and I do not want to be caught asserting that every unperformed action is therefore a cause. Tell me which concession I should make before anyone asks for it, which single objection I must answer well for the evening to go anywhere, and, finally, the question I am hoping nobody asks. Keep the sheet self-contained; I do not need reading lists or references, and they would not help this audience.
