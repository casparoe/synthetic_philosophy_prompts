You are an experienced examiner in political philosophy and the theory of collective action, writing the final assessment for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on commons, public goods, and cooperation. Your task is to produce one exam question worth 20 marks and answerable in about 90 minutes, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Before drafting, reason through where the crux of the exercise lies — the point at which a student who can only recite the familiar story about shared resources being doomed to overuse will be forced to stop — and build the question around that crux rather than around a summary of the topic.

Use the following scenario as the spine of the question, since its labels do not settle it: a research institute holds one pooled annual allocation of compute hours on a shared cluster. No lab can practically be locked out, and there is no per-lab cap. Off-peak, one lab's jobs do not slow anyone else's; at peak, jobs queue behind one another and some labs need fast turnaround. The allocation is renewed each year by voluntary contributions from member labs, and two of the larger labs also maintain the shared software environment that everyone relies on.

The question should require candidates to do more than name the situation. At minimum it should make them (i) diagnose the scenario at the relevant margin, distinguishing a provision problem from an appropriation problem and saying whether the good is rival, excludable, or neither, and whether the answer changes with load; (ii) state the strategic structure of the over-use problem and the assumptions under which over-use is a dominant strategy, then identify a change to the payoffs that turns the same situation into a coordination problem with a cooperative equilibrium; (iii) explain why shared ownership is neither necessary nor sufficient for a "tragedy," giving one case of each; and (iv) assess three remedies — assigning private quotas, charging a congestion price, and devolving to user self-governance with monitoring and graduated sanctions — saying what each gets right and what it leaves untouched. If all four parts will not fit 90 minutes and 20 marks, cut the weakest part rather than thinning the others.

Then supply a model answer of roughly 750 words that demonstrates the reasoning rather than gesturing at it, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the error that typically costs candidates marks — for instance, treating rivalry as a binary property, or assuming that any repeated interaction automatically solves the problem.

The assessment must be completable with no literature: require no citations, no author names, and no reading list, and do not let the model answer rest on contested empirical claims about particular historical commons. Write the model answer in continuous prose, put nothing in quotation marks, and award no marks for name-dropping. Finish with a two-sentence note on what separates a 15–17 answer from an 18–20 answer.
