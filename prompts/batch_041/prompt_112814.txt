I chair a small foundation that has spent sixty years paying for memorials — plaques, a restored meeting hall, a garden with a stone for a shipwreck, an obelisk for a mill fire. This year, for the first time, our annual award goes to a project that did the opposite: a village that took down its monument to a nineteenth-century massacre and, instead of replacing it with a corrected one, planted an orchard and let the ground go back to being ordinary. I have to give the award address, and I need it to do something difficult. It has to honor that choice without turning our sixty years of building memorials into a long mistake, and it has to carry the whole weight of the argument on a single distinction that I have come to believe is the only one that matters in this territory: the difference between forgetting that can be reversed and forgetting that cannot. Sealed records with a date written on the seal, a monument crated and put in a warehouse, an anniversary quietly allowed to pass unremarked, a name left off a new plaque that could always be added to an old one — these are forgettings that can be undone, and I think a community may undertake them almost freely, as a kindness and a form of rest. Records burned, a monument ground to dust, a name struck from every register in the county — these cannot be undone, and the case for them is far harder, even when the thing remembered is monstrous. I want the address to turn on that line, and to use it to explain what our foundation has actually been doing all these years and why this year's award belongs in the same story rather than against it.

The room will be mixed, and that is the point of the difficulty. In it will be families of the people the old monument named, some of whom are glad it is gone and some of whom feel that the orchard is one more erasure; local officials who will want to hear that nothing illegal or shameful happened; a school group; and a handful of historians who know this literature far better than I do and will notice at once if I am being sentimental. So the speech has to land for people who have lived inside this quarrel and for people who have never heard of it, without ever sounding like a lecture to either. I do not want it to name any theorists, cite any books, or gesture at a reading list — I am speaking aloud to a crowd, and anything that sounds like a footnote will lose them. I do not want it to simply restate the familiar opposition between a duty to remember and a case for forgetting; I want my reversible-versus-irreversible line to do the structural work, deciding what the speech can say about our own past grants, which examples it can lean on, and how it frames the award itself. And it has to give the strongest opposing view its due rather than a polite nod: the serious claim that some remembering is itself the disease, that a community can be held hostage by an injury it has agreed never to stop rehearsing, and that even an irreversible forgetting is sometimes the only mercy available. If that claim is right, my line has to survive it, not dodge it.

Please write the address. Roughly ten minutes spoken, in continuous prose with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables — the way one would write a letter that has to be read aloud.
