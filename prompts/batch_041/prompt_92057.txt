I'm a philosophy MA student doing a term placement with a small anti-corruption NGO, and I keep getting pulled into an arguement I don't have the vocabulary to win. The consultant they've brought in -- economist, very attached to his own blazer -- closes every discussion with 'sunlight is the best disinfectant', usually to mean we should publish officials' asset declarations and gift registers. Last week he sharpened it in a way I can't stop chewing on: he said that anything an official would be happy to see on the front page of the paper isn't corruption, anything he'd want kept quiet is, and that this gives us a clean test that doesn't depend on anybody's culture. Our local office lead, who grew up somewhere the district officer is expected to find his nephews work and is thought a disgrace if he won't, says that test just bakes in one particular picture of what an official is. Her line is that whether a gift to an official counts as corruption or as reciprocity depends on what the office is *for*, and on whether the obligation you fail by taking it is owed to the public or to your own people. I'm not asking you to referee that fight for me, though if a verdict falls out of the analysis I'll take it.

What I want is to see how much is actually packed into the slogan itself. So: lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, roughly ordered from the ones that are nearly empty to the ones that are load-bearing -- does it mean transparency improves detection, does it mean it deters because officials price in the odds of being seen, does it mean visibility changes what people are willing to treat as normal, does it mean that corruption just *is* whatever can't survive publication, does it mean something about who gets to hold the lamp (because the metaphor cuts both ways -- sunlight bleaches, and disinfectant kills the useful bacteria too, like the informal trust and discretion that keep an underfunded office running). For each one, tell me what it commits you to, whether you'd defend it, and whether it would actually support the move the consultant is making with it -- and be straight with me about which reading he needs, because I suspect he's quietly sliding from one to another mid-sentence. Also say which of them settle the culture question and which just presuppose an answer to it. No references needed, I just want the distinctions.

Give it back as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no wrap-up -- I'm pasting it straight into my notes before the next meeting. And if the way I've set this up is confused -- if the slogan really carries only one claim and the rest is decoration, or if 'corruption' won't stand still long enough to have readings at all -- don't pad the list to five to be polite, just say so.
