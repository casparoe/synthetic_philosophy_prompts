In popular commentary on democratic theory, Arrow's impossibility theorem is routinely introduced through an analogy with Gödel's incompleteness theorems: just as Gödel is said to have shown that any sufficiently rich formal system must be incomplete, Arrow is said to have shown that any sufficiently fair voting rule must be defective, so the pursuit of a perfect electoral mechanism is as hopeless as the pursuit of a complete and consistent axiomatization of arithmetic. The analogy is then used to license a deflationary conclusion: all voting rules are equally arbitrary, reform is mathematically naive, and we should simply choose which defects we find tolerable. I want an audit of the analogy, not a restatement of either theorem. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Item 1 should state the mapping the analogy asks us to accept and the conclusion it is meant to support. Item 2 should map the correspondences in compressed prose: what plays the role of the formal system, its axioms, its expressive power, the Gödel sentence, consistency, completeness, and undecidability, and what plays the corresponding roles in the aggregation of individual preferences. Item 3 should identify where the mapping breaks, attending especially to self-reference, the ordinal versus cardinal character of preferences, independence of irrelevant alternatives, the finite domain, the pivotal-voter construction, and the difference between a formal limit and a manipulability result such as Gibbard-Satterthwaite. Item 4 should separate the disanalogies that matter for the deflationary conclusion from those that do not, including escape routes such as domain restrictions, strategic voting, and cardinal or interpersonally comparable information. Item 5 should say whether the deflationary conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, what a better analogy would be.
