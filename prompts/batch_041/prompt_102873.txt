I am putting together a long-form interview for a series on moral psychology, and next month's guest is a philosopher whose reading of Smith I want to probe rather than showcase. Her line is that the impartial spectator has been badly misread as an idealized observer — a better-informed, cooler-headed version of ourselves — when it is in fact a thoroughly socialized figure: the internalized voice of the standards of the people around us. On her account, Smith is offering a theory of how we become answerable to one another, not a device for stepping outside our culture; the impartial spectator is less a vantage point we ascend to than a second-personal demand we learn to hear. She also holds that this reading exposes a vulnerability the ideal-observer reading conceals: because the spectator we internalize is drawn from our actual audience, the mechanism can be captured by audiences that are neither impartial nor well informed, and she proposes the imagined online crowd — a mass of strangers whose reactions we anticipate but who have no claim to detachment — as a test case. She has a name for the captured version: the counterfeit spectator.

Draft eight to ten interview questions. They should engage the substance of her position rather than invite a summary of it, and I want at least one question on each of the following:

(a) how her reading distinguishes the impartial spectator from the internalized judge Smith personifies as the man within the breast, and whether that distinction survives once the spectator is socialized;
(b) how, on her view, the standard gets corrected at all — if the spectator is drawn from our community, what corrects the community;
(c) whether sympathy, understood as the imaginative fellow-feeling Smith starts from, can generate a spectator with any authority beyond the sympathy it started from;
(d) whether her counterfeit-spectator case shows the mechanism failing in a genuinely new way or merely failing by degrees, as she must admit it always could.

For each question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I am not left flat-footed when she reaches for her standard reply. End with two closing questions: one that forces her to say what would count against her reading, and one that asks her to name a concrete case in which her reading and the ideal-observer reading deliver different verdicts about what a person should feel.

Keep the questions free of jargon she would have to decode, avoid anything that invites her to list her influences or recommend further reading, and do not ask how she came to philosophy.
