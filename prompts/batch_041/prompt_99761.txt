I keep the books for a small family manufacturing firm — payroll, quarterly filings, the works — and after enough years of discounting future obligations at our borrowing rate I have started to wonder what exactly I am doing when I do it. When we set aside a provision for cleaning up a contaminated lot that we will not have to remediate for twenty years, I take the estimated cost, divide it by a compounding factor, and book the smaller number today; the auditor signs off, and everyone in the room treats the smaller number as the true one. Not long ago I heard a discussion about whether it is defensible to care less about future people merely because they are in the future, and the comparison offered was to caring less about people who happen to live far away. It struck me that I could not say which part of my discounting was a fact about money and which part was a moral position I had never consciously adopted. So here is what I would like from you. Take the philosophical debate over pure time preference and the discounting of the future — the charge that a preference for the present just because it is present is ethically indefensible, the replies that lean on uncertainty about the future and on the likelihood that future people will simply be richer than us, and the underlying question of whether the discount rate is something we discover in markets or something we choose — and restate that debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in: present value and future value, discount factors, the time value of money, cost of capital, opportunity cost, provisions and impairment, accrual versus cash accounting. I have no philosophical training whatsoever; treat me as fluent in bookkeeping and accounting conventions and a complete beginner everywhere else.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' disagreement map cleanly onto mechanics I use every day — and I suspect you will have to tell me something uncomfortable here, because in my world the discount rate is simply whatever our lender charges, and I have never once asked whether it was the right rate to apply to people who do not exist yet. Which parts survive the translation only if you distort them, and which parts have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? In particular, I want to know whether the thing the philosophers call a pure rate of time preference — as distinct from a rate that merely reflects growth, or risk, or the plain fact that capital is productive — is something my books can even represent, or whether the moment I write down a single rate I have already smuggled in a moral judgment under the guise of a market fact. And if the residue turns out to be large, tell me honestly whether that is a gap in accounting or a sign that the philosophers have been arguing about something less precise than they imagine.
