I have been asked to write a short statement of my interpretive commitments for a judicial selection questionnaire, and before I commit to it I want a candid audit of whether the positions actually hang together. Here is where I have landed:

(1) The meaning of a statute is its public meaning at the time of enactment — what a competent speaker of the language would have understood the words to mean — and not whatever the drafters privately hoped to accomplish. (2) It follows that legislative history has no authority to fix a statute's meaning; at most it can confirm what the enacted words already said. (3) When the enacted text is genuinely indeterminate, a court must go beyond the words and settle the case by asking which reading best serves the statute's apparent purpose and yields the more sensible result. (4) A text that is clear must be applied as written even when it produces a result the enacting legislature did not anticipate or would have disliked; the remedy for a bad statute is a new statute. (5) A statutory holding that has stood for a long time and been relied upon should not be overruled merely because the court is now persuaded that the original decision misread the text. (6) Constitutional provisions are different in kind: a clause such as the prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment should be read to track society's evolving standards of decency rather than frozen to the moment of ratification.

Which of these pull against each other, which only appear to, and what would have to give? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, say what each would cost me, and tell me which single concession would resolve the most.
