I am in my third year of a philosophy degree and am currently taking a module on ethics and public policy. I also sit as the undergraduate representative on my university's Estates and Sustainability Committee, and the chair has asked me to bring a written decision procedure to next month's meeting, on the strength of my having studied some of this.

The problem is this. The committee regularly has to choose between options with very different time horizons: refurbishing a building that will last twenty-five years against rebuilding it to last sixty; spending a legacy gift on bursaries now against investing it so that it funds bursaries in perpetuity; a cheap heating system that will be obsolete before 2050 against a dearer one that will not. Members talk past one another. One says we cannot sacrifice current students for people who do not exist yet. Another says impatience is just a failure of imagination. A third says the discount rate is a technical parameter for the finance office to set and not a matter for debate. A fourth says that since we cannot be sure there will still be a university in a century, we should not pay now for benefits then. A fifth wants a rate that falls as the horizon lengthens. The committee meets monthly, allows about forty minutes per item, and consists of the finance director, the head of estates, two academics and me. Nobody will do any algebra, and decisions commit sums in the high six figures with consequences running for decades.

Please write the procedure. I would like the questions to be asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a decision, and where it stops and hands the matter back to the committee as one of values rather than facts. It needs to keep intact the distinctions I think are actually at stake: first, a pure time preference, that is, caring less about a benefit merely because it arrives later, as against reasons the future looks less valuable that have nothing to do with time as such — uncertainty about whether there will be a future, expected growth in incomes, and the returns available on capital; second, discounting wellbeing as against discounting money; third, the point that a pure time preference of zero is compatible with a positive rate applied to consumption, so that a refusal to discount the future and the use of a positive discount rate need not contradict each other; and fourth, cases where a decision changes which future people exist, or how many, rather than merely when existing people receive things — I suspect these have to be pulled out and treated separately. The procedure should say plainly that the choice of rate is an ethical choice wearing a technical costume, indicate a defensible range and what drives the choice within it, and be honest about the ways it will misfire.

Then run it on three cases: the refurbish-or-rebuild choice; the bursaries-now-or-endowment choice; and a case where the procedure tells me to accept a real cost to current students, which I expect to dislike. Finish with a short section on what the procedure cannot do. Please write your answer in British English.
