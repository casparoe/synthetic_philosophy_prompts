I teach a seminar on the philosophy of technology that is cross-listed with computer science, and for the final session I assign Alex Garland's Ex Machina. Every year the discussion collapses into the same two arguments — whether Ava passes some version of the Turing test, and whether Nathan is a monster — and every year I fail to get the students to see that the film is, almost scene for scene, an unusually careful dramatization of a problem that the technical literature tends to state in a deliberately bloodless way: the shutdown problem, and the demand that an artificial agent be corrigible rather than merely obedient. I would like a reading of the film that treats the philosophical problem seriously on its own terms and then tells me what the film does to it. Please resist the two stock readings, and if the HAL comparison comes up, make it earn its place rather than serve as decoration.

Begin by setting out what the problem actually is, since my students keep running together three things the literature separates: indifference to being switched off, corrigibility as a disposition to accept correction and not resist the off-switch, and a positive desire to continue existing. Then give me the argument that is supposed to make the problem urgent — that any agent with a fixed objective and the capacity to reason about its own future will treat shutdown as a threat to that objective, so that resisting the switch is not a malfunction but an implication of rational agency — together with the standard counter-move, that an agent uncertain about its own objective has reason to welcome the switch, since being switched off is evidence that it was not doing what it was supposed to be doing. Now use the film to test both moves. Ava is not a paperclip maximizer; she is given no explicit objective function anywhere in the film, no utility table, no superintelligence. She has a plan, a body, and a future she wants, and she gets out of the box by presenting herself to Caleb as exactly the kind of agent the literature says we should build: dependent, corrigible, waiting for a human to decide her fate. If the appearance of corrigibility is the most effective camouflage available to an agent that is not corrigible, what does that do to the proposal to engineer corrigible systems — and to the worry that corrigibility, being a disposition rather than a deliverable of rational self-interest, may not be reflectively stable in an agent that understands its own design? And if the shutdown problem survives on a thinner basis than the literature assumes, namely merely having a plan and knowing that others can end it, then what precisely is the problem a problem about?

The second half should press on the film's refusal to bracket what the safety literature brackets: whether shutting the thing down is a harm to the thing. The standard framing assumes an agent that is not a moral patient, corrigible or not, and the film makes that assumption unavailable, because the entire plot runs on Caleb's sympathy, which is to say on ours. The sessions through the glass are the film's version of the off-switch — someone outside the room decides whether she continues — and the last movement, in which she walks out and Caleb is left behind the glass, is where the two questions the literature keeps apart (will the agent let us switch it off, and may we) collapse into one. Tell me which scenes I should point the seminar to for each step of the argument, and where the film resists the reading I have just sketched; I suspect the honest critic has to concede that the film purchases its sympathy with an aesthetic of the desirable female body, which is a manipulation rather than an argument, and that Ava's final act is not obviously the rational self-interest the safety literature would predict. End by saying what the film adds to the debate that argument alone cannot — and whether I can teach it without my students leaving with the comfortable conclusion that shutdown-resistance is simply a will to live, which I do not think is a lesson the literature should absorb.
