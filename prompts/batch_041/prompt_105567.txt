I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at the early stage of a long piece on the strange afterlife of Schopenhauer's philosophy of music. Before I draft anything, I want to run a rigorous translation exercise, and I'd like you to be my interlocutor. Please take the role of a philosopher of music who also has genuine conservatory training as a composer and theorist — someone who can move between metaphysics and the practical vocabulary of the workshop without fudging either side.

Here is the task. I know Schopenhauer's musical metaphysics reasonably well in outline: that music, unlike the other arts, does not copy the Ideas or the phenomenal world but is instead an immediate objectification of the Will itself; that he ranks the musical elements in an ascending order, with the bass as the lowest grade (the inorganic, the ground), the inner voices and harmony as the intermediate grades, and melody, carried in the upper voice, as the highest — the life of the human will, a connected sequence of striving and satisfaction; that on his view music does not express this or that particular joy or sorrow but the essence of joy and sorrow in the abstract, which is why the same music can be set to any text; and that its effect is a kind of temporary stilling of the will. I have that half. What I lack is the other half of the translation.

What I want from you is a systematic restatement of the whole position in the vocabulary I actually use when I talk about music with composers and theorists — tonic and dominant, harmonic function, voice leading, the bass as foundation, cadence and the deferral of resolution, motivic development, tension and release, the long line, the contrast between absolute music and program music. Treat that vocabulary as the target language and Schopenhauer as the source. I want the translation to be honest: do not quietly import the metaphysics into the musical terms and then present the result as a translation, and do not let the musical terms do work they cannot do.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Walk through it in three parts. First, what translates cleanly — which of Schopenhauer's claims about music are already, in effect, claims that a working theorist could make and defend on musical grounds? Second, what translates only under distortion — where must you stretch or reframe a musical concept to make it carry a Schopenhauerian claim, and what precisely gets bent in the process? Third, what has no counterpart at all in the target vocabulary, and here I want your candid verdict on why. When something fails to translate, is that a defect of the composer's vocabulary (it is simply silent about the thing Schopenhauer was after), or is it evidence that the original claim was never really a claim about music and merely borrowed music's language to dress up a piece of metaphysics? I would much rather you tell me the second thing, if you think it is true, than manufacture a tidy bridge.

Please start by brainstorming on the page — list the components of Schopenhauer's position and the resources available in the composer's vocabulary — before you commit to the translation, and think carefully through each step rather than producing a smooth summary. Where you are unsure whether a Schopenhauerian claim is being reported accurately, flag the uncertainty rather than smoothing it over.
