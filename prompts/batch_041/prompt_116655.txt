I retired from a marine biology lab a few years back and I've been trying to teach myself philosophy since — badly, probably, but it beats crossword puzzles. Forty years of modelling fish stocks taught me that the interesting part is never the equation, it's the assumptions nobody bothered to write down, and I think that's what I'm sniffing for here.

Here's the thing. My daughter married a Norwegian, so I've spent a lot of time over there. There's this unwritten code they call Janteloven, after a 1930s satirical novel by Aksel Sandemose — ten rules that all come down to: don't think you're anything special, don't think you're as good as us. In the book it's meant as a joke at the expense of small-town conformity, but everyone I've met over there treats it as live social furniture. Nobody mentions the promotion, nobody hangs the medal in the hallway. The Australians and New Zealanders have a name for the same reflex, tall poppy syndrome, which goes back to an old story about a king who walks through a garden and lops the heads off the tallest poppies — meaning, get rid of the most eminent citizens. And we do the reverse here: commencement speeches, the Forbes list, the whole self-made-man industry. We build the pedestal and then resent the guy standing on it.

What I can't work out is what the Jante norm actually assumes. Notice it's all about what you're allowed to think and say about yourself, not about whether you're in fact good at anything — you can be the best fisherman in the village so long as you don't carry on like it. So what view of the person, or of value, is buried in that? A few readings I've floated: it's just envy with a folk name, assuming nothing except that envy gets to have its way; or it assumes claims to merit are mostly self-serving noise and can't be trusted; or it's about equality of standing rather than of money — that letting people claim superiority is itself a form of domination, so the cure is refusing the whole game; or it assumes success is mostly luck, so taking credit is a kind of lie; or it's a genuine ethics of humility with pride as the vice. I'd like the American Dream side given the same treatment: what does celebrating the self-made presuppose about desert, agency, and what a life is for?

And the bit that really trips me up — my wife's cousin put it well: where she's from everybody's equal and nobody's supposed to say so, where I'm from everybody's free to rise and nobody's allowed to mention luck. Which makes me suspect the two cultures disagree less about equality than about whether admiration is a fixed pie. You can raise everyone's income; you can't raise everyone's standing. Is esteem a positional good, and does the Jante norm only hold together if it is? Is that the load-bearing assumption, or is there another one underneath it?

So: reconstruct the implicit theory for me. Tell me which of my readings the practice actually needs and which it merely happens to fit — I suspect it fits most of them, which may be the real answer — whether the pieces are consistent with each other, and what would have to give if you swapped the underlying view out. And tell me if I'm asking a badly formed question, because I've been out of the seminar game for forty years and I probably am.
