Bit of a wierd request, but I've just moved to Munich for work and my first payslip has a line on it I wasn't expecting: Kirchensteuer. I was baptised Catholic as a baby, haven't set foot in a church in about twenty years, and apparently that's enough for the tax office to count me as a member and skim 8% of my income tax — not 8% of my income, I checked, it's 8% of the tax itself, and it's 9% in most other German states, which is about the only part of this I've actually managed to understand. So now I'm wondering whether to do the whole Kirchenaustritt thing.

Anyway, that's not really what I'm asking. At lunch two people got into it about the system itself and I genuinely couldn't tell who was making sense, so I'd like you to referee it.

Dan is British and thinks the church tax is a scandal. He says it's a straightforward breach of the seperation of church and state: the state is using its tax machinery to raise money for religious bodies, it only does it for certain churches that have been handed some special public status, and so the state is effectively picking religious winners. He says you couldn't do this in America, and that the whole point of disestablishment there is that the state doesn't collect revenue for churches — churches get funded by whoever chooses to give, and the tax system stays out of it.

Anja is German and thinks Dan is being naive. She says nobody is forced to pay: you can leave, and then you pay nothing. The state isn't funding the church out of general taxation, it's just collecting a voluntary contribution from people who are registered as members, the way an employer might deduct union dues at source. And she says the American model isn't neutral either — exempting churches from tax is a huge implicit subsidy, and the state still has to decide which organisations count as churches in the first place. She'd rather have a system where the entanglement is out in the open and non-members aren't paying for it.

Dan came back with a few things. He said 'voluntary' is doing a lot of work there, because leaving isn't like cancelling a subscription — it's a formal act you have to go and perform in front of the authorities, there's a fee, and the state keeps a cut of whatever it collects, so the state is effectively making money off religion. He also said it's a tax and not a subscription because it's assessed on your income tax bill and it follows you onto your capital income as well. And he said the collection isn't even-handed: the big churches are set up to use it, but plenty of smaller congregations and faiths aren't, so it's not a level playing field.

Anja said that's rich coming from someone defending France and America. She said France is supposed to be the pure secular model, but the state there still owns and maintains the churches built before 1905, and in Alsace-Moselle the state still pays the salaries of the clergy and religious education sits on the school timetable — so the supposedly cleanest wall has a great hole in it. And she said England has an actual established church, with bishops who sit in the legislature by right, so by Dan's own standards the UK is worse than Germany.

Dan said Alsace-Moselle is just a leftover nobody defends, and that England proves his point: an established church gets automatic seats in parliament that no other religion gets, which is exactly the skew he's talking about. He also said the fact that the German state has to keep a register of which citizens are Catholic or Protestant is itself the problem — the state shouldn't be tracking people's religion at all.

Anja said the register is just the tax office's membership list and it's opt-out, and that the American arrangement isn't the neutral baseline he thinks it is — there are military chaplains, a national motto about trusting in God printed on the money, and religious exemptions carved into all sorts of laws. Her point was that there's no such thing as a purely neutral church-state arrangement; the question is which entanglement is least coercive.

So: who's right about what? Please go through it move by move rather than just declaring a winner — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say what each of them is missing. Then, if you can, say who's closer to right overall. I'd rather have the arguments adjudicated than be told what to do about my own Kirchenaustritt — I'll make that call myself.

One thing: please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just write it out the way you'd explain it to me in an email. And British English please (I've been here long enough that 'recognise' is starting to look right).
