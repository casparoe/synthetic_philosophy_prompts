I write historical novels, and I have been trying to state the rule I actually follow about what I may invent. I do not mean what is legally safe or what an estate would sue over; I mean what a writer owes. My verdicts, by feel:

- Two long-dead diplomats whose papers are lost: I invent a private meeting, an argument, a parting. Fine.
- A figure who kept a day-by-day diary: I have him, on a day the diary puts him at home with a fever, slipping out to join a conspiracy. Not fine.
- A film that turns a composer who was genuinely celebrated in his own lifetime into a talentless schemer, to make a point about genius. Fine; I would defend it.
- A politician who died ten years ago, with a widow and grown children still living: I invent an unrecorded cruelty to a servant. Not fine.
- The same invented cruelty, attached to someone dead two hundred years, with no descendants and no living partisans. Fine.
- A real abolitionist, alone at his desk, drafting the case for slavery and then burning it. Not fine, and I cannot see who alive is harmed by it.
- A real person given an invented motive for something he demonstrably did. Fine.

My provisional rule was: invent freely wherever the record is silent, and never contradict it. The composer case contradicts a documented fact about a real man's standing, and I still say fine; the cruelty to the servant contradicts nothing, and I still say no. So the rule is either false or means something other than what it says. And the case I keep going back and forth on: putting a real writer who died a few years ago into a comic novel as a minor character, doing something mildly absurd he plainly never did, with no libel and no living plaintiff in view.

Give me the principle, or a small set of candidates, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible; tell me which verdict I must surrender if none does; and say what the surviving principle predicts for a case I have not listed — make it one that would separate the candidates.
