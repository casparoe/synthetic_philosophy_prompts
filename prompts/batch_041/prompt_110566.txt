I did a philosophy of science MA a few years back and just started a job where I have to defend 30-to-50-year projections for a living — I'm at a small shop that builds long-run energy and materials scenarios for investors, so basically my whole week is people telling me our 2011 report was wrong because the thing it predicted didn't happen on schedule, and me saying it isn't wrong, it's early. I keep reaching for the stuff I was taught about falsification and research programmes and it never actually settles anything, because the live question is never whether the prediction came true, it's what the defender is allowed to do when it hasn't come true yet. So here's what I want: what are the genuinely open conceptual questions in this neighbourhood, and which ones should someone actually work on? Treat it as an agenda-setting thing — if you had a philosopher with three years and no quantitative skills (me, essentially), which question is most worth the time, and in what order? I care way less about the list than the reasoning: which questions are upstream of the others, which can be answered from the armchair versus which quietly need econometrics I don't have, and which ones look deep but wouldn't change anyone's behaviour if they got resolved.

To make the shape concrete, here's the kind of thing I keep chewing on. Malthus gets the right-but-early defence constantly, and I think there's more going on there than people admit — his model runs on positive checks (famine, disease, war pushing death rates up) and preventive ones (people marrying later, restraint pushing birth rates down), and the actual history was a giant preventive check, the demographic transition, which he half allowed for and definately didn't expect at that scale. So he named a real constraint and mis-located the mechanism, which seems like a different failure mode than merely being early. Meanwhile the Simon–Ehrlich wager gets used as the thing that kills Malthusianism, and I'm not convinced one ten-year window on five metals is carrying the epistemic weight people pile on it. So: what makes a rescue of a failed long-run prediction legitimate rather than ad hoc, and is there a usable test for it — does the fix add independent, checkable content, or does it just move the goalposts? Is right-but-early even a coherent category, or is it a polite name for unfalsifiability with better PR? Who gets epistemic credit when a prediction pans out after the predictor is dead, and is that retroactive credit a real form of confirmation or a hindsight illusion? And how do you design a bet or a benchmark when the payoff date outlives everyone involved? Rank those, tell me which are upstream, which are overrated, and what would move the ranking — like, if someone showed that most long-run resource predictions fail on mechanism rather than timing, would that reshuffle things? No reading list please, I won't track it down, just argue it out.
