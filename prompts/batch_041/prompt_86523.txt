I'm a backend engineer at a company that runs a persistent online world: one shared space, a few tens of thousands of daily users, an in-world economy, and a moderation team. Through a run of meetings I did not ask to attend, I've ended up on the small group drafting our first formal policy on which losses users can ask us to make good on. Legal's position is blunt and, I suspect, wrong: only losses with a dollar figure attached count, because those are the only ones they can defend. I need a principled line rather than a case-by-case vibe check, and I'd rather get it from you than from another week of meetings.

Here are the five cases in front of us.

1. The garden. A user we'll call R spent about fourteen months, roughly four hundred logged hours, tending a garden plot that other users can walk through. Another user used a duplication bug to spawn several hundred explosive objects on the plot and destroyed it. We can restore the plot from a blueprint our servers saved three weeks ago: same plants, same layout, same coordinates, takes about ten minutes. R says the restored version isn't her garden. The plants she grew from seed and the state she built up over months are gone, and a copy of an old snapshot isn't the thing.

2. The twenty-dollar skin. A bug on our side deleted a cosmetic item a user bought for twenty dollars four years ago. We can restore it or refund the twenty dollars. Legal regards this as the only clear-cut case on the list.

3. The arena. Two users were in an area that requires you to check a box saying you accept being killed by other players. One killed the other repeatedly over about ninety minutes, including standing over the body and using taunts. The loser says she agreed to being killed, not to what came after. Six other users were watching, and several filed reports.

4. The companion. A user's in-world animal companion — an NPC that follows you, that our systems give a persistent memory of everywhere it's been with you, and that we never respawn once it's gone — was killed by another user who had the ability to do it. There is no way to buy the companion and no blueprint of it. The owner had it for six years.

5. The crash. A duplication exploit inflated in-world currency until prices rose by a factor of fifty for about a week. Nothing was deleted, but a few thousand users who had been saving in-game currency found that what had been a few months of grinding was now worth about a day's. Nobody lost a dollar.

A colleague on the group, otherwise the most sensible person in the room, proposes a single test: a loss counts only if the user could convert it into money, or if it would still matter to them the day after we shut the service down. He thinks this is elegant. My instinct is that his test makes the twenty-dollar skin the most real thing on the list and the garden the least, which seems exactly backwards, but I can't articulate why in a way that survives contact with our lawyer. I also don't want to land on the opposite extreme, where anything that upsets anyone is a loss we owe for.

I have a nagging sense that this question has a name and that people have already worked out positions on it, and that I'm about to reinvent it badly. So tell me what kind of question I'm actually asking.

What I want:

- A definite verdict on each of the five cases: does it count as a loss we owe something for, and what should we actually do — restore, refund, ban, nothing, or something else. Take a position and defend it. I don't want both sides laid out with the choice left to me.
- The single rule that generates your five verdicts, stated in one or two sentences that a support agent could apply without a law degree.
- A direct ruling on my colleague's test: adopt it, reject it, or adopt a modified version — and if modified, state the strongest version you'd accept.
- The case you're least sure about, and what would move you.
- If you think I've mis-framed the whole thing, say so and give me the better framing.

Write in American English, roughly 600 to 900 words of prose. Don't send me a reading list or citations; I won't follow up on them. After the prose, give me a structured summary as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "position_summary": one sentence stating your overall verdict.
- "cases": an array with one object per case, each having "case_id" (one of "garden", "skin", "arena", "companion", "currency"), "counts" (true or false), "confidence" ("high", "medium", or "low"), "reason" (one sentence), and "action" (one sentence saying what we should do).
- "governing_rule": one or two sentences.
- "dollar_test": an object with "ruling" ("adopt", "reject", or "adopt_with_modification"), "reason" (one sentence), and "strongest_acceptable_version" (one sentence, or null if you reject it outright).
- "least_confident_case": the case_id.
- "what_would_change_my_mind": one sentence.
