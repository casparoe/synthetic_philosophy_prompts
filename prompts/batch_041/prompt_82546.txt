I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar in social epistemology on the Condorcet jury theorem and its use, and overuse, in epistemic democracy, and I want the centerpiece of the session to be a single case analysis rather than a survey. The case is the decision to launch the shuttle Challenger on the morning of 28 January 1986, and in particular the teleconference the previous evening, at which Thiokol's engineers recommended against a launch in the forecast cold, were pressed hard by NASA's program managers, and were then reversed by their own management after a closed caucus. Treat that teleconference, and the decision chain around it, as an aggregation of judgments, and run it against the theorem.

Begin with the mapping, done carefully: state the proposition the group was supposed to track; identify the jurors at each stage; and specify what individual competence and what independence would have to mean for the theorem's premises to hold at all. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. My own hypothesis, which I want tested rather than endorsed, is that it breaks along three axes at once. The voters were not independent, since each participant's judgment was partly constituted by the participant's estimate of what the others, and in particular what the customer, wanted to hear. Competence was heterogeneous in the sense that matters, with the relevant expertise concentrated among precisely those whose recommendations were overridden. And the two errors available, launching into a known and possibly catastrophic risk versus delaying a national program, were grossly unequal in cost, which a simple-majority apparatus is not built to register.

I also want the analysis to run in the other direction. Does the case tell against the theorem, or does it show only that the theorem was never a descriptive model of institutional decision-making? Is a nested, two-stage aggregation — engineers, then contractor management, then agency — better or worse than a single-stage jury of the same size, and why? Was the deliberation here truth-tracking at all, or did it function as an engine for manufacturing unanimity? And be explicit about the point where the theorem simply has nothing to say: the question before the group was not a proposition with a determinate answer but a decision about a risk threshold under uncertainty, and I suspect the binary correct-or-incorrect framing is doing far less work here than the asymmetry of the losses.

Assume the well-documented facts of the case are common knowledge; I do not need them rehearsed, and I do not need a literature review or citations. What I want is the disciplined mapping and an honest account of the theory's limits.
