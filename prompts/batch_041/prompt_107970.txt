I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding blogosphere for years — Sequences and all that — and I've absorbed just enough mechanism design from comment sections to be dangerous, which is why I want a second opinion instead of trusting my own vibes. A friend of mine, econ-adjacent, reads alot of pop game theory, sent me this paragraph he wrote up summarizing the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. We're currently arguing about whether our book club should switch to ranked-choice voting for picking next month's book (yes, this is the most important thing happening in any of our lives right now), and he dropped this as though it settles the question. Before I concede anything I want to know if its actually a fair summary. Here's what he wrote:

"Gibbard–Satterthwaite is basically the reason no voting system is fair. It says that once you've got three or more candidates, any rule where a voter can never do better by misrepresenting their preferences has to be a dictatorship — one voter whose top choice wins no matter what everyone else says. It's really the same theorem as Arrow's, just stated in terms of incentives instead of aggregation, since both of them are saying you can't turn individual rankings into a group ranking without violating something reasonable. The only way out is to stop assuming people vote sincerely and instead engineer the mechanism so that honesty is the dominant strategy, which is what auction theory does with Vickrey auctions. And it only applies if the rule is allowed to output any of the candidates, so you can dodge it by restricting what's allowed to win."

Go through that basically sentence by sentence and tell me what a social choice theorist would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong. I have a nagging suspicion that two different theorems are getting smushed together here, and that the "way out" he offers isn't actually a way out of the thing the theorem is about, but I can't articulate why — if I'm wrong about that, say so. Where a sentence is technically true only under hypotheses he's left out, spell out the fine print; I'd much rather have the hedged version than a clean one that collapses the moment someone leans on it. And separately, I'm curious what happens to the result if you start relaxing assumptions one at a time — the usual moves people reach for when they don't like an impossibility theorem — so I can see which of his claims survive that and which just evaporate.
