I have no academic background in philosophy and have never studied the subject formally, but there is something I keep running into in my reading and in ordinary life, and I would like to know whether it has a name — and, if it does not, what it ought to be called, and how precisely it should be distinguished from the things it resembles.

The case is this. A person is plainly feeling pain. They can say where it is, whether it is sharp or dull, burning or aching, and if you ask them they will tell you without hesitation that yes, it hurts, and hurts a good deal. And yet the pain does not appear to trouble them. They do not wince or pull away, they do not ask for anything to be done about it, and they may even smile or laugh while describing it. The sensation is present and is reported accurately; the reaction one expects it to produce is simply absent.

I first came across something like this in an account of people who had suffered certain injuries to the brain, and I assumed at the time that it must be a rare and strange condition. But since then I have kept finding fainter versions of it closer to home: the colleague who tells you his headache is bad today in a tone that makes clear it is nothing to him, or a friend who can discuss a pain that would have me climbing the walls as though she were reading out the weather. Each time I try to say what is distinctive about these cases I find myself reaching for comparisons that are not quite right, and I would like the vocabulary to get it right.

What they are not, I think, is numbness, where the sensation itself is missing. They are not the same as having taken something for the pain, where again the sensation is dulled or gone. They are not the same as gritting one's teeth and carrying on, because the person who endures pain still minds it — he would rather not have it, he is simply refusing to be governed by it. And I do not think they are the same as enjoying pain, because nothing about these people suggests pleasure; it is rather that the pain seems to have been stripped of whatever it is that normally makes pain matter.

So what I want from you is not just a label. I want to know how the thing should be carved. Is such a person still feeling pain at all, or feeling something that merely resembles it? Is one element missing here, or several? How does the phenomenon relate to each of the near neighbours I listed, and where exactly are the boundaries — what would a borderline case look like, and on which side of the line would it fall? And what does all this imply for the ordinary assumption that pain is bad simply in virtue of how it feels? It seems to me that if the feeling and the mattering can come apart in this way, then much of what people say about pain being bad in itself needs to be stated more carefully, and I would like to know what the most careful version of that claim is.

Please write your answer as continuous prose. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — just paragraphs, in the manner of a long email to someone who asked a genuine question.
