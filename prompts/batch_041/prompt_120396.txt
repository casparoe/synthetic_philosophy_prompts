I teach the graduate decision theory seminar and I am writing the take-home portion of the final. I need one essay question, worth 20 marks and answerable in roughly 90 minutes, on money pumps and diachronic rationality. The question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar argument that cyclic preferences expose an agent to exploitation; it has to make students work on a case they have not seen. It must also be answerable without citing any literature — no reading list, no names, no dates — and the marking scheme must not reward name-dropping, only arguments.

Build the question around the following case, which I have invented for the exam and which you should reproduce in full.

Nadia's preferences over three bundles are strictly cyclic at every moment: she ranks A above B, B above C, and C above A. A trader makes her three offers on three consecutive days, each to be accepted or declined on the spot and never repeated: on Monday, B in exchange for the C she holds plus one pound; on Tuesday, A in exchange for her B plus one pound; on Wednesday, C in exchange for her A plus one pound. Nadia begins the week holding C, she cannot bind her future self in advance, and the three trades cannot be had as a single package.

The question should ask students, first, whether and to what extent the trader can extract money from Nadia in each of three variants — (i) she is naive and does not foresee her later choices; (ii) she foresees them perfectly but decides each offer by comparing the two bundles then on the table; (iii) she foresees them perfectly and decides each offer by the final holding she would reach if she accepted it, given what she would go on to accept and decline — and, second, what the three variants jointly show about the relation between intransitivity, exploitability, and diachronic rationality. In particular it should force a position on whether the pump shows that cyclic preferences are irrational, or only that they are irrational together with some further premise about commitment, foresight, or how a choice is to be evaluated, and on whether variant (iii) is still a case of cyclic preference at all.

Deliverables: (1) the exam question, with the case stated and its parts lettered; (2) a model answer of about 700 words that argues for a definite line on the crux rather than surveying positions, and that states and answers the strongest objection to that line; (3) a marking scheme of five must-hit points, with the marks each carries (total 20) and a one-line note on how candidates typically lose that point; (4) a short statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer.
