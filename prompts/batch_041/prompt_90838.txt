I'm setting the take-home section of a final for a third-year logic-and-metaphysics module -- mostly philosophy students who've done a bit of formal logic but who are not maths specialists. I want one essay question on dialetheism and paraconsistent logic that a student can't pass just by writing out "the Liar is both true and false, and paraconsistent logic blocks explosion." It has to force them to seperate two things and then say whether they can be held apart at all.

Here's the framing I want the question built around. On the paper I'll introduce a stipulative pair of terms -- the students wont have seen these before, so define them inside the question itself:

- "thin dialetheism": the view that some contradictions are true, but only in a bounded class of cases (the semantic and set-theoretic paradoxes), and that outside those cases classical reasoning is perfectly fine.
- "thick dialetheism": the view that true contradictions tell us something deep about truth, negation and the structure of reality, so classical logic is not merely locally inapplicable but systematically wrong.

Then the question should make them do three things. (a) Give the strongest reason for being a *thin* dialetheist rather than a thick one -- I'm after the "classical reasoning still works wherever it matters, so we can quarantine the trouble" idea, but they must supply the reason, not the label. (b) Explain the most serious problem for thin dialetheism; what I have in mind is the worry that the very reasoning you'd use to quarantine the contradictions is classical reasoning you've just called into question at the limit, but don't feed them that phrase -- make them find it. (c) Take a stand on whether paraconsistency *without* dialetheism (using a non-explosive logic while still denying that any contradiction is actually true) is a stable position or just a way-station that collapses into one of the two.

Please write the question as it would appear on the paper, then a model answer of about 700 words, then a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a first-class answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks -- e.g. conflating paraconsistency with dialetheism, thinking explosion is a theorem of *logic* rather than of a particular logic, or reading "both true and false" as "neither true nor false". Give a one-line note per must-hit point on how candidates typically trip up on it.

And keep it all in British English -- I'll mark them down for "analyze"! Thanks.
