I am designing a two-hour session on anthropic reasoning for a deliberately mixed room — graduate students and postdocs in physics, statistics, and philosophy — almost none of whom has given any thought to self-locating belief. My subject is the fork between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption: SSA is the premise that makes the doomsday argument run, SIA is the premise that licenses the presumptuous philosopher to settle cosmology from an armchair, and each is standardly offered as the antidote to the disease the other causes. What I want from you is a teaching plan, not an exposition, and not a reading list; assume the audience has no texts in front of them and no internet.

Give me the order in which the components must come and the reason each depends on the one before it. Decide, in particular, where the reference-class problem enters: SSA cannot be stated without one, and the doomsday argument's entire bite comes from the choice, so the placement matters, and I want it defended against the alternatives.

For the carrying example, here is what I have drafted, and I want you to attack it before you build on it. A fair coin is tossed; on heads one person is created, on tails a thousand are; all of them wake with identical memories in indistinguishable rooms. I then tell the audience: "You are equally likely to be any of the people who were created, so you should now think it is overwhelmingly likely that the coin landed tails." Work out what SSA and what SIA actually deliver on this setup. I suspect my sentence presupposes the very assumption it is meant to illustrate; if so, identify it, and tell me whether the example can be repaired into one that carries the whole session or must be replaced. If it must be replaced, supply the example that can, and say what it needs and what it must lack — for instance, no indexical evidence that would let the audience sample its way to an answer.

Then the misconception map. I expect at least these, and I want them checked, corrected, and extended: that self-locating credence is not credence at all and the topic is a confusion; that SIA amounts to the claim that you are special, or that the universe exists for your sake; that the doomsday argument is a plain probabilistic fallacy; and that SSA and SIA exhaust the options. For each, say where in the sequence it will form and exactly where I should head it off.

Sleeping Beauty is the obvious set piece, but this audience has half-heard it and will dig in. Tell me whether to use it as the carrying example, hold it back as a late test case, or omit it, and how to handle my worry that the halfer/thirder labels are not quite the same dispute as SSA versus SIA.

Close with the single question I should put to the room that would show whether they hold the distinction rather than the vocabulary, and with the things I should openly refuse to resolve at this level — along with wording that refuses them without leaving the room feeling cheated.

Please write your answer in American English.
