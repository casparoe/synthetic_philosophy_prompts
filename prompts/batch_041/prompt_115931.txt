I've been trying to reverse-engineer the theory of work and leisure that our institutions actually run on, as opposed to the one they profess, and the pieces don't fit together. American overtime law requires time and a half for hours past forty for most hourly workers while exempting salaried professional, administrative, and managerial employees from the premium entirely, as though the same hour of effort were a burden in one case and not in the other; on-call time, meanwhile, is generally unpaid as long as you can use the time for yourself and is paid only once you are actually called in, which suggests that what gets compensated is the expenditure of effort rather than the surrender of control over your own hours. France's legal 35-hour week was justified in part as work-sharing, a way to spread a scarce good around, which only makes sense if paid work is something people have a claim to rather than something they must be bribed into. Japan compensates death from overwork (karoshi) as a work injury, treating a long enough stretch of overtime as a real and sometimes fatal harm. And against all of this, unemployment insurance conditions benefits on active search and, in many states, on accepting offers that grow less attractive the longer you have been out of work; retirement pensions are paid on the basis of age and contribution history, with no requirement that you demonstrate incapacity or keep looking for work; and disability benefits sit between them, conditioned on proving that you cannot work rather than that you cannot find work.

What view of work and leisure does this pattern presuppose? Is there a single implicit theory that reconciles all of it — work as a duty of reciprocity, leisure as something earned across a lifetime of contribution, and a sharp distinction between voluntary and involuntary idleness — or does the pattern require at least two incompatible theories, one treating work as a disutility that must be compensated and another treating it as a good that justice requires distributing? Which rival views does the pattern quietly rule out: that leisure is an unconditional right, that work is nothing but a cost, that desert tracks effort rather than contribution, that idleness is a matter of indifference to justice? Is this finally just the Protestant work ethic with a pension attached, or is the reciprocity element doing genuinely different work? Distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit comfortably with, tell me where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined and several theories would license it equally, and say what would have to change in these institutions if we replaced whatever implicit view they share with a single consistent alternative. Please write your answer in American English.
