I have been trying to work out what it takes for a collection of things to have a mind, and I have a set of verdicts that I cannot get to fit together. A single ant, I am confident, has no mind. The colony it belongs to — fifty thousand of them, foraging, building, and rerouting when I block a path — I want to say does have one, small and dim, though I would not say it feels like anything to be it. A single neuron has no mind; my brain does. But a city of people, which moves traffic, stocks food and responds to a flood, I do not think has a mind at all, and neither does a company with a board, minutes and a memory of its past mistakes, even though a company appears to do more of the things minds do than an anthill does. A slime mould that finds the shortest route through a maze has no mind, and neither does a flock of starlings turning as one.

Is there a principle that captures all of these, or close to it? I suspect it is something about the parts being mindless while the whole has a single point of view, but that seems to let the city back in. Please state the principle, or a small set of rival candidates, giving the case that motivates each clause; say what it predicts for two cases I have not settled — a colony in which every ant carries a tiny radio and merely relays instructions from a central program, and a colony whose ants are replaced one by one until none of the originals remain; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I would have to abandon if I wanted the simpler principle instead.
