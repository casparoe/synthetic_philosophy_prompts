I am a teaching assistant grading the final exam in an upper-level history of philosophy course. The exam question and one student's answer appear below. Please grade the answer and supply the specific comments that justify the grade.

Exam question: Hobbes treated geometry as the model of certain knowledge and claimed to have made original discoveries in it. Explain his account of why geometry is demonstrable; say how he thought that model applied to civil philosophy; and assess whether his actual mathematical work and his quarrels with contemporary mathematicians strengthen or weaken the claim that his appeal to geometry was primarily a matter of method rather than rhetoric.

Student's answer:

Hobbes believed that geometry was the one science whose results could be known with certainty, and he thought that philosophy should imitate its method. The reason geometry is demonstrable is that its objects are eternal and independent of the mind: the properties of a triangle do not depend on anyone's opinion, so a valid demonstration cannot be overturned by disagreement. He then transferred this model to civil philosophy. Because the commonwealth is something human beings construct, Hobbes held that we can know its causes in the way a craftsman knows what he has built, and he therefore presented his political conclusions as formal deductions from self-evident axioms about human nature, in the manner of Euclid's Elements. Once the axioms were granted, he thought the rest followed with the same necessity as a geometrical theorem.

Hobbes did not merely praise mathematics from a distance. He believed he had solved problems that had defeated the ancients, including the squaring of the circle, and he defended his results against the mathematician John Wallis, who attacked them in print. The quarrel was bitter and went on for years. Because Hobbes kept returning to it, some interpreters have suspected that his mathematical enthusiasm was less a matter of method than of borrowing the prestige of mathematics for his politics. That suspicion is too cynical. Hobbes's engagement with geometry was serious and sustained, and he continued to defend his mathematical claims for the rest of his life. The disputes therefore show the depth of his commitment rather than a merely rhetorical use of mathematics.
