I've agreed to review, for a general-interest magazine, a colleague's trade book on the ethics of climate change, and I have reached a chapter that I cannot decide how to handle in print. The book has been well received, which is part of my worry. His argument, compressed: philosophers who have thought hardest about individual contributions to collective harms have converged on the conclusion that individual action is morally idle. Climate change is the sum of billions of tiny emissions; no single person's emissions make any perceptible difference to any identifiable person; so no individual act of emitting is wrong, and the exhortation to shrink one's carbon footprint is at best symbolic. He calls this the inefficacy objection and writes as though it were settled. Three supporting moves in particular need scrutiny. First, he says the appeal to expected value cannot rescue individual duties, because the climate case involves no threshold — damages accumulate smoothly, so one extra tonne has a marginal effect of exactly zero and the probability that my act tips anything is nil. Second, he presents as the upshot of the long-standing discussion of imperceptible contributions — the drop in the ocean, the single voter — a principle he calls the no-drop rule: no act whose individual effect is imperceptible can be wrong. Third, he concludes that individual morality has nothing to say about emissions, and that only collective and political action can be a proper object of moral concern.

I suspect the chapter is wrong somewhere, and wrong interestingly rather than stupidly, but I cannot yet say where, and I would rather not put into print a criticism that a competent consequentialist would treat as a soft target. So work through the summary claim by claim: tell me what a philosopher who works on collective harm would accept, what is defensible but contestable, and what is a misrepresentation — I am especially interested in whether "imperceptible" is doing epistemic work in one place and metaphysical work in another, and in whether the expected-value response is actually defeated or merely waved away. Then tell me what survives the corrections. If I grant everything in his summary that deserves to be granted, does his practical conclusion — that individual morality is silent here and that only collective action counts — still follow, or does the repaired version of the argument point somewhere else? I do not need citations or a reading list; I know the terrain well enough, and what I want from you is the audit itself, in prose I can adapt.
