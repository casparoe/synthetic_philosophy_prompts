I have been going back and forth with two friends about conspiracy theories, and I have found that I hold quite firm verdicts on particular cases while having no idea what general principle, if any, unifies them. What I want from you is not a survey of what various authors have said, nor a reading list, but the principle itself — or a small set of competing candidates — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh case on my own. Tell me what each principle implies about cases I have not considered, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) Senior officials in an administration coordinated to cover up a break-in and then lied about their involvement: believing this was not merely reasonable but correct, and I would not call the believer a "conspiracy theorist" at all. (2) A coalition of tobacco companies coordinated for decades to manufacture doubt about the link between smoking and cancer: likewise reasonable, and every bit a real conspiracy, even though it was large and lasted a long time. (3) The moon landings were staged: unreasonable. (4) A recent national election was stolen by rigged voting machines: unreasonable. (5) A large bank knowingly laundered money for a cartel, believed on the strength of a leaked internal memo together with the bank's implausible denials: reasonable. (6) A consortium of governments and airlines is concealing the true cause of a plane crash, where my friend treats the fact that no insider has ever come forward as proof of how airtight the cover-up is: unreasonable. (7) The same friend counters that the tobacco companies' public position was equally airtight — every new study was dismissed as inconclusive and the demand for further research never ended — and yet I still say it was reasonable to believe them guilty. That is the pair I cannot reconcile: I want to say the self-sealing quality of (6) is what damns it, but (7) appears to have the very same quality, so either my verdict on (6) or my verdict on (7) has to give, and I cannot see which.

One friend has offered a tentative principle and I would like it tested and repaired: that a conspiracy theory is unwarranted just when it explains the absence of evidence as itself part of the conspiracy, so that nothing could count against it. Another friend put it differently — that a conspiracy theory is what you get when stray anomalies are explained by hidden agency rather than by noise, error, or ordinary self-interest. I suspect both are too crude: the first seems to make warrant turn on the content of the claim rather than on how the believer came to hold it, and I am not sure a purely procedural account, which asks only whether the belief was formed by a reliable-enough method, can explain why (1) and (2) feel like knowledge while (4) and (6) feel like a failure of reasoning, given that the believers in all four may be equally confident and equally unable to produce the decisive document. Please give me a principle or a few candidates, each stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on at least two further cases I have not listed, and an explicit statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would also like to know whether the principle is meant to govern the warrant of the belief or the aptness of the label "conspiracy theory," since I suspect those two come apart.
