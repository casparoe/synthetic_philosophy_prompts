I am a novelist at work on a book whose most tiresome character, a retired cosmologist who has reinvented himself as a wellness guru, has built a small following on a single borrowed idea, and I need you to write the piece that made his name. Please draft, in his voice, the essay he is famous for: a deadpan Sunday-supplement meditation on time, of roughly 650 words, that he has titled "There Is No Deadline." It should read as sincere, unruffled, and faintly sacerdotal, with no winking and no signal that the author knows he is being laughed at; the reader must be able to see the joke without being told where it is.

The engine of the piece is a genuine result, and I want you to get it exactly right, because the whole pleasure of the thing depends on specialists recognising that we understand what is being sent up rather than flinching at a howler. The guru's premise is Kurt Gödel's 1949 rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations: a cosmos filled with swirling dust, turning about a common axis, which admits closed timelike curves, worldlines that return to their own past, and therefore possesses no global, objective division of events into earlier and later. From this he draws his counsel: if simultaneity is a matter of where the observer stands rather than of how the world is, then there is no cosmic now, and so no privileged instant at which anything is due; the Tuesday on which your taxes fall due is, in his phrase, merely a coordinate choice. Have him press the argument the way Gödel himself pressed it, toward the question of whether the passage of time is objective or merely ideal, and let him lean on the comparison Gödel drew with Kant, and on the suggestion that time might be ideal in something like the way space is. He must also, somewhere, concede honestly that this is one solution among many and need not describe our own universe, and then decline to let the concession trouble him.

I want the essay to catch itself in its own loop. His closing advice should turn out to be his opening advice, verbatim or near enough, and he should be serenely unaware that he has, in effect, quoted himself from a column he has not yet written, a closed timelike curve running through the prose. Everything he claims must be something a consistent Gödelian could actually claim, so that he never slips into a contradiction he would notice; the only inconsistency is the one built into the loop, and that one is invisible to him.

The target is not the mathematics but the popular reflex that turns a technical result about a possible spacetime into sweeping comfort about deadlines, regret, and the irreality of the present moment. Close the piece with a brief editor's note, in the voice of an invented rival physicist, who grants the mathematics in full and denies the moral, and whose objection should itself be technically correct. No citations, no references, no reading list; I want the voice and the reasoning to carry it.
