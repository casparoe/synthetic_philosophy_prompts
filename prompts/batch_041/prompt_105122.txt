I keep getting dragged into fights about whether lying is actually worse than merely misleading, and everyone always reaches for one case and thinks they've settled the whole thing. Before I read another blog take on it, I want the spec. What does *any* adequate theory of the moral difference (or non-difference) between lying and misleading have to deliver?

Give me the requirements list, each one with the case or consideration that motivates it. Stuff like: the cases it has to get right (the literally-true answer that exploits an implicature; the "no study has shown X causes cancer" answer where the studies just were never run; the true answer that banks on a false belief the hearer already walked in with; the case where the misleading move feels *worse* than a flat lie would've been; the case where the two feel morally identical; the self-defense / Nazi-at-the-door case where nobody thinks either is wrong). The distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse (speaker's belief vs. what the hearer actually comes to believe vs. what a reasonable hearer would infer; the act vs. the outcome; what's asserted vs. what's merely implicated). And any constraints from psychology it shouldn't contradict — like how people actually assign blame, or the fact that hearers can't reliably tell the two apart after the fact.

Then the part I actually care about, because it's the idea that got me going. A friend of mine has this half-baked view that the real difference isn't between lying and misleading at all — it's between manipulating the *inputs* to someone's reasoning (feeding them a false premise: the lie) and manipulating the *process* (letting them run perfectly valid inference on true premises to a false conclusion: the misleading). And he claims the second is often worse, because a lie just hands you bad data you could in principle catch and correct, while the misleading move corrupts the machinery itself and is way harder to notice. I don't know if that survives contact with the cases but I want it taken seriously. So: does that input/process framing actually earn its keep as a requirement on the theory, or is it just a redescription of the old distinction wearing a lab coat? Be honest if it's the latter.

Also: which single requirement on your list does the most work — i.e. knocks out the most of the theories people actually hold? And flag any two requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied; that's the bit I most want to see. Yeah I know "lying" probably needs tabbooing first, but assume we've done that and landed on something usable. Skip the scorecard of every position, I'll do that myself, and don't bother with a reading list — I'm not going to read the literature, I want the criteria. The purpose here is just a spec good enough that I can adjudicate real arguments instead of vibing off one clever case.
