OK so here's a thing that's bugged me for a while. In federal perjury law, the rule is basically that a witness can only be nailed if what he actually said was false. If the answer is literally true but he's clearly trying to mislead the questioner -- giving a non-answer, talking about the company's account when he was asked about his own, that kind of thing -- it's not perjury. That's the holding in Bronston v. United States, 1973, unanimous Supreme Court, and it's still the standard. The reasoning is that it's the questioner's job to ask better follow-ups.

But then you look at consumer protection and securities law and the whole attitude flips. The FTC will come after an ad that's literally true but likely to mislead, and the SEC treats a literally true statement that leaves out a material fact as fraud. Nobody over there says "well, technically the words were accurate, so we're done here." Same country, same underlying idea that you shouldn't be able to deceive people under oath or in a prospectus, but a totally different answer to wether misleading-without-saying-anything-false is the thing we actually care about.

So what does the perjury rule presuppose about the moral difference between lying and misleading? My hunch is it has to rest on some view that the wrong of lying lives in the falsity of what you assert, not in the deception you intend or the false impression you create -- but spell that out properly and tell me whether that's really what the rule commits to, or whether there's some other story that explains it just as well. Does it need a sharp line between asserting and merely implying, and does it need that line to be morally load-bearing, or is it really just administrative convenience (intent is hard to prove, juries get confused)? And is it consistent with the securities/FTC side, or are we just running two different theories of deception depending on which agency you're standing in front of? If the perjury rule does presuppose a "misleading isn't lying" view, what would have to change about how depositions and cross-examination work if we dropped it -- or is the literal-truth rule fine on either theory?
