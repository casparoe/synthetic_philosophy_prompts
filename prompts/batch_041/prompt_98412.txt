I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about a puzzle that keeps eating my weekends. Here's the case that started it. Take a job offer: $90k a year, but an hour of commuting each way, every day. Now imagine the same job with $100 less salary but one minute less commuting per day -- you'd take that trade every time, right? Now do it again, and again, all the way down the chain. Eventually you land on a job that pays nothing at all and has a zero-minute commute, i.e. unemployment on the couch, and if "better than" is transitive you're stuck saying the couch beats the $90k job. Each step looks like an improvement, the two endpoints look like the couch is clearly worse, and there's no particular $100 where the improvements flip into worsenings -- and yet it seems you can't keep all three of those. I keep finding people who defend one of the three and hand-wave the other two, and I've been going back and forth on this for awhile now, so before I read another theory I want the spec first.

So: write me the desiderata any adequate theory of "better than" has to meet. Not a survey -- the requirements list. Which cases must it get right (the adjacent steps in a spectrum like that one, the endpoints, cases where two options are neither better nor worse nor equal but also not merely incomparable, cases where a tiny gain gets pitted against something people treat as sacred); what formal properties does the relation need if we're going to use it both for choosing and for adding things up (asymmetry, transitivity, totality or not, and what changes if it turns out to be only a partial order); which distinctions can't be collapsed (better vs. permissible-to-choose, incomparable vs. indifferent, a genuine gap in the ordering vs. a tie); and which results from decision theory or from the sorites literature it can't contradict. For each requirement, give the consideration behind it -- the case or intuition that makes it a real requirement and not just a preference of one camp.

And then tell me the part I actually want: which subset of these can't all hold at once, and which single requirement does the most to kill off the most popular positions. I have a hunch its transitivity itself, but tell me if I've got that backwards, and say which one on my list is really just a prejudice of some camp dressed up as a requirement. If a few of the standard families -- the ones that bite the bullet on the endpoint, the ones that give up transitivity, the ones that stick a sharp cutoff somewhere in the chain -- can be scored against the list in a sentence or two each, great, but keep the weight on the list itself. No citations needed, I can't look anything up from where I'm writing this anyway.
