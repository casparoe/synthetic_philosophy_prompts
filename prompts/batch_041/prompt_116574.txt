I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, and I am trying to state the principle behind my own verdicts about what counts as a language game. I take the familiar builder case—two people passing a block and calling for it—to be a language game. A courtroom oath is one. A child's peekaboo exchange with a parent is one, though it has no words. A proof assistant's type-checking dialogue with a user is one. A chess engine's internal search is not one, even though it manipulates symbols and follows rules. A market transaction between strangers who barely speak is one. A poet reciting alone is not one, though it is language use. A chatbot generating a fluent answer is not one unless something in the exchange is being done and mutually recognized as done. But I also think a formal language in a logic textbook is a language game only when it is being taught or used, not as an abstract object. My tentative principle is that a language game is a rule-governed, socially recognized practice of doing something with signs, where the signs and the moves are mutually intelligible to participants. That handles the builders and the oath, but it seems to make the chess engine's search a language game if the engine's modules communicate with each other, and it seems to exclude the peekaboo exchange unless I stretch the notion of signs to include gestures and cries. I want the principle that best captures the verdicts, not a restatement of them. Please consider whether family resemblance, prototype theory, or a homeostatic property cluster account—where the category is held together by an underlying causal mechanism rather than a shared feature list—fits better, and state the principle precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Then tell me what it predicts for two cases I have not listed: a bilingual speaker's code-switching in a single sentence, and a formal proof checked by a machine with no human participant. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to give up, and why.
