ok, long story but I think it's relevant: I'm a first-year cog sci PhD student, I came in from a math background, and my roommate is a physics guy who's building this analog optical setup that he swears is going to solve the halting problem in finite time. He's told me maybe fifteen times now that when it works, the Church-Turing thesis gets experimentally refuted, that it was never anything more than an empirical guess that happened to hold so far, and that a couple of textbooks are going to have to be rewritten. I laughed at first, but he's annoyingly good at this stuff, and now I'm worried, because I have a seminar paper due on computationalism and I don't want to build an argument on top of a thesis that some guy in my living room could falsify next spring.

Here's the version of things I've been carrying around, so tell me where it's wrong. The thesis says that every function computable by any finite mechanical procedure is computable by a Turing machine. Turing proved it in 1936 by showing that his machines can do anything a human "computer" can do with pencil and paper, so it's really a theorem, and it's only called a thesis because nobody at the time had a formal definition of "mechanical procedure" to prove it against. Also someone told me the real evidence for it is that all the different formalisms people came up with in the thirties -- recursive functions, lambda calculus, Turing machines -- turned out to compute exactly the same things, which honestly sounds circular to me, like saying five people guessed the same number so it must be right. So: if my roomate's machine works, doesn't that just falsify the thesis, full stop? And even if it does, does that actually threaten the claim that the brain is a Turing machine, or have I mixed up two seperate claims that happen to share a name? I keep getting told in seminar that I'm "conflating" things and I genuinely cannot tell which things.
