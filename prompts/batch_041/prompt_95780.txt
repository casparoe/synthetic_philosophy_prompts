I teach an upper-level seminar on paradox and philosophy of logic -- 14 students, 80 minutes -- and the unit on dialetheism always falls flat. They nod along at the liar paradox and then can't see why anyone would take "some contradictions are true" seriously instead of writing it off as a trick sentence. I want a game where they run into the force of it themselves.

My rough idea, take it or rebuild it: teams of 2-3 act as clerks in an office whose rulebook says every OFFICIAL record card is true. One of the cards is official and says of itself that it isn't true, so straight off the rulebook forces a contradiction. Each team also gets a sheet of inference licenses -- conjunction intro/elim, disjunction intro, disjunctive syllogism, ex falso (explosion), double negation, modus ponens -- and a list of target propositions worth points, which they try to derive from the cards. The catch: anything derived using explosion gets stamped VACUOUS and scores zero. So round one they discover they can technically reach every target, and that it's worthless. Round two I pull explosion off the license sheet and see what they can still get -- I want them to find that a lot survives, and that they can keep holding the self-referential card as both true and not true without the whole thing collapsing. Round three is the bit I care about most: I quietly give back disjunctive syllogism but keep explosion banned, and see if anyone notices they can still explode (disjunction intro plus DS takes you from a contradiction to anything). I want the "oh no" moment where they realize you cant just delete one rule, you have to hunt down where it's hiding.

Design it for me: exact materials, card texts (a handful of filler facts plus the ones that matter), the license sheet, the scoring scheme with no real money, round-by-round timing, and roles if you think roles help. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual pieces -- bivalence vs. completeness, gluts vs. gaps, why explosion is what makes a single contradiction lethal, and what the paraconsistent move of restricting a rule is actually buying. Be honest about what the game can't reproduce (I assume the semantic machinery is beyond it, and it obviously can't settle whether dialetheism is true), and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- I'm betting at least one team refuses to stamp the self-referential card and one team scores zero in round one and gets demoralized.
