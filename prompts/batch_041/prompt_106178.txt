I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been trying to formulate a view I would call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and I would like to test it against Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. As I understand the view, suffering is lexically prior to pleasure up to a threshold: any suffering above that line cannot be compensated by any amount of happiness, while below the line ordinary trade-offs are permitted. Read the story through that lens. Does Omelas dramatize the lexical priority of suffering, or does it show something stronger—that the city's happiness is not merely outweighed but morally corrupted by the child's misery, so that pleasure and pain are not commensurable in the way my view assumes? I am especially interested in where the story resists the view: the narrator's refusal to condemn the citizens who stay, the suggestion that the child may not fully understand its own suffering, and the ending, where the ones who walk away seem to act without a principle. Tell me which scenes carry the philosophical weight, where my view illuminates the story and where the story exposes a limit in my view, and what the story adds to the debate that a straight argument about lexical priority cannot.
