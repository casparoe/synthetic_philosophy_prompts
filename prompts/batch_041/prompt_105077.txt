I am leading a two-hour graduate reading group, half economists and half philosophers, on money pumps and diachronic rationality. Do not give me the arguments; design the session. The group already knows the synchronic Dutch-book argument, assumes a money pump is merely its temporal twin, and takes diachronic rationality to be consistency of preference across time. I want to introduce a distinction they will not have seen: between a pump that exploits the preferences the agent has at each moment and a pump that exploits her inability to bind her future self—between preference-driven and commitment-driven pumps. Build the explanation around one extended example that makes this distinction unavoidable, perhaps an agent who pays a fee to avoid a future offer and is then offered a fee to undo the avoidance, and specify what the example must contain and what it must leave out; say also why the standard three-good cycle is or is not the carrying case. What must come first, and in what order, so that the thought that diachronic rationality concerns an agent’s relation to her own future choices arrives as a discovery rather than a slogan? Which misconceptions should I expect—that the pump proves intransitive preferences irrational, that it presupposes naïveté, that it is just a Dutch book, that sophistication dissolves it—and exactly where in the sequence should each be headed off? What should I explicitly refuse to cover at this level? End with one question I can ask that would reveal whether they grasp the difference between exploitability and inconsistency.
