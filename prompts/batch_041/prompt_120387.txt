I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I also sit on my residential college's peer accountability panel — three students and a faculty advisor who signs off on our determinations. We hear about fifteen cases a term, and for each one we get forty-five minutes, a written statement from the responding student, and a one-page determination due to the advisor by the end of the week; she returns them for revision more often than she overrules them. Our current rubric asks whether the student violated the rule, which is a question we can answer, and then how severe the sanction should be, which is a question we improvise. The improvisation is what bothers me, because the real disagreement on the panel is never about the facts. It is about whether the student in front of us is someone who owes the community an answer, or someone whose conduct is a problem to be managed — and we slide between the two in the middle of a case, sometimes in the middle of a sentence.

Our seminar has just finished Strawson on the reactive attitudes and moral responsibility, and I think he is describing precisely this slide: the difference between the participant stance and the objective attitude, and the difference between conditions that exempt a person from our demands altogether and conditions that merely excuse one act. I would like to turn that into something the panel can actually run. Please write me a decision procedure.

It should take the form of questions asked in order, and for each question it should say what would count as a sufficient answer, what to do when we cannot get one, and whether a given answer ends the inquiry. Be explicit about the points at which the procedure stops and returns a flat no, and the points at which it must hand the decision back to us because what remains is a question about what our community is willing to demand rather than a question about this student. Distinguish at every stage between the panel's standing as a body speaking for the community and the standing of an individual who was actually wronged, since our handbook treats those as interchangeable and I do not think they are. Assume the panel has no philosophical training beyond the reading we did this term, so anything that requires a distinction we would not recognize on our own has to be stated in the procedure itself.

Two constraints come from a rule the panel adopted last month, over my objection. First, every determination must classify the recommended response as either attributive — the conduct is treated as the student's own and the student is addressed as someone who can answer for it — or managerial — the conduct is treated as a condition to be changed, and we work on the condition. The rule forbids mixing the two in one determination, on the theory that a determination which both blames and treats is not honest about which of the two it is doing. I need the procedure to say what to do with the cases where the honest answer seems to be both, and whether the rule is defensible as written or should be amended. Second, the rule requires a relational test: before recommending anything, the panel must say whether the student and the community stand in a relationship whose demands it makes sense to press. I am not sure what that test is supposed to measure, and I suspect it is quietly doing something other than what its authors intended.

Then apply the procedure to three cases and show the whole path, not just the verdict. One: a student who plagiarized a paper and, when questioned, gave an account that our advisor believes is sincere and that the panel reads as coached. Two: a student with a documented condition who was verbally aggressive at a house meeting, where the conduct is real but the panel keeps reaching for the objective attitude, and I cannot tell whether that is respect or condescension. Three: a student who left a candle burning against a rule she says she did not know existed, and whose roommate was injured — the case where I expect the procedure to give an answer I will not like.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. In particular, I want to know whether an institution can hold anything like the attitudes Strawson describes or only a public imitation of them, and whether any step of the procedure actually depends on the answer. I also want to know which of our cases the procedure will handle badly, so that I can say so in the note I have to write for the seminar.

Please write in American English.
