I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and right now I'm deep in a feature about a pain researcher I'll call Dr. Reyes — she's spent years on the question of what actually makes pain bad for the person having it. Over two long interviews she walked me through how she'd rule on a whole string of cases, and I'm trying to get ahead of the one she hasn't ruled on yet. Her verdicts so far, as best I can reconstruct them:

- Ordinary stuff, a headache or a stubbed toe. Bad, full stop. She gets annoyed if you suggest it's an illusion or that we only call it bad by convention.
- Her favorite case: a patient after a stroke who reports that yes, he feels the pain, can point right to it, can rate how intense it is — but it doesn't bother him, isn't unpleasant, and he never flinches or asks anyone to make it stop. She's adamant this is NOT bad for him. Not bad at all.
- A man with chronic nerve pain from a wound that healed years ago — no ongoing damage, nothing "wrong" anymore — who describes it as agonizing and can't stop wanting it to end. Bad.
- A case where they stimulate a spot in the brain directly and the person reports a horrible burning sensation with no injury anywhere in the body. Bad.

Now the new case she hasn't addressed. It's a patient who, after a certain drug, reports the pain is intensely unpleasant — "it really hurts, it's awful" — but who has zero urge to make it stop, doesn't avoid it, doesn't care, and when you ask him straight out whether the state is bad for him just shrugs: "no, I guess not, it's not bothering me."

What I need from you:

1. Your best guess at her verdict on the new case, and the reasoning.
2. The rule she's actually running. Give me the plausible rules that fit ALL of her earlier verdicts (I can think of a few, they don't all say the same thing), and for each, what it predicts about the new case.
3. Whether any of her earlier cases already kills off the "it's bad because it signals tissue damage" reading, and which one.
4. The single further case that would best tell the surviving rules apart, and how the verdict on it would point to which rule.

And please give it to me as structured data, since I'm going to drop it into a spreadsheet. A JSON object, with these fields:

- "predicted_verdict": one of "bad", "not bad", or "genuinely underdetermined by her record"
- "best_guess_and_why": a short paragraph
- "candidate_rules": an array, each item with "rule_name" (a short label), "rule_stated" (one sentence), "fits_all_earlier_verdicts" (true/false), and "predicted_verdict_on_new_case" ("bad" or "not bad")
- "harm_rule_killed_by": the earlier case that rules out the tissue-damage reading, or "none"
- "discriminating_case": an object with "description" and "what_each_verdict_would_imply"
- "confidence": "low", "medium", or "high"

I don't need citations or anything like that — just the reasoning and the table-ready version. Also, if you think she's running a rule I haven't thought of, definately throw it in.
