Here's the question on the paper:

Q3. Darwin derives the moral sense from the social instincts, and yet he treats the word ought as something more than an instinct — a rule the agent recognises as binding even when it cuts against a strong present desire. Explain how he reconciles the two, and then say whether his account of where the moral sense comes from leaves any room for a religious foundation for morality. Refer to the Descent of Man and to what Darwin says about his own beliefs.

And here's what one student handed in (within the word limit, on time, all the rest of it):

Darwin's strategy is to take the moral sense away from the theologians and treat it as a natural product. The raw material is the social instincts. Social animals already come equipped with dispositions — parental affection, sympathy for companions, a readiness to aid one another — and Darwin thinks these were favoured by natural selection because groups whose members help one another do better than groups whose members don't. Man inherits all of it, so the difference between us and the higher animals is one of degree rather than kind.

What gets added in man is the intellect. Once a creature has memory and can reflect on its own past conduct, it can hold an earlier action up against an instinct that is still present, and feel dissatisfied when the two don't match. Darwin's answer to the puzzle about ought is that the social instincts are the enduring ones, while the appetites that make us violate them — hunger, fear, lust — are strong but short-lived. The man who grabs the food or runs from the fight is left with the persistent social instinct reasserting itself, and that after-the-fact dissatisfaction is conscience. The moral sense is therefore not a special faculty implanted in us; it's the social instincts become self-aware.

He adds a social dimension as well: we care a great deal about the good opinion of our fellows, and praise and blame, repeated over time, harden into habit and eventually into custom. This is part of why he thinks the moral sense is so much more developed in civilised communities, where these pressures are constant.

Where I'd push back on Darwin is over the reach of sympathy. His picture is basically tribal: the social instincts were shaped for small bands, and he doesn't think they carry us much past them. On his view, any concern we have for people we will never meet, or for animals, is a late cultural overlay rather than part of the moral sense itself. And he seems fairly relaxed about this. For Darwin the moral sense is at bottom a set of inherited feelings, and there is no further question to be asked about whether a given moral code is the right one.

That connects to the religious side of the question. Darwin had lost his faith, and he says so openly: once you see morality as an inherited instinct, the idea that it needs a divine lawgiver behind it simply falls away, and he was content to be described as an agnostic. So the theory doesn't merely explain morality without God — it shows that God was never needed for it in the first place.
