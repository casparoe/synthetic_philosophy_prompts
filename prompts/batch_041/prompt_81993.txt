Two philosophers of practical reason have been arguing for years about whether values are commensurable, and I want the document they should have been able to sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. The first holds that for any two options there is always a fact of the matter — one is better, one is worse, or they are equally good — and that apparent incommensurability is always ignorance of the relevant considerations, vagueness in the predicates, or indecision dressed up as a metaphysical thesis. The second holds that some pairs of options are genuinely unranked: neither better, nor worse, nor equal, so that no further information and no refinement of description will rank them, and that the first position mistakes a decision procedure for a description of value.

Write their joint statement. I want, first, a numbered list of claims both can sign: which supposed cases of incommensurability are not cases of it at all; what a comparative relation has to do to earn its place; what decision theory does and does not presuppose; and what each side concedes the other has got right. Second, a disagreements table in which every surviving dispute is stated in a formulation its own side would accept and the other side would not call a caricature. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — each with the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. I want at least these among the cruxes: what follows when one of two supposedly unranked options is improved by a hair — does the improvement create a ranking, and if it does not, what does that show about the original pair? whether unrankedness is best modelled as a third comparative relation beyond better, worse and equal (call it parity if you want a label), as vagueness, or as indeterminacy of some other kind; whether the fact that a chooser with unranked values can be led through a sequence of trades into a position she prefers less tells against the values or only against her handling of them; and whether choosing well among unranked options requires her to find a reason or to create one by committing. Then say which of the residual disagreements you judge to be merely verbal — including whether the vocabulary of incommensurability and the vocabulary of incomparability mark different things or the same thing twice.

Finally, take the following distinction as stipulated. It is mine, not the literature's, and you should say so plainly if it collapses. Call a pair of options stably unranked if no further information about them could rank them, and unstably unranked if they are unranked as things stand but a finer description or more information would settle it. Indicate which of the disagreements your document leaves standing turn on this distinction rather than on anything else, and whether the practical cases — a public body dividing a fixed budget between saving a few lives outright and improving many lives moderately, say — give either side a reason to care which of the two kinds of unrankedness is at issue.
