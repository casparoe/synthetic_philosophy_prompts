I am a novelist, and I am at work on a book that places a real and recently dead woman on the page. She was a public figure, documented well enough that readers will arrive already knowing the outline of her life, and she left survivors who are still, in the ordinary ways families do, arguing over what she was. I have drafted the essay below as a preface to my own working notes, in order to fix what I think I owe her before I go any further. I would like your comments, and I would rather have them hard than kind.

On the Three Debts

Every defense of dramatic license begins from the same confession: the record will be altered. The interesting question is which alteration counts as a betrayal. I want to propose that we owe the dead three distinct debts, that these are usually run together, and that running them together is what makes the ordinary arguments about historical fiction so unhelpful.

The first debt is to the Record: the dead person's interest in being described accurately as to what she did. The second is to the Person: her interest in being characterized fairly as to who she was. The third, and the one I take to do the real work, is to the Stake: her interest in not being conscripted — in not being made to carry a meaning she never chose to carry.

My claim is that these debts are ordered inversely to how much noise is made about them. The Record is the weakest debt, because the record is not hers. Accuracy about the past is owed to the living, who must reason from it and act on it; the dead are past reasoning. To insist on the Record in the name of the dead is usually to serve the living while borrowing the dead's authority for it. The Person is a stronger debt but a stranger one, since character is contestable even among those who knew her well, and a novelist is not obviously worse placed than a niece. The Stake is the strongest debt and the least discussed, because it is violated not by any particular falsehood but by the whole transaction of being put to use. A scrupulously accurate portrait can conscript its subject; a freely invented one may not.

To this I add the asymmetry of address. She cannot answer. And here I want to resist the usual inference from that fact. One inference is that her silence entitles us — that because she cannot object, we may proceed. The other is that because she cannot consent, we may not. Both make consent the hinge. I want to replace consent with legitimacy: ask not whether she would have consented (unknowable, and a question she might have answered badly) but whether she would have had grounds for complaint. That shift is meant to preserve the force of the third debt without requiring us to consult a person who is no longer available to be consulted.

But I am troubled by a difficulty I cannot dispose of. The interest I am protecting may be one she never had. In life she was indifferent to her reputation, or actively contemptuous of it; it is we who have made her reputation into an interest of hers by caring about it, and by being willing to trade on it. If so, the third debt is owed to a person I invented in the course of arguing about how not to invent her. Call this the problem of the posthumous career. I do not know whether it dissolves the framework or is the most interesting thing in it.

The conclusion I want to reach is that the novelist's obligation runs to the Stake, and that this licenses more invention than the defenders of the Record allow and less than the defenders of Art claim. I am not sure I have earned it.

What I want from you: first, whether the three debts are genuinely distinct or a false trichotomy — in particular, whether the Stake is just the Person seen from another angle, so that my ordering collapses. Second, whether replacing consent with grounds for complaint does any work, or merely renames the difficulty while leaving it intact. Third, whether the problem of the posthumous career is fatal to the framework or is its best part, and what follows for the rest if it is fatal. Fourth, which of the three sections is doing the least work and ought to be cut. Fifth, whether the conclusion follows — whether the Stake really licenses more invention than the Record does, or whether I have smuggled in a permission the argument does not grant. And finally, the strongest objection to the whole thing that I have failed to raise, together with the place where a reader who is not a philosopher will stop following me.
