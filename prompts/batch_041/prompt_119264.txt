Please explain a gap in the epistemic foundations of game theory that is easy to state and surprisingly hard to close: the sense in which common knowledge of rationality falls short of Nash equilibrium. Start by separating two claims that are routinely run together, namely that each player's choice is a best response to that player's own beliefs about what the others will do, and that those beliefs are correct. Then, using a two-player game of your own construction (a 2×2 game with two pure-strategy equilibria will serve), exhibit an outcome that is not an equilibrium — neither player's choice is a best response to the other's actual choice — and yet each player is rational, each knows the other is rational, and this is common knowledge; identify whose belief is false, and explain why holding a false belief is not by itself a failure of practical rationality. Next, explain why deepening the hierarchy of knowledge cannot repair the gap: say why even the full infinite hierarchy of mutual knowledge of rationality constrains only the relation between each player's beliefs and that player's own choice, and never the fit between those beliefs and the choices they are beliefs about. Then state as precisely as you can what further condition has to be imposed — a condition on the content of the players' beliefs rather than on the depth of their knowledge — and say whether it deserves to be called a rationality requirement at all, or whether it is a coherence requirement of a different kind. Finally, carry the point into extensive-form games, where the difficulty sharpens because a player's rationality must be assessed at information sets that play never reaches: contrast the reading on which calling a player rational says something about what that player actually does with the reading on which it says something about what that player would do at every node, explain which reading backward-induction reasoning requires, and say why the second reading is a substantive addition to the epistemic assumptions rather than a merely verbal one. If it helps to sharpen the last step, you may also say what becomes of the gap when one adds the assumption that the players' beliefs derive from a common prior.
