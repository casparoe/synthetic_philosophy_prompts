I teach a third-year applied ethics course on animals, food and land, and I am writing the final exam. It is closed-book and closed-notes, which matters for what I am about to ask you: I want an essay question that can only be answered by doing the accounting and the philosophy on the spot, not by recalling a position the students have read about. The question grew out of an argument I lost in the departmental kitchen last term, when a colleague told me that if I really cared about field mice I should be eating steak, and I could not say on the spot why he was wrong. For the exam I have written out a short argument that will be printed above the question. A broiler chicken yields something on the order of a kilogram and a half of meat and a steer something on the order of three hundred kilograms. Both are fed on crops, and harvesting crops kills small animals in the field, from mice and birds up to however many invertebrates you are willing to count, at some rate per hectare that I will instruct students to treat as fixed and disputed. If deaths per hectare are roughly constant, deaths per kilogram of food must fall as body size rises: beef should cost far fewer deaths per kilogram than chicken, and quite possibly fewer than bread. So the eater who counts deaths should prefer beef to chicken, and the familiar advice to eat lower on the food chain is either empty or backwards. Please draft the question, a model answer of roughly six hundred to seven hundred words, and a marking scheme out of twenty marks.

I want the question to turn on three things and not to let a student settle for less than all three. First, whether that comparison survives being redone per hectare of land, per calorie, and per kilogram of feed rather than per kilogram of meat, and whether the ordering of beef, chicken and bread reverses when the denominator changes. Second, what the argument has to assume about the lives of the field animals in order to count their deaths as harms at all, and whether that same assumption can be held while the farmed animals in the comparison are treated as the ones that matter. Third, whether the arithmetic settles anything ethically even when granted, that is, whether the conclusion follows from the numbers or from a choice about which denominator matters that was made before any number was collected. The question should be answerable in fifty minutes by a student who has done the reading but has memorized nobody's estimates, so keep the empirical demands to what can be reasoned out from the stipulated figures rather than from remembered numbers, and do not ask for citations or sources, since they cannot have them. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves a full-mark answer must contain, the marks each is worth, and a short note on how students typically get each one wrong.

Please write the question, the model answer and the marking scheme as continuous prose throughout, with no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold and no tables, because I paste the whole thing into a single email to the other graders and read the model answer aloud at the review session; where the marking scheme needs to enumerate, do it in sentences.
