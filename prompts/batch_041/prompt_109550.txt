I am putting together a session on confirmation theory and want a considered ranking rather than a survey. The starting point is the familiar puzzle: the hypothesis that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the hypothesis that all non-black things are non-ravens, so if a generalization is confirmed by its positive instances (the instance criterion), then a green apple — a non-black non-raven — ought to confirm that all ravens are black. Most people find that unacceptable, yet rejecting it appears to cost us either the instance criterion or the equivalence condition (the principle that logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same evidence).

Below are eight responses one might give. Rank them in tiers — from "I would stake the seminar on this" down to "this is not really a response at all" — according to how much of the pre-theoretic package each one manages to keep: that black ravens confirm the hypothesis, that green apples and white shoes do not, and that logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed alike. Where a response must sacrifice one of these, say which one it sacrifices and whether the sacrifice is worth it. Give each response a tier and one or two sentences of justification. Then, in a closing paragraph, tell me which single trade-off you think is forced on everyone and why.

(A) The small-but-real response: the green apple does confirm the hypothesis, but so weakly that the effect is below the threshold of what we can notice or care about; the puzzle is a fact about our psychology, not our logic.

(B) Reject the equivalence condition for confirmation: contraposition preserves truth but not evidential support, because support flows in the direction of the generalization's subject matter, so the raven-form and the non-black-form are not confirmed by the same evidence.

(C) The potential-falsifier response: an observation confirms a hypothesis only if it could have falsified it — only if the object could have been a counterexample. A raven could have been non-black; an apple could not have been a raven, so the apple is evidentially inert with respect to the hypothesis.

(D) The selection response: what the apple tells you depends on how it was obtained. If it was drawn at random from everything there is, it raises the probability of the hypothesis by a hair; if it was chosen because you already knew it was an apple, it tells you nothing. Confirmation is a property of evidence-gathering procedures, not of propositions in isolation.

(E) The projectibility response: confirmation attaches only to hypotheses framed in terms of kinds that support induction, and the class of non-black non-ravens is not such a kind. The apple is an instance of a gerrymandered class, so its membership in that class carries no evidential weight.

(F) The explanatory response: evidence confirms a hypothesis to the degree that the hypothesis would explain the evidence. That all ravens are black explains why this raven is black, but it does not explain why this apple is green — the apple's colour has its own explanation — so the apple is not confirming evidence.

(G) The contrastive response: the hypothesis is really a claim about a contrast, that ravens are black rather than some other colour, and only evidence bearing on that contrast confirms it. The apple bears on no contrast the hypothesis makes, so it is irrelevant.

(H) The formalization response: the puzzle is an artefact of modelling confirmation as a relation between a proposition and an instance. Model it instead as a measure of relevance — how much the evidence moves the probability of the hypothesis relative to a specified alternative — and the asymmetry between ravens and apples falls out of the mathematics rather than having to be stipulated.
