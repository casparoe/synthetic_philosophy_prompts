So I'm giving the toast at my old PhD advisor's retirement dinner in a couple weeks and I need help writing it. Here's my problem. She spent basically her whole career defending the idea that there is such a thing as objective chance in the world — real propensities sitting in the situations themselves, not just our degrees of belief, and not just long-run frequencies in some reference class you pick out. Half the room at this dinner are philosophers of science who've heard her give that talk a hundred times and will clock it immediately if I say anything sloppy. The other half are her two brothers, a bunch of nieces and nephews, and about six people from the choir she's sung in for twenty years, who just want a warm five-minute speech about a woman they love.

Last night my friend Dan, who is a committed subjectivist about all of this — everything is credence, there's nothing else for a probability to be — and I got into it for like two hours. It started because I'd drafted a line about how the odds were always good that she'd end up exactly where she is now, and Dan said I can't say that, because odds is either a fact about frequencies in some class of similar careers or else it's a fact about somebody's confidence, and neither of those is what I actually meant. What I meant was that there was something in her, and in the situation, that made this outcome likely in a way that has nothing to do with anyone's opinion and nothing to do with counting up similar cases. Which is, of course, her view. So now I feel like the toast has to be a toast Dan can't object to and the experts in the room won't wince at, without it turning into a lecture about interpretations of probability, because that would be a terrible retirement speech.

Can you write it for me? Something in the five minute range when read out loud. It has to actually land for the relatives — they should come away feeling like they learned a little about what she cared about, not like they got a seminar. And it can't just paper over the disagreement either; the whole thing should turn on the fact that she really did think chance was out there in the world, so the speech has a spine and isn't just generic nice things about a mentor.

One more thing, please no headings or bullet points or bold or tables — just continuous prose, like you'd write an email.
