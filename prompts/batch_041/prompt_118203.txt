Write a satirical advice column composed from inside the equal-weight view of peer disagreement — the view that when you find yourself disagreeing with an epistemic peer (someone as well informed and as competent as you are on the question at hand), you are required to move your credence toward theirs, roughly to the midpoint between the two. The columnist, whom you may call Aunt Concordia, holds the view sincerely and has resolved to run her column by it. She is not being ironic; the irony is ours. The target is the equal-weight view taken as a total policy for belief — and, at a lower heat, the professional habit of treating every disagreement as a datum to be averaged. The advice column is collateral damage. Aim for the deadpan register of a syndicated agony-aunt page, the kind that runs beneath a photograph of a kindly woman in a cardigan, and let the comedy come from the view's own consequences rather than from caricature. A conciliationist should recognize her own position in the piece and wince at where it leads, not at a misstatement of it.

What the column must do:

1. Be structurally incapable of giving advice. Aunt Concordia may not tell a correspondent what to do. She may report her own credence, the correspondent's, and the midpoint she is now obliged to occupy, and she may explain why that is the whole of what she is entitled to say. Every reply ends with a one-line ledger of the arithmetic, and the arithmetic must be correct.

2. Take the difficulty of identifying peers seriously. Do not write the easy joke, in which she treats someone who is plainly not a peer — a cousin who has read one website and denies the moon landings — as though he were one; and do not write the reverse joke, in which she is simply too sensible to be fooled. The difficulty is real: "peer" is a verdict she has to reach by judgment, on evidence that is itself contestable, and her correspondents dispute her verdicts. By her own lights she owes them conciliation about who counts as a peer, too.

3. Include a correspondent who is himself a steadfaster: he holds that in a disagreement between peers the right response is to keep one's own view, since what counts is the total evidence and the fact of disagreement is only one part of it. She must treat him as a peer, which obliges her to move halfway toward a position that forbids the halfway move. Do not resolve this. Sit in it.

4. No real names, titles, dates, or citations — no philosophers, no papers, no -isms, no laboratories. Nobody is quoted except invented correspondents; invented in-universe authorities are fine.

5. Deadpan throughout. No narrator, no winking, no "of course this is absurd", no apology on the author's behalf. The piece should be legible to a reader who has never heard of the problem of peer disagreement and a pleasure to one who has.

Give the piece headings, in this order:

- "Standing Disclosure" (40–60 words): she states her credence that she is competent to advise, and notes that she is obliged to revise it downward in proportion to the correspondents who doubt it.
- "Letter One: The House" (100–130 words), from one of two spouses who share the same evidence and disagree about whether to buy — followed by "Aunt Concordia Replies" (130–160 words) and the ledger.
- "Letter Two: The Cousin" (100–130 words) — followed by "Aunt Concordia Replies" (130–160 words) and the ledger.
- "Letter Three: The Steadfast Reader" (100–130 words) — followed by "Aunt Concordia Replies" (130–160 words) and the ledger.
- "Corrections and Revisions" (80–110 words): among the correspondence is a reader who says the column is worthless, since anyone who disagrees with her advice has, by her own standard, already cancelled half of it. She accepts the point, reports the revised credences — including her credence in the view she has been using — and signs off.

About 900 words in total.
