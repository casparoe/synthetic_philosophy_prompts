Take the role of a philosopher who works on social choice theory and has written a few toasts in her time. I'm giveing the retirement toast for my colleague next month; she spent forty years defending Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and then became one of its sharpest internal critics. Half the room will be economists and philosophers who know the theorem and its axioms inside out; the other half is her family, who just want something true and warm. Write me a five-minute toast. The theorem needs to do real structural work — it should shape what I can say about her career and what I can't, and it should give the toast its turn. Her late worry, which she never published, was that the veil-of-ignorance setup makes the chooser speak for everyone, and then Harsanyi's theorem treats that ventriloquized voice as an aggregation of preferences, so the theorem may be proving something about a single imagined self rather than about how to add us up. Use that worry, but don't turn the dinner into a seminar. Before you write, brainstorm a couple of ways to make the theorem land as affection rather than as a lecture, then give me the toast. Think carefully about the mixed audience, and please don't mention any papers, books, or citations — most of the room won't know them and the rest will find it stiff.
