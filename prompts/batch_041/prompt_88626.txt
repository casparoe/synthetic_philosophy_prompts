I've been falling down a rabbit hole on the socialist calculation debate and I need to turn it into something, because my reading group is sick of me bringing it up. Quick version of where I've landed: Mises and Hayek argue that a centrally planned economy can't really do economic calculation at all, because without market prices there's no way to aggregate the dispersed, mostly tacit knowledge about relative scarcities that's scattered across millions of people's heads and changes faster than anyone could collect it; Lange fires back that this is overblown, because a Central Planning Board can just act like a Walrasian auctioneer. It posts prices for producer goods, tells every factory manager to (a) minimize average cost and (b) produce up to the point where marginal cost equals the posted price, and then it nudges prices up or down by trial and error -- tâtonnement -- until supply and demand clear. Lange thought this "market socialism" would hit competitive-market efficiency, and Hayek's reply was basically: sure, in principle, if your board could actually know the things it needs to know, which it can't, and the whole point is that the knowledge problem is the problem, not the incentives. I find both sides weirdly underrated as comedy material, so here's what I want.

Write me the text of a leaked internal quarterly report from the Price Adjustment Directorate of a Central Planning Board in a country that has fully implemented the Lange model. It's addressed up the chain to the Presidium, deadpan and bureaucratic start to finish, around 600 words, with section headings in that flat civil-servant register -- stuff like "Section 3: Tâtonnement Progress," "Section 5: Managerial Compliance," "Annex B: Queue Reconciliation." All the humor should come from the actual economics of the debate, not from jokes about gulags or five-year-plan clichés: the board sincerely tâtonnement-ing its way toward equilibrium and always being one adjustment behind, its accounting prices drifting ever further from anything resembling real scarcity, managers gaming the output rules in ways that quietly defeat the cost-minimization instruction, the black market gradually becoming the de facto price signal that the directorate then copies while officially maintaining that no such market exists, and a slow dawning, never stated outright, that the information it needs is dispersed and tacit and simply won't centralize no matter how good the filing system is. I want someone who's read Hayek's knowledge essay and Lange's two-parter to laugh and also nod rather than wince, so exaggerate real features of the model instead of inventing new ones, and don't bother naming or citing anything -- just get the mechanism right. And please don't make it a straight hit job on Lange; the funnier and truer version is one where the board is smart and sincere and the task is still impossible. Maybe throw in one line about a price for something nobody could possibly want staying stubbornly high for three consecutive quarters, I feel like that's the detail that sells it.
