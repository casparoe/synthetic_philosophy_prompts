I'm a political theory PhD and I keep circling a puzzle about national heritage. Look at what modern states actually do: they designate heritage sites, revive "traditional" crafts and dances, fund national museums, and make citizenship applicants learn a national story — and all of it presents the nation and its customs as ancient and inherited. But the same states' own archives and universities routinely show that plenty of these traditions are recent, and some were basicly invented in the nineteenth century. What view of the nation, of tradition, and of the authority of the past does the practice presuppose? The bit that looks incoherent to me is that the practice's message to citizens is primordialist (this is who we've always been) while its mechanism — selecting, staging, reviving, teaching — is constructivist (this is who we're making). Does the practice need the traditions to be genuinely old, or does it only need people to believe they are, and does it come apart if everyone knows the belief is false? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it just fits comfortably with, and tell me whether a state could keep funding all of it after fully accepting that its traditions are invented. No reading list needed, I know the literature.
