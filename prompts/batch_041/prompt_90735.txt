I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I'm trying to write down what I actually believe about how a group ought to settle questions that hang together logically. I have a set of gut verdicts and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to — and which of them I should give up. In every case below the individual members are internally consistent; only the group's judgments come apart.

1. Hiring committee, nine members. Three questions: whether the candidate clears the research bar (R), whether she clears the teaching bar (T), and whether to hire (H), with the committee's rule being that H holds just in case R and T do. Four members vote R, T, H; three vote R, not-T, not-H; two vote not-R, T, not-H. The majority therefore affirms R (7–2) and affirms T (6–3), while the majority rejects H (5–4). My gut: we do not hire. Four of nine want to hire, and I don't think a decision to hire can be assembled out of two majorities made of different people. If someone said we must hire because the premises passed, I'd say they had substituted the committee's reasons for the committee's decision.

2. Appellate panel, three judges. Whether the statute was violated (V), whether the affirmative defense applies (D), judgment for the plaintiff just in case V and not-D. Judge 1: V, not-D, for the plaintiff. Judge 2: V, D, for the defendant. Judge 3: not-V, not-D, for the defendant. Majorities for V, for not-D, and against the plaintiff, all 2–1. My gut flips here: the plaintiff wins. The court has found the violation and found no defense, and its judgment should follow from what it has found. This is the opposite of what I said in (1), and I cannot yet say what makes it different.

3. Two-party contest, nine voters, three planks. The two platforms differ on all three planks. Three voters agree with A on all three planks and vote for A. Six voters agree with A on exactly one plank each — two on plank one, two on plank two, two on plank three — and vote for B. So A's position wins every plank 5–4, and B wins the election 6–3. My gut: B governs. A has no mandate; plank-majorities are not a majority for anything.

4. Agenda. A nine-member committee choosing among X, Y, and Z, with majorities X over Y, Y over Z, and Z over X, each 5–4. The chair, who prefers X, puts Y against Z first and then X against Y, and X wins. My gut: illegitimate. There is no majority verdict for the committee to give, and the chair has manufactured one; the honest output is that the committee cannot settle this by majority.

5. Entrenchment. A clause requiring two-thirds of the legislature for amendment; an amendment with 58 percent support fails. My gut: legitimate, and no one has been wronged. So my majoritarianism is not absolute.

When I try to state what I believe, I get roughly this: the group's answer to the question it was convened to answer is whatever a majority of members say to that question, while the reasons it gives are settled separately, and it is permissible for a group's decision and its reasons to come apart. That yields no-hire in (1), B in (3), and silence in (4), where no majority answer exists. It yields not-liable in (2), against my gut, so I add a clause: where the body's output is itself an authoritative statement of the elements — a court's findings, a standard-setter's report — the elements must be voted first and the output derived from them. That clause looks like an exception invented for a single case, and I suspect it is doing no real work.

I have also tried a formalization that has nothing to do with majorities: choose the logically consistent set of judgments closest to the members' judgments, counting each member's disagreement equally, proposition by proposition. In (1) that gives hire. The set (R, T, H) sits at total distance 10 from the nine members; (R, not-T, not-H) at 12; (not-R, T, not-H) at 14; (not-R, not-T, not-H) at 17. So the nearest consistent set is precisely the one I want to reject, and it coincides with the premise-majority answer. In (2) the same criterion leaves three consistent sets tied at distance 4 — (V, not-D, for the plaintiff), (V, D, for the defendant), (not-V, not-D, for the defendant) — so it falls silent exactly where my verdict is firmest.

What I want: a precise statement of the principle, or small set of principles, that captures these verdicts, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. Then its verdicts on cases I have not considered — in particular (a) the same nine-member profile as (1), but where the body is enacting a general rule rather than applying one to a single applicant; (b) a court whose judgment commands a majority while each judge's route to it differs, so that no single set of findings commands a majority; and (c) two further cases of your own choosing that pull the clauses apart. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate — I would rather abandon a verdict than keep a clause that exists only to save it — and whether my two attempts, majority on the question and nearest consistent set, can be reconciled or whether one must go.
