I teach political philosophy and the history of American pragmatism, and twice this year I have watched one sentence do heavy lifting in an argument. The sentence is "democracy is a way of life" — the formula usually attributed to Dewey, and in my experience recited as though it settled a question rather than raised one.

The first occasion was a departmental governance meeting. We elect our chair by a vote of the tenured faculty, and the chair then settles most matters: teaching assignments, hiring priorities, the shape of the budget. A colleague argued that this arrangement is not democratic at all, on the ground that democracy is a way of life and not a procedure. If it is a way of life, she said, then everyone whose working life is shaped by those decisions — the untenured, the graduate students, the staff — must have a share in making them, and the body should deliberate until it agrees rather than count heads. She moved that we replace the election with a consensus process.

The second occasion was a seminar, where a student used the same sentence with the opposite tendency: to argue that a state with free elections, an independent judiciary, and a lively press may nevertheless be undemocratic, because its citizens' ordinary dealings — in the office, the congregation, the household — are conducted on authoritarian lines, and a way of life is what happens in those places rather than at the ballot box.

I can see that the sentence is doing genuine work in both cases, and also that it cannot be doing the same work in both. What I want from you is not an exposition of Dewey but an inventory. Set out the distinct, precise claims the sentence could be making; for each, say what kind of claim it is — a proposal about how the word "democracy" ought to be applied, a causal claim about what sustains democratic institutions, a claim about the dispositions democracy demands of individuals, a claim about the point or goal of political association, or a piece of rhetoric that makes no claim at all — and give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, contentious, or false.

I have long glossed the sentence in lecture as follows, and I want you to say whether the gloss is a precisification of the slogan or a different thesis smuggled in under it: a group is democratic to the degree that its members have interests they knowingly share and communicate to one another, and to the degree that the group's life is open to other groups rather than closed in on itself. Treat that as one candidate among others, not as the target.

Then three further things. First, for each reading, say what would count as evidence against it, and flag the readings on which the sentence becomes true by definition and so unfalsifiable. Second, say which reading my colleague needs if her motion is to follow from the slogan, whether that reading is true, and whether her conclusion actually follows from it; do the same for the student's claim about the authoritarian household. Third, tell me whether, on the reading my colleague needs, she and the rest of us are having a genuine disagreement about how the department should be run or a merely verbal one about how to use a word, and what turns on the answer.

Please give me no citations or reading lists; I know the texts, and I would rather have the menu and the verdicts.
