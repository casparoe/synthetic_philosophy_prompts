I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist and have spent the first years of my retirement teaching myself philosophy, working mostly through social choice and political philosophy with a patient friend who teaches the subject. I have reached the point where I want to test what I have learned against something with more texture than a theorem, and I would like you to help me do that as a professional philosopher of the social sciences, not as a film reviewer. The target is 12 Angry Men, the 1957 film adapted by Reginald Rose from his own television play, and the apparatus I want to bring to it is judgment aggregation: the family of results built on the observation that a group can be impeccably even-handed in the way it votes on each of several logically connected propositions and still arrive at a combination of judgments that no individual member holds, the pattern usually called the discursive dilemma or the doctrinal paradox. I am comfortable with the formalism. I understand the setup of individuals, a set of propositions standing in entailment relations, a profile of yes-or-no judgments, and an aggregation rule, and I understand the standard contrast between a premise-based procedure, in which the group votes on the reasons and derives the verdict, and a conclusion-based procedure, in which it votes on the verdict and lets the reasons fall where they may, together with the impossibility results showing that no rule is simultaneously consistent, responsive to individual judgments, and immune to dictatorship.

What I want from you is a reading of the film in that light and, just as importantly, a reading of that light in the light of the film. Begin by setting out as precisely as you can what this jury is aggregating and under what rule, given that a criminal jury is not a majority-rule body but a unanimity-rule one: I want to know whether the discursive dilemma survives the change of rule or merely changes costume, and whether the film's movement from a quick vote on the verdict to a long argument about the individual pieces of evidence is a shift from a conclusion-based to a premise-based procedure or the reverse. Then work through the film as evidence about the theory. Which of its turns does judgment aggregation explain well, and which features of the jury room does the theory misdescribe or simply lack the vocabulary for? Three things in particular interest me. First, the decisive interventions are not votes at all but changes to the agenda and to the information the other jurors hold, so that the inputs to the aggregation are produced by the very process that aggregates them. Second, at least one juror's judgment is plainly driven by something that is not a judgment in the theory's sense, which the assumption of reason-responsive inputs seems not to anticipate. Third, the film's resolution depends on deliberation, which is exactly what undermines the independence of the jurors on which the Condorcet-style defense of juries as truth-tracking devices depends, so the film may be a better advertisement for talking things over than the epistemic arguments for juries can license. End by telling me what the film knows that the literature does not: whether there is a defensible sense in which a jury ought to be conclusion-based, or whether the film is quietly showing us a group reasoning backward from a verdict it has already reached.

Please think this through carefully before committing to an interpretation, and open with a brief brainstorming section in which you list the candidate mappings between the film's scenes and the elements of the aggregation problem, flagging which ones you expect to be strained. Then give the reading itself in continuous prose, organized around the two directions of illumination, and close with a section of honest concessions naming the scenes or the possible readings that resist your account. Write in American English, in a register suitable for a seminar paper rather than a blog post. I do not need a bibliography or citations to particular authors; I care only whether the concepts do real work here. And I would rather have a reading that takes the film seriously as a drama about twelve particular men than one that treats the jury as a worked exercise from a textbook.
