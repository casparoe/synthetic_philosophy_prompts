Ok, I write a column on science and ideas, and I keep circling back to the Hofstadter/Sander picture from Surfaces and Essences — that analogy isn't some special-occasion tool for IQ puzzles, it's the engine, that categorizing a thing just is analogizing it, that basically every noun we utter is a micro-analogy. Beautiful. Also possibly a land grab: if analogy is everything, whats not analogy?

So I want a dialogue. Two people, evenly matched, an actual back-and-forth where each one answers what the other just said instead of waiting their turn — not two essays with names slapped on them. One is a real believer in the everything-is-analogy view. The other is sharp and thinks analogy is real but downstream: a symptom, not the source. The skeptic's line I find most fun: what the believer calls analogy-making is just what prediction-by-compression feels like from the inside. You meet something new, you compress it by reusing structure from something old, and 'analogy' is just the name for the felt result. If thats right then the famous surface/essence distinction isn't a discovery about the mind at all, it's just whatever compression the case happened to buy.

A few things I want them to genuinely fight about, because I think thats where it gets juicy:

- Post-hoc analogies. People act on an analogy before they can name it — someone manages a new colleague exactly like an old friend and only figures out months later thats what they were doing. Does the believer have a good story for why the analogy runs invisibly? Does the compression story do better or worse there?
- The unfalsifiability worry. The skeptic should push: any two things are alike in indefinitely many ways depending on which features you weight, so what fixes the weighting, and what would it even look like for the theory to be wrong? The believer needs a real answer, not a hand-wave — maybe something about the goal at hand doing the constraining.
- One concrete case, walked all the way through. A doctor recognizing a rare presentation as 'the same thing' as a case from years ago; or a physicist saying a new problem is just like an old one. Let them disagree about what is actually happening in that moment, step by step.

Dont have them trade citations or name-check studies — I'm not printing a reading list and neither of them should sound like they're at a conference panel. Argue it on the merits. And dont invent quotes or pin specific claims on specific books; keep the attribution general enough that I'm not standing behind something I cant check.

Start right in with the dialogue itself — no scene-setting, no 'here is a dialogue between X and Y', just the two of them going at it, and let it stop when they've said enough rather than tying it off with a bow.
