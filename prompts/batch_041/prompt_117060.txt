By day I write novels; two afternoons a week I teach an advanced fiction workshop to twelve graduate students, and for the last three terms I have been trying to diagnose a failure that keeps recurring in their drafts and, more embarrassingly, in my own. The failure is not a shortage of feeling but a shortage of method. The writers I teach either assume an unearned omniscience, narrating other people's interiors as though they had a key to the room, or they retreat into a defensive behaviourism in which characters do nothing but smoke and look out of windows; the middle ground — building a person's inner life out of what that person says and does, plus everything anyone else can see — gets lost between the two.

At a dinner last month a friend who teaches philosophy of mind asked me why novelists never borrow anything from the science of consciousness, and she described Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology, which she called the standard third-person method in the field and which I have since looked into on my own. As I understand it: you treat everything the subject says as a text, a set of utterances to be interpreted rather than a transparent window on the mind; you take those utterances seriously and completely, so that the subject is treated as authoritative about how things seem to them, while you suspend judgment on whether things are as they seem; you bracket the metaphysical question of what the inner life is really made of, answering it neither way; and you bring in every other available source — behaviour, physiology, the setting, what the subject could not have known — to build up the picture of the world as the subject takes it to be, allowing for confabulation without concluding that the reports are worthless. That last point is what interests me: it is a licence to keep a character's testimony while treating it as evidence rather than as truth.

I would like you to turn this into a procedure my students and I can actually run on a scene. Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what a satisfactory answer to each looks like, where the sequence terminates in a verdict (the scene stands, the scene must be rebuilt, the testimony cannot be used at all), and where it has to stop and hand the decision back to the writer because the question is about the design of the book rather than about the character's world — and say plainly which of the steps are judgement calls wearing the costume of procedure. Then run it on three cases: first, a character whose account of the accident that injured her brother is fluent, detailed, and contradicted by a photograph the reader has already seen; second, a character in the middle of a dissociative episode whose testimony the reader has every reason to think is confabulated, which one of my students wants to resolve with a late reveal that the confabulation was true all along; third, the case I am afraid of, a chapter in my own novel in progress — three siblings circling their mother, who is losing her language to a neurodegenerative illness — in which the narrator renders the mother's dying interior across eight pages of lyrical first person that I love and that I suspect the procedure will not license. For that third case I want the honest verdict and the reasoning behind it, not a rescue. Close with what the procedure cannot do, and with the two or three failure modes you would expect to see once twelve people are applying it every week to two-thousand-word scenes; I need to be able to answer the student who will tell me that all of this is just a rule for writing thin characters.
