I'm teaching a grad seminar on taste and testimony this term, and I've been having a running argument with a colleague in the department — she's an epistemologist, I'm the one who does aesthetics — about what she calls (her coinage, she's very pleased with it) "borrowed taste." It started at a conference dinner where the sommelier poured something and she said "I'm told this is a great wine, so it's a great wine," and I said that isn't a way to have an opinion about wine, and she said it's exactly a way, just not mine. We've been kicking it around for weeks and I want to hand it to someone who'll lay it out properly.

So: take the six people below and rank them, worst to best, on how much they're doing something genuinely defective — by "worst" I mean most defective, the one you'd push back on hardest if you were being pedantic over dinner. Nadia doesn't drink wine, has no palate for it, and says of the pour "the sommelier says it's excellent, so it's excellent"; she'd say the same of any bottle the sommelier praised. Owen reads the reviews, comes to believe a novel is a masterpiece, tells people it's a masterpiece, and never opens it. Priya saw the painting, was unmoved by it, and now sincerely reports "it's a great painting, I just don't feel it" — she's read the criticism and thinks the critics are right. Quinn is blind from birth, accepts a friend's word that the sculpture is beautiful, and says so. Rosa hasn't seen the film, won't call it good or bad, will only say she's heard it's good, and won't recommend it to anyone. Sam saw the film, disliked it, but signs off on the review calling it a masterpiece because he defers to people with better taste than his, and says so out loud.

Two things, both in prose please — no tables, no bullet points, no headers, no bold, just write it out the way you'd write me an email, I hate getting a document back. First, the ranking, with a sentence or two per person on what's wrong or not wrong with them. Second, and this is the part I actually care about: tell me whether that ranking is stable under a distinction my colleague keeps pressing on me — between deferring about the belief ("it is good"), deferring about the response ("I feel the beauty"), and deferring about what to do ("I'll spend my evening and my money on it"). She thinks the whole puzzle is that these come apart, and that the interesting defects only live in one of them. If the ranking flips depending on which of the three we're asking about, say so and say which people flip. And if there's a defensible reading on which some of these six aren't defective at all, I want to hear it — I'd rather find that out now than at dinner.
