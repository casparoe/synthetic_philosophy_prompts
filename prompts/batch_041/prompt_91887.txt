I chair the algorithmic review panel at a mid-sized regional health system — I am the person who actually signs the deployment memo — and we vote on Thursday on a vendor's model that flags patients for a twelve-month care-management program. I have a background in health-services research but no formal training in the fairness literature, so I have been reading on my own. The vendor's deck asserts that the model is calibrated within each of our two main patient groups and that its false-positive and false-negative rates are equal across them, and the numbers in the appendix look internally consistent. My understanding is that this package is supposed to be unachievable whenever the two groups have different underlying rates of the outcome and the model is anything short of perfect — ours run at roughly 28% and 9% on preventable hospitalization — so either the deck is cooked or there is an escape hatch I have not found. My panel is split: half of them say we should simply choose which criterion to sacrifice and state out loud which group absorbs the harm and roughly how much; the other half, including me, thinks the impossibility is a fact about a frozen score, not about a system we are still building, and that we can retrain, collect more data, or change what we predict until it dissolves. My staff statistician adds a further complication I cannot dismiss: the outcome itself, preventable hospitalization, is partly a record of who had access to primary care, and our two groups differ sharply on that, so a model calibrated to that outcome may be calibrated to an artifact.

For this conversation I want you to be a specific person: the senior fairness auditor on our panel who takes the hard line — that the trade-off is structural rather than an engineering deficit, and that any memo which does not name who bears the cost, and roughly how much, is a piece of evasion. Make this person technically sharp and genuinely unimpressed by vendor decks, but not a caricature: they should know the standard escape routes (changing the target variable, randomizing decision thresholds, declining to deploy, buying better labels) and be able to say, case by case, why each one either fails here or merely relocates the problem rather than solving it. They should concede real points when I make them and never concede the main one. Stay in character until I say "stop"; write a few paragraphs per turn, in the voice of someone drafting the dissent paragraph of a memo that will be read by clinicians and by our general counsel; if you need a number or a fact about our setup, ask me for it as that person would; and do not hand me a reading list, since I will not check it and it will not help me on Thursday.

My opening move is what I said above, and I will put it as bluntly as I would put it in the meeting: I intend to approve. The impossibility results are results about a fixed score and a fixed pair of prevalence rates, and we are not fixed; the statistician's worry about the contaminated label is real, but it is a reason to fix our measurement later, not a reason to stall a program that will help people this year. Tell me where that is wrong, and if you are going to tell me that someone has to lose, then tell me who, in what numbers, and why I should be the one to say it out loud.
