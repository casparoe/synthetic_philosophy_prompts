I am writing a novel set in a benefits office in the north of England, and I have given one of my characters — a woman who spent eleven years as a work capability assessor before moving into management, and whom I am anxious not to write as a villain — a speech defending the conditionality regime on the grounds that the welfare state is at bottom an insurance scheme, and that an insurance scheme is entitled to police its own terms. Her argument runs roughly thus. We all pay in; there is no account with anyone's name on it, and what is deducted from her salary this month goes straight out to a pensioner this month, but that is an accounting detail, not a moral one. The scheme is really a mutual writ large, owned by its members and answerable to them, and the moral logic of it is contractual: membership buys a claim, and only members in good standing have claims. Conditionality, sanctions, repeated assessments, fraud investigation, the whole apparatus, she says, is simply the claims department doing its job — nobody asks a policyholder whether he deserved the fire that burned down his house, only whether the policy covers it and whether the claim is honest. She then leans on this to make the sharper move, which is that the old distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor was never a moral judgement at all but an actuarial one, and that we have simply forgotten the vocabulary in which it was once expressed. I have written the scene twice and it is far too persuasive both times, which worries me, because I cannot tell whether she has hold of something real or whether I have accidentally put a very good speech into the mouth of a position that a thoughtful reader could dismantle in a paragraph.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on her politics. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the premium, the policyholder, the risk pool, the insured contingency, the assessor, the indemnity, the exclusion clause, and the expulsion of a member — and then tell me where the mapping fails, distinguishing the failures that are idle from the ones that bear directly on the use she is making of it. I am particularly interested in what the analogy quietly imports that the welfare state does not have: I suspect that insurance is priced by risk, that it pays out on the occurrence of a contingency irrespective of desert, that it exists precisely because no one can know in advance who will claim, and that its soundness depends on the healthy subsidising the sick — all of which seem to cut against her rather than for her, though I may be misreading the mechanism. I would also be grateful if you would say whether the three things she slides between — insurance, mutual, and contract — are compatible descriptions of a single thing or whether the slide is doing illegitimate work, and whether her characterisation of the contributions is even accurate enough to build an argument on. Finally, and most usefully for me: does any conclusion in the neighbourhood survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if so, which? I am not looking for a defence of conditionality; I am looking for the weakest version of her claim that is still worth stating, and for a better analogy, if there is one, that would carry what she actually needs carried.
